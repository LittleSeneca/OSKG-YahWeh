# Source Inventory

Every source used in the OSKG-YahWeh knowledge graph. This is not a recommended reading list — it is a complete inventory of the 17 monographs, 11 primary source classes, and supplementary materials from which 723 claims were extracted.

---

## Copyright and Fair Use

**OSKG-YahWeh does not reproduce or distribute full texts of any copyrighted work.** No PDFs, no full chapter transcripts, no substantial quotations. What the project contains:

- **Chapter notes:** Original summaries and analysis written in our own language, typically 2-5KB per chapter. These capture the argument structure, evidence presented, and scholarly interactions — not the author's prose.
- **Claims:** 723 discrete, atomic assertions extracted from scholarly arguments and expressed in our own words. Each claim is a single falsifiable proposition with metadata (scholar, source work, confidence, evidence type, topic tags).
- **Edges:** Typed relationships between claims (supports, contradicts, depends on, challenged by) representing scholarly argument structure, not the underlying text.

This is a **transformative enrichment project**. The knowledge graph adds substantial value beyond the original works: structured metadata, cross-scholar contradiction detection, dependency mapping, convergence scoring, and synthesis from graph structure. No human reads the OSKG as a substitute for the original monographs. The graph is a tool for navigating and analyzing scholarly argumentation — it presupposes and drives readers toward the original works.

The project operates within established fair use principles for scholarly knowledge graphs. The ORKG project at Leibniz University Hannover has established this pattern at scale (millions of papers) with institutional legal review. OSKG-YahWeh applies the same principles at depth to a small corpus in the humanities.

### 1. Mark S. Smith — *The Early History of God: Yahweh and the Other Deities in Ancient Israel* (2nd ed., 2002)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Publisher** | Eerdmans |
| **Role in OSKG** | **Central framework.** The convergence-and-differentiation model that structures the graph's understanding of how Israelite religion related to Canaanite religion. Smith argues Yahweh absorbed El, Baal, and Asherah through a process of convergence (borrowing traits) and differentiation (denying the borrowing). |
| **Chapters noted** | Foreword, Preface, Introduction, Ch 1-7 |
| **Key contributions** | Three-stage El-Yahweh convergence model; Asherah as Yahwistic cult symbol (not goddess); development of Israelite monolatry from Canaanite polytheism; the aniconic tradition as dominant but not exclusive |
| **Notes** | `notes/theology/Smith Chapter 0` through `Smith Chapters 5-7` |

### 9. Yehezkel Kaufmann — *The Religion of Israel* (1960, trans. Greenberg)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|

| **Role in OSKG** | **Primary counter-position.** Kaufmann's thesis that Israelite monotheism was a revolutionary break from the beginning — not an evolution from polytheism — is the graph's most contradicted position (37+ edges). ### 12. Konrad Schmid — *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Publisher** | Eerdmans |
| **Role in OSKG** | **Maximum-damage counter-position.** Schmid argues the Pentateuch achieved its current form in the Persian period. If correct, 59% of the graph's evidential foundation collapses — biblical texts cannot be read as pre-exilic evidence for polytheism. |
| **Chapters noted** | 7 + Preface |
| **Key contributions** | The Pentateuch is Persian-period in its final form; Torah as imperial authorization document; the Hebrew Bible preserves opposing voices; canon pluriformity and relativity; the OT/HB distinction is historically contingent |

### 13. Jeffrey H. Tigay — *You Shall Have No Other Gods: Israelite Religion in the Light of Hebrew Inscriptions* (1986)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Publisher** | Scholars Press |
| **Role in OSKG** | **Empirical challenge.** Tigay's onomastic data (94%+ Yahwistic names in pre-exilic inscriptions) is the strongest single empirical challenge to the "Israel was thoroughly polytheistic" narrative. The graph absorbs his data while noting his own methodological caution. |
| **Chapters noted** | 3 (Introduction + Ch I + Ch II + Conclusions) |
| **Key contributions** | The epigraphic onomasticon is MORE Yahwistic than the biblical onomasticon; Samaria ostraca, Lachish letters, and Arad ostraca all show near-100% Yahwistic names; onomastics do not disprove polytheism but require explanation; popular Yahwistic devotion was the public norm centuries before the exile |
| **Notes** | `notes/theology/Tigay — Introduction` through `Tigay — Chapter II + Conclusions` |

| **Kuntillet Ajrud Inscriptions** | c. 800 BCE | Hebrew | "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" / "Yahweh of Teman and his Asherah." Central evidence for Asherah debate. |

| **Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (DSS/LXX/MT)** | Various | Hebrew/Greek | The textual keystone. DSS reads "sons of God"; MT altered to "sons of Israel." |
| **Merneptah Stele** | c. 1208 BCE | Egyptian | Earliest mention of "Israel" as a people. Contains El, not Yahweh. |
| **Elephantine Papyri** | 5th c. BCE | Aramaic | Jewish colony in Egypt worshipping Yahu alongside Anat-Yahu. Polytheistic Yahwism after monotheism's emergence. |
| **Ugaritic Baal Cycle** | 14th-13th c. BCE | Ugaritic | The comparative foundation. El's divine council, Baal's Chaoskampf, Asherah as consort — the religious world out of which Israel emerged. |
| **Mesha Stele** | c. 840 BCE | Moabite | "I took the vessels of Yahweh" — extrabiblical confirmation of Yahweh worship and the Moabite god Chemosh. |

| **Lachish Ostraca** | c. 590 BCE | Hebrew | Military correspondence with 100% Yahwistic theophoric names. Tigay's key evidence. |
| **Ketef Hinnom Amulets** | c. 600 BCE | Hebrew | Oldest surviving biblical text (Priestly Blessing). Text replaces image — early evidence of aniconic turn. |
| **Siloam Inscription** | c. 700 BCE | Hebrew | Hezekiah's Tunnel. Context for Assyrian crisis and centralization. |
| **Gezer Calendar** | c. 925 BCE | Hebrew | Early Hebrew inscription; agricultural context of folk religion. |
| **Kurkh Monolith** | c. 853 BCE | Assyrian | Shalmaneser III's battle of Qarqar; mentions Ahab of Israel. |
| **Black Obelisk** | c. 825 BCE | Assyrian | Depicts Jehu of Israel; extrabiblical confirmation of Israelite king. |
| **Ekron Inscription** | c. 675 BCE | Philistine | Names the goddess Ptgyh; regional religious context. |
