# Primary Source: Siloam Inscription

**Location:** Siloam Tunnel (Hezekiah's Tunnel), City of David, East Jerusalem
**Date:** c. 700 BCE (late 8th century BCE, based on paleography)
**Discovery:** 1880 by Jacob Eliahu (a 16-year-old pupil of Conrad Schick), found carved into the tunnel wall about 19 feet from the Pool of Siloam. Removed from the tunnel and taken to Istanbul.
**Medium:** Limestone wall panel inscription, 6 lines
**Script:** Paleo-Hebrew alphabet (regional variant of Phoenician script)
**Present location:** Istanbul Archaeology Museum, Turkey
**Standard reference:** KAI 189

---

## Overview

The Siloam inscription is the **only known ancient Israelite or Judahite inscription commemorating a public construction work** — a genre common in Egyptian and Mesopotamian archaeology but almost completely absent from Israel and Judah. It records the completion of Hezekiah's Tunnel, an engineering feat that brought water from the Gihon Spring outside Jerusalem's walls into the Pool of Siloam inside the city, securing Jerusalem's water supply against the anticipated Assyrian siege.

The tunnel is mentioned in 2 Kings 20:20 ("Hezekiah... made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city") and 2 Chronicles 32:30 ("Hezekiah closed the upper outlet of the waters of Gihon and directed them down to the west side of the city of David"). The inscription provides archaeological confirmation of this biblical account and evidence for state-level engineering and literacy in 8th-century Jerusalem.

---

## Translation

**Scholarly consensus translation (after Sayce's initial reading):**

> [...when the tunnel was driven through.] And this was the manner of the tunneling: while the stone-cutters were wielding the axe, each man toward his fellow, and while there were still three cubits to be cut through, the voice of a man calling to his fellow could be heard — because there was a fissure (Hebrew: *zadah*) in the rock running right and left. And on the day the tunnel was cut through, the stone-cutters struck, each man toward his fellow, axe against axe. And the water flowed from the source to the pool for one thousand two hundred cubits. And one hundred cubits was the height of the rock above the heads of the stone-cutters.

---

## Key Features

1. **Two teams digging toward each other**: The inscription describes workers starting from both ends of the tunnel and meeting in the middle. Archaeological examination confirms this — the tunnel has a sharp right-angle turn at the meeting point where the two teams corrected course to find each other.

2. **"The voice of a man calling to his fellow"**: The workers could HEAR each other through the rock when only three cubits remained — a vivid detail that humanizes the engineering achievement.

3. **1,200 cubits**: The tunnel is approximately 533 meters (1,750 feet) long. A cubit of ~44 cm gives very close to 1,200 cubits.

4. **No mention of YHWH or the king**: Unlike Mesopotamian royal inscriptions, the Siloam text names neither the king (Hezekiah) nor the deity (YHWH). This silence is striking — and characteristic of Israelite inscriptions, which rarely claim royal credit for public works.

5. **The "zadah" problem**: The Hebrew word *zadah* on line 3 is of uncertain meaning — possibly "fissure," "deviation," or "overlap." It describes the geological feature that helped the two teams align.

---

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Literacy in 8th-century Jerusalem**: A city that can inscribe a 6-line commemorative text on a tunnel wall has a literate administrative class. This supports the view that written texts (including early biblical materials) could have been produced in Jerusalem during Hezekiah's reign.

2. **State infrastructure and royal power**: The tunnel required significant engineering knowledge, labor organization, and resources — evidence for the administrative capacity of the Judahite monarchy. Hezekiah could mobilize labor and capital for major defensive works.

3. **The absence of YHWH**: The inscription's silence about God is notable. It describes a purely human achievement — workers swinging axes, voices calling through rock, water flowing. This contrasts sharply with the biblical account in 2 Chronicles 32:30, which attributes the project to Hezekiah's faithfulness. The inscription represents a different genre of writing than biblical narrative — secular, commemorative, focused on engineering details rather than theological meaning.

4. **Only Israelite construction inscription**: The uniqueness of this text is itself evidence. Why did Israel and Judah NOT produce the kind of royal building inscriptions common in Egypt and Mesopotamia? Possibly because Israelite royal theology did not permit kings to claim credit in the same way — or because such inscriptions existed but were destroyed.
