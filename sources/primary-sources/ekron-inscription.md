# Primary Source: Ekron Royal Dedicatory Inscription

**Location:** Tel Miqne (ancient Ekron), Philistine plain, Israel
**Date:** First half of 7th century BCE (most likely second quarter, reign of Ikausu/Achish, c. 675 BCE)
**Discovery:** Excavated 1996 by Seymour Gitin and Trude Dothan (Albright Institute Tel Miqne excavations), found in primary context in temple ruins
**Medium:** Rectangular limestone block (H: 39 cm, W: 60 cm, D: 26 cm)
**Script:** Phoenician alphabet — a northern Canaanite dialect similar to Phoenician and Old Byblian
**Present location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem (IAA 1997-2912)
**Standard reference:** KAI 286

---

## Overview

The Ekron inscription is the first connected body of text identified as "Philistine" and one of the most important archaeological finds of the 20th century in Israel. Discovered in 1996 in the ruins of a temple at Tel Miqne (Ekron), it names five kings of Ekron in a father-to-son dynastic sequence — Ya'ir, Ada, Yasid, Padi, and Ikausu (Achish). Padi and Ikausu also appear in Neo-Assyrian royal annals (Sennacherib and Esarhaddon), securely dating the inscription to the first half of the 7th century BCE.

The inscription confirms the identification of Tel Miqne as biblical Ekron (Joshua 13:3, 1 Samuel 6:17) and provides the primary epigraphic evidence for the Philistine royal dynasty. The temple is dedicated to a goddess whose name is fragmentary: `pt[ ]yh` — possibly Pidray (daughter of Baal), an unknown Philistine deity, or a form of the goddess name.

## Key Features

1. **Five-generation dynasty**: The inscription names five kings of Ekron in succession: Ya'ir → Ada → Yasid → Padi → Achish (Ikausu). This is the only such dynastic list from a Philistine city.

2. **The goddess pt[ ]yh**: The third letter is damaged. Scholarly proposals include:
   - **Pidray** (*ptryh*) — Baal's daughter in Ugaritic texts, "daughter of mist/light"
   - **Ptgyh** — a previously unknown Philistine deity
   - **Ptnyh** — another proposed reading
   - **Ptyh** — a simple theophoric name

   The dedication is "to pt[ ]yh, his lady" (*ʾdth* = "his lady"), indicating a goddess as the temple's patron deity.

3. **Ashart at Ekron**: Other short inscriptions from the same excavation include *kdš l'šrt* — "dedicated to the goddess Asherat" (Asherah/Astarte). The Philistine pantheon included Canaanite goddesses alongside possibly unique Philistine deities.

4. **Phoenician language in Philistine context**: Despite being a Philistine city, the inscription is written in a northern Canaanite dialect closely resembling Phoenician and Old Byblian. This suggests cultural assimilation — by the 7th century, Ekron's elites used Phoenician script and language for official monuments, even while the city retained its Philistine identity.

5. **Confirms biblical Ekron**: The inscription securely identifies Tel Miqne as ancient Ekron, one of the five Philistine city-states (with Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, and Gath).

---

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Regional polytheism in the 7th century**: Ekron's temple was dedicated to a goddess — confirming that polytheistic worship was the norm in Philistine cities contemporary with late monarchic Judah. This is the religious environment Josiah's reforms were reacting against.

2. **"His lady" — goddess worship**: The phrase *ʾdth* ("his lady") used for the temple's patron goddess parallels the language used for Asherah in Judahite inscriptions. The pattern of a male ruler dedicating a temple to a goddess "his lady" is structurally similar to the YHWH-Asherah pairing.

3. **Neo-Assyrian context**: Padi and Ikausu appear in Assyrian annals from 701 and 673-667 BCE. This places the inscription in the period when Assyria dominated the entire region — including Judah under Manasseh. Ekron was a loyal Assyrian vassal, and its prosperity (including this temple construction) was tied to Assyrian favor.
