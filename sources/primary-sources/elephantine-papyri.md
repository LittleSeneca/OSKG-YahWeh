# Primary Source: Elephantine Papyri

**Location:** Elephantine Island (Yeb), Nile River, Aswan, Egypt — a Persian-period border fortress
**Date:** 495-399 BCE (Achaemenid Persian period, mostly reign of Darius II, 423-404 BCE)
**Discovery:** First papyri acquired by dealers 1815-1904; formal excavations by German (Rubensohn, 1906-1908) and French (Clermont-Ganneau, 1906-1911) expeditions
**Medium:** Aramaic papyri and ostraca
**Script:** Imperial Aramaic
**Key collections:** Berlin State Museums, Brooklyn Museum, Egyptian Museum (Cairo), British Museum, Bodleian Library (Oxford)
**Standard references:** Cowley 1923 (87 papyri, the foundational edition); TAD = Porten & Yardeni, *Textbook of Aramaic Documents from Ancient Egypt* (4 vols, 1986-1999, the current scholarly standard); Kraeling 1953 (Brooklyn Museum papyri)

---

## Overview

The Elephantine papyri are the single most important primary source for the Truth Project. They document an actual, lived Judean community in 5th-century BCE Egypt that **worshipped Yhwh (called "Ya'u" in the Aramaic texts) alongside other deities** — including a goddess named Anat-Bethel (or Anat-Yahu) — in a functioning temple with an altar, sacrifices, and a priesthood. This was not fringe folk practice. It was institutional polytheistic Yahwism, complete with a temple, priests, and legal oath formulas.

The community was a military garrison (a "Jewish garrison" of the Persian army) stationed at the southern border of Egypt. Their temple to Ya'u had existed since before the Persian conquest — "already in the days of the kings of Egypt our fathers had built that temple" — and Cambyses (525 BCE) had left it untouched even while destroying Egyptian temples. In 410 BCE, the temple was destroyed in a conspiracy between Egyptian priests of the god Khnub (Khnum) and the corrupt Persian governor Waidrang. The papyri document the community's desperate three-year campaign (410-407 BCE) to get permission to rebuild.

## Key Texts

### 1. The Temple Petition (Papyrus 30 / TAD A4.7)

**Date:** 408 BCE (17th year of Darius II)
**Authors:** Yedoniah (chief priest) and his colleagues, the priests of Yeb
**Addressee:** Bigvai (Bagohi), Persian governor of Judaea
**Copies also sent to:** Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat, governor of Samaria

**Full English Translation (Cowley 1923, Papyrus 30):**

> To our lord Bigvai, governor of Judaea, your servants Yedoniah and his colleagues, the priests who are in Yeb the fortress. The health of your lordship may the God of Heaven seek after exceedingly at all times, and give you favour before Darius the king and the princes of the palace more than now a thousand times, and may he grant you long life, and may you be happy and prosperous at all times.
>
> Now your servant Yedoniah and his colleagues depose as follows: In the month of Tammuz in the 14th year of Darius the king, when Arsames departed and went to the king, the priests of the god Khnub, who is in the fortress of Yeb, were in league with Waidrang who was governor here, saying: "The temple of Ya'u the God, which is in the fortress of Yeb let them remove from there."
>
> Then that Waidrang, the reprobate, sent a letter to his son Nephayan who was commander of the garrison in the fortress of Syene saying: "The temple which is in Yeb the fortress let them destroy." Then Nephayan led out the Egyptians with the other forces. They came to the fortress of Yeb with their weapons, they entered that temple, they destroyed it to the ground, and the pillars of stone which were there they broke. Also it happened, five gateways of stone, built with hewn blocks of stone, which were in that temple they destroyed, and their doors they lifted off, and the hinges of those doors were bronze, and the roof of cedar wood, all of it with the rest of the furniture and other things which were there, all of it they burnt with fire, and the basons of gold and silver and everything that was in that temple, all of it, they took and made their own.
>
> Already in the days of the kings of Egypt our fathers had built that temple in the fortress of Yeb, and when Cambyses came into Egypt he found that temple built, and the temples of the gods of Egypt all of them they overthrew, but no one did any harm to that temple.
>
> When this was done, we with our wives and our children put on sack-cloth and fasted and prayed to Ya'u the Lord of Heaven, who let us see our desire upon that Waidrang. The dogs tore off the anklet from his legs, and all the riches he had gained were destroyed, and all the men who had sought to do evil to that temple, all of them, were killed and we saw our desire upon them.
>
> Also before this, at the time when this evil was done to us, we sent a letter to your lordship and to Johanan the high priest and his colleagues the priests who are in Jerusalem, and to Ostanes the brother of Anani, and the nobles of the Jews. They have not sent any letter to us. Also since the month of Tammuz in the 14th year of Darius the king till this day we wear sack-cloth and fast. Our wives are made widow-like, we do not anoint ourselves with oil and we drink no wine. Also from that time till the present day in the 17th year of Darius the king, neither meal-offering, incense, nor sacrifice do they offer in that temple.
>
> Now your servants Yedoniah and his colleagues and the Jews, all of them inhabitants of Yeb, say as follows: If it seem good to your lordship, take thought for that temple to build it, since they do not allow us to build it. Look upon your well-wishers and friends who are here in Egypt, and let a letter be sent from you to them concerning the temple of the God Ya'u to build it in the fortress of Yeb as it was built before, and they shall offer the meal-offering and incense and sacrifice on the altar of the God Ya'u on your behalf, and we will pray for you at all times, we, our wives, our children, and the Jews, all who are here, if they do so that that temple be re-built, and it shall be a merit to you before Ya'u the God of Heaven more than a man who offers to him sacrifice and burnt-offerings worth as much as the sum of a thousand talents. As to gold, about this we have sent and given instructions.
>
> Also the whole matter we have set forth in a letter in our name to Delaiah and Shelemiah the sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria. Also of all this which was done to us Arsames knew nothing.
>
> On the 20th of Marheshwan the 17th year of Darius the king.

### 2. The Passover Letter (Papyrus 21 / TAD A4.1)

**Date:** 419 BCE (5th year of Darius II)
**Author:** Hananiah (a Jewish official with Persian imperial authority)
**Addressees:** Yedoniah and his colleagues, the Jewish garrison at Yeb

**Full English Translation (Cowley 1923, Papyrus 21):**

> To my brethren, Yedoniah and his colleagues the Jewish garrison, your brother Hananiah. The welfare of my brethren may the gods seek.
>
> Now this year, the 5th year of King Darius, word was sent from the king to Arsames, saying: "In the month of Tybi let there be a Passover for the Jewish garrison."
>
> Now you accordingly count fourteen days of the month Nisan and keep the Passover, and from the 15th day to the 21st day of Nisan are seven days of Unleavened Bread. Be clean and take heed. Do no work on the 15th day and on the 21st day. Also drink no beer, and anything at all in which there is leaven do not eat, from the 15th day from sunset till the 21st day of Nisan, seven days, let it not be seen among you; do not bring it into your dwellings, but seal it up during those days. Let this be done as Darius the king commanded.
>
> (Address:) To my brethren Yedoniah and his colleagues the Jewish garrison, your brother Hananiah.

### 3. Polytheistic Yahwism: The Divine Economy at Elephantine

From Cowley's Introduction (1923, pp. xviii-xix), based on the actual papyri:

**Temple fund divided between multiple gods (Papyrus 22, lines 123-125):**

The temple treasury was **divided between Ya'u and Anathbethel in nearly equal shares**, with Ishumbethel receiving a smaller portion. This is not private superstition — it is the official allocation of temple funds.

**Oath formulas invoking multiple deities:**

- **Papyrus 44, line 5**: An oath recorded "**by the temple and by Anathya'u**"
- **Papyrus f**: A man challenged to swear "**by Herembethel the god**"
- **Papyrus 14, line 5**: A Jewish woman swears **by Sati the Egyptian goddess** in a transaction with an Egyptian
- Standard legal oaths by "**Ya'u the God**" — but only as one option among several

**Cowley's own assessment:**

> "It was not a case of falling away from a monotheistic ideal, but a continuation of the pre-exilic popular beliefs." (Introduction, p. xix)

> "It would seem that besides Ya'u they recognized Anath, Bethel, Ishum and Herem. There may have been others, but it is at least a coincidence that we have the names of five gods and that there were five gates to the temple." (Papyrus 30, line 9)

> On Anat-Yahu: "It has been suggested that Anathya'u was intended as a consort of Ya'u — the Queen of Heaven (Jer. 44:17), as He was the God of Heaven." (Introduction, p. xix)

## Key Features

1. **A functioning temple to Ya'u outside Jerusalem, with animal sacrifice, priesthood, and altar** — in direct contradiction to the Deuteronomistic principle that sacrifice could only occur in Jerusalem. This was a pre-Deuteronomistic temple ("built in the days of the kings of Egypt") that Cambyses had spared.

2. **Ya'u worshipped alongside other deities as a matter of official, institutional practice** — not private folk religion. Temple funds were literally divided between Ya'u and a goddess (Anathbethel). Legal oaths incorporated multiple divine names. Personal names included Bethelnathan and Heremnathan alongside Yahwistic names.

3. **The Jerusalem temple authorities IGNORED them.** The Elephantine priests sent a letter to Johanan the high priest in Jerusalem asking for support. "They have not sent any letter to us." Three years of silence from Jerusalem while they mourned in sackcloth. This is devastating evidence that post-exilic Jerusalem already regarded the Elephantine temple as illegitimate — not because of polytheism (which Jerusalem itself may have tolerated) but because of its LOCATION outside Jerusalem, violating Deuteronomic centralization.

4. **The Persian king and the "Passover"**: The Passover Letter shows that the Persian imperial administration took an active role in regulating Jewish religious practice — King Darius II himself (via his satrap Arsames) ordered the Jewish garrison to observe Passover. Persian kings functioned as patrons and regulators of local cults.

5. **"The gods" in a Jewish letter**: Hananiah, writing to fellow Jews, opens with "The welfare of my brethren may **the gods** seek" — using a plural form (N\\"vK / 'elahayya'). Cowley notes this had "perhaps become stereotyped in use, and had ceased to be consciously regarded as plural, as was the case with Hebrew Elohim."

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Polytheistic Yahwism was institutional, not fringe**: The Elephantine community were not uneducated peasants. They were a military garrison with a temple, priests, scribes, and legal archives. Their polytheism was embedded in the structure of their religious economy — temple funds literally divided between Ya'u and a goddess, oath formulas incorporating multiple divine names. This is the strongest evidence in the entire corpus that pre-exilic and early post-exilic Yahwism was compatible with polytheistic practice at the institutional level.

2. **Jerusalem's silence is as important as Elephantine's practice**: The Jerusalem high priest Johanan received their letter and did nothing. For three years, while the Elephantine Jews mourned in sackcloth and abstained from wine, Jerusalem said nothing. This cannot be explained simply by theological disagreement about polytheism — Jerusalem's concern was the legitimacy of any temple outside Jerusalem. Elephantine was collateral damage in the centralization program.

3. **Anat-Yahu as consort**: Cowley, writing in 1923, already connected Anat-Yahu to the "Queen of Heaven" in Jeremiah 44:17. The Elephantine papyri provide direct documentary evidence for the goddess-worship that the biblical texts polemicize against. When Jeremiah condemns Judeans for worshipping the "Queen of Heaven," the Elephantine papyri show us exactly what that looked like in practice.

4. **The persistence of pre-exilic religion**: The Elephantine community had been in Egypt since before the Babylonian exile. Their religion preserved features that the exilic and post-exilic community in Babylon/Jerusalem reformed away — the temple outside Jerusalem, the goddess consort, the recognition of multiple divine beings. Elephantine is a window into what Israelite religion looked like BEFORE the Deuteronomistic reforms fully took hold.

5. **Persian imperial religion policy**: The fact that Darius II ordered the observance of Passover demonstrates that Persian kings actively regulated Jewish religious practice. This external imperial pressure may have been a significant factor in the standardization and codification of Jewish religious law — the Torah as imperial authorization.

## TAD Translation: Temple Petition (A4.8 / Second Draft, Nov 25, 407 BCE)

Extracted from Porten & Yardeni, *Textbook of Aramaic Documents from Ancient Egypt*, Vol. 1 (1986), p. 75. TAD uses different transliterations than Cowley: "Bagohi" (not Bigvai), "Vidranga" (not Waidrang), "Naphaina" (not Nephayan), "YHW" (not Ya'u).

> To our lord Bagohi, your servant Jedaniah the priest and his colleagues the priests who are in Elephantine the fortress and the Jews, all of them.
>
> May the God of Heaven seek after the welfare of our lord at all times, grant you favor before King Darius and the princes more than now, and give you long life, and may you be happy and strong at all times.
>
> Now, your servant Jedaniah and his colleagues the priests and the Jews say thus: In the month of Tammuz year 14 of King Darius, when Arsames had departed and gone to the king, then the priests of Khnub the god who are in Elephantine the fortress gave silver and goods to Vidranga the Chief who was here, saying, "Let them remove from there the Temple of YHW the God which is in Elephantine the fortress."
>
> That Vidranga, the wicked, sent a letter to Naphaina his son, who was garrison commander in Syene the fortress, saying, "Let them demolish the Temple of YHW the God which is in Elephantine the fortress." Then, that Naphaina led the Egyptians with the other troops. They came to the fortress of Elephantine with their weapons, broke into that Temple, demolished it to the ground, and the stone pillars which were there — they smashed them.
>
> Moreover, it happened that they demolished five great gateways, built of hewn stone, which were in that Temple. And their standing doors, and the bronze hinges of those doors, and the roof of that Temple, all of it cedarwood, with the rest of the fittings and other things which were there — all of these they burned with fire. But the gold and silver basins and other things which were in that Temple — all of these they took and made their own.
>
> And during the days of the kings of Egypt our fathers had built that Temple in Elephantine the fortress and when Cambyses entered Egypt he found that Temple built.

### Cowley vs. TAD: Translation Comparison

The TAD translation is more precise than Cowley 1923. Key differences:

| Element | Cowley 1923 | TAD 1986 |
|---------|------------|----------|
| God's name | "Ya'u" | "YHW" |
| Governor | "Waidrang" | "Vidranga" |
| His son | "Nephayan" | "Naphaina" |
| Governor of Judah | "Bigvai" | "Bagohi" |
| Egyptian god | "Khnub" | "Khnub" (same) |
| Temple description | "pillars of stone which were there they broke" | "stone pillars which were there — they smashed them" |
| Waidrang's epithet | "the reprobate" | "the wicked" |

TAD's translation of the destruction is more vivid: "broke into that Temple" vs. Cowley's "entered that temple." TAD also renders the Cambyses passage more clearly.

## Porten 1996: The Rebuilt Temple — No Animal Sacrifice Allowed

Extracted from Bezalel Porten, *The Elephantine Papyri in English* (Brill, 1996). Porten's commentary on the joint reply of Bagavahya (Bagohi) and Delaiah (TAD A4.9, p. 83) reveals a crucial detail absent from Cowley's 1923 edition:

The governors' authorization for reconstruction (TAD A4.9, Cowley 32) specified the temple be rebuilt "on its site as it was formerly" but the offer of payment letter (TAD A4.10, Cowley 33, Porten p. 84) explicitly **excluded animal sacrifice**:

> "And sheep, ox, and goat as burnt-offering are not made there but only incense and meal-offering they offer there."

Porten's note (p. 84 n. 15):

> "The implicit exclusion of animal sacrifices in the recommendation of Bagavahya and Delaiah was here spelled out explicitly."

This is a critical finding for the Truth Project: the Elephantine temple was rebuilt, but **without the animal sacrifices that had defined its pre-destruction cult**. The Persians and Jerusalem authorities collaborated to restrict Elephantine's temple to a lesser form of worship — incense and grain offerings only. This was likely a compromise: the Jerusalem temple retained its monopoly on animal sacrifice (Deuteronomistic centralization), while Elephantine got its temple back in a diminished form. The Elephantine Jews accepted these terms. After 407 BCE, there is no further mention of the temple in the surviving papyri — suggesting the compromise held.
