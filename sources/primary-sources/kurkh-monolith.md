# Primary Source: Kurkh Monolith (Shalmaneser III)

**Location:** Kurkh (modern Üçtepe Höyük), Bismil district, Diyarbakir province, Turkey — on the right bank of the Tigris
**Date:** c. 852 BCE (reign of Shalmaneser III, r. 858-824 BCE)
**Discovery:** 1861 by John George Taylor, British Consul-General in the Ottoman Eyalet of Kurdistan
**Medium:** Assyrian stele (limestone monolith) with cuneiform inscription
**Script:** Akkadian cuneiform
**Present location:** British Museum, London
**Battle recorded:** Battle of Qarqar (853 BCE)

---

## Overview

The Kurkh Monolith of Shalmaneser III contains the description of the **Battle of Qarqar** (853 BCE), in which an anti-Assyrian coalition of twelve Levantine kings faced the Assyrian army. The inscription lists the coalition members and their forces, including **"A-ha-ab-bu Sir-ila-a-a"** — Ahab the Israelite — providing the **first extra-biblical reference to a named Israelite king**.

Ahab is recorded as contributing 2,000 chariots and 10,000 foot soldiers to the coalition — making Israel the largest chariot contributor among the allies. This confirms Israel's military significance in the mid-9th century BCE, consistent with the biblical portrayal of Ahab as a major regional power (1 Kings 16-22).

The monolith is one of only four known Iron Age inscriptions containing the name "Israel" (with the Merneptah Stele, Mesha Stele, and Tel Dan Stele).

---

## The Ahab Passage

**English Translation (scholarly consensus, from the Assyrian annals):**

> I approached the city of Qarqar. I razed, destroyed, and burned Qarqar, his royal city. 1,200 chariots, 1,200 cavalry, 20,000 soldiers of Hadadezer of Damascus; 700 chariots, 700 cavalry, 10,000 soldiers of Irhuleni of Hamath; **2,000 chariots, 10,000 soldiers of Ahab the Israelite**; 500 soldiers of Byblos; 1,000 soldiers of Egypt; 10 chariots, 10,000 soldiers of Irqanatu; 200 soldiers of Matinu-ba'al of Arwad; 200 soldiers of Usanatu; 30 chariots, [N] thousand soldiers of Adunu-ba'al of Shianu; 1,000 camels of Gindibu the Arabian; [N] hundred soldiers of Ba'asa son of Ruhubi the Ammonite — these twelve kings he took as his allies. They marched against me to do battle. With the supreme forces which Ashur my lord had given me and with the mighty weapons which the divine standard going before me had granted me, I fought with them... I defeated them from the city of Qarqar to the city of Gilzau. I felled with the sword 14,000 of their soldiers. Like Adad I rained down a flood upon them. I scattered their corpses far and wide, and filled the plain with their widespread soldiers.

## Key Features

1. **"Ahab the Israelite"**: *A-ha-ab-bu Sir-ila-a-a* — the first named Israelite king in any extra-biblical source. Ahab ruled c. 874-853 BCE according to biblical chronology, placing the battle near the end of his reign. He died shortly after (1 Kings 22:29-38).

2. **Israel's 2,000 chariots**: This is the largest chariot contingent in the entire coalition — more than Damascus (1,200) and Hamath (700). If accurate, it indicates Israel possessed significant military resources under Ahab, consistent with the biblical portrayal of the Omride dynasty's wealth and power.

3. **The coalition against Assyria**: Twelve kings including Israel, Aram-Damascus, Hamath, and Phoenician city-states allied against Shalmaneser. This unprecedented coalition suggests Assyria was perceived as an existential threat — and that Israel and Aram-Damascus (normally enemies) could cooperate when needed.

4. **Assyrian propaganda**: Shalmaneser claims victory ("I defeated them") but the fact that he returned to Assyria and had to fight the same coalition again in subsequent years suggests the battle was a stalemate at best. The 14,000 enemy dead is standard Assyrian hyperbole.

5. **The "House of Omri"**: Assyrian records after Ahab refer to Israel as *Bit Humri* ("House of Omri"), regardless of which dynasty ruled. This is the same designation used in the Black Obelisk and later Assyrian annals.

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Israel as a significant regional power in the 9th century**: The Kurkh Monolith confirms that Israel under the Omride dynasty was a major military player, capable of fielding 2,000 chariots. This aligns with the archaeological evidence for Omride building projects (Megiddo, Samaria, Hazor) and contradicts minimalist claims that Israel was a minor chiefdom.

2. **The political world of the 9th century**: The coalition includes Aram-Damascus, Hamath, Israel, Phoenician cities, Egypt, Arabia, and Ammon — the entire Levantine political landscape. This is the world in which YHWH worship developed as a national cult: a world of competing kingdoms, shifting alliances, and imperial threats.

3. **Israel's name**: The Assyrian scribe wrote "Israel" (*Sir-ila-a-a*) — not "House of Omri" or "Judah." This suggests that in 853 BCE, the name "Israel" was the recognized political designation for the Northern Kingdom.
