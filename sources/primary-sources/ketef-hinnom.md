# Primary Source: Ketef Hinnom Silver Amulets (KH1 and KH2)

**Location:** Ketef Hinnom, Jerusalem (burial cave 24, chamber 25, southwest of the Old City, adjacent to St. Andrew's Church, now Menachem Begin Heritage Center)
**Date:** Late 7th to early 6th century BCE (c. 650-587 BCE, pre-exilic First Temple period)
**Discovery:** Excavated 1979 by Gabriel Barkay (Tel Aviv University); found by a 13-year-old volunteer in a bone repository beneath chamber 25
**Published:** Barkay, G.; Vaughn, A.G.; Lundberg, M.J.; Zuckerman, B. (2004). "The Amulets from Ketef Hinnom: A New Edition and Evaluation." *BASOR* 334:41-71. **Definitive scholarly edition.**
**Medium:** Two tiny rolled silver scrolls (amulets), inscribed in Paleo-Hebrew characters
**Script:** Paleo-Hebrew alphabet (not the later square Aramaic script)
**Present location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem
**Dimensions:** KH1: 27 × 97 mm; KH2: 11 × 39 mm

---

## Overview

The Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls are the **oldest surviving texts from the Hebrew Bible ever discovered**, predating the Dead Sea Scrolls by approximately 400 years. Found in a burial cave in Jerusalem, they are inscribed with a variation of the **Priestly Blessing from Numbers 6:24-26**, plus additional confessional statements about YHWH. The scrolls were physically worn as amulets — rolled and presumably hung around the neck — providing direct archaeological evidence that biblical text was used in apotropaic (protective) practice.

The scrolls were discovered rolled up and fragile. It took three years to develop a method to unroll them without disintegration. The definitive reading was only established in 2004, when the West Semitic Research Project at USC used advanced photographic and computer enhancement techniques to read the inscriptions clearly and confirm the paleographic date.


**Key features of KH1:**
- **"Who keeps the covenant and graciousness towards those who love him and those who keep his commandments"** — parallels Exodus 20:6, Deuteronomy 5:10, Deuteronomy 7:9, Daniel 9:4, and Nehemiah 1:5. This formula ("keeping covenant and mercy to those who love him and keep his commandments") is a Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic signature phrase. Its presence on a late 7th-century amulet is strong evidence that Deuteronomistic language was circulating in Judah before the exile.
- **"YHWH is our restorer and rock"** — "Rock" (*ṣur*) as a divine epithet appears throughout the Psalms and Deuteronomy 32.
- **The Priestly Blessing begins**: "May YHWH bless you and keep you. May YHWH make his face shine..." The scroll breaks off where Numbers 6:25b-26 would continue.

### The Priestly Blessing Comparison

| KH2 (c. 600 BCE) | Numbers 6:24-26 (MT) | English (NRSV) |
|---|---|---|
| *ybrk yhwh yšmrk* | *yəḇāreḵəḵā YHWH wəyišməreḵā* | YHWH bless you and keep you |
| *y'r yhwh pnwy 'lyk* | *yā'ēr YHWH pānāw 'ēleḵā wīḥunneḵā* | YHWH make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you |
| *wyšm lk šlm* | *yiśśā YHWH pānāw 'ēleḵā wəyāśēm ləḵā šālōm* | YHWH lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace |

**Note:** KH2 omits "and be gracious to you" from Numbers 6:25 and compresses "lift up his countenance" into "grant you peace." This may reflect a shorter liturgical version, or the scroll's tiny size forced abbreviation. KH1 may have contained the fuller blessing on its now-broken lower portion.

## Key Features

1. **Earliest biblical text**: At c. 600 BCE, these are the oldest surviving citations of texts found in the Hebrew Bible — older than the Dead Sea Scrolls by about 400 years. The Priestly Blessing existed in substantially its current form before the Babylonian exile.

2. **YHWH as "Rebuker of Evil"** (*hā-ḡō'êr bā-rā'*): This epithet appears in later Jewish incantations and protective amulets. The scrolls are both the earliest example of this epithet AND the earliest physical amulet invoking YHWH for protection. The "magical" use of YHWH's name is not a late superstition — it was practiced at the highest levels of Judahite society (those buried in caves with silver amulets were wealthy).

3. **Apotropaic function**: These were protective amulets worn on the body. They demonstrate that in the late 7th/early 6th century, the written word of YHWH (and the name YHWH itself) was believed to have protective power. Jeremy Smoak (2010) connects them to Psalm 12:6-9, which describes YHWH's words as "silver refined in a furnace" that will "guard" and "protect" the believer.

4. **Text replaces image**: This is Keel & Uehlinger's key argument. In Iron Age IIC (late 7th century), figurative deity representations largely disappear from Judahite archaeological contexts — and in their place, we find texts. The Ketef Hinnom amulets are the perfect example: instead of a figurine of a protective deity, a Judahite wore a silver scroll inscribed with YHWH's blessing. **Text functions as image replacement.**

5. **Deuteronomistic language**: The covenant/commandments formula on KH1 ("those who love him and keep his commandments") is characteristically Deuteronomistic. Its appearance on a personal amulet from c. 600 BCE suggests that Deuteronomic theology had penetrated popular religious practice — it wasn't just a scribal program confined to the Jerusalem temple.

6. **No other deities**: Both scrolls invoke **only YHWH**. No Asherah, no Baal, no host of heaven. For personal protective magic — an area where polytheistic practice would be most expected — these Judahites chose YHWH alone. This is striking evidence for practical monolatry in personal religion, even if it coexisted with more diverse practices elsewhere.

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **The Bible before the Bible**: These scrolls prove that biblical texts (Num 6:24-26; Exodus/Deuteronomy covenant formulae) existed in written form and were important enough to inscribe on personal amulets by 600 BCE. This doesn't prove the entire Torah existed, but it shows that specific passages now in the Torah were already circulating and regarded as authoritative.

2. **Text as image, image replacement**: For the Keel & Uehlinger argument that forms the backbone of many of our claims, Ketef Hinnom is Exhibit A. The aniconic turn in late monarchic Judah wasn't just the absence of images — it was the substitution of text for image. The word of YHWH physically replaced the figurine of the goddess.

3. **Popular Yahwism**: These amulets were found in a family burial cave, not a temple or palace. They represent personal, popular religious practice — not state-sponsored orthodoxy. The fact that such personal piety was exclusively Yahwistic (at least in this case) complicates the simple "official Yahwism vs. popular polytheism" dichotomy.

4. **YHWH as personal protector**: The amulets show YHWH functioning as a personal protective deity — the same role that household gods, Bes figurines, and goddess plaques served in earlier periods. YHWH absorbed functions previously distributed across a pantheon.
