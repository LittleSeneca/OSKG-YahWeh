# Primary Source: Gezer Calendar

**Location:** Tel Gezer, 20 miles west of Jerusalem, ancient Canaanite/Israelite city
**Date:** 10th century BCE (commonly dated c. 925 BCE, though the excavation was not stratified)
**Discovery:** 1908 by Irish archaeologist R.A. Stewart Macalister
**Medium:** Small limestone tablet, inscribed
**Script:** Paleo-Hebrew or Phoenician script — scholarly debate about whether the language is Northern Hebrew or Phoenician
**Present location:** Istanbul Archaeology Museum, Turkey
**Standard reference:** KAI 182

---

## Overview

The Gezer Calendar is one of the earliest known Hebrew/Phoenician inscriptions, dated to the 10th century BCE — roughly the period of the United Monarchy (if historical) or the early divided kingdom. It is not a formal calendar but a list of agricultural seasons with their associated tasks: two months of ingathering, two of planting, two of late planting, one of cutting flax, one of barley harvest, one of general harvest, two of pruning, one of summer fruit. Some periods span two months rather than one, and none correspond to known month names from the Hebrew Bible.

The text has been variously interpreted as a schoolboy's memory exercise, a folk song, a children's rhyme, or a tax collector's schedule. At the bottom of the tablet, the name "Abiyah" (or "Abi") appears — possibly the scribe's signature. The name means "Yah is my father" (*'byh*), making this one of the earliest attestations of the Yahwistic theophoric element.

## Key Features

1. **Earliest Hebrew inscription?** — If the language is Hebrew (as argued by Aaron Koller), this is the oldest known Hebrew text. If Phoenician (Dennis Pardee), it still represents the earliest example of the script that Hebrew would adopt.

2. **"Abiyah" — "Yah is my father"**: The name at the bottom contains the theophoric element *Yah*, a shortened form of Yahweh. This is one of the earliest attestations of the divine name in any inscription. The name Abiyah appears in the Bible (1 Kings 14:1, etc.).

3. **Agricultural rhythm**: The calendar describes a year organized around agricultural tasks — ingathering, planting, harvest, pruning — not around religious festivals. There is no mention of Passover, Shavuot, Sukkot, or any cultic observance. This may reflect a secular administrative document, or it may predate the integration of agricultural and religious calendars.

4. **No deity named in the calendar text itself**: Only the scribal signature contains the divine name. The calendar proper is entirely practical — a schedule of work, not worship.

5. **The *yrḥ* (month) terminology**: Uses the Canaanite word *yrḥ* ("moon/month") rather than Hebrew *ḥodeš*. This is one reason some scholars argue for Phoenician rather than Hebrew.

---

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Literacy in 10th-century Canaan/Israel**: The calendar demonstrates that alphabetic writing was in use in the central hill country by the 10th century BCE. This supports the possibility that early written traditions (royal chronicles, administrative documents, early poetry) could have existed in the period traditionally associated with David and Solomon.

2. **Yahwistic name in the 10th century**: "Abiyah" is Yahwistic — "Yah is my father." This is one of the earliest epigraphic attestations of the divine name Yahweh. It suggests that by the 10th century, Yahwistic personal names were in use in the region, consistent with the emergence of YHWH worship among early Israelite populations.

3. **The secular/agricultural world**: The calendar is a window into the economic rhythms of early Israelite life — planting, harvesting, pruning. Religion is absent from the calendar itself. This is the material world in which Yahwism developed: a world of seasons, crops, and labor, not temples and priests.
