# Primary Source: Ugaritic Baal Cycle and Related Texts

**Location:** Ras Shamra (ancient Ugarit), Mediterranean coast of Syria; also Ras Ibn Hani
**Date:** 13th-12th centuries BCE (Late Bronze Age, final phase of Ugarit before destruction c. 1200 BCE)
**Discovery:** Accidentally discovered 1928-29; excavated by Claude Schaeffer and French archaeological mission; additional tablets found 1958 (Claremont), 1973, 1994
**Medium:** Clay tablets inscribed in Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform
**Script:** Ugaritic cuneiform alphabet (30 signs, the earliest known alphabet)
**Approximately 1,500 texts and fragments found**
**Standard reference:** KTU = *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit* (Dietrich, Loretz, Sanmartin, 3rd ed. 2013)

---

## Overview

The Ugaritic texts are the comparative foundation of all modern scholarship on the origins of Yahweh and Israelite religion. Discovered in the ruins of the ancient city-state of Ugarit, they provide a complete Canaanite religious world from the 13th-12th centuries BCE — **the religious environment out of which early Israel emerged**. Every scholar in the Truth Project — Smith, Day, Cross, Dever, Keel & Uehlinger, Lewis, Fleming — builds their arguments on Ugaritic parallels.

The texts include approximately 50 epic poems, 150 ritual/cultic tablets, 100 letters, and hundreds of administrative documents. The three major literary works are the **Baal Cycle** (KTU 1.1-1.6), the **Legend of Keret** (KTU 1.14-1.16), and the **Tale of Aqhat** (KTU 1.17-1.19). These are the earliest known narrative epic poems in any Northwest Semitic language, predating the Hebrew Bible by centuries.

## The Baal Cycle (KTU 1.1-1.6)

The Baal Cycle is the longest and most important Ugaritic text. It was written down by a scribe named **Ilimilku** and deals with the struggle for kingship among the gods under the high god **El**. The narrative unfolds in three major episodes:

### Episode 1: Baal vs. Yamm (Sea) — KTU 1.1-1.2

**Summary:**

Yamm (the god of the Sea) demands that the divine council hand over Baal as a captive. El, the high god, consents — he favors Yamm and grants him a palace. The craftsman god Kothar-wa-Hasis forges two magical clubs for Baal. Baal strikes Yamm with them, defeats the Sea god, and scatters him. The goddess Astarte rebukes Baal for not finishing Yamm off completely, and Baal delivers the final blow.

**Key passage (the divine council scene):**

The messenger of Yamm enters the council of El on Mount Lel, where the "seventy sons of El" are assembled. The messenger does not bow before El — a sign of Yamm's arrogance. The gods are feasting. El sits at the head, and the messenger delivers Yamm's demand: "Give up Baal and his partisans, the son of Dagan, that I may inherit his gold."

**Significance:**

- **The divine council of "seventy sons of El"** — directly parallel to the "seventy sons of God" in Deut 32:8-9 (4QDeut-j reading) and the "sons of God" in Psalm 82. El presides as the aged high god over a council of lesser deities.
- **Baal as divine warrior defeating the Sea** — the *Chaoskampf* motif. YHWH's defeat of Leviathan/Rahab/the Sea (Psalm 74:13-14, Isaiah 27:1, Job 26:12) is the Israelite version of this same mythic pattern. The Sea represents chaos and rebellion against order.
- **El's initial favor toward Yamm** — El is not omnibenevolent; he can be persuaded, appeased, and even opposed by other gods.

---

### Episode 2: Baal's Palace — KTU 1.3-1.4

**Summary:**

After defeating Yamm, Baal has no palace — a sign he lacks full kingship. His sister Anat (a violent warrior goddess) intervenes. She threatens El, who reluctantly grants permission for a palace to be built for Baal. Kothar-wa-Hasis constructs it on Mount Zaphon. A crucial detail: Baal insists the palace have NO WINDOW, fearing Yamm or Mot (Death) might enter. Kothar persuades him to include one. When the window is opened, Baal thunders from his palace — the voice of the storm god manifested as thunder.

**Key passages:**

Anat's threat to El (KTU 1.3.v):

> "I will make your grey hair run with blood, your grey beard with gore... Strike me down, El, and I will trample you underfoot..."

Asherah's intercession (KTU 1.4.ii-iii):

> The Lady Asherah of the Sea approaches El on behalf of Baal. She brings gifts and flatters El, who relents: "Let a house be built for Baal like the gods, a palace like the children of Asherah."

**Key epithet — "Rider of the Clouds"** (*rkb 'rpt*, KTU 1.4.v.122):

> Baal is repeatedly called "Rider of the Clouds" (*rkb 'rpt*) — the EXACT same epithet applied to YHWH in Psalm 68:4 ("Extol the Rider of the Clouds; his name is Yah!").

**Significance:**

- **Asherah as intercessor and queen mother** — She is El's consort, the "Lady Asherah of the Sea," and the mother of the seventy gods. Her role in the divine council is to mediate and intercede. This is the background against which "his Asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom must be understood.
- **"Rider of the Clouds"** — When Baal's imagery is applied to YHWH in the Hebrew Bible, it is not simple borrowing. It is the transfer of Baal's divine portfolio to YHWH. YHWH absorbs Baal's titles, his storm theophany, and his Chaoskampf victory over the Sea.
- **Anat as violent goddess** — Anat wades in blood and gore, hanging severed heads and hands from her belt. This is the goddess who appears at Elephantine as Anat-Yahu, paired with YHWH. She is not a gentle fertility goddess; she is a warrior.

---

### Episode 3: Baal vs. Mot (Death) — KTU 1.5-1.6

**Summary:**

Mot (Death) demands Baal descend into his throat — the underworld. Baal, terrified, submits. He descends into the realm of Death and dies. The land withers — no rain, no fertility. El mourns: "Baal is dead! What will become of the people?" Anat searches for Baal, finds his body, and buries him with ritual mourning. Then she confronts Mot, seizes him, and destroys him: she winnows him like grain, burns him, grinds him, and scatters him for the birds.

Baal returns to life, and the land revives. But after seven years, Mot returns and confronts Baal. They fight to exhaustion. Finally, the sun goddess Shapash intervenes, threatening Mot with El's wrath. Mot yields, and Baal is confirmed as king.

**Key passages:**

Mot's invitation to Baal (KTU 1.5.i):

> "I will swallow Baal like an olive, like a lamb in the opening of my throat..."

Shapash's intervention (KTU 1.6.vi):

> "How can King of Eternity (El) not hear you? He will tear out the supports of your dwelling, he will overturn the throne of your kingship, he will break the scepter of your rule."

**Significance:**

- **The dying and rising god pattern** — Baal's death and return is tied to the seasonal cycle of rain and fertility. Mot represents the dry season, sterility, and death. This pattern is explicitly rejected in Israelite religion but its traces remain: YHWH's power over Death, the resurrection language in later Jewish texts, the "Death swallows" imagery.
- **El as "King of Eternity"** — El is the ultimate authority. Even Mot must yield to his power. This is the El who appears in Genesis as the benevolent patriarch, but here he is the cosmic high god who can "overturn the throne" of any lesser deity.
- **YHWH absorbs Baal's victory over Death** — In later Israelite theology, YHWH replaces Baal as the lord of life who defeats Mot/Death. Hosea 13:14: "I will ransom them from the power of Sheol; I will redeem them from Death. O Death, where are your plagues? O Sheol, where is your destruction?"

## Other Key Ugaritic Texts

### The Legend of Keret (KTU 1.14-1.16)

King Keret loses his entire family and seeks a new wife. El appears to him in a dream and gives detailed instructions for a military campaign to win the princess Hurriya. Keret succeeds, but later falls ill as punishment for breaking a vow to the goddess Asherah. El intervenes to heal him. His son tries to usurp the throne, and Keret curses him.

**Significance:** Shows El functioning as a personal patron deity who appears in dreams, gives instructions, heals, and blesses with children — closely resembling the patriarchal El of Genesis (who also appears in dreams and makes covenant promises of descendants).

### The Tale of Aqhat (KTU 1.17-1.19)

The righteous Danel (Daniel) receives a son, Aqhat, after El intervenes. Kothar-wa-Hasis gives Aqhat a magnificent bow. The goddess Anat covets it and offers Aqhat immortality in exchange. Aqhat refuses: "Do not lie to me, O Virgin! For to a mortal, what is immortality?" Anat arranges Aqhat's murder. His sister Paghat seeks vengeance.

**Significance:** Aqhat's rejection of Anat's offer of immortality — "to a mortal, what is immortality?" — reflects the Ugaritic understanding of the unbridgeable gap between gods and mortals. Humans die; gods do not. This is the existential framework of the Hebrew Bible as well.

### "The Gracious Gods" (KTU 1.23)

A ritual text describing the birth of **Shahar** (Dawn) and **Shalim** (Dusk), the twin sons of El. Contains explicit sexual imagery: El's penis is described as "a rod" as he impregnates two goddesses. The text prescribes a ritual feast.

**Significance:** El is a sexually active, procreating deity — not the abstract, asexual god of later theology. Divine procreation is a normal feature of the Canaanite religious world that early Israel inherited and eventually rejected.

## The Ugaritic Pantheon

| Deity | Role | Biblical Connection |
|-------|------|-------------------|
| **El** | High god, "Father of Years," "Creator of Creatures," head of the divine council of 70 sons. Lives on Mount Lel at the "source of the two rivers." | El is the original god of Israel (the name *Yisra-El* = "El strives"). Genesis 1-11 uses Elohim. El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam are all El epithets. The divine council imagery in Psalm 82 and Deut 32:8-9 preserves El's original role. |
| **Baal** | Storm god, "Rider of the Clouds," divine warrior, king of the gods (after El). Defeats Yamm (Sea) and Mot (Death). His voice is thunder. | YHWH absorbs Baal's portfolio: storm theophany (thunder, lightning, clouds), defeat of the Sea/Leviathan, "Rider of the Clouds" (Psalm 68:4), kingship over the gods. |
| **Asherah** | El's consort, "Lady Asherah of the Sea," "Creatress of the Gods," mother of the 70 divine sons. Intercessor in the divine council. | "His Asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom. The biblical *asherim* (cult poles) were her symbols. Her role as divine consort/mother persists in popular Israelite religion into the monarchic period. |
| **Anat** | Warrior goddess, Baal's sister, violent and bloodthirsty. Mourns and avenges Baal's death. | Anat-Yahu at Elephantine. Possibly reflected in the "Queen of Heaven" (Jeremiah 44). | **Yamm** | Sea god, chaos monster, Baal's first opponent. | Yam = Hebrew *yam* (sea). YHWH's defeat of the Sea at creation (Psalm 74:13-14) and the Red Sea crossing is the Israelite version of Baal's Chaoskampf. |
| **Mot** | Death god, Baal's second opponent. Swallows all life into his throat (the underworld). | Hebrew *mawet* (death). YHWH's power over Death/Sheol in later texts (Isaiah 25:8: "He will swallow up Death forever"). |
| **Kothar-wa-Hasis** | Craftsman god, builds Baal's palace, forges divine weapons. | Divine craftsman parallels to the building of the Tabernacle/Temple by Bezalel (filled with "wisdom" from God). |
| **Shapash** | Sun goddess, the "Lamp of the Gods," intervenes to resolve the Baal-Mot conflict. | The sun as divine witness/eye (Psalm 19:4-6). |

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **The divine council**: Ugarit gives us the original template — El presides over 70 sons, each a god of a nation. Deut 32:8-9 (4QDeut-j reading: "according to the number of the sons of God") preserves this cosmology. Elyon (El) distributes the nations, and YHWH receives Israel as his portion. YHWH was originally one of El's sons — a junior deity in the Canaanite pantheon who eventually absorbed the high god's identity and portfolio.

2. **YHWH absorbs Baal's mythology**: The Baal Cycle gives us the mythic pattern YHWH inherited: defeat of the Sea, divine kingship, storm theophany, "Rider of the Clouds," victory over Death. The Hebrew Bible is not a radical break from Canaanite religion but a selective absorption and transformation of it. YHWH becomes what Baal was.

3. **El and YHWH were originally separate**: The Ugaritic texts prove conclusively that El and Baal were distinct deities. This supports the scholarly consensus that early Israel worshipped El as its high god and YHWH as a separate deity who later merged with El. Exodus 6:3 ("I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shaddai, but by my name YHWH I did not make myself known to them") preserves the memory of this distinction.

4. **Asherah as YHWH's consort**: Ugarit shows Asherah as El's consort and the mother of the gods. The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions ("YHWH and his Asherah") make sense only against this background. The biblical polemics against Asherah (*asherim*, "Queen of Heaven") are evidence of her persistent worship alongside YHWH, not a foreign import.

5. **The Baal-YHWH rivalry**: The biblical texts' furious polemic against Baal (especially Hosea, Elijah/Elisha cycle, Deuteronomistic History) reflects a real competition for Israel's religious allegiance. Baal, not Asherah, was YHWH's primary rival (Keel & Uehlinger's key argument). The Ugaritic Baal Cycle is the foundational text for understanding what YHWH was competing AGAINST.

## Direct Translations from Parker 1997 (SBL Edition)

Extracted via vision model from *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*, ed. Simon B. Parker (Scholars Press, 1997). The Baal Cycle translation is by Mark S. Smith. Page numbers refer to Parker 1997.

### 1. El Names Yamm as His Son (KTU 1.1.iv, Parker p. 89)

> "And Beneficent El the Benign speaks: 'The name of my son is Yw, O Elat...' And he pronounces the name Yamm... 'Drive him from his royal throne, from the resting place, the throne of his dominion.'"

**Significance:** El grants his son Yamm (Sea) kingship over the gods and orders Baal driven from his throne. The divine name "Yw" here may be a shortened form of Yahweh — one of the few extra-biblical occurrences of the name outside Israelite contexts.

### 2. Baal Defeats Yamm (KTU 1.2.iv, Parker pp. 104-105)

Kothar-wa-Hasis forges two magical clubs and names them:

> "'Your name, yours, is Ayyamarri; Ayyamarri, expel Yamm, expel Yamm from his throne, Nahar from the seat of his dominion.' Leap from Baal's hand, like a raptor from his fingers. Strike the head of Prince Yamm, between the eyes of Judge River."

The weapon strikes:

> "Yamm collapses and falls to the earth. His joints shake, and his form collapses. Baal drags and dismembers Yamm, destroys Judge River."

Astarte then rebukes Baal:

> "By name Astarte rebukes him: 'Scatter, O Mighty Baal, scatter, O Cloudrider.'"

**Significance:** The Chaoskampf pattern — Baal the storm god defeats the Sea god with divine weapons. YHWH inherits this exact victory pattern: "You divided the sea by your might; you broke the heads of the dragons in the waters" (Psalm 74:13). Notice Astarte uses the title "Cloudrider" (*rkb 'rpt*) at the moment of Baal's victory.

### 3. Anat Threatens El (KTU 1.3.v, Parker p. 115)

Anat reacts to Baal's lack of a palace by threatening the high god El:

> "I will drag him like a lamb to the ground; I will make his gray hair run with blood, his gray beard with gore."

**Significance:** El is not omnipotent. His own daughter threatens to assault him physically, and he backs down. This is the Canaanite divine world that early Israel inherited and eventually rejected: gods who can be threatened, intimidated, and forced to change their decisions.

### 4. The "Cloudrider" in the Divine Council (KTU 1.4.iii, Parker p. 124)

Baal speaks in the divine council, recounting his shameful treatment:

> "Mightiest Baal answers, the Cloudrider testifies... For two feasts Baal hates, three, the Cloudrider: a shameful feast, a base feast, and a feast of handmaids' lewdness."

The section heading reads: "Events in the Divine Council are Recounted."

**Significance:** The divine council setting — gods assemble, testify, recount grievances — is the same context as Psalm 82 ("God stands in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment") and Deut 32:8-9. The specific epithet "Cloudrider" (*rkb 'rpt*) is applied to Baal repeatedly. Psalm 68:4 applies the same epithet to YHWH: "Extol the Rider of the Clouds; his name is Yah!"

### 5. Do Not Approach Mot (KTU 1.4.viii, Parker p. 139)

Baal sends messengers to Mot (Death) but warns them:

> "But take care, divine servants: do not get too close to Divine Mot, do not let him take you like a lamb in his mouth, like a kid crushed in the chasm of his throat."

**Significance:** Mot is described as a devouring mouth — Death swallows everything. This imagery of Death's insatiable throat appears in biblical texts: Isaiah 25:8 ("He will swallow up Death forever") and the "Sheol has enlarged its appetite" of Isaiah 5:14. Baal's eventual descent into Mot's throat represents the seasonal death of the rain god and the land's fertility.

### 6. Mot's Challenge to Baal (KTU 1.5.i, Parker p. 141)

Mot sends messengers to Baal with a death threat:

> "Mot threatens to tear Baal to pieces... Baal will descend into Divine Mot's throat, the gullet of El's Beloved."

The passage references Baal's earlier victory over "Litan, the Fleeing Serpent, the Twisty Serpent with seven heads" — the Ugaritic predecessor to biblical Leviathan. Despite Baal's past triumphs, Mot claims dominion over him.

**Scribal colophon (top of p. 141):** "The scribe is Ilimalku, under Niqmaddu, King of Ugarit" — confirming the Baal Cycle was written down in the 13th century BCE by a named scribe in the royal court.

### 7. Baal Is Dead (KTU 1.5.vi, Parker p. 149)

Messengers report to El at his mountain:

> "Dead is Mightiest Baal, Perished the Prince, Lord of the Earth."

El's mourning ritual is described in graphic detail:

> El leaves his throne and sits on the ground. He pours dust and ashes on his head. He puts on sackcloth. He scrapes his skin with a stone, cutting his cheeks and chin with a blade.

**Significance:** This is one of the most vivid ritual mourning scenes in ancient Near Eastern literature. El — the high god — humbles himself, performs self-laceration, and wears sackcloth. The same mourning practices appear in the Hebrew Bible (Job 2:12, Jeremiah 6:26) and persist in Jewish and Middle Eastern mourning customs to this day.

### 8. Anat Confronts Mot (KTU 1.6.ii, Parker p. 155)

Anat searches for her dead brother Baal:

> "Like the heart of the cow for her calf, Like the heart of the ewe for her lamb, So is the heart of Anat for Baal."

She finds Mot and seizes him:

> "She grabs Mot by the hem of his garment, She seizes him by the edge of his cloak. She raises her voice and cries: 'You, O Mot, give up my brother.'"

**Significance:** Anat — the warrior goddess who appears at Elephantine as Anat-Yahu — is here shown as a grieving sister, not a violent destroyer. The simile of the mother animal for her young is one of the most tender passages in Ugaritic literature. This is the goddess whose name is paired with YHWH's in the Elephantine papyri.

### 9. Baal Returns to Life (KTU 1.6.iii, Parker p. 158)

El receives a vision:

> "The heavens rain oil, The wadis run with honey. Beneficent El the Benign rejoices... He breaks into a smile and laughs."

And the proclamation:

> "**For Mightiest Baal lives, The Prince, Lord of the Earth, is alive.**"

**Significance:** This is the dying-and-rising god pattern at its most explicit. Baal's death brought drought and sterility; his return brings oil and honey — signs of restored fertility. The "good news" of a dead god returning to life is proclaimed to the high god, who rejoices. While Israelite religion explicitly rejected the dying-and-rising pattern for YHWH, the language of resurrection and restoration in later Jewish texts (Ezekiel 37, Daniel 12, Isaiah 26:19) emerges from this cultural matrix.

### 10. Baal and Mot Battle (KTU 1.6.vi, Parker p. 162)

The final confrontation:

> "They eye each other like goring oxen. They gore each other like buffalo. They bite each other like serpents. They drag each other like wrestlers. **Mot falls, Baal falls.**"

**Significance:** The battle is a stalemate. Neither god can decisively defeat the other. This reflects the Ugaritic understanding that the conflict between life and death, fertility and sterility, is cyclical and unresolved — no permanent victory is possible.

### 11. Shapash Intervenes — The Resolution (KTU 1.6.vi, Parker p. 163)

The sun goddess Shapash descends and threatens Mot with El's supreme authority:

> "Hear now, Divine Mot: How can you fight with Mightiest Baal? How will Bull El, your Father, hear you? Surely he will remove the support of your throne, Surely he will overturn the seat of your kingship, Surely he will break the scepter of your rule."

Mot is terrified:

> "**Divine Mot is afraid, The Beloved of El, the Hero, is scared.** Mot trembles at her voice."

And Mot capitulates:

> "**Let Baal be enthroned on his royal throne, On the resting place, the throne of his dominion.**"

**Significance:** This is the theological climax. Shapash invokes **El's ultimate authority** — the "King of Eternity" can overturn any other god's throne. Mot, who defied Baal and swallowed him, immediately yields when threatened with El's wrath. This demonstrates that even in the polytheistic Ugaritic system, there is a hierarchy: El is supreme. The Baal Cycle is not a story of competing equals; it is a story of El's delayed but decisive enforcement of cosmic order.

### 12. The Scribe's Colophon (Parker p. 164)

The Baal Cycle closes with the scribe's signature:

> "**The scribe is Ilimalku the Shubanite, Student of Attenu the diviner, Chief of the priests, Chief of the shepherds, the Thaite of Niqmaddu, King of Ugarit, Lord of YRGB, Master of THRMN.**"

## Gibson 1978: Facing Ugaritic Transliteration

Extracted from J.C.L. Gibson, *Canaanite Myths and Legends* (2nd ed., T&T Clark, 1978). Gibson provides the Ugaritic text in Roman transliteration alongside English translation on facing pages. This section provides the original Ugaritic for the key passages translated above from Parker.

### Divine Council: Baal in the Assembly of the Gods (KTU 1.4, Gibson pp. 54, 70-73)

**Ugaritic (Gibson p. 73):**

> `ytb.aliyn.b'l`
> `yt'dd.rkb.'rpt`
> `btk phr bn ilm`

**Literal reading:** "Mightiest Baal sits enthroned, The Rider on the Clouds testifies, in the assembly of the sons of El."

**Key term:** `phr bn ilm` = "assembly of the sons of El/gods" — the divine council. The phrase `bn ilm` ("sons of El/gods") is the Ugaritic equivalent of Hebrew *bene ha'elohim* ("sons of God") in Genesis 6:2, Job 1:6, and Psalm 29:1, and *bene 'el* ("sons of God") in Deut 32:8 (4QDeut-j).

### Baal's Enemies: "Rider on the Clouds" Defended (KTU 1.3, Gibson pp. 45-46)

**Ugaritic (Gibson pp. 45-46):**

> `frt l rkb 'rpt`
> `lmhšt mdd il ym`
> `lklt nhr il rbm`
> `lištbm tnn ištmll`
> `mhšt btn 'qltn`
> `šlyt d šb't rašm`

**Anat's boast (English translation):**

> "What foe rises against Baal, what enemy against the Rider on the Clouds?
> Did I not destroy Yam the darling of El, did I not make an end of Nahar the great god?
> Was not the dragon captured and vanquished? I did destroy the wriggling serpent,
> the tyrant with seven heads."

**Key terms:**
- `rkb 'rpt` = "Rider on the Clouds" (Baal's epithet, identical to YHWH's in Psalm 68:4)
- `ym` = Yamm (Sea), the chaos god defeated by Baal — cognate with Hebrew *yam*
- `nhr` = Nahar (River/Judge River), Yamm's alternate name
- `btn 'qltn` = "the wriggling serpent" — cognate with Hebrew *nachash* and *livyatan* (Leviathan)
- `šb't rašm` = "seven heads" — Leviathan's seven heads in Psalm 74:14 and Revelation 12:3
