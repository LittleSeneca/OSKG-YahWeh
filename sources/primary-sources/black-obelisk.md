# Primary Source: Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III

**Location:** Nimrud (ancient Kalhu), northern Iraq
**Date:** 825 BCE (reign of Shalmaneser III, r. 858-824 BCE)
**Discovery:** 1846 by Sir Austen Henry Layard
**Medium:** Black limestone obelisk with bas-relief panels and cuneiform inscriptions
**Script:** Akkadian cuneiform
**Present location:** British Museum, London (with cast replicas in multiple museums)
**Height:** Approximately 2 meters

---

## Overview

The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III is one of two complete Neo-Assyrian obelisks ever discovered. It features twenty relief scenes (five on each side) depicting conquered kings bringing tribute and prostrating before the Assyrian monarch. The second register from the top shows a figure kneeling before Shalmaneser, identified by the accompanying inscription as **"Yaua of Bit Omri"** — **Jehu of the House of Omri**. This is the **earliest known depiction of a biblical figure** in ancient art.

The traditional identification of "Yaua" as Jehu (king of Israel, r. c. 842-814 BCE) is widely accepted, though some scholars propose Jehoram (Joram) of Israel instead. Either way, this is an Israelite king depicted on an Assyrian monument, providing independent confirmation of the Northern Kingdom's political subordination to Assyria in the late 9th century.

---

## The Jehu Register

**English Translation (scholarly consensus):**

> Tribute of Yaua of Bit Omri: I received from him silver, gold, a golden bowl, a golden vase, golden cups, golden buckets, tin, a royal scepter, and spears.

The relief scene shows the Israelite king (or his envoy) kneeling before Shalmaneser III, with Israelite attendants carrying tribute behind him. The king is depicted in Israelite dress — a pointed cap and fringed robe — distinct from the other tributaries shown on the obelisk.

---

## Key Features

1. **Earliest depiction of a biblical figure**: This is the only known visual representation of an Israelite king from the monarchic period. The relief provides evidence for Israelite dress, gesture (prostration), and tribute practice in the 9th century.

2. **"Yaua of Bit Omri"**: The name *Yaua* (or *Ya-ú-a*) contains the Yahwistic theophoric element, confirming Yahweh worship in the Northern Kingdom. *Bit Omri* ("House of Omri") was the standard Assyrian designation for Israel, regardless of which dynasty ruled — Jehu was not Omri's descendant (he exterminated the Omride dynasty in his coup), yet Assyria continued to call Israel the House of Omri.

3. **Tribute items**: The list of tribute — gold bowls, silver, tin, royal regalia — indicates Israel's wealth and Assyria's extraction of it. This tribute relationship began under Jehu and continued under his successors, with Assyrian pressure eventually contributing to Israel's destruction in 722 BCE.

4. **Jehu's coup in context**: The biblical account (2 Kings 9-10) describes Jehu as a Yahwistic reformer who killed Jezebel, the Omride royal family, and the prophets of Baal. Yet on the Black Obelisk, he is simply a tribute-paying vassal of Assyria. There is no hint of his religious reforms — Assyria cared about tribute, not theology.

5. **Prostration**: The king kneels before Shalmaneser — the posture of complete political submission. This is the reality behind the biblical prophets' warnings against foreign alliances: Israel's kings were literally kneeling before Assyrian monarchs.

---

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **Yahwistic king in Assyrian records**: The name "Yaua" contains the Yahwistic element (*Yau-*), confirming that Israel's kings bore Yahwistic names and that this was recognized by Assyrian scribes. Yahweh was the acknowledged dynastic deity of the Northern Kingdom in the 9th century.

2. **Jehu's Yahwistic coup and Assyrian vassalage**: The contrast is striking — the biblical Jehu is a Yahwistic purist who destroys Baal worship; the Assyrian Jehu is a tribute-paying vassal. This duality is the reality of Israelite politics: Yahwistic ideology at home, political subordination abroad.

3. **"House of Omri"**: Israel's Assyrian name memorialized the dynasty Jehu destroyed. This confirms the Omrides' international reputation — Omri and Ahab were significant enough that their dynastic name persisted in Assyrian records for over a century after the dynasty fell.

4. **The first Persian reference**: The obelisk also contains the first known reference to *Parsua* (Persia), making it significant beyond biblical studies.
