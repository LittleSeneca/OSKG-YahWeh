# Primary Source: Tel Dan Stele

**Location:** Tel Dan, northern Israel (ancient city of Dan, territory of the tribe of Dan, later Aram-Damascus)
**Date:** c. 870-750 BCE (most scholars: late 9th century BCE, reign of Hazael of Aram-Damascus, c. 842-796 BCE)
**Discovery:** Fragment A discovered July 1993 by Gila Cook of Avraham Biran's archaeological team; Fragments B1 and B2 discovered June 1994. Found in secondary use (reused as building material in a later wall), not in primary context
**Medium:** Basalt stele, fragmentary (three pieces: A, B1, B2)
**Script:** Old Aramaic in Phoenician alphabet
**Present location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem
**Published:** Biran, A. & Naveh, J. (1993, 1995). *Israel Exploration Journal* 43:81-98 and 45:1-18.

---

## Overview

The Tel Dan Stele is a fragmentary triumphal inscription in Old Aramaic, most likely erected by **Hazael of Aram-Damascus** (or possibly his son Ben-Hadad). It boasts of victories over "the king of Israel" and the king of the **"House of David"** (*bytdwd*). This is the **only extra-biblical inscription referring to David as a dynastic founder**, and one of only four Iron Age inscriptions mentioning "Israel" (with Merneptah, Mesha, and Kurkh).

The discovery in 1993 was a bombshell. Prior to Tel Dan, some minimalist scholars argued David was a literary fiction with no historical existence. The reading *bytdwd* — "House of David" — made that position far harder to sustain. The consensus now accepts the Davidic dynasty as a historical reality, though debates continue about David's actual power and scope.

### Alternate Translation (Lemaire, 1998)

Andre Lemaire's reading differs on lines 6-7, suggesting **two kings** rather than seventy, with **two thousand** chariots and horsemen:

> [I killed Jo]ram son of [Ahab] king of Israel, and I killed [Achaz]yahu son of [Joram king] of the House of David.

The "House of David" reading (*bytdwd*) is the same in both reconstructions.

## Key Features

1. **"House of David" (𐤁𐤉𐤕𐤃𐤅𐤃, *bytdwd*)**: Line 9. The sequence B-Y-T-D-W-D is read by the vast majority of scholars as "House of David" — a dynastic name referring to the kingdom of Judah. Written as a single word (*bytdwd*) without a word divider, which some skeptics use to argue for an alternative reading (e.g., a place name). The scholarly consensus strongly favors "House of David."

2. **Two kings killed**: The stele claims Hazael (or his predecessor) killed both Jehoram (Joram) of Israel AND Ahaziah of Judah. The biblical account in 2 Kings 9 attributes these deaths to Jehu's coup — but Hazael may be claiming credit for deaths that occurred during the chaos his campaigns caused. This matches 2 Kings 8:28-29 and 9:14-16, which place Jehoram and Ahaziah together at Ramoth-Gilead fighting Hazael shortly before Jehu's revolt.

3. **Hadad as dynastic god**: The Aramean king attributes his rise to the god **Hadad** (the storm god, Baal's equivalent in Aram). "Hadad made me king" and "Hadad went in front of me." This parallels the Mesha Stele's "Chemosh said to me" and Israelite YHWH theology — national deities legitimating royal power and granting military victory.

4. **"Seventy kings"**: The claim of killing "seventy kings" may reflect the "seventy sons" divine council motif. In Ugaritic texts, El has seventy sons. The number may be symbolic rather than literal (cf. the "seventy sons of Ahab" killed in 2 Kings 10).

5. **Author**: Almost certainly **Hazael of Aram-Damascus** (r. c. 842-796 BCE), who seized the throne after the death of Ben-Hadad (Hadadezer) and became Israel's most dangerous northern adversary. The biblical narrative (2 Kings 8:7-15) describes Hazael as anointed by Elisha to punish Israel. The stele confirms Hazael's successful campaigns against Israel and Judah.

---

## Significance for the Truth Project

1. **David as historical dynastic founder**: Before 1993, no extra-biblical reference to David existed. Minimalist scholars (Davies, Lemche, Thompson) argued David was a literary fiction retrojected into Israel's past. The Tel Dan Stele — erected by a foreign enemy who had no reason to invent a Judahite dynastic founder — makes this position much harder to sustain. A king of Aram-Damascus in the 9th century BCE recognized a neighboring kingdom as "the House of David." David's dynasty was real enough to be the political designation of Judah from an outsider's perspective, within about 150 years of when David would have lived.

2. **Hadad as YHWH parallel**: The Aramean royal theology — Hadad chooses the king, goes before him in battle, grants victory — is structurally identical to YHWH theology in Israel. This reinforces the "national god" model: each kingdom has its patron deity who legitimates the dynasty and guarantees military success.

3. **Confirms biblical political geography**: The stele confirms the existence of Israel and Judah as separate kingdoms in the 9th century, with Israel as the more powerful of the two (it's listed first, and its king is the primary target).

4. **Archaeology and the biblical narrative**: The stele, the Mesha Stele, and the Kurkh Monolith together provide a network of external references that align with the 9th-century political world described in Kings. These are not "proof" of biblical accuracy in detail, but they confirm the broad geopolitical framework.
