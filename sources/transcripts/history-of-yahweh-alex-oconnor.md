# The History of Yahweh — Storm God to Israelite Deity

**Source:** Within Reason Podcast with Alex O'Connor
**Guest:** Justin Sledge (Esoterica)
**Date:** April 28, 2024

---

## Chapter 1: Pronunciation of YHWH

**Alex:** Am I right in thinking that we don't even know how Yahweh is supposed to be pronounced?

**Justin Sledge:** No, we don't know. The last people to have a tradition of how to pronounce the name Yahweh — again, Yahweh is just an academic vocalization for the Israelite God — but the last time anyone had any sort of tradition about how that was actually pronounced was 2,000 years ago. It was the folks in the old Jerusalem Temple. But with the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple and the destruction of the priesthood that would have pronounced that name once a year, the tradition's totally amputated. So no, it's been lost.

**Alex:** So where do we get this "Yahweh" from? We know what some of the letters are, but why wouldn't we know how it's pronounced?

**Justin:** It's basically we take Yahweh and the reason why we use that is because that is typically how noun sequences like that would have been constructed in Hebrew, with the "a" sound. It's just really typical for names to follow that vowel construction, so we just shove those vowels in there because that seems to be the best guess at a vocalization. Also, vocalizations change enormously over time.

The earliest attestation that we might have of something like the name Yahweh is probably closer to "Yahoo" in an Egyptian inscription. So we don't know, but there have been lots of attempts at vocalizing this name — Jehovah being the most famous, where you take the vowels of Adonai and then shove them in German into this and sort of look at it sideways and you get Jehovah out of the deal.

We don't even know what the name means. It seems to be connected to the Hebrew word *hayah*, which means "the one that is" or "to be." It might be connected to "he who creates," although that would need to be in a verbal form called the *hiphil* in Hebrew, and that form is never found in the Hebrew Bible. So it means something like "the one that is" — I think that's the best guess on the street about what this name means, which is a pretty good name for a God, I suppose. "I am that I am" — you just straight-up say that you exist.

## Chapter 2: The Origin of YHWH

**Alex:** There's a fascination about the fact that there's this God who kind of has a name, and that name "Yahweh" is more attached to the God of the Old Testament than to the God of the New Testament, and certainly to Jesus. People don't refer to Jesus as Yahweh very often, though theologically that would make sense for some Christians.

What I really wanted to dig into here was the origin of this term, of this God. There are of course two ways of looking at this. If you come from a confessional approach — well, what's the origin of Yahweh? There is no origin; he is just the God of the universe, the Creator, with his begotten son. That's fine. But as you do on your channel Esoterica, you try to check your religious convictions at the door, put on a more scholarly cap, and look at the anthropological history of what Yahweh is. So where can we start?

**Justin:** I think it makes a lot of sense. The idea that Yahweh is a God among other gods is patently obvious if you look at the actual historical circumstance in which the ancient Israelites existed. There are lots of gods there. We don't have to look far — you can look at the Hebrew Bible and see the prophets complaining constantly about the Israelites worshiping other gods. You don't complain about what people aren't doing, so they're clearly doing that.

So yeah, Yahweh was one God among lots of gods that existed in what we might call the Israelite or Canaanite pantheon — as much as there's a distinction to be made between Israelites and Canaanites. Yahweh was one God among several. And not just that, but it's also pretty clear **this God changed**. This being, this God, **was an importation into Israelite religion**. It is not indigenous to the Canaanite pantheon, which we know a good bit about.

> **Key claim:** Yahweh is an imported deity, not indigenous to Canaan. He probably came from the South.

So this God is interesting in that not only did it become God — a god became God — but also it was an importation. And that's fascinating. It is not a god indigenous to that region, probably coming from the South.

The only caveat I would add is that when scholars are honest about what we know about Yahweh, the origins of Yahweh, is that **we don't know much**. You can list the archaeological evidence on one hand. When you really want to get into the Israelite narratives about Yahweh, what we get is a really complicated story about how this God is being imported into the area that we now think of as Israelite territory, and this God is mutating pretty rapidly — which is unsurprising. That happens a lot when you see a god being removed from their geographical location and imported into a different cultic site.

Something similar happens to Osiris — Osiris is moved from one location to another and goes from being an agricultural God to being a god of the dead. Something similar happens with Yahweh. But the only thing I would say is that we don't know much about the origins of this God. It's one of the great ironies of history that **this relatively obscure God has become God**. We can probably say in 10 minutes everything we know about the origins of this being, which just goes to show you — never doubt your origins. You might come from nowhere and really rise to prominence, and there's no greater success story in the history of theology than Yahweh.

## Chapter 3: Oldest References to This God

**Alex:** What can we know about the origins of Yahweh? Do we have an idea of the oldest attestations to this God that are still extant?

**Justin:** The earliest attestation that we think we have of Yahweh is a mention in an Egyptian record of the 14th century BCE where there is a group of people called the **Shasu**, and they're referred to as the **"Shasu of Yahweh"** — again, we don't know how it's pronounced, probably closer to "Yahoo" at that time.

What's important to know is that in Semitic languages like Egyptian and Hebrew, Egyptian being an Afroasiatic language, what really matters is the consonants. The consonants are doing most of the grammatical work for you, and the vowels are often not written — they're pronounced but they're not written, and those vowels and pronunciations change a great deal over time. So we think that the Shasu probably had as their God this God Yahu or Yahweh. So that's our earliest possible mention of this deity.

The other definite earliest mention we have is the **Moabite Stele** or the **Mesha Stele**, and that is a victory stele where, interestingly enough, it's the Moabites — who are historical rivals to the Israelites — actually bragging about the fact that they looted some Israelite cultic objects, specifically some Temple artifacts, and they dragged those before their God Chemosh. And so there we see **Yahweh being spelled out for the first time in any inscription**.

The term "Israel" shows up on the Merneptah Stele in 13th century BCE — Yahweh is not mentioned there, but Israel is mentioned. It's a great example of Egyptian propaganda, mostly Merneptah bragging about having destroyed them, although they're definitely still there, so the destruction must not have been terribly complete.

But that's it. If you want to talk about the earliest archaeological evidence: **one mention in an Egyptian record, and another mention on the Mesha Stele. That's it.** After that we have to get several hundred years into the Iron Age before we get another attestation of this being.

**Alex:** You say a few hundred years pass and we start to get a bit more information. What kind of God are we dealing with in the Iron Age?

**Justin:** If we go from the archaeological evidence, we basically know nothing. We know that Yahweh was a god associated with the Shasu who may have been proto-Israelites. We don't know who the Shasu were — they seem to be a sort of semi-nomadic, maybe raiding culture. The word "Shasu" in Semitic languages ultimately becomes the word for "to raid." They were something like that, which is also maybe connected to the *Habiru* — the Hebrews as we know them.

And then we know they were the national god of the Israelites — we know that from the Mesha Stele. So that's it. That's what the archaeology gives us.

The other side of it is that we could go to the surviving literature of the ancient Israelites, which is what we now know as the Hebrew Bible or the Christian Old Testament. But what's really important, if we're going to make that move, is to **separate off what we think of as the earliest strata of Hebrew language texts in the Bible**.

> **Key claim:** The Hebrew Bible contains multiple linguistic strata. The oldest sections use archaic Hebrew — as different from later Hebrew as Chaucer or Beowulf is from modern English.

One of the things that people don't really realize when they're reading a Bible in translation is that the Hebrew of the text sometimes shifts, and we can see it shift in a way that we can tell — this language here is very, very archaic, and this language is much more recent. In the same way that if you're reading along in a newspaper and it switched to Chaucer's English, you would immediately know that something has happened. If it switched to Beowulf language, you would absolutely not be able to read it at all without specialized training.

The Hebrew Bible does that too. Now, you don't see it in translation because the translations obliterate all that. So there are sections of the Hebrew Bible where the language is incredibly archaic — maybe back to the very, very late Bronze Age, certainly by early Iron. And if we want to learn about Yahweh, what we have to do is go to those sections, because those sections represent the most archaic remnants of Israelite mythology.

Those sections are going to give us our best glimpse into how those very archaic, primitive Israelites understood their God. And the two things that jump out from that literature:

1. **This being is associated with storms** — probably thunderstorms.
2. **It's a war god.**

Almost all the literature we have from this early archaic period are **war songs** — two of which were attributed to women, by the way, which is interesting. There seem to have been women war-band prophets associated with this God. So it's Deborah's Song and Miriam's Song, both of which are songs about successful conquest or successful warfare.

> **Key claim:** The earliest Yahweh is a storm/war deity, not the universal Creator. Think "desert Viking" raiding party god, not King of England.

And by warfare, we shouldn't think about Game of Thrones — huge battles. We should think about skirmishes at a raiding party level — you're talking 50, 150 guys engaging in skirmishes. Much more like desert Vikings than Kings of England.

**Alex:** Where are these from? You're talking about the earliest and most archaic parts of the Hebrew Bible. What are we talking about here?

**Justin:** You can think about:
- The **Song of the Sea** found in the Book of Exodus
- **Deborah's Song** found in the Book of Judges
- **Psalm 18** (quite archaic)
- **Psalm 29**
- **Psalm 68**

These are some of the really archaic texts. They seem to largely have been composed somewhere in the area of Shiloh (Shiloh as people may know it in English), and that seems to be where the Yahweh cult originated at least in that region. Shiloh is just a little bit north of Jerusalem in the Hill Country.

The backdrop of all this is sort of the Bronze Age collapse. For reasons that we don't fully understand, there were widespread ecological shifts and climate shifts, and there was a sort of cascading system collapse that happened in the Eastern Mediterranean at the very end of the Bronze Age. What that meant was a refugee crisis among Aegean peoples that led to the rise of the Sea Peoples and what we now know as the Philistines. But also Egyptian hegemony somewhat collapsed in that region, Hittites totally collapsed, other regions lost urban centers.

> **Key claim:** Israel emerged in the power vacuum of the Bronze Age collapse. The hill country was very difficult to conquer, giving small ethnic groups like the Israelites political autonomy.

It's in that power vacuum that small ethnic groups like the Israelites could actually get some political power, because the Big Kings of the time were having to deal with bigger problems.

What we can tell from these early expectations of Yahweh is that **this God comes from the South**. It's associated with **Mount Seir**, which is south of what is now Israel — the Hill Country, the desert area, the Arabah, and what is now the north section of what is now Saudi Arabia. So far as we can tell, **Yahweh probably originated in that region around Mount Seir**, and eventually through caravan movement was imported into what is now Israel.

That caravan movement is actually described in some of these more archaic poems found in the Hebrew Bible, especially the Song of Deborah.

> **Justin's recommendation:** "If you want to glimpse into this, go take those sections of the Bible and just read those. Just forget about everything else — the Ten Commandments and all the other — and just focus on those sections, and you'll get a pretty good glimpse of what Yahweh was, at least to the earliest Israelites. That is just the most archaic part of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament."

## Chapter 4: How Translation Influences the Bible

**Alex:** One thing that's most interested me as I've begun to develop an interest in the study of the Bible is just the extent to which translation can completely destroy your interpretation — not just of the content of the text, but of the text itself and what it is and what it's doing.

**Justin:** And not just translation, but to know that the text is the result of a redactional history that's incredibly complex. I tell people that we should not think of the Hebrew Bible as a book — we should think about it as **a library of Israelite literature**, and it's a pretty haphazard put-together library. It's like a quilt, and often when you look at a quilt there are patches sewn all over the quilt, and if you look close enough you can see the stitching where something's been added. And imagine a quilt being composed over the course of 1,500 years — that's the Hebrew Bible.

> **Key claim:** The Hebrew Bible is not a book. It's a library compiled over ~1,500 years with layers of editing, redaction, and stitching together.

What's interesting is that some of these really archaic sections — when they were added to the Bible as we know it — **the editors didn't fully understand them**. The Hebrew was archaic to them. It's the equivalent of you or I trying to add a quotation from Beowulf into a paper and we only kind of understand Old English. These texts were archaic even to the writers of the Bible, so much so that they didn't even know fully what they meant. Again, it shows you that this text is incredibly complex.

## Chapter 5: Evolution of YHWH's Influence

**Alex:** We've got the possibly Desert god, localized, maybe worshipped amongst other gods. There's some kind of development that we get from this localized war god, Desert Storm God, to eventually being considered as *God* with a capital G. How does that happen, and when does that happen?

**Justin:** We can tell that by Iron I, Yahweh has been imported into the area that we think of as Judea — the area around Jerusalem, mostly slightly to the north up in the Hill Country. And that matters because that Hill Country is incredibly difficult terrain. If anyone's ever been to Israel/Palestine, they'll know that it's incredibly difficult country, and it's very difficult if you want to try to conquer it.

The idea of getting chariots up into those Highlands — very difficult. Highlands are very difficult militarily to seize. And so they're able to carve out some degree of political autonomy, and with that political autonomy comes theological autonomy. Those two things are completely united at this time.

In and around the area of Shiloh — we know there was a Yahweh shrine there; it's never been found, and trust me, they've been looking for it — but it seems like around there that's where the Yahweh cult is centered. And what begins to happen is there's a northern evolution and a southern evolution of the cult.

> **Key claim:** Yahweh is assimilated into the Canaanite pantheon, absorbing characteristics of El (the fatherly creator god) and competing with Baal (the coastal storm/war god).

**Yahweh is assimilated into the Canaanite pantheon**, absorbing aspects of the two big gods that really matter for the Canaanites: **El** and **Baal**.

- **El** is a kind of grandfatherly, wise god. This is the god that people imagine — the god sitting on the big throne with a big white beard. If you've ever imagined that, you've imagined El. That's how successful that assimilation went. When we imagine Yahweh, we don't imagine a raiding God killing a bunch of people (although that's what the earliest texts show). When we imagine God — even on the Sistine Chapel — it's the old guy with the beard. That's El. So that God is assimilated pretty easily to Yahweh.

- **Baal** is where things are more complicated, because Yahweh and Baal share almost the same theophoric profile. They're **both storm gods** — although Baal is a god of the coastal storms that come in over Lebanon and sweep into Israel/Palestine. Yahweh probably was a thunderstorm god (thunderstorms still kill people in southern Jordan and around Petra because they generate flash floods). And **both are warrior gods**. The problem: the town's not big enough for two warrior storm gods.

If you've read the Hebrew Bible, you know that one of the big theological beefs is between Baal and Yahweh, because they're competing over the same people and they have very similar profiles. For the average guy on the street, they're the same God in some ways. So what's going to happen is elements of Baal and elements of El are assimilated to Yahweh. Yahweh and Baal are going to be put at each other's throats in theological duels — literally theological duels.

> **Key claim:** Some Psalms were originally written for Baal and had Yahweh's name pasted in. The competition was that direct.

There are Psalms in the Hebrew Bible where scholars think it was originally a song for Baal, and they just cut and pasted Yahweh's name in. Even more dramatic than "God Save the King" becoming "God Save the President" — more developed than that.

**Alex:** To be clear, we're talking about the Canaanite gods — El and Baal are the indigenous gods of the Canaanite people living in Syria, Lebanon, all the way south into the Arabah and the Sinai Peninsula. That entire region would have been sort of Canaan, and in Canaan there would have been lots of ethnic groups sharing a Pantheon but probably prioritizing different gods. What ends up emerging is that the Israelites, as one of those groups, have imported a God — Yahweh — and that imported God is going to be set over and against the indigenous god Baal.

The word "Baal" just means "Lord." If you get married in Judaism, you're referred to as *Baal* and *Bat-ah* — Lord and virgin for the day. The word Baal still just means "husband" in modern Hebrew. It just means Lord or master. And "Adonai" actually means something similar, as a taboo replacement for using the word Yahweh in Jewish prayer life.

> **Key claim:** The average Israelite probably didn't distinguish clearly between Yahweh and Baal — they were functionally the same type of god. The theological battle was an elite project.

This is where things get dicey: the Israelite monolatrists — the people who want Yahweh to be the best God even though the other gods exist, a position called *henotheism* — in the south in Judea they tend to want to drift toward *monolatry*: they only want to worship Yahweh even if they acknowledge the other gods are real. They only want to have Yahweh worship.

So Yahweh is going to have to beat Baal. You see this play out over and over in stories of the Elijah/Elisha cycle and other prophets — they're constantly condemning people for worshiping Baal. Frankly, I think for most common Israelite people, they didn't know a difference. They were like, "this is the god that makes it rain — they're just different names for the same God." I think the average Israelite on the street, the average Canaanite on the street, would have been like, "I don't really care what you call this God as long as the rain comes."

Ancient Canaan and modern Israel/Palestine have a wet season and a dry season, and without those thunderstorms and without those winter rains, you have full-blown famine. So it's unsurprising that a storm god is going to be very popular — because you want to be on the right side of those storms.

## Chapter 6: Assimilation of the Gods

**Alex:** When we look at our anthropological evidence — writings, engravings, pictorial representations of gods — what evidence do we have of this theological assimilation?

**Justin:** Archaeologically, nothing. We have some statues of El, some statues of Baal (almost always raising a spear/lightning bolt). We have very little representations of Yahweh — maybe two. One at **Kuntillet Ajrud** and one at **Khirbet el-Qom**. Those are cultic sites — one in Samaria (what is now Palestine) and one down in the Sinai. Those seem to depict Yahweh, and they seem to depict Yahweh with a consort — although the grammar in that whole sequence is very strange; there's a bunch of debate about exactly what the Hebrew grammar is supposed to do there.

> **Key claim:** The only two known ancient depictions of Yahweh show him with a consort (Asherah) and associated with bull imagery.

What ends up happening is we see in those depictions something like a bull — which is again more assimilation. El is associated with bulls. It seems like in the North (Israel), they were pretty okay with this — the northern temples actually have bull imagery. Whereas the southern temples are very *aniconic* — they don't like to represent Yahweh.

But we do have two pictures of Yahweh. The most famous one at Kuntillet Ajrud depicts Yahweh as a bull-looking figure with a consort, **Asherah**. We know from Israelite literature that Asherah was another Goddess that existed in the Israelite/Canaanite pantheon. We know that she was hated by at least the writers of the Israelite literature as we have it, but she was clearly popular — because again, they're constantly saying "don't worship her." So that gives us a good indication that people were.

> **Key claim:** Yahweh acquired Asherah as a consort — the "Taylor Swift of the day." Northern Israel was comfortable with this; southern Judah was not.

This is another example of assimilation. We know that El had a consort, Athirat. Probably what ends up happening is Yahweh comes in — you don't really want a bachelor God — so what do you do? You pair your God with the most popular... the Taylor Swift of the day. And that's Asherah. Asherah becomes the divine consort of Yahweh.

Again, the northern Israelites were more comfortable with this theologically. The southerners never seemed to have been comfortable with it. There seemed to have been a tendency toward aniconism — not representing Yahweh at all — but also to get rid of this whole consort business.

So between the South and the North, there's economic tension, but also theological tension, political tension. We can never separate these in the ancient world — there is no separation between the political and the theological. They're all knitted together in a very, very tight way.

## Chapter 7: From Many Gods to One God

**Alex:** We've got from Yahweh the local storm god to Yahweh being assimilated to Baal and the gods of the Canaanites. But it seems to me we're still a ways off from God as not just the Supreme God among Gods, but just the only God that exists — and not just the god of the desert storms, not just the god of the coastal storms, but *God* with a capital G. So there's got to be more to this story. How does this development continue?

**Justin:** It seems like what ends up happening next is there's a movement in the direction of *henotheism* — there are lots of gods, but our God is the best God. "There are a lot of dads, and my dad can beat up your dad" — something like that. Henotheism seems to develop both in the North and the South, not homogeneously and not totally.

But the key thing that seems to happen is that in **722 BCE, the Assyrians attack and destroy the Northern Kingdom of Israel**. When that destruction happens, there's a refugee crisis — a great deal of Israelites coming down from the north into the South, into Judea and its capital in Jerusalem.

Remember, the Northerners are much more comfortable with representing Yahweh with bull imagery, even with some degree of syncretism between the local religion (Baal worship and other things). The story of Jezebel is a great example — you can hear the word "Baal" in her name; her name means "woman of Baal."

Once Israel is destroyed in 722, this refugee crisis means there's a bunch of people coming from the north to the South. The southerners, it seems, are never comfortable with the bull imagery, never comfortable totally with representing Yahweh. There had been a tendency among the southerners not only to attack non-Yahweh worship, but also to be interested in **consolidating Yahweh worship only in the Jerusalem Temple**.

> **Key claim:** The Assyrian destruction of the Northern Kingdom (722 BCE) and the resulting refugee crisis was the critical pressure cooker that forced theological consolidation.

These are people like **Hezekiah** and especially **King Josiah**. Josiah led something like an inquisition where not only were non-Yahweh sites destroyed, but even Yahweh sites that weren't in the Jerusalem Temple were also knocked down. **Tel Arad** is probably one of our better preserved Yahweh worship sites in the South; **Tel Dan** is the best preserved in the North.

**Cannabis at Tel Arad:** The Holy of Holies where the incense was burned for Yahweh in the Tel Arad shrine was recently analyzed, and **cannabinoids were actually found in the incense**. It looks like at least some part of whatever they were burning as incense did contain some degree of cannabinoids. "Were they hot-boxing inside of the Holy of Holies? I don't know. I can't say that, but the archaeology is what the archaeology is." When the Tel Arad shrine was destroyed, they literally pulled it down, and by pulling it down and effectively burying it, that's great for archaeologists because it seals that entire shrine in time. The altar came down, was faced down, flipped over, and is now in the Israel Museum. The thick oils that go into making incense (frankincense, mastik, myrrh, cannabis oil) built up in the cracks and crevices of these stones. They scraped it out and tested it.

## Chapter 8: Link Between Religion & Intoxication

**Justin:** Most religions have some degree of negotiated relationships with various forms of alterations of consciousness — whether breathing techniques, psychedelic drugs, alcohol, or coffee. We should always imagine and know that the Kabbalah (Jewish mysticism) and Sufism (Islamic mysticism) — those late night vigils that gave rise to all of that kind of crazy mysticism stuff in the 17th century — those dovetail in the Ottoman Empire with the import of coffee. So they're drinking coffee all night. There's a direct correlation and I would say some degree of causation, although it's overdetermined, that caffeine has played a huge role in the development of forms of Jewish and Islamic mysticism.

In the Hebrew Bible, there's a moment where we get the first king of Israel, Saul. There are these roaming prophets who are singing in some kind of ecstatic state, and Saul goes and joins them and he also goes into some kind of ecstasy. In fact, there's a phrase in Hebrew when someone kind of goes crazy — "Saul is among the prophets" — because that phrase is found in the Hebrew Bible. So there clearly was some kind of ecstatic Yahweh worship that existed. You've got to be pretty hyped up to go into battle, especially late Bronze Age or early Iron Age battles where you're fighting at close distance.

## Chapter 9: How Different Regions Worshipped YHWH

**Justin:** One of the things that we see is that the hard Yahwism — where Yahweh really is emphasized, the "Yahwist" structure of the text — is probably Southern. The "Elohist" stuff, that's more abstract, and that may be more Northern material.

The emphasis on bull imagery is also coming from probably the North. I think the Golden Calf story is probably a big shade thrown at the Northerners — "you guys did this, you worship these Bulls." We see Southerners complain about the Bulls of the North. When Moses comes down from Sinai and discovers his people worshiping the Golden Calf and has to destroy it, and a plague comes among the people — I think that's shade being thrown at the Northerners in the way that they worship Yahweh.

> **Key claim:** The Golden Calf story in Exodus is likely a polemic against the Northern Kingdom's bull-iconography Yahweh worship.

The Northerners come from a place of prestige — the northern tribes and the northern kingdoms always were politically stronger, richer, more powerful than the southerners. When they're destroyed, they're not in a position of power anymore, but they probably had the better scribes, they probably had money. It's a much more urban elite group of people coming down as refugees to — well, kind of bumpkins. Jerusalem was not the center of anything. It was a relatively small walled city or semi-walled city on a mountain — not a great place to live, defensible, but not a city of tens of thousands. Think a city of 5,000, 6,000, maybe 10,000. It's relatively small.

Josiah and those guys have to figure out a way of accommodating theologically these different groups of people. And one of the interesting moments we have in the Hebrew Bible — perhaps one of the strangest — is that Josiah is doing renovations on the Temple, and doing renovations, they knock a wall down and inside they find a scroll — the *Torat Moshe*, the laws of Moses. They take this scroll they found in a wall — which this story is about as believable as whatever; it's very unlikely this really happened — but it's interesting because they take this text, which was probably most of Deuteronomy as we know it, and in order to vouchsafe that it should be followed, they take it to a **northern priestess/prophetess** named **Huldah**, who is one of these refugees.

> **Key claim:** The "discovery" of Deuteronomy in the Temple wall under Josiah was likely a scribal production, but it was validated by a northern prophetess (Huldah) to get the northern refugees on board. This is a political-theological negotiation, not divine revelation.

It's an interesting moment where the southerners are religiously innovating — I'm pretty sure Josiah's scribes are just writing that scroll out — but in order to get the northern refugees on board, they take it to one of their prophetesses, Huldah, and she basically says, "Yeah, it's from God, it's from Yahweh." And it becomes Deuteronomy as we know it.

So this is a great example where you have some theological and scriptural accommodation between the Northerners and the Southerners. But even at this time, Yahweh is not the only game in town. There are definitely people still worshiping celestial objects — the Prophet Jeremiah complains about this, people worshiping the Stars, the Queen of Heaven is a goddess that Jeremiah mentions. There's probably Asherah worship still happening — we have lots of Asherah idols around.

But basically between 722 BCE and 586 BCE, when Jerusalem is destroyed by the Babylonians, what's probably happening is that **Yahweh monolatry is beginning to take shape** — the exclusive worship of Yahweh, especially in Jerusalem and especially with other shrines being destroyed.

Then this is all interrupted of course in 586 BCE when the Babylonians come through and just trash everything. That inaugurates the Exile — the Judean Exile. And the argument that I make is: **Yahwism went into Exile, and Judaism comes back.**

> **Key claim:** Yahwism went into exile; Judaism came back. Monotheism was born in the trauma of exile.

It's in that Exile period — for which we have very meager, confused data — that **monotheism as we know it emerges**. It's in those texts like *Trito-Isaiah*, the very end of Isaiah, where we do get what appears to be radical monotheism. Trito-Isaiah is the first time where we get clear articulations that Yahweh is the only God at all — every other God is false; Yahweh is the only God.

> **Key existential claim:** "When Yahweh becomes the only God, Yahweh ceases to be Yahweh." All the defining characteristics of the primitive period — storm god, raiding god — those elements disappear. If you're going to make God God of everything, God can't be one thing. There's an interesting theological trade: Yahweh becomes the only God, but ceases to be Yahweh in any sense we would recognize from the primitive literature.

**Why the exile produced monotheism:** For most ancient peoples, gods literally dwelled in geographic regions. When the Bible says Yahweh lived in "the house of Yahweh," that's not a metaphor — God literally lived in that building, at least during some parts of the year. This God is a God for this region. Geography and theology are completely linked.

There's obviously a theological problem here: if your God lives in a house and that house gets destroyed, you have one of two options. You can pick another God and worship those gods, or you can theologically do something very radical. The Judeans did what I think is one of the most radical things theologically in the history of that region: **they maintain belief in their God, but to do that they had to remove the geographic specificity of that God.** "No, God's not God of this house in this region of Judea. God has to be God of everything, and therefore no matter where we go, God is God."

It's a theological innovation of titanic importance — a shift toward the generalization of theology.

What ends up emerging is (1) monotheism and then (2) apocalypticism. This is the birth of **Jewish apocalypticism**, born out of the theological failure of the old Deuteronomic theology. The old theology was: if you follow God and do what you're supposed to do, I'll give you land and agricultural success; if you don't, I'll wipe you out. Well, neither happened — the Temple was destroyed *and* they weren't wiped out. So what do you do? That theology doesn't make any sense anymore. You develop a new theology, and that new theology is Jewish apocalypticism.

That theology would, along with monotheism, set the stage for Christianity and basically the Abrahamic religions as we know them. We really should give it to those unnamed Judean theologians — people like whoever wrote Trito-Isaiah. They did something completely daring theologically that would have been shocking to their contemporaries: "Hold on, there's just one God, and that God is the God of the whole *olam* — the whole universe." A bizarre idea. Absolutely bizarre.

Trito-Isaiah theologically pushed the envelope in a way that no monotheism has ever done before. They were more radical monotheists than anyone else, because they were willing to let God be the God of evil too. There's a moment in Trito-Isaiah where God says, **"I am good and evil. I am the light and the dark."** That's a pretty radical theological move — so radical that even the apocalypticists weren't comfortable with it, because they had to invent the Devil to be sort of a bad guy. God could not be associated with evil. So the theological innovation there was to invent the Devil to do that work.

Even the rabbis are so uncomfortable with that language that in the traditional Jewish prayer book, they quote that section of Isaiah and say "I am the good" and stop there — they will not say "I am the good and the evil." So even rabbinical Judaism is uncomfortable with the radical monotheism of Trito-Isaiah. And of course Christianity is as well, because they invent the Devil to do all the dirty work.

## Chapter 10: What is Jewish Apocalypticism?

**Justin:** Jewish apocalypticism is a mode of religiosity that primarily has to do with the idea that God, often through intermediaries like angels, will reveal to people things about Ultimate Reality. The word "apocalypticism" is from the Greek *apocalypsis*, which means "to unveil" or "to part the veil."

This literature has the idea that rather than Moses went up the mountain, got the law, brought it down, and now you have to follow it — there are these people for whom God reveals certain kinds of things, especially secrets. The most famous example in the Hebrew Bible is Ezekiel, where Ezekiel is literally lying there by the Rivers of Babylon and God sort of splits the heavens open and he's given a vision and shown all kinds of things about the Universe.

Jewish apocalyptic literature typically has several features:
- Very interested in the **origin of evil** (obviously something you'd be interested in if living in exile — why bad things happen to good people)
- Interested in the **end of the world**
- Interested in the **nature of the cosmos**
- **Hyper-deterministic** — God is in control of everything, history is part of a divine plan, even bad things are part of that plan
- Very interested in the **structure and function of the cosmos** — how planets move, what angels are associated with what
- Shifted Israelite eschatology — there is no afterlife in Israelite religion, just *Sheol*, a dark place of basically nothing

> **Key claim:** The concepts of heaven, hell, and the afterlife were born out of Jewish apocalypticism, likely under Zoroastrian influence. They didn't exist in earlier Israelite religion.

Well, that's not great if you want to know what happens to good and bad people. Good people got exiled by bad people — what's going to happen to those bad people? If we're all going to go to the same place, why in the world do anything? So the apocalypticists, probably under the influence of some Zoroastrian religion, begin to develop a more robust system of the afterlife — and **where we get heaven and where we get hell**. That is born out of Jewish apocalypticism.

Also, apocalypticism is very interested in salvation and the ultimate end of things, and typically Messianic ideas are born in the apocalyptic mode of Judaism.

So in many ways: **Israelite religion/Yahwism goes into Exile; what comes back is apocalyptic Judaism. Judaism set the stage for Rabbinical Judaism, Islam, Christianity, and things like that.**

## Chapter 11: The Introduction of Jesus

**Alex:** One of the important shifts here is this preacher from Nazareth who shows up and seems to change the story a little bit. We've now got this idea of Yahweh as God — the one God that's worshipped, the god without location.

It's interesting hearing you describe the development of Yahweh before the appearance of Jesus in similar terms — that in the Babylonian Exile you get something a bit like what Jesus seems to have been doing: "you don't need to worship there, you can worship anywhere." I was surprised to hear that that mode of thinking predated Jesus.

**Justin:** The move toward theological generalization — that a God that is everywhere can be worshipped anywhere, because that God is anywhere — you can see it as a stepping stone. Because you still keep that attachment to the Temple, that attachment to ritual and to location. That is only really fully undone by the time you get to Jesus, and I think mostly by Jesus's followers and not so much Jesus himself.

Jesus clearly is religiously observant — he's attending the Temple, he's going on the Temple pilgrimage. So I think his followers certainly — once Jesus undergoes the process of becoming God or something like a Godlike figure (I think that process is still going on in Paul's lifetime) — once Jesus is deified or undergoes apotheosis, then you get the idea that Jesus was the Temple and was the sacrifice. But I think that's happening mostly in the 40s and 50s CE, rather than in Jesus's own lifetime.

**Alex:** Why is it that even when you get to the time of Jesus, there still is this attachment to the Jewish Temple, to a god that seems to be connected to particular identifiable locations on Earth? Why does that stick around?

**Justin:** For lots of reasons. One, ritual and tradition die hard. There's some sense in which even if God is everywhere, there is a correct way of worshiping God. They had not totally given up on the idea that this is the correct way. There's good reason to believe that if you believe these texts were at some level divinely revealed. God may be the God of the whole world, but God still needs to be worshipped in this way, in this place.

Not all Judeans believed that — there were temples in other places. There was a temple down on Elephantine Island in what is now Egypt. We know of a later Temple in Alexandria. So we do know of non-Jerusalem temples that Judeans/Jews used to worship Yahweh.

Also, there's a split among the Jews at this time. There are Jews moving in the direction of a more radical generalization, typically represented by the **Pharisees**, and then the old guard, the **Sadducees**, who really hold on to the old Israelite religion — they reject the existence of the afterlife, they reject the existence of angels and demons, and they are insistent upon Temple sacrifices.

> **Key claim:** The Pharisees were already envisioning a post-Temple theology — a shift to the home and dinner table as the center of religious life — before the Temple was actually destroyed. This is why rabbinical Judaism could pivot so quickly after 70 CE.

The rabbis of the time were already envisioning a kind of post-Temple theology — not post-Temple worship, but post-Temple theology — where the home becomes the center of religious life and the dinner table becomes the center of religious life. I would argue it's because there was already a theological prefiguring of a post-Temple theology among the Pharisees that when the Temple was eventually destroyed by the Romans in 70 CE, they were able to pivot very quickly, because theologically it was already intact.

Rabbinical Judaism emerges as a post-Temple theology because they were kind of already doing post-Temple theology. Once the Temple's destroyed, the shift was — I won't say easy, because of course it must have been terrifyingly traumatic — but they had already begun thinking that way.

You can even see in some of the later prophets: Isaiah is saying, "Do you think I need burning grains? Do you think I need incense? No, I'm the master of the whole world. You need to do this to worship me — I don't need it." Isaiah says, "Is this the fast that you should be doing? No. You should be fasting by helping the poor." So there's already a theological shift happening even in the late prophets, away from the idea that Yahweh needs these things.

## Chapter 12: How Jesus Became Identified With YHWH

**Alex:** At some point this man who went around performing miracles and gaining followers — who then on some accounts is crucified and then seen by his followers as risen — becomes identified with Yahweh, with this Israelite God. How does that happen?

**Justin:** We're still very much in the midst of trying to sort out exactly how the Jesus movement developed. But it seems that Jesus was some kind of **apocalyptic prophet**, and he probably preached for about a year before he was executed — probably less than a year. Most scholars now say probably less than a year. He entered Jerusalem, caused a riot, and was executed because he's probably one of a dozen Jewish rebels executed during that time period for causing a riot during Passover.

What seems to make the most sense to me is that **Paul has some kind of mystical vision**. He's probably involved in the semi-official/quasi-official persecution of Christians (or what we might call followers of the Jesus movement). He has some kind of vision — I suspect he was what we call a *Merkabah Mystic*. There's evidence from 2 Corinthians that he's having out-of-body experiences, going into the heavens.

> **Key claim:** Paul was a Merkabah mystic who had visions, not a convert who met the physical Jesus. He never met a flesh-and-blood Jesus.

There's a form of mysticism called *Merkabah Mysticism* that was pretty typical among Jewish mysticism associated with apocalypticism. And it seems that Paul goes into the heavens in a mystical experience and he sees the apotheosized Jesus. We know that Paul believed that physical bodies could be transformed into something like angelic bodies, and we think that Jesus managed to accomplish this. Paul seems to believe that because Jesus has accomplished it, we can all accomplish it — we can all go live in glorified bodies forever after we die — and Jesus sort of led the way.

What I think ends up emerging is this: already in Judaism at the time, there was an emergent theology, a heretical theology called what we now refer to as **Jewish Binitarianism** or the so-called **"Two Powers in Heaven."**

There was already the idea that there was some kind of *Deus Absconditus* — some kind of God beyond all knowing — but that God, in order to be knowable, made some other version of itself that people could know. In one version of the story, it's the angel Metatron. Sometimes in the apocalyptic literature, the angel Metatron is referred to as **Yahweh Katan** — **"the Little Yahweh."** Which is just shocking for a religion that had just recently adopted radical monotheism. Now binitarianism emerges where you have a greater Yahweh and a lesser Yahweh.

> **Key claim:** Jewish binitarianism ("Two Powers in Heaven") — with a greater Yahweh and a "Little Yahweh" (Metatron or the Divine Glory) — predated Christianity and was the theological framework Paul used to understand Jesus.

This theology was around, and I think what happens is that Paul, having had a mystical experience of the apotheosized Jesus, links it with this emerging Jewish binitarianism. Jesus becomes that *Yahweh Katan*. For other forms of Jewish binitarianism, it was the angel Metatron or the *Kavod* (the Divine Glory) — there's the revealed glory and the hidden glory. Lots of versions of this Jewish binitarianism were around at that time.

So Paul, at some level, takes his mystical experience of the resurrected Jesus and then combines it with this nascent Jewish binitarianism — and Jesus basically becomes *Yahweh Katan*.

**Alex:** Christians will listen to this and say, "Hold on now. Paul had an idea that Jesus was just God. Are we saying that Paul was not a monotheist?" These are the Epistles that made it into the New Testament canon. What's a Christian going to think listening to this?

**Justin:** I think it's less reading it into it and more **putting Paul into the world of his Jewish contemporaries**. If we take Paul out of his Jewish contemporaries and then read Christian history back into him, then we can say, "Yeah, Paul always believed that Jesus was God." But if you want to read Jesus back through the history of Christianity — I would suggest the more historically accurate way of reading Paul is to put him among his Jewish contemporaries and the Jewish theologies of the time. Then we'll realize really quickly: Paul looks a lot like the other Jews that we know of.

The same thing is true of Jesus. If you take Jesus as an apocalyptic Galilean miracle worker — yeah, that story sounds a little weird if you've only heard the Christian story that he was a unique Godlike figure who was able to raise the dead. Until you read other Jewish literature where there's lots of these Galilean miracle workers that are able to work miracles — we have at least three or four of them that we know of that were somewhat contemporaries of Jesus.

> **Key claim:** Jesus was one of several Galilean miracle workers and apocalyptic prophets operating at the time. Paul was one of several Jews involved in Merkabah mysticism and "Two Powers" theology. They make more sense in their Jewish context than through later Christian theology.

I would suggest that it's better to take Jesus, and Paul for that matter, and put them into their historical theological context, and then try to understand their life and writings. And it turns out they make a great deal of sense in that context, without having to backload a bunch of Christian theology that developed centuries afterwards.

We don't need to do that to make sense of them. *They* needed to do that because Gentiles didn't know much about the Jewish world. Therefore, to make sense of what it meant to say that Jesus was God and that the Father was God — and now you have to have something in between the two and the form of the Holy Spirit — there's a lot of really amazing Greek philosophical heavy lifting to make all that make sense. You would need that to make all that make sense.

Another way of making it make sense is just to put these guys back in their Jewish context in the first century, and it turns out they make sense in that context quite well.

## Chapter 13: Paul's Mystical Visions of God

**Alex:** Unlike the other Apostles, Paul never meets a flesh-and-blood Jesus. Paul has visions that seem to be more aptly described as mystical experiences than meeting a physical person and shaking his hand. So for whatever reason, after the death of Jesus, Paul is having these visions of Jesus. At the same time, there is this developing Jewish theology that there are kind of two gods — a God and then a sort of small God, almost a Son of God. And Paul is the one who begins to identify this Jesus figure with the "Little Yahweh."

That implies to me — because Paul's Epistles are the earliest written sources of the New Testament, but chronologically in terms of the story, he's not the earliest person to interact with Jesus. You have the disciples. Tradition assumes that two of those actually ended up going on to write Gospels. Was it not amongst this community that the idea first arose that Jesus was God? Is it Paul who's the first person you think to start identifying Jesus with God?

**Justin:** I don't think so. If we look at, let's say, James — assuming it is actually written by James, which is hard to say — there's no indication in those texts that Jesus has become a divine figure. He's important, but this idea I think is really developing in the shadow of Paul's theological genius.

The Jewish Christianity — the idea that Jesus was the Messiah, had died, and had risen to heaven to come back very shortly and deliver on the Messianic promises that were sort of typical of the time — I think that would have probably lasted, and I think it did last until the Bar Kokhba rebellion. I think that distinctively Jewish way of being what we call Christian did survive in Jerusalem.

But Paul's theological shifts — especially the shift away from the Jewish law, the necessity of the law, and the universalization of Jesus as God, as a God character — I think ensured its success.

> **Key claim:** The original Jerusalem church (Peter, James) likely did NOT believe Jesus was God. That was Paul's theological innovation. The Jerusalem church was wiped out in the Bar Kokhba rebellion (135 CE) — history went to the survivors.

History is not just on the side of the victors — it's also sometimes on the side of the survivors. The Jesus movement that was exclusively Jewish, that seems to have been focused among Peter and James in Jerusalem — assuming it survived them, which I'm sure it did — they would have been wiped out in the Bar Kokhba rebellion. So by 135 CE, it's extinct. And the Pauline version of Christianity, with its appeal to the Gentiles, not being localized in Jerusalem, meant that it was going to succeed.

## Chapter 14: Is This Theology Gnostic?

**Alex:** This is all new to me. As I'm hearing about the big God, the little God, Jesus identified with the little God, the mystical — it's all sounding a bit Gnostic. It sounds like this is laying the groundwork for an idea of Jesus as a divine being but separate from the God of the universe. There are sort of different gods all competing, doing different things — one is a bit more material, one is a bit more mystical.

And yet within practically no time, Gnosticism is condemned as a heresy, and we end up with this idea that Jesus IS Yahweh — not little Yahweh, not a different kind of Yahweh, not a friend of Yahweh or a different kind of God, but it's the same God. What happens there? Am I right in saying that this interpretation of the early Paul is maybe a bit Gnostic?

**Justin:** I suppose it depends on what you mean by Gnostic. Two things:

First, Paul definitely thinks of Jesus as becoming something divine. Now, whether he's identified with — numerically identical, to use the philosophical language — is Yahweh numerically identical with Jesus? I think Paul never quite makes that move. Because he never quite makes that move, they're going to maintain their distinction. Jewish binitarianism is maintained.

Now, there's a theological and philosophical problem about what is the relationship between these two beings. And that's where you get the Holy Spirit — sort of the Trinity that links them together. They're of the same substance (*homoousios*) but they're distinct. The Father, the Son, and the Spirit are God, but the Father is not the Son is not the Spirit — that sort of classic Triune Athanasian stuff that you get a few centuries later.

But what made Christians Christians was that they all believed that Jesus had come to this world, had taught something unique, had died in a meaningful way, had beaten death, had survived somehow, and that something salvific had happened. That's what unites the members of the Jesus movement and ultimately unites early Christians.

> **Key claim:** Early Christians agreed THAT Jesus saved. They vehemently disagreed on HOW. Even within the New Testament, there's no coherent soteriology.

Now, *that he died and that he did something salvific* — everyone agreed on. *What that was in the details* — no one agreed on. Not even Paul. It's fantastic to see this: even Paul is still figuring out what it meant for Jesus to have saved people. Did it mean that he opened the door to people being transformed into glorified bodies after they die? Is that it? Or is it some other thing?

And then you get this sort of theology of sacrifice where Jesus has become the last sacrifice, freeing everyone from the necessity of the Jewish law. Even exactly how Jesus saved people is interesting, because the early theory seems to have been what's called the **Ransom Theory**, where Jesus literally went into Hell and tricked the Devil, and then was able to extract all the souls from Hell as a kind of double agent. That would seem to have been the earlier theology that eventually was abandoned for the sacrifice theology — that Jesus had become the final sacrifice and had freed everyone from Death that way.

So not even in the New Testament is the theology of what any of this even means totally worked out. And as you can imagine, if it's not worked out in the New Testament, it certainly isn't by the time various communities begin to accept the salvific event of the Jesus movement. You have a thousand stories about what exactly has happened. Some of those stories become the Pauline story. Some of those stories become the Orthodox story. Some of those stories become the Gnostic story — of which there are several different versions.

> **To Bart Ehrman's point:** "We shouldn't think about there being a Christianity. We should think about there were *Christianities*. And there still are Christianities."

And those Christianities tend to differ on what exactly it meant for Jesus to save us by dying and rising again. Lots of communities came up with a wide range of answers.

One answer was **Marcion's answer**. Marcion's answer was: "Yeah, Jesus came as the true God to basically show that the Creator" — Marcion never calls the Creator a god, that's a mistake — "but the Creator of this world is bad. That's the Old Testament God. And Jesus is the actual God, and Jesus came here to liberate us from that Creator God." Marcion sets the stage for what we now call the Gnostic interpretation. And of course, Marcion also develops the **first canon** as we have it — he's the first person to give us a list of texts that are authoritative.

Marcion is eventually condemned. So it's important to point out: **what Jesus is in terms of his apotheosis is contested theologically. What his death and resurrection meant and what salvation meant was by no means agreed upon by early Christians.** And it wasn't even agreed upon even in the New Testament. There's no coherent theory of any of that even in the New Testament.

## Chapter 15: Christians Believing Jesus is YHWH

**Alex:** If you ask a Christian today "Who is Yahweh?" they might just say to you, "Oh, Yahweh is Jesus. They're one and the same." When does that happen? It sounds like in early Christianity it's all over the place — who knows who this Jesus guy is, who knows what his connection is to the God or Creator of the Old Testament. But now it's a numerical identity. When does that emerge?

**Justin:** In Orthodox circles, it never does. Yahweh becomes the Father, Jesus is the Son. When does the Trinity in that kind of language begin to emerge? As early as Origen, in some sense, it's already being packed into the New Testament. There's a great — I think it's in the Gospel of John — where it says "the Father and the Son" and they eventually add back in the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit doesn't occur in the earliest manuscripts, so already the manuscript tradition itself is being theologically manipulated to build some of these ideas back in.

> **Key claim:** The Johannine Comma (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit) doesn't appear in the earliest manuscripts. The text was being theologically edited to support developing Trinitarian doctrine.

But I don't think you get a really rigorous sense of the Trinity — or what I'd rather call a "divine economics," where there are entities that represent the Godhead and those entities relate to one another in some kind of dynamical way — whether that's a trinity, a binitarian position, modalism, or whatever version you want to pick. There are a thousand Trinitarian theologies.

I don't think you really begin to see that in a coherent way until sort of the Apostolic Fathers, some of the Epistles of maybe Barnabas. And then you begin to pick it up in some of the really early Apostolic teachings of people like Irenaeus.

But the most systematic — the first real systematic attempt at getting a coherent theory of how all this is supposed to work — is going to be **Origen**, *On First Principles*. Now, Origen is a tragic character in the history of Christianity, because in many ways he's the first real theologian to systematically use Jewish scripture AND Greek philosophy to make sense of what all this meant. But ultimately he's thrown under the bus. Origenism is ultimately condemned as heresy. Origen is kept at arm's length but never condemned; Origenism is ultimately condemned in the rise of the Athanasian version of the Trinity.

I would say it's not until a couple centuries later that we get a really theologically, philosophically coherent idea of this. I could teach a whole class where one could take the concept of the Trinity and the debates around the Trinity, and you could teach all of Greek philosophy through those. Because the great genius of making the Trinity work is that you had to bring to bear **Aristotelian logic, Stoic metaphysics, Platonic forms** — all the machinery of what we would recognize as Middle Platonism, the philosophy of the Hellenistic world. All that had to be brought to bear to make the Trinity work.

> **Key claim:** The Trinity is the crowning achievement of Greek philosophy — Aristotelian logic, Stoic metaphysics, and Middle Platonism all deployed to make the biblical data coherent. It's ingenious. Whether it's also *true* is a separate question.

In many ways, the Trinity is the crowning achievement of Greek philosophy. If people want to tout their Greek philosophy — go read Athanasius's book on the Trinity. It's a tour de force of bringing to bear Stoicism, Aristotelian philosophy, and Platonism (Middle Platonism specifically) to make that system work. And then on top of that, to have mastered the Hebrew Bible in such a way to make it hermeneutically all work. It's a real work of genius. Now, it being a work of genius doesn't make it not crazy, or true — but genius it still is. I still awe when I read the works of someone like Athanasius. Still mind-bogglingly amazing. I think it's all nonsense — but ingenious nonsense.

## Chapter 16: What is the Demiurge?

**Alex:** I wanted to ask you about the concept of the Demiurge. A lot of what we've been talking about — the idea of there being this mysterious Creator who's separate from this God of Jesus — I've heard this Creator described as a Demiurge. What is the Demiurge?

**Justin:** The Demiurge is an entity that emerges first in Plato's *Timaeus*. It just means "craftsperson," "craftsman." The Demiurge is, in Plato's *Timaeus*, the entity that created the world. You can think about the World of the Forms, the Demiurge, and the World of Matter. What the Demiurge does is take the World of the Forms and impress them into matter, creating our world. Because our world is material and not infinite and unchanging and perfect, it can only imperfectly take those forms. Therefore our world — the material world — is a world of change and imperfection. That's Plato.

When you take Hellenistic philosophy that basically accepted the idea of the Demiurge and they Stoic-ized it a little bit (it becomes the *Logos*), by the time of Christianity you take this Platonic-Stoic idea of the Demiurge and then you try to hammer it together with the Hebrew Bible. Well, you get interesting sparks that fly.

One answer: the Demiurge just IS Yahweh, and Genesis is just a story of Yahweh's making of the world. That's one story.

> **Key claim:** The Demiurge is Plato's concept of a craftsman-creator who shapes the material world from the Forms. Two Christian interpretations emerged: (1) The Demiurge = Yahweh (orthodox), (2) The Demiurge = an evil/incompetent creator, and Jesus came from a higher God to liberate us (Gnostic).

Another story: if you take a good look around, the world's pretty hellish. It's bad. The third century was especially bad. We live in these cages of clay. The world seems to be a place of intense suffering. Good people die and are persecuted for no good reason, and bad people flourish. The world's pretty bad. So: **what if the god that made this world were evil?** And Jesus, not being the son of that God —

In fact, there's a great hermeneutical move in the Gospel of John where Jesus says to the rabbis, "Your God is not my God. Your God is the father of Satan" — depending on how you read the Greek. There's something really weird that happens where, for very complicated reasons, **the God of the Hebrew Bible, for some Christians, becomes identified with the Demiurge but is now made malevolent.** These are what are called *malevolent demiurgic traditions*, or sometimes we refer to them as Gnosticism.

You can see this most famously in the **Apocryphon of John**, where the God that makes this world is the result of a kind of primordial mistake. That primordial mistake gives rise to an idiot, violent, rapacious God. And that God creates this world as a kind of prison. We are spiritual beings imprisoned in this world — specifically imprisoned in physical bodies. Jesus came as a spiritual being; he took on the prison to show that you could escape it, and he teaches the means by which to escape this world — escape the world of fate, escape the world of the Demiurge — and then go back to our true home, which is the world of the spirit and pure divine goodness.

That becomes a variant of Christianity we typically refer to as Gnosticism. To be very clear: if you were to go to those Christians and ask them "Are you a Gnostic?" they would say, "What's that? I'm a Christian." They didn't think of themselves as Gnostics. We only know them as Gnostics, Sethians, Valentinians, or Marcionites because their enemies named them that — typically named them after their so-called founder — and then polemicized against them. And those polemical works are what survived.

**Alex:** For the longest time, the writings of their enemies were essentially the only knowledge that we had of these Gnostics. It was in the 1940s that we really start to discover at Nag Hammadi the Gnostic texts themselves. It always stuns me to reflect on how recently we've discovered these Gnostic texts. 1945 seems like a long time ago, but in the scheme of scriptural tradition — if you're talking about a text written 100 years after events, that's considered to be nothing. If you're looking at the commentary of early Christians writing 100, 200, 300 years after the death of Christ, we think "wow, how close to the source." And that's how close we are to the discovery of these texts. There's still so much more to discover.

**One question:** In that Christian Gnostic tradition, now that we have access to their actual texts and can hear it in their own words — do you think that this Demiurge figure in the Gnostic tradition is identified with Yahweh, the Creator of the Old Testament?

**Justin:** Oh, definitely. Definitely, no doubt. They say as much. In fact, there's already an Egyptian precedent for this. The Egyptians had already begun, even prior to the rise of Christianity, to associate Yahweh with the god Seth — their sort of demonic chaos foreigner god. Already the Egyptians in their own theological polemics with Judaism had already set the stage for this transition. Making Yahweh evil is something the Egyptians had already done. Then when Egyptian Christianity adopts this demiurgic tradition, they fuse all that together, and what emerges is Gnosticism as represented by texts like the Apocryphon of John or the Gospel of Truth (although Valentinus is a bit more moderated in his dualism than the Apocryphon of John).

> **Key claim:** The demonization of Yahweh as an evil creator had already begun in Egyptian polemics against Judaism (associating Yahweh with the chaotic god Seth) centuries before Gnostic Christianity.

**Alex:** So you have this concept of Yahweh, this God of the Old Testament of the Hebrew Bible. And some Christians say, "Well, this is the God of the Old Testament, this is the Creator, and thus if Jesus is in some way divine, they must be in some way the same figure." Yahweh morphs into the Father, Jesus morphs into the Son, and they're both God. In the other strand of this tradition: "Yes, Yahweh is the Creator of the Old Testament, but clearly Jesus is a totally different entity." Therefore evolves this concept of the Demiurge — that is not the God we should be worshipping and is actually a totally separate entity to Jesus. Jesus is the real God, and Yahweh becomes the Demiurge.

So in one case, Yahweh becomes the Father. In one case, Yahweh becomes the Demiurge. And it's essentially a case of historical contingency that the Demiurge view is condemned as heretical and "Yahweh the Father" is the Christianity that we've inherited today.

**Justin:** One can wonder to what degree it's a matter of historical contingency why one camp won and the other didn't — or to what degree both camps kind of won. People still say things like, "Oh, the God of the Old Testament is mean and about rules and justice, and the God of the New Testament is hippie-dippy Jesus." It's still kind of there. You still hear people in an amateur theological way recapitulate this sort of quasi-Gnostic, quasi-Marcionite position.

These represent pretty divergent Christianities, theologically divergent Christianities. Valentinianism survived in the East until the 6th century at least. We even have warnings in the 6th century of Orthodox priests telling people, "When you go into a new city, don't ask to go to the Christian church — make sure you ask to go to the Catholic church, because you'll likely end up in a Valentinian one."

It's the Game of Thrones — who got the throne? Well, the Orthodoxy — what became the Orthodoxy — ultimately became the official religion of the Roman Empire. That's a recipe for success. When you become the Imperial cult, minority religions aren't going to do so well, or the minority variants.

One thing I will say about the Apostolic Fathers and the early Church Fathers: to their credit, when we did discover the Nag Hammadi library and we were now able to read some of the major texts in the actual language and words of the so-called Gnostics, it's amazing how close Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius were. They did not misrepresent their enemies. They did stretch things, but in many cases, it's often shocking to realize that Irenaeus was pretty accurate. He really tried not to straw-man them.

This is an important lesson we can all learn in debate culture: **steel-manning your enemy is a far better tactic for historically actually winning the debate than straw-manning them.** Irenaeus really did do his due diligence, and we can see that when we compare the texts we've recovered — some of the same texts he was looking at when he was composing his book.

## Chapter 17: A More Attractive Christian Narrative

**Alex:** The more I learn about Gnosticism — and it's always been adjacent to the stuff that I've been doing, but I've never really done a deep dive — the more I hear about it... My friend recently said to me, "A lot of this is interesting for the same reason The Da Vinci Code was so popular — there's this sort of secret knowledge, and you have to resist a little bit against that because it is a bit fun, a bit radical and interesting."

But I must say that also realizing that there were some **significant Christian communities** who believed this — this wasn't some kind of crazy heretic who gets condemned. These are communities of people who really think this is the way to interpret the existence of God and Jesus.

I must say that it seems to me a much more attractive prospect than the Christianity that we've inherited. A lot of my criticisms of Christianity, and a lot of the reasons why I would say that it doesn't make sense to me as a worldview, have to do with things like the Problem of Evil, Divine Hiddenness, and pointing to Old Testament atrocities. I talk about this kind of stuff all the time. And all of that kind of melts away if you can just attribute all of that to a Creator Demiurge and say that Jesus cuts through.

**Justin:** Gnostic dualism — dualism in general — is an attractive solution at first. I think it creates more problems than it solves, or it creates another set of problems. Now the world is evil and human bodies are bad. Can you have an ecologically positive idea about saving the planet or taking care of your body if all matter is fundamentally evil? It generates another set of problems. In fact, even other pagans didn't like Gnosticism — Plotinus wrote a whole book against them because he thought they really missed the mark.

But there it is — it does solve at least the Problem of Evil. It does provide an elegant solution to the Problem of Evil in a way that feels much more tactile than Augustine's solution that evil is just a privation, it's not really real. Go tell someone who's been violently attacked that what they're experiencing is a privation — that's not going to really land. But the Gnostic solution does give some better chops to it.

And to your point earlier — these are big communities. Irenaeus wrote his book because **Valentinus nearly became the Bishop of Rome** in the 140s CE. Valentinus, the sort of classic Gnostic writer — he nearly became the Bishop of Rome! Marcion also tried to kind of bribe his way to becoming high up in the church, ultimately took his toys and went home. But Valentinus — and again, we should not overstate what it means to be Bishop of Rome in the second century; it's not like the Pope now — but it wasn't nothing. The fact that Valentinus had a real shot at becoming the Bishop of Rome in the 2nd century should tell us that these are not one-off weirdo people secretly scribbling crazy stuff by themselves in their houses. **These are whole communities of people for whom these stories were theologically important and meaningful.**

> **Key claim:** Gnosticism was not a fringe heresy. Valentinus nearly became the Bishop of Rome. These were major, mainstream Christian movements that simply lost the political-theological battle.

It's a form of Christianity that — and again, I don't have a dog in the fight of which Christianity I'm rooting for, because I'm not rooting for any of them particularly — but I do think that **a history that leaves out the mess isn't really a history**. History is messy, and we should be unsurprised that the history of Christianity is actually quite messy. When we impose upon it a teleology — that it was always going to go a certain kind of way — then we totally distort what it is that we're studying.

This is the same true of Judaism: rabbinical Judaism was just one Judaism. There were lots of competing Judaisms at that time. The accident of history that one managed to become the hegemonic, normative form of Judaism or Islam or Christianity is no indicator of its correctness, or philosophical goodness, or theological correctness. The accident of history is a much better guide to why we are in the boat that we are than some "cunning of history" à la Hegel or something.
