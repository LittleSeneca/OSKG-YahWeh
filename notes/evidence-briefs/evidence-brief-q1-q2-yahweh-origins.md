## QUESTION 1: WHO WAS YAHWEH ORIGINALLY?

### (a) Scholarly Consensus with Key Evidence

**The Southern/Non-Canaanite Origin:** There is near-consensus among critical scholars that Yahweh originated outside Canaan, specifically in the southern regions of Edom/Midian/Seir/Teman/Paran. This is the single most widely accepted claim in the field — even arch-minimalist N.P. Lemche endorses it (Day, p. 16 n. 12).

**Key Evidence for Southern Origin:**

1. **The Soleb/Amarah West Shasu Inscriptions (c. 1400-1250 BCE):** Egyptian topographical lists from Amenhotep III (Soleb, 14th c.) and Ramesses II (Amarah West, 13th c.) record a place called *tA SAsw yhwA* — "The Land of the Shasu of Yhw(h)." The hieroglyphic rendering *y-h-wʒ* corresponds precisely to the Hebrew Tetragrammaton YHWH. This antedates the Mesha Stele by over 500 years. The Shasu were nomadic pastoralists connected in other Egyptian texts with Seir and Edom. As Van Der Toorn concludes: "By the 14th century BC, before the cult of Yahweh had reached Israel, groups of Edomites and Midianites worshipped Yahweh as their god" (cited in primary sources file). Romer evaluates four candidate origins (Ebla, Ugarit, Mari, Soleb) and eliminates the first three; Soleb remains the best candidate (Romer Ch. 2).

2. **The Four Biblical Poems (Southern March Tradition):** Four independent poems preserve a tradition of Yahweh "coming from" the south:
   - **Deut 33:2:** "Yhwh came from Sinai, he shone forth from Seir, he was resplendent from Mount Paran"
   - **Judges 5:4-5:** "Yhwh, when you came forth out of Seir, when you advanced from the land of Edom, the earth trembled, the sky quaked"
   - **Psalm 68:8-9:** Nearly identical to Judges 5 (with Yhwh replaced by Elohim in the Elohistic Psalter)
   - **Habakkuk 3:3:** "God comes from Teman, the Holy One comes from Mount Paran"

   Judges 5 in particular "is at least in its primitive form often considered to be one of the oldest texts in the Hebrew Bible" (Romer Ch. 2). The convergence of four independent poems on the SAME southern geography is hard to explain as coincidence.

3. **Kuntillet Ajrud (c. 800 BCE):** The inscription "Yahweh of Teman" (*yhwh tmn*, Pithos B) independently confirms the southern association. Teman is a southern region in Edom. This epithet survives centuries after the poetry and shows the tradition persisted.

4. **Archaeology of NW Arabia:** Day notes that civilization in the Hejaz area (NW Arabia/Midian) in the Late Bronze/Early Iron Age contrasts with the "general lack of this in this period in the Sinai peninsula" (Day, p. 17). The Midianites had distinctive pottery (13th-11th c.), camel domestication, and mining operations at Timna (Romer Ch. 3).

**Storm/War God Profile:** Most scholars agree Yahweh's original character had storm and warrior features:

- **Smith (Origins Ch. 7):** Yahweh's "original profile may approximate Athtar (desert warrior/precipitation god)." The old poetry shows a god who makes the earth quake and clouds pour water (Judges 5:4-5), who fights cosmic enemies (Sea/Leviathan — Cross), and who "marches" from the south as a divine warrior.
- **Cross (Part II):** "The central religious concept of early Israel was Yahweh as DIVINE WARRIOR — a concept borrowed directly from Canaanite myth, specifically the Ba'l cycle." The Canaanite pattern of the god defeating chaos (Sea/Death) was "historicized" — Yahweh the Divine Warrior leads Israel's armies in historical battles.
- **Albertz (§2.2):** Yahweh was a "southern Palestinian mountain/storm god (Hadad type) worshipped by Midianites/Kenites before Moses." His "dynamic properties... are twisted round into the political and historical sphere."
- **Lewis (Ch. 6):** "Yahweh's origin seems to be southern, yet many of the garments put onto Yahweh are sewn from northern and inland Canaanite cloth." He fights the same foes as Baal (Yam, Leviathan, Mot), rides the clouds like Baal, and inherits El's divine council.

### (b) Counter-Positions with Their Evidence

**Fleming's Challenge to Southern Geography (2021):**

- **Soleb's Seir connection is unproven:** Fleming argues through physical measurement of column N4 at Soleb that Seir (*s'rr*) was probably NOT in the original Amenhotep III list — it was added in the 13th-century Ramesses II copy (Fleming Ch. 2). If Seir was absent, the sole geographical anchor for Yhwʒ disappears. Without Seir, Yhwʒ could be anywhere in "the inland back country."
- **Early Shasu point NORTH:** Fleming documents that 15th-14th century Shasu references cluster in Syria (Thutmose IV's chariot list, Amenhotep II's prisoner lists). The southern Shasu association only emerges in the 13th century.
- **Yhwʒ was a people-name, not a divine name:** "It is much more likely that the divine name Yahweh derives from the Shasu group without divine association than that the Shasu group already related to the eventual Israelite god" (Fleming Ch. 5).
- **Biblical poetry = theophany, not origin:** "Yahweh is no more originally 'from' Sinai and Seir than Zeus is 'from' Olympus or Baal is from Sapan" (Fleming Ch. 4). The Ugaritic Baal myth shows gods traveling from distant mountain homes — these are mythological divine residences, not evidence of historical cult origins.

**Fleming's Alternative Model:** Yahweh was NOT originally a storm god or young warrior — "All of these associations, however archaic, came afterward" (Fleming Ch. 7). The best analogies for early Yahweh are South Arabian "political gods" like Almaqah of Saba' and 'Amm of Qataban — deities who bind people and king through kinship, deriving power from identification with a specific people, not from a primordial role in the natural world (Fleming Ch. 7).

**Kaufmann's Rejection (1960):** Kaufmann argues the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is "baseless." The Bible consistently distinguishes a "prophetic-only" sanctity south of Beersheba from a "cultic + prophetic" sanctity north of it. No Israelite ever returns to Sinai/Horeb to worship. Jethro is priest "of Midian," not "of YHWH." If the Midianites really worshipped YHWH, "there is no reason why the biblical tradition should have obscured the fact" (Kaufmann Ch. VI).

### (c) Points of Agreement Across Scholars

1. **Yahweh was not originally a Canaanite deity.** His complete absence from Ugaritic texts despite hundreds of named deities is "the strongest argument against northern origins" (Lewis Ch. 6). The Ebla, Ugarit, and Mari candidates for his origin have all been eliminated (Romer Ch. 2).

2. **Yahweh predates Israel.** Whether as a Shasu toponym (Egyptian lists, 14th c.), a people-name (Fleming), or a Midianite god (Albertz, Romer), all agree the name Yahweh is older than the entity "Israel" as known from the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE).

3. **The biblical texts preserve real traditions about Yahweh's non-Israelite connections.** Whether these are historical memories (Romer, Albertz) or kinship traditions without religious implications (Fleming), all agree that the Moses-Midian connection, the southern poetry, and the Jethro narrative are not pure scribal inventions.

4. **Yahweh's early character involved warrior functions.** Even Fleming, who disputes storm-god origins, acknowledges that "fighting for a people" was central — he just argues this doesn't require a "war god" typology.

### (d) Points of Genuine Dispute

| Issue | Majority/Consensus Position | Dissenting Position |
|-------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| **Was Seir in the Soleb list?** | Yes (Giveon, Redford, Ahituv, almost all scholars) | No — physical measurement argues against it (Fleming 2021) |
| **Yhwʒ = divine name or people-name?** | Divine name/toponym derived from divine name (consensus) | People-name; the deity inherited the people's name (Fleming) |
| **Original meaning of Yhwh** | Relates to *hyh* "to be" (Lewis, majority); non-causative preferred | "He blows" from southern Semitic *h-w-y* (Romer); causative "he causes to be" = El epithet (Cross) |
| **Original pronunciation** | "Yahweh" (Lewis: VERY HIGH confidence) | "Yahô/Yahû" was the more ancient form (Romer); "Yahweh" later |
| **Storm god originally?** | Yes — storm/warrior features embedded in oldest poetry (Smith, Romer, Cross, Albertz, Day) | No — storm imagery is secondary; best analogies are "political gods" (Fleming) |
| **Dating of the old poetry** | Range from 12th century to Hellenistic (field is split) | Romer: "at least primitive form" of Judges 5 is very old; Fleming: probably 9th-8th c. monarchic |

## QUESTION 2: HOW DID YAHWEH BECOME ISRAEL'S GOD?

### (a) Scholarly Consensus with Key Evidence

**El Was Israel's Original God; Yahweh Was Imported Later:**

This is a foundational consensus shared by Smith, Day, Lewis, Romer, Albertz, and Fleming (the only notable dissenters being Cross, who argued Yahweh was an epithet of El, and Kaufmann, who argued for Mosaic monotheistic revolution).

**Key Evidence for El's Priority:**

1. **The Name "Israel" Itself:** *Yiśrā'ēl* contains the theophoric element *'ēl* — the proper name of the Canaanite god El. As Smith bluntly puts it: "If Yahweh had been the original god, the group would be named something like *Yiśrâ-yāhû*" (Smith, *Early History*, Ch. 1). Romer prefers the etymology "May El reign" from *ś-r-r* ("rule, govern"), supported by names like *Śĕrāyāhû* ("Yhwh reigns") that later replace El with Yhwh (Romer Ch. 4). Fleming agrees: "The first 'god of Israel' was El, as the deity in the personal name yiśrā'ēl" (Fleming Ch. 7). Albertz: "The conclusion is unavoidable that the element 'el in Israel originally points to a stage in its religious history in which El and not yet Yahweh was the god" (Albertz §2.3).

2. **Deuteronomy 32:8-9 — The "Smoking Gun":** The LXX/4QDeutj reading "sons of God" (*bene 'elohim*) versus the MT's "sons of Israel" (*bene yisra'el*) preserves an older polytheistic theology in which Elyon (the Most High, a title of El) apportions nations to divine beings, and Yahweh receives Israel as HIS portion. This passage "presents a cosmic order in which each deity received its own nation. Israel was the nation which Yahweh received, yet El was the head of this pantheon and Yahweh only one of its members" (Smith, *Origins*, Ch. 7). The MT alteration is deliberate theological censorship. Day adds: the OT NEVER calls them "sons of Yahweh" — always "sons of God" with words containing *'l* — revealing "their origin in the sons of the Canaanite god El" (Day Ch. 1).

3. **Exodus 6:2-3 — The Bible's Own Admission:** "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." Smith reads this as an honest textual memory: the patriarchs worshipped El, not Yahweh. Romer makes the same argument (Romer Ch. 1). Lewis: P's claim that the name was revealed only at Moses' time "is more historically reliable than J's claim of antediluvian Yahwism (Gen 4:26)" (Lewis Ch. 6).

4. **El Sanctuaries Later Absorbed by Yahweh:**
   - **Shechem:** The temple of "El-Berith" (El of the Covenant) in Judges 9:46 — this title appears in a Late Bronze Age Ugaritic text (KTU 1.128). Jacob builds an altar to "El, god of Israel" (Gen 33:20). Rehoboam's coronation assembly there (1 Kings 12). Three separate traditions all associate collective Israel with El at Shechem, with no role for Yahweh (Fleming Ch. 7).
   - **Shiloh:** The tent tradition, incubation-dreams, and the name Elqanah all point to El origins before Yahwistic takeover (Smith, *Origins*, Ch. 7). Archaeology confirms Shiloh was a major cult site in the 12th-11th centuries, destroyed c. 1050 BCE (Romer Ch. 5).
   - **Jerusalem:** Melchizedek is priest of "El Elyon" (Gen 14:18-20) — the city's original deity was El Elyon, not Yahweh (Romer Ch. 5; Fleming Ch. 7).
   - **Deir 'Alla (9th/8th c. BCE):** Plaster inscriptions east of the Jordan record Balaam's visions by "the utterance of El" — entirely El-focused, with no mention of Yahweh (Lewis Ch. 4; Fleming Ch. 7). This is independent evidence for persistent El worship.

5. **No Biblical Polemics Against El:** Unlike Baal (who is vehemently opposed) and Asherah (eventually rejected), El was never condemned. He wasn't rejected — he was absorbed. "This selective appropriation (El = good, Baal = bad, Asherah = rejected, bull = eventually rejected) reveals a deliberate theological filtering process" (Day Ch. 1). The identification happened early and peacefully.

6. **Systematic Transfer of El's Traits to Yahweh:** Aged bearded figure enthroned amidst divine council → "Ancient of Days" (Daniel 7). "Father of years" → "Eternal God" (Deut 33:27). "Kind El, the Compassionate" → "Merciful and gracious God" (Exod 34:6). El's consort Asherah → Yahweh's Asherah (Kuntillet Ajrud). El's bull iconography → Jeroboam's golden calves (Day Ch. 1).

**The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE):**

The stele is the earliest extra-biblical mention of "Israel" — and it is the *terminus ad quem*: Israel as a recognizable entity existed in Canaan by 1208 BCE. Key details:

- The determinative for Israel is an **ethnic group** (man + woman + plural strokes), NOT a city or territory (unlike Ashkelon, Gezer, Yanoam). This confirms an early-stage tribal/ethnic entity — consistent with a semi-nomadic or recently settled population.
- Israel is located in the mountains of Ephraim (Romer Ch. 4).
- There is NO indication Israel worshipped Yahweh at this point. The name "Israel" suggests El worship.
- The stele "in no way presupposes an 'exodus' or any kind of emigration of this group from the land of Egypt" (Romer Ch. 4).

Albertz draws the chronological implication: "The Egyptian mention is probably to be related to a still pre-Yahwistic Israel" — since the Exodus group had not yet arrived from Egypt with Yahweh (Albertz §2.3). This makes Merneptah's Israel an **El-worshipping** entity.

**The Kenite/Midianite Hypothesis:**

The mechanism by which Yahweh moved from the southern deserts to Israel. The hypothesis: Moses encountered Yahweh through his Midianite father-in-law Jethro (a "priest of Midian") and brought this god to the El-worshipping group "Israel." Key evidence:

- **Moses' Midianite connection:** Moses flees to Midian, marries Zipporah daughter of Jethro, and encounters Yahweh at a "mountain of God" in Midianite territory (Exodus 2-3). Jethro offers sacrifices to Yahweh (Exodus 18:12).
- **Jethro already knows Yahweh:** The narrative implies Yahweh was already worshipped among Midianites before Moses. Exodus 18:12 has Jethro officiating at a sacrifice to Yahweh — "this can hardly be the action of a new initiate" (Lewis Ch. 6).
- **The "criterion of embarrassment":** Albertz argues "this account cannot have been invented" because later relations with Midianites were hostile (Albertz §2.2). Israel wouldn't fabricate a foreign origin for its god.
- **Kenite connections:** The Kenites (a Midianite subgroup) appear as metalworkers associated with Israel (Judges 1:16, 4:11). The Rechabites (a Kenite subgroup) were known for "zeal for Yahweh."
- **Sinai's location:** Old poetic traditions locate Yahweh's mountain home in Edom/Seir/Paran — right next to Midianite territory (Albertz §2.2). The traditional Sinai peninsula has almost no Late Bronze Age settlements; the archaeological evidence favors a location east of the Arabah (Albertz, Romer).

Romer's cautious verdict: "The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis elegantly connects the dots but depends on taking the biblical narrative as preserving genuine historical memory." He gives it LOW-MEDIUM confidence (Romer Ch. 3).

**The Political Mechanism: From Shiloh to Jerusalem**

Romer traces Yahweh's journey into Israel's identity:
1. **Shiloh** was Yahweh's first major sanctuary in Israel (12th-11th centuries), a modest hill-country sanctuary in Ephraim. Samuel (whose name is El-based: "El is exalted") is the transitional figure — a prophet of the old El-worshipping tradition who anoints Saul as king.
2. **Saul and David:** Saul's name contains no theophoric element. David's rise brings Yahweh fully into prominence. The Davidic dynasty adopted Yahweh as its patron and used his warrior profile to legitimate military expansion.
3. **Jerusalem:** David conquered the Canaanite/Jebusite city (whose original deity was El Elyon), made it his capital (politically neutral — belonged to no tribe), and brought the ark of Yahweh there (2 Samuel 6). This was the theological masterstroke: Yahweh, the northern war god, was installed in the old El city. "Yahweh now ruled where El had ruled" (Romer Ch. 5).

Fleming's alternative: Rather than Yahweh being imported via Midianites, the name reflects Israel's own diverse pastoralist origins. The "people of Yahweh" (*'am Yhwh*) in Judges 5:13 is the collective name of the pre-Israelite alliance fighting the "kings of Canaan" — and "Israel" is imposed only by the secondary introductory hymn (Judges 5:2-11). Two separate poems (Judges 5 from the north, Exodus 15 from the south) both know a people defined by Yahweh without the name Israel. Yahweh's people preceded Israel as a political entity (Fleming Ch. 6).

### (b) Counter-Positions with Their Evidence

**Cross: Yahweh as El's Epithet (1973)**

Cross argued Yahweh was never a separate deity — he originated as a cultic epithet of El: *ˀil ḏū yahwī ṣabaˀôt* ("El who creates the heavenly hosts"). On this reading, El and Yahweh were always the same god. The name "Yahweh" was a liturgical formula. The convergence model (Smith's three-stage process) is unnecessary because there was nothing to converge.

**Counter-evidence against Cross:**
- The formula *ˀil ḏū yahwī ṣabaˀôt* is unattested anywhere — it's a scholarly reconstruction (Day Ch. 1)
- Kuntillet Ajrud shows proper names CAN appear in construct ("Yahweh of Samaria"), weakening Cross's grammatical objection (Lewis Ch. 6; Day)
- The Soleb evidence shows Yhwʒ as a southern people/place-name, not a liturgical formula (Fleming Ch. 5)
- Deut 32:8-9, with El Elyon superior to Yahweh, is hard to square with "always the same god" (Smith, Lewis)

**Fleming's Challenge to the Midianite Hypothesis (2021)**

Fleming systematically dismantles the biblical prose foundation:

- **Exodus 3 and 6 are schematic bridge texts,** not ancient memory: "No matter the literary-historical system, there is no good case that Exodus 3 and 6 represent such ancient religious realities" (Fleming Ch. 3). These texts solve a narrative problem — how to connect ancestor stories where Yahweh is known with the need to make Moses the mediator of something new.
- **Exodus 18 does not portray Jethro introducing Yahweh:** Jethro's "Now I know that Yahweh is greater than all the gods" (v. 11) is based on new experience — a response to Moses' report, like Rahab in Joshua 2. The "priest of Midian" title lacks any specified deity. "Without the expectation that the people of Israel only learned of Yahweh by name in connection with the exodus, Jethro's meeting with Moses would never have suggested a Midianite origin for the god" (Fleming Ch. 3).
- **The real tradition is about population kinship, not religious borrowing:** Diverse texts show southern/inland kinship (Esau as Jacob's twin, friendly Kenites, Moses' Midianite marriage, Deuteronomy's "your brother" Edomite). But NONE suggests Yahweh was borrowed from these peoples. "Tiele's explanation of Yahweh's origin... depended first of all on the notion that Israel began as a desert people" — not on borrowing a god FROM desert peoples (Fleming Ch. 3).

**Heiser: Yahweh IS Elyon — Same God, Created Divine Council**

Heiser accepts the "sons of God" reading of Deut 32:8-9 but interprets differently: Elyon IS Yahweh — they're the same deity. The "sons of God" are created divine beings receiving administrative assignments, not independent deities in a pantheon. Multiple *elohim* does not mean polytheism: *elohim* is a "place of residence" term denoting beings whose proper domain is the spiritual world. Yahweh is unique AMONG the *elohim*, not the only *elohim* (Heiser Part 2). The denial statements ("there is none besides me") are incomparability claims, not existential denials — just as Babylon says "there is none besides me" (Isa 47:8) without denying other cities exist.

**Kaufmann: Mosaic Monotheistic Revolution**

Kaufmann argued Israelite monotheism was original — a sudden, radical break with everything pagan, originating with a single "creative genius" (Moses). The patriarchal period was NOT monotheistic (Kaufmann honestly admits), but the Mosaic revolution established monotheism instantly. The Kenite hypothesis is "baseless" — the biblical text never says Jethro worshipped Yahweh (Kaufmann Ch. VI). This position has been almost universally rejected by critical scholarship but represents the most thorough traditional counter-argument.

### (c) Points of Agreement Across Scholars

1. **The name "Israel" contains an El-element, not a Yahweh-element.** All scholars agree on this philological fact. Disagreement is about what it MEANS: El as original god vs. generic "god" vs. El and Yahweh always identified.

2. **Yahweh and El were originally separate entities.** This is the dominant view (Smith, Day, Lewis, Fleming, Romer, Albertz), contra Cross and Heiser. The separation is supported by southern geography making Yahweh foreign to Canaan, Deut 32:8-9 showing Yahweh as subordinate to Elyon, Exodus 6:2-3 acknowledging the patriarchs didn't know the name Yahweh, and the lack of anti-El polemic in contrast to anti-Baal polemic.

3. **The convergence/identification of El and Yahweh happened relatively early and peacefully.** However it occurred, there are no biblical traces of conflict. El was absorbed rather than defeated. "The erasure of the father was a 'requisite condition for monotheistic identity'" (Smith, *Origins*, Ch. 7).

4. **The Exodus group (if it existed) was small,** not "all Israel." Albertz describes it as "an economically assimilated but socially declassed group of foreign conscripts to forced labour" — of 'Apiru/Hebrew type (Albertz §2.2). Most critical scholars accept a small-group model.

5. **The monarchy was the key institutional context for the El-Yahweh convergence.** David's installation of the ark in Jerusalem (Romer Ch. 5) and the royal adoption of Yahweh as dynastic patron were pivotal. "Israel chose Yahweh just as Moab chose Chemosh, Edom chose Qos" (Lewis Ch. 6).

6. **The Bible itself preserves memory of the transition.** Exodus 6:2-3, the different divine names in different sources, and the name "Israel" itself all testify that Yahweh was not originally Israel's god — the Bible's own editors couldn't completely smooth over this.

## SYNTHESIS: THE EMERGING PICTURE ACROSS Q1 AND Q2

The cumulative evidence across both questions supports a coherent narrative:

1. **Yahweh originated in the southern deserts** (Edom/Midian/Seir/Teman) as a warrior-storm deity worshipped by Shasu pastoralists, attested as early as the 14th century BCE at Soleb.

2. **"Israel" originally belonged to El**, the Canaanite high god — the name itself contains 'ēl, Merneptah's Israel (c. 1208) shows no Yahweh connection, and El's sanctuaries at Shechem, Shiloh, and later Jerusalem predate Yahwistic takeover.

3. **Yahweh was adopted by Israel through a historical process** — most likely mediated through Midianite/Kenite contacts (the Kenite hypothesis), perhaps involving a historical Moses figure, and accelerated by political developments (Shiloh as first Yahwistic sanctuary, the monarchy's adoption of Yahweh as dynastic patron, David's installation of Yahweh in Jerusalem's El city).

4. **The El-Yahweh merger** was peaceful and comprehensive — El was absorbed, not defeated. His attributes, consort, divine council, sanctuaries, and titles were transferred to Yahweh. The "son" (Yahweh) became the "father" (El). This process created the composite deity of the Hebrew Bible.

5. **Fleming's counter-arguments are important but not fatal.** His deconstruction of the Soleb-Seir connection is original and testable but unverified by independent Egyptologists. His reading of Yhwʒ as a people-name is plausible but not demonstrable. His challenge to the Midianite Hypothesis's biblical foundations is textually careful but doesn't explain all the data — particularly the Soleb inscription's pre-Israelite Yahweh attestation and the convergence of four poems on southern geography.

6. **The dating problem is the single greatest vulnerability** in the entire reconstruction. If the key biblical texts (Judges 5, Deut 33, Exodus 15) are Persian-period compositions rather than pre-monarchic, the "traces of memory" approach collapses. The Schmid challenge remains the most serious threat to all historical reconstructions of pre-exilic Israelite religion.
