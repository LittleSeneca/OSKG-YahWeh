# QUESTION 5: HOW DID MONOTHEISM EMERGE?

## (a) SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS — with Key Evidence

### The Broad Arc: Polytheism → Monolatry → Monotheism

All major scholars (Smith, Römer, Day, Albertz) agree on the basic trajectory, though they differ on timing and causal mechanisms:

**1. Pre-exilic Israel was polytheistic/monolatrous, not monotheistic.**

- **Smith (Origins, Ch8):** "Most of the references to monotheism derive from the exilic period or later." The First Commandment is explicitly monolatrous (cultic exclusion), NOT monotheistic. The Shema (Deut 6:4) declares exclusivity of relationship, not ontological exclusivity. True monotheism requires denying other gods' EXISTENCE, not just prohibiting their worship.
- **Smith's definitional rigor:** True monotheistic statements use exclusivity formulas ('alone,' 'besides') and denial-of-reality language ('nothings,' 'dead'). The pun on *'ĕlōhîm/'ĕlîlîm* in Ps 96:5 shows monotheism operating through wordplay, not philosophical argument.
- **Day (Ch8) places the consensus precisely:** "Absolute monotheism was first given explicit expression by the prophet Deutero-Isaiah in the exile and became fully operative in the post-exilic period" — preceded by a minority monolatrous "Yahweh-alone" movement.
- **Römer (Ch12):** Second Isaiah (c. 540 BCE) contains "the first unambiguous assertion that other gods do not EXIST — not just that they shouldn't be worshipped, but that they are non-entities."

**2. The exile was the crucible for monotheism's emergence.**

- **Römer (Ch12):** Three theological moves of the exile: (1) Yhwh is no longer tied to land or temple — he transcends place; (2) Other gods don't EXIST — Second Isaiah goes further than any pre-exilic text; (3) Yhwh uses foreign empires as instruments — Cyrus is called Yhwh's *māšîaḥ* (Isa 45:1).
- **Albertz (Vol2, Ch4):** Deutero-Isaiah "was the first to formulate consistent monotheism — as a consequence of proclaiming Yahweh's universal power in history." The universal claims of the hymns were radicalized (Yhwh creates darkness and disaster — Isa 45:7, contra Gen 1:2). The explicit denial formula: "I am Yahweh and there is no other" (Isa 45:5).
- **Smith (Origins, Ch10):** All monotheistic statements in Second Isaiah are confined to Isaiah 40-48 and are embedded in three thematic contexts: Yahweh as cosmic creator, Yahweh chooses Israel, idols are empty. None can be read as abstract philosophical theology.

**3. Monotheism was a RHETORIC serving monolatry, not a new "stage" of religion.**

- **Smith (Origins, Ch8):** This is the book's central thesis. "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms." Monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe" (Seitz) — a minority discourse persuading a polytheistic majority. The evolutionary model (polytheism → henotheism → monotheism) is wrong.
- **Smith (Origins, Ch10 capstone):** "Monotheism is not a new stage of religion but a new stage of rhetoric in a situation never known prior to the threat of exile. It represents not a change of religious policy but a new formulation or interpretation of religious reality delineating along cosmic lines what was no longer well delineated in the human, political lines."
- **Function:** Monotheistic rhetoric served inner-community discourse — using absolute language to reinforce exclusive Yahwism under extreme pressure.

**4. The preconditions: Convergence and differentiation (Smith's framework).**

- Israel inherited the four-tier Canaanite pantheon. Over centuries, multiple deities' traits "converged" into Yahweh (he absorbed El's identity, Baal's storm-warrior imagery, Asherah's nurturing roles). Simultaneously, Yahweh was "differentiated" from other deities through polemic.
- **Smith (Origins, Ch9):** Three social locations produced three transformation strategies: priests (Genesis 1 — enclosure of cosmic enemies), wisdom scribes (Proverbs — Woman Wisdom replaces Asherah), and apocalyptic visionaries (Daniel 7 — preserves conflict structure but removes human king).

**5. Josiah's reform (c. 622 BCE) was a pivotal political mechanism.**

- **Römer (Ch11):** The "book of the law" discovered in the temple was Deuteronomy — written to justify Josiah's centralizing program: one God, one temple, one people. The reform INVENTED exclusive Yahwism; it didn't restore it. "Before Josiah, kings who worshipped Yhwh at multiple sanctuaries with steles and asherah poles were the NORM. After Josiah's reform, this became 'idolatry.'"
- **Day (Ch8):** Defends the historicity of Josiah's reform. The contrast between Zephaniah (pre-reform syncretism) and Jeremiah (hails Josiah as just king) implies real change. The purged cults were Canaanite, not Assyrian.

### Exile as Catalyst — The Detailed Mechanism

- **The political catastrophe:** 597 (first deportation), 587 (temple destroyed), 582 (third deportation). Population dropped from ~100,000 to ~40,000. The elite (priests, scribes, administrators) were in Babylon — exactly the people who would write the Bible.
- **Smith (Origins, Ch8):** "As Judah's situation on the mundane level deteriorated in history, the cosmic status of its deity soared in its literature." The Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian empires made the national-god model obsolete.
- **Albertz (Vol2, Ch4):** The destruction of temple and monarchy directly falsified unconditional promises of Zion and kingship theology. But Deuteronomic reform theology had NOT failed — it predicted judgment for disobedience. The prophets of judgment became a second basis for coping with crisis. "The dissolution of political and cultic institutions" paradoxically opened space for theological innovation.
- **Albertz's key insight:** Monotheism was formulated in a situation of absolute political helplessness — NOT imperial ambition. This has implications for how monotheism relates to violence and intolerance.

### Second Isaiah as Watershed

- **Smith (Origins, Ch10):** All monotheistic statements confined to Isaiah 40-48, embedded in polemic against Babylonian cult images, specifically the *mīs pî* ("mouth-washing") ritual that animated statues into living gods. Second Isaiah inverts every element: stresses human manufacture, denies senses to images, mocks processions.
- **Smith's crucial observation:** The polemic has a DOUBLE AUDIENCE — outsiders (Babylonian idol-makers) AND insiders (Judeans tempted to participate). "My idol caused them" (Isa 48:5) — monotheism as INTRA-faith consolidation, not interfaith crusade.
- **Smith (Origins, Ch10):** Isaiah 44 reuses older tradition — Deuteronomy 32's "no other god" polemic provides the template. Isaiah 40 reverses Isaiah 13-14's oracles against Babylon. Monotheism emerges from scribal reuse of older texts — scripture creates monotheism.

### Elephantine as Anomaly

- **Albertz (Vol2, Ch4):** The Egyptian diaspora at Elephantine had their own Yahweh temple (founded before 525 BCE). They "largely dropped out of the theological renewal movement and were viewed negatively by it (Jer 24.8; 44)."
- **Significance:** Elephantine shows a form of Yahwism that didn't go through the Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic filter — preserving a more "conservative" pre-exilic pattern, including a possible consort (Anat-YHW). This is a crucial control on the Jerusalem-centered narrative.
- **The tripartite exilic community:** Those who remained (majority, benefited from land redistribution), the Babylonian Gola (deported upper class — disproportionate influence on religious renewal), and the Egyptian Gola (voluntary emigrants, more integrated, preserved older Yahwistic patterns).

### Monotheism as Transformed Canaanite Myth

- **Smith (Origins, Ch9):** "Biblical monotheism represents not the rejection of Canaanite myth but its creative transformation." Three strategies: enclosure (Genesis 1), absorption (Woman Wisdom replaces Asherah), and preservation-with-replacement (Daniel 7).
- **Genesis 1's "monotheistic poetics"** (Smith, Ch1/Ch9): Sets up expectations of cosmic conflict (*tehom*, darkness, wind in v. 2), then subverts them — God speaks and it is done without battle. *Tanninim* appear not as pre-creation enemies but as created beings (v. 21). Sun and moon are depersonalized as "greater light" and "lesser light" — traditional divine titles stripped of divine status.

## (b) COUNTER-POSITIONS — with Their Evidence

### Zoroastrian Influence Question

- **The question:** Did Persian Zoroastrianism (with its dualism of Ahura Mazda vs. Angra Mainyu, and its eschatological framework) influence Jewish monotheism?
- **Smith (Origins, Ch8):** Persian/Zoroastrian influence "reinforced but was not progenitor" — the rhetoric developed from older Yahwistic language. Mesopotamian precedents (Assur, Marduk as sum-total of all gods) show the pattern wasn't unique.
- **Römer (Ch12):** Cautious — "it remains difficult to prove direct borrowing." Persian imperial theology was not monotheistic, but Ahura Mazda was supreme. This may have influenced Jewish monotheism.
- **Albertz (Vol2, Ch4):** Explicitly rejects external stimulus theories: "some tendencies to monolatry or 'monotheism' can be recognized in the late Babylonian period, but these either go quite different ways theologically... or are chronologically later." Monotheism was "an inner-Judahite theological development."
- **Kaufmann (ChIII):** Argues from a completely different angle: Israelite religion was non-mythological FROM THE BEGINNING — therefore the question of external influence is moot. (See Kaufmann section below.)

### Kaufmann's Counter — The Case for Original Monotheism

Yehezkel Kaufmann's *The Religion of Israel* (1960) represents the most sophisticated counter to the evolutionary consensus. His claims:

**1. The Bible is "utterly ignorant" of the nature of pagan religion (ChI).**
- Across centuries of polemic, law, and narrative, biblical writers consistently misrepresent paganism as mere fetishism (worship of wood and stone) rather than engaging with living gods, myths, and cosmic dramas.
- "No one, apparently, has ever doubted this assumption or criticized it in the light of the data."
- Named gods (Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh) are "mere names" stripped of mythological content. No functional epithets. No sexual differentiation. Astral deities not connected to "host of heaven."
- **The Dagon story (1 Sam 5) as model:** Dagon is a statue that falls and breaks; not even the Philistines suggest the fall portends anything for the living god.

**2. The absence of theogony is radical and unique (ChIII).**
- "The store of biblical legends lacks the fundamental myth of paganism: the theogony. All theogonic motifs are similarly absent. Israel's god has no pedigree, fathers no generations; he neither inherits nor bequeaths his authority."
- The critical explanation ("it was edited out") is inadequate — there are no detectable seams where theogonic material was excised.

**3. The Bible's fetishism polemic is not a late development — it permeates all sources (ChI).**
- "There is no difference between sources" — whether J, E, P, or D, the conception of paganism as fetishism is the same.
- The prophets (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Second Isaiah) are unanimous: "not one word have the prophets for mythological beliefs, not once do they repudiate them."

**4. The argument from candor (ChIII).**
- The biblical texts are CANDID about embarrassing details (God walking in the garden, God's regret at creating humanity, God trying to kill Moses, the sons of God mating with women). If editors were systematically purging mythology, they did a terrible job.
- What's missing (theogony, divine sexuality, God's death and resurrection) must be missing because it was never there.

**5. Israelite religion is non-mythological — this is what makes it unique (ChI).**
- "It is precisely this non-mythological aspect that makes it unique in world history; this was the source of its universal appeal."
- Biblical demonology is non-pagan — demons are under God's control, and evil comes from sin, not a metaphysical principle. The pagan gods did not become demons in Israel.
- The biblical ban on magic and divination is fundamentally different from pagan bans — it's a blanket prohibition rooted in monotheistic logic.

**6. The prophets didn't create monotheism — they inherited it (Ch XI-XIII).**
- Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel all worked within an already-existing monotheistic framework. They were calling Israel back to covenant faithfulness — not to a new religious consciousness.
- Jeremiah is "the prophet of Deuteronomy" — his entire worldview is Torah-derived, not his own innovation.
- "Between Jeremiah and the people there was, then, no fundamental religious difference. His prophecy of destruction is not founded on a denial of the sanctity of Jerusalem and the temple."

**Assessment of Kaufmann by the notes author:** 
- **Genuine contributions:** The absence of theogony IS remarkable; the Bible's fetishism polemic IS a real pattern the critical model struggles to explain; the argument from candor IS a genuine methodological challenge.
- **Major weaknesses:** The archaeological evidence (Kuntillet Ajrud — "YHWH and his Asherah" — discovered after Kaufmann's death) directly contradicts his claim that popular religion was non-mythological. His treatment of Amos 9:7 as "satirical hyperbole" is special pleading. The Ugaritic parallels to divine council and Chaoskampf show Israel shared mythological FRAMEWORKS even if it didn't share mythological NARRATIVES.
- **The Ezekiel/Tammuz problem (Ezek 8:14):** When the biblical text DOES mention a pagan mythological practice, Kaufmann's response is revealing: maybe they weren't Israelites, maybe they didn't understand the myth. This is textbook special pleading.
- **Bottom line:** Kaufmann identifies real features of the biblical texts that the critical model struggles to explain. Where he overreaches is in claiming these features were present "from the beginning." The non-mythological character of the Bible is more plausibly an ACHIEVEMENT than an INHERITANCE.

### Schmid's Maximal Skepticism (Counter to Early Sources)

- **Schmid (§11):** The Torah formed in the PERSIAN PERIOD (5th-4th centuries BCE) — driven by Persian imperial authorization of local laws. "It seems somewhat concrete that [the formation of the Torah] took place in the Persian period."
- **Implication:** If Schmid is right, the Pentateuch's narratives about Israel's origins are Persian-period theological constructs — NOT windows into pre-exilic religion. Smith's use of Genesis and Exodus as evidence for pre-exilic polytheistic belief becomes methodologically indefensible.
- This is the European maximal skepticism — Romer follows this approach (rejecting the Documentary Hypothesis), while Smith retains it cautiously.

## (c) POINTS OF AGREEMENT

1. **True ontological monotheism (denial that other gods EXIST) first appears unambiguously in Second Isaiah (Isa 40-55, c. 540 BCE).** Smith, Römer, Day, and Albertz all agree on this. Even Kaufmann acknowledges Second Isaiah's rhetorical innovation, though he sees it as unfolding implications of an existing monotheism rather than creating it.

2. **Pre-exilic Israel was at most monolatrous, not monotheistic.** The Shema and First Commandment prohibit worship of other gods without denying their existence. This is the consensus position (Smith, Day, Römer, Albertz). Kaufmann dissents.

3. **The exile was the catalyst for monotheistic rhetoric.** All agree on the timing, though they differ on mechanism. Smith emphasizes rhetorical strategy; Römer emphasizes political centralization and exile trauma; Albertz emphasizes the theological logic of universal divine power in a situation of political powerlessness.

4. **Josiah's reform (c. 622) was a real historical event that centralized worship and enforced monolatry.** Day and Römer agree; differences are about how much of the biblical account is historical vs. retrojection.

5. **Monotheism involved transformation of Canaanite heritage, not creation ex nihilo.** Smith's "transformed myth" thesis is broadly accepted.

6. **The fall of Samaria (722 BCE) transformed Judah and set the stage for monotheism.** Römer emphasizes this most strongly; Albertz affirms the refugee-scribe thesis.

7. **The Persian period institutionalized monotheism as orthodoxy.** Smith sees the rhetoric emerging in the exile; Römer extends the process into the Persian period — the two positions are partially reconcilable.

## (d) GENUINE DISPUTES

1. **Was monotheism a new STAGE of religion or a new RHETORIC?** Smith says rhetoric — "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe." The evolutionary model (polytheism → henotheism → monotheism as progressively higher stages) is wrong. The counter: rhetoric and ontology are not exclusive categories. If monotheism was "just" rhetoric, why did it STICK and become orthodox doctrine? The unresolved tension in Smith's model: at what point does the rhetoric BECOME the belief?

2. **Persian/Zoroastrian influence:** Was it progenitor, reinforcement, or irrelevant? Smith says "reinforced but was not progenitor." Römer is cautious ("difficult to prove direct borrowing"). Albertz rejects external stimulus entirely. This remains an open question with the chronological evidence ambiguous.

3. **Kaufmann vs. everyone:** Was Israelite religion non-mythological FROM THE BEGINNING? Kaufmann's thesis directly contradicts the entire evolutionary model. The archaeological evidence (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom, pillar figurines) is the strongest counter to Kaufmann — discovered after his death.

4. **Dating of the Pentateuchal sources:** Was the Torah formed in the Persian period (Schmid, the European maximalist position) or does it preserve substantially earlier material (Smith's cautious retention of the Documentary Hypothesis)? This single dating question is the most important unresolved issue for the entire Truth Project.

5. **Smith vs. Römer on timing:** Smith sees the transition happening in the late 7th-6th centuries (late monarchy + exile). Römer extends institutionalization into the Persian period. The notes author suggests partial reconciliation: Smith is right that RHETORIC emerges in the exile; Römer is right that INSTITUTIONALIZATION as orthodoxy is Persian-period.

6. **Albertz vs. Schmid on the Torah's formation:** Albertz sees it as a Persian-backed compromise between Deuteronomistic (lay) and Priestly parties. Schmid sees it more radically as a Persian-period imperial authorization construct. Both agree on Persian-period final form; they disagree on how much earlier material survives and how organic the process was.

7. **The fall of Jerusalem — moral decay or geopolitical theodicy?** Kaufmann argues Judah was NOT exceptionally corrupt — the fall was theodical interpretation, not historical necessity. Jeremiah's treatment (calling for surrender yet surviving) is his evidence. Critical scholars see real social crisis in the prophetic record.

# QUESTION 6: WHAT ABOUT THE DIVINE COUNCIL?

## (a) SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS — with Key Evidence

### The Divine Council is a Pervasive Biblical Reality

All scholars agree that the Hebrew Bible describes a divine council — a heavenly court of divine beings surrounding Yahweh. This is not a single obscure verse but a pervasive concept across multiple genres and books.

**Key biblical evidence:**
- **1 Kings 22:19-22:** Micaiah's vision — "I saw Yahweh sitting on his throne with all the host of heaven standing beside him"
- **Job 1-2:** "The sons of God came to present themselves before Yahweh" — including *haśśāṭān*, "the adversary"
- **Isaiah 6:** "I saw the Lord sitting on a throne... seraphim were standing above him"
- **Psalm 89:5-7:** "Who in the sky is equal to Yahweh? Who is like Yahweh among the sons of God, a God feared greatly in the council of the holy ones?"
- **Psalm 82:1:** "God (*'ĕlōhîm*) stands in the assembly of El (*'ădat 'ēl*); among the *elohim* he judges"
- **Genesis 6:1-4:** The "sons of God" (*bĕnê hā'ĕlōhîm*) taking human wives

**Key terminology catalogued (Heiser):** *bĕnê hā'ĕlōhîm* ("sons of God"), *bĕnê 'ēlîm* ("sons of gods/God"), *sôd qĕdōšîm* ("council of holy ones"), *'ădat 'ēl* ("assembly of El"), *kōl ṣĕbā' haššāmayim* ("all the host of heaven").

### The Council Derives from Ugaritic/Canaanite Precedent

**Smith (Origins, Ch2-3):** The Ugaritic divine council was a multi-tiered structure of assemblies centered on El, Baal, and Ditanu. Israel inherited this four-tier structure, but collapsed it through convergence: El (tier 1) merged with Yahweh (originally tier 2), and the middle tiers telescoped.

**Day (Ch1):** The OT's "sons of God" derive directly from the Ugaritic concept of the seventy sons of El and his consort Asherah. "That this is certain can be established from the fact that both were seventy in number."

**Cross (Part I):** El's epithets were systematically absorbed by Yahweh. The Ugaritic El is aged and wise ("Father of Years"), creator ("Creator of Creatures"), compassionate, royal, enthroned at the cosmic rivers — all traits later applied to Yahweh.

**Direct Ugaritic parallel:** KTU² 1.4.VI.46 — "the seventy sons of Asherah (Athirat)." Since Asherah was El's consort, these are El's sons.

### Deut 32:8-9 — The Text-Critical "Smoking Gun"

This is the single most important text for the debate.

**The reconstructed original (from 4QDeut-j, confirmed by LXX):**
> When Elyon (the Most High) apportioned the nations,
> when he divided humankind,
> he fixed the boundaries of the peoples
> according to the number of the **sons of God** (*bĕnê 'ēl*).
> For Yahweh's portion is his people,
> Jacob his allotted inheritance.

**Text-critical data:**

| Tradition | Deut 32:8b |
|-----------|-----------|
| **MT** | *bĕnê yiśrā'ēl* ("sons of Israel") |
| **LXX** | ἀγγέλων θεοῦ ("angels of God") |
| **4QDeut-j** | *bny 'lwhm / bny 'l* ("sons of God" / "sons of El") |

**Scholarly assessment:** The MT reading is widely regarded as a later theological "correction." The MT change from "sons of God" to "sons of Israel" is the smoking gun — scribes altered the text because the original implied polytheism. The contextual logic is decisive: how do "sons of Israel" determine the boundaries of ALL peoples?

**What it preserves (Smith, Ch8):** "In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god but one of his sons." Yahweh was one of approximately 70 divine sons, each assigned a nation, under Elyon/El.

### Psalm 82 — The Divine Prosecution

**Lewis (Ch9):** "Psalm 82 is one of the most visionary poems in the entire Hebrew Bible, as it applies Yahweh's judicial authority to the wrongdoings of the gods."

The psalm is saturated with legal vocabulary. The gods are charged with failing to protect the poor, orphan, and destitute. The sentence is death: "Like humans you shall die, like one of the officials you shall fall" (v. 7). The psalm ends with a plea for Yahweh to "rise up" and judge the earth because he inherits all nations.

**Two possible readings (Lewis's framework):**
- **Mythopoeic:** Yahweh prosecutes the gods in El's divine council — with El as the presiding judge. Yahweh is subordinate to El/Elyon.
- **Mythopoetic:** Yahweh IS Elyon, and the "gods" are rebellious created divine beings (Heiser's position).

**Smith (Ch2, Ch8):** Psalm 82 "preserves a tradition that casts the god of Israel not in the role of the presiding god of the pantheon but as one of his sons." The gods are "sons of Elyon" (v. 6) — Elyon being a title of El.

### The Council Persists in Monotheism — Only Status Changes

**Smith (Ch2):** "Divine council language and scenes also appear in the 'priestly work' of the Pentateuch and post-exilic books (Zechariah and Daniel), which assumedly are monotheistic. In other words, monotheism requires that one divine assembly headed by one divine ruler, but it makes little or no impact on the language of assembly in itself."

Isaiah 40 (the opening of Second Isaiah) involves a divine council scene, yet this section contains "the greatest number of monotheistic statements in the Bible." The STRUCTURE (ruler + court) persists; only the ONTOLOGICAL STATUS of court members changes — from "deities" to "subservient powers." Monotheism is a revaluation within an inherited structure, not a new structure.

### The Council as Family — Ugaritic Social Parallels

**Smith (Ch3):** CAT 4.360 (an administrative text from Ugarit) uses identical household terminology to the myths: *'adn* (patriarch), *b'l* (family head), *bn* (sons), *'aḫt* (daughters), *n'rm* (retainers). The pantheon IS a divine household, mapped onto actual Ugaritic social structure.

**Smith's theoretical contribution:** The divine family provided polytheism with its own form of "oneness." "In a society where the highest level of social association and identification was the family and not the individual, the polytheism of a divine family would have been far more intelligible than any notion of monotheism." The family model's structural flexibility (outsiders integrated through marriage and adoption) is what eventually made monotheistic convergence possible.

**Baal as outsider (Smith, Ch3):** El's family is astral in character. Baal is an outsider because he is a storm-god with separate paternity (*bn dgn*, "son of Dagan"). Philo of Byblos confirms the narrative. The structural flexibility that allowed Baal's integration is the same mechanism that later let Yahweh absorb both El and Baal.

## (b) THE FOUR POSITIONS — Heiser vs. Smith/Römer/Cross vs. Sommer

### Position 1: Heiser — Yahweh Supreme from the Beginning

**Core claim:** The divine council consists of created *elohim* (divine beings), subordinate to Yahweh who is unique as the uncreated Creator. This is consistent with monotheism properly understood.

**Key arguments:**
- **Deut 32:8-9:** Elyon IS Yahweh. "To a biblical writer, the Most High (*elyon*) was the God of Israel." One God, two titles, one action: distributing nations to subordinate divine beings. "The whole point of Deut 32:8-9 is that Israel is uniquely blessed because they are Yahweh's DIRECT portion — the other nations got lesser beings. There is no hint in the passage that Israel's God is a lesser being."
- **The "denial statements" are incomparability claims, not existential denials:** Isaiah 47:8 has Babylon saying "there is none besides me." Zephaniah 2:15 has Nineveh saying the same. These obviously don't deny other cities EXIST — they claim incomparability. Same logic for Deut 4:35.
- ***Elohim* as "place of residence" term:** It denotes beings whose proper domain is the spiritual world — not "beings with all divine attributes." Yahweh is unique AMONG the *elohim*, not the only *elohim*.
- **Multiple *elohim* does NOT mean polytheism:** "There is no warrant for concluding that plural *elohim* produces a pantheon of interchangeable deities. A biblical writer would not presume another *elohim* had the same attributes as Yahweh. That is polytheistic thinking. It never enters the mind of any biblical writer."
- **Psalm 82:** God judges corrupt divine beings — created, subordinate, fallible. Not polytheism; hierarchical monotheism.

**Heiser's framing:** He positions himself between evangelicalism (which filters out the divine council) and critical scholarship (which calls it polytheism). He accepts the DATA (divine council is real) but rejects both traditional and critical INTERPRETATIONS.

### Position 2: Smith/Römer/Cross — Yahweh Was Originally NOT Supreme

**Smith's core claim (Origins, Ch2, 7, 8):** "In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god but one of his sons."

**The tripartite development:**
1. El is the high god, Yahweh is a national patron among ~70 divine sons (Deut 32:8-9, Ps 82)
2. Yahweh absorbs El's identity and titles (convergence — El epithets applied to Yahweh)
3. Monotheistic rhetoric declares the other gods don't exist (Second Isaiah)

**Römer's core claim:** Yahweh was originally a southern desert/storm god (from Seir/Edom/Teman), adopted by "Israel" (who originally worshipped El), installed in Jerusalem by David as a political-theological merger with El's city. The distinction between El and Yahweh is visible in Exodus 6:2-3: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them."

**Cross's core claim (Part I):** Yahweh was originally NOT identified with El — the identification happened historically and is visible in the texts. Yahweh may have begun as an EPITHET of El ("He creates the heavenly armies"), which became a proper name and displaced the original deity. El's epithets were systematically absorbed by Yahweh. The "God of the Fathers" was a distinct deity type (personal clan god) that was later merged with the Canaanite high god El.

**Key evidence common to all three:**
- Deut 32:8-9 (LXX/DSS): Elyon apportions nations; Yahweh receives Israel as his portion
- Psalm 82: Yahweh/Elohim judges in El's assembly; the gods are "sons of Elyon"
- The name "Israel" contains *'ēl*, not *yāhû*
- Exodus 6:2-3: Explicitly acknowledges patriarchs worshipped El, not Yahweh
- El epithets applied to Yahweh: "Ancient of Days," "Creator of Heaven and Earth," "Compassionate and Gracious"

### Position 3: Sommer — The Fluidity Model (Reframes the Entire Debate)

**Core claim:** The key distinction is not monotheism vs. polytheism — it's FLUID vs. ANTI-FLUID divinity.

**The ANE fluidity model:** "A single deity could exist simultaneously in several bodies. Further, a deity could have a fragmented or ill-defined self, for gods were not fully distinct from each other, at least not all of the time." Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh appear as distinct goddesses in treaties, addressed as plural — yet are functionally one.

**J and E preserve the fluidity model (Ch2):**
- Yhwh had local manifestations: "Yhwh of Samaria," "Yhwh of Teman" (Kuntillet Ajrud), "Yhwh of Hebron" (2 Sam 15:7)
- The *mal'akh* (angel/messenger) is an AVATAR of Yhwh, not a separate being: Genesis 18 (Yhwh as three men), Exodus 3 (the *mal'akh* IS Yhwh), Judges 6 (alternating identity mid-conversation)
- J and E are MONOTHEISTIC — but their monotheism is FLUID. One God, many bodies.

**P and D rejected fluidity (Ch3):**
- P: God's *kavod* (Glory) replaces God's body — a hypostasis that allows divine presence without embodiment
- D: God's *shem* (Name) dwells in the temple — NOT God himself. "The Deuteronomic editors wished to make a subtle distinction that the older texts lack: It is not God who dwells in the temple; only God's name does."

**The historical arc:**
| Source | Date | Theology | Body Count |
|--------|------|----------|------------|
| J/E | 10th-8th c. | Fluid monotheism | Many bodies, one God |
| D | 7th c. | Anti-fluid monotheism | No body; only the Name |
| P | 6th-5th c. | Anti-fluid monotheism | No material body; only the Glory |
| Later Judaism | Post-biblical | Radical anti-fluid | NO body at all |

**Implication for the divine council debate:** The "sons of God" and the divine council members in J and E may not be SEPARATE GODS — they may be fragments, avatars, or fluid extensions of the ONE God. What Smith reads as polytheism, Sommer reads as fluid monotheism. The same data, radically different interpretive framework.

**Sommer has Heiser-like implications but arrives there differently:** Heiser says Yahweh was always supreme and the *elohim* are created subordinates. Sommer says Yahweh was always ONE but had multiple bodies — and the council members are fluid extensions, not independent deities. Both deny polytheism; they differ on mechanism.

### Summary Table: The Four Positions on the Divine Council

| Question | Heiser | Smith/Römer/Cross | Sommer |
|----------|--------|-------------------|--------|
| Were El and Yahweh originally distinct? | No — Elyon = Yahweh | Yes — El presided; Yahweh was a son | Fluid — one God, multiple manifestations |

## (c) POINTS OF AGREEMENT

1. **The divine council is a pervasive biblical reality.** All four positions agree on the DATA — the Hebrew Bible describes a heavenly court of divine beings. Disagreement is about INTERPRETATION.

2. **Deut 32:8-9 originally read "sons of God," not "sons of Israel."** Even Heiser accepts the LXX/DSS reading — the MT is a theological correction. The debate is about what this MEANS: two distinct deities (Smith), or one God with two titles/roles (Heiser), or one God with fluid manifestations (Sommer).

3. **The number 70 is significant.** Ugaritic texts attest "seventy sons of Asherah/El" (KTU² 1.4.VI.46). Genesis 10 lists seventy nations. 1 Enoch and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan speak of seventy guardian angels. The pattern is too specific to be coincidence.

4. **The council structure persists in monotheistic texts.** Isaiah 40, Daniel 7, Zechariah 3 all have council scenes. Monotheism doesn't eliminate the council — it revalues its members.

5. **The council derives from Canaanite precedent.** All agree the conceptual framework comes from Ugaritic/Canaanite religion. Smith and Day emphasize direct derivation; Heiser accepts the shared framework but distinguishes Israelite theology from Canaanite polytheism.

6. **Genesis 6:1-4 "sons of God" = divine beings.** This is the scholarly consensus, even among critical scholars. Heiser's defense of this reading against the Sethite interpretation is strong.

---

## (d) GENUINE DISPUTES

1. **Elyon = Yahweh or Elyon ≠ Yahweh in Deut 32:8-9?** This is THE hinge question. Heiser: "To a biblical writer, the Most High was the God of Israel." Smith: The text distinguishes two actors — one who apportions (Elyon) and one who receives (Yahweh). If they're the same, the structure is redundant. The text-critical data supports the LXX/DSS reading, but doesn't resolve whether the two names refer to one deity or two.

2. **Were the council members originally gods or always sub-*elohim*?** Heiser: they were always created, subordinate divine beings. Smith: they were originally gods in a polytheistic pantheon, later demoted. This is a dispute about ORIGINAL ONTOLOGY, not about textual data.

3. **Does Psalm 82 depict God judging his subordinates or a junior god prosecuting in El's court?** Lewis presents both the mythopoeic (Yahweh subordinate to El) and mythopoetic (Yahweh = Elyon) readings without forcing a choice. The ambiguity IS the point.

4. **Is the name pattern "sons of God (*'l*), never sons of Yahweh" significant?** Day argues this proves the council was originally El's, not Yahweh's — the name pattern preserves the older theology. Heiser would say the terminology was inherited but revalued.

5. **Sommer's fluidity model vs. the others:** If Sommer is right, the entire "polytheism → monotheism" narrative is wrong — Israel was ALWAYS monotheistic, but its monotheism was fluid. What evolved wasn't the number of gods but the nature of divine embodiment. This reframes the debate in a way that neither Heiser nor Smith/Römer fully address.

6. **Cross's "Yahweh as El-epithet" thesis:** Was Yahweh originally an epithet of El ("He creates the heavenly armies") that became a proper name? This is philologically plausible but unprovable. If correct, Yahweh is not a foreign god at all — he's a TITLE of El that broke free. This would collapse the El-Yahweh distinction in a different way than Heiser's model.

## METHODOLOGICAL NOTE FOR CAPSTONE SYNTHESIS

The Q5 and Q6 debates are deeply interconnected. The divine council question (Q6) is essentially the STARTING POINT for the monotheism question (Q5). If Yahweh was originally a second-tier son of El (Smith's position), then monotheism required both promoting Yahweh to the top tier and absorbing/demoting the other gods — a dramatic theological transformation. If Yahweh was always supreme (Heiser, Sommer via different mechanisms), then monotheism is less a revolution than a clarification.

The unresolved dating question (Persian-period Torah per Schmid vs. substantially earlier sources per Smith) cuts across both questions. If the Torah is Persian-period, then its narratives about the divine council and Israel's polytheistic past may be theological constructs rather than historical evidence.

Kaufmann remains the outlier — his argument that Israelite religion was non-mythological FROM THE BEGINNING would, if sustained, make both Q5 and Q6 moot. But the archaeological evidence (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom) poses severe challenges to his position.

The Elephantine community serves as a crucial control: a Yahwistic community that didn't go through the Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic filter, preserving older patterns (including possible divine consort). This suggests the "official" monotheism of post-exilic Jerusalem was one trajectory among several possible Yahwisms.

**Key unresolved questions to flag for the capstone:**
1. Does the rhetoric/ontology distinction collapse? If monotheism is "just" rhetoric, why did it become orthodox belief?
2. Was Persian/Zoroastrian influence progenitor, reinforcement, or irrelevant?
3. Does Sommer's fluidity model genuinely reframe the debate or is it a distinction without a difference?
4. Can Heiser's hierarchical monotheism accommodate the Deut 32:8-9 evidence without special pleading?
5. How do the Elephantine papyri constrain the narrative of inevitable monotheistic development?
