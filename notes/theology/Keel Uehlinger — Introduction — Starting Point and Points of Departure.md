# Keel & Uehlinger — Starting Point & Points of Departure

The introduction and methodology sections that establish why iconography — and specifically the ~8,500 stamp seals from Palestine/Israel — provides the most reliable primary-source evidence for reconstructing preexilic Israelite religion. Text-based reconstructions (Smith, de Moor) are working with a puzzle "missing many or most of its pieces" because they ignore the largest category of evidence.

---

## Claim 1: The polytheistic consensus about preexilic Israel must be tested against material evidence, not just texts

**Authors' claim:** "This conclusion regarding the polytheism of preexilic Israel is correct if it is limited to a strictly 'theoretical level.' The texts cited by Weippert (Psalms 82, 89; Deut 32:8f.) do in fact assume that other gods existed in addition to Yahweh. But the texts say nothing about whether these deities were venerated in any way in Israel and Judah — whether that be carried on in a public cultic setting or else in family or even in personal and private piety."

Keel and Uehlinger acknowledge that scholars like Lang, Weippert, and Knauf are right that biblical texts (Psalms 82, Deut 32:8f.) assume other gods exist. But they draw a sharp distinction between a text's *theological assumption* and the *actual religious practice on the ground*. The key question is not "did some texts assume polytheism" but "were other gods actually worshiped in Israel and Judah?"

**Evidence presented:**
- Lang (1981, 1983) posits a three-tiered polytheism: personal/ancestral gods, regional/national gods (Yahweh), and specialist gods (weather, fertility)
- Weippert (1990) argues for polytheism persisting even into the Hasmonean period — including a divine consort for Yahweh
- Knauf (1988b) posits different divine triads at Bethel, Jerusalem, and Hebron
- But all these scholars work almost exclusively from texts whose dating is notoriously uncertain
- "Exegetical discussion has been complicated still more during the last two decades by widespread uncertainty about how to date the biblical texts"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The methodological point is well-taken. Asserting polytheism from texts that are difficult to date and theologically polemical is circular if untested against material evidence.

**What's at stake:** This is the book's entire justification for existing. If textual reconstructions are adequate, the 8,500 seals are just decoration. If they're not, every text-based reconstruction — Smith's, de Moor's, even the critical consensus — is a half-built puzzle.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (1990), de Moor (1990), and the broader text-based tradition. Smith acknowledged the problem in his own preface: "working with a puzzle that is missing many or most of its pieces" (Smith 1990, xxix).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that iconographic evidence is just as ambiguous as textual evidence — a seal of a goddess doesn't tell you who worshiped it or how. Keel & Uehlinger address this in their methodology section.

**My assessment:** The criticism is devastating because it's so simple. Smith's Origins cites almost no archaeological evidence. Dever demolished him on this point, and Keel & Uehlinger provide the comprehensive alternative. The burden of proof now falls on text-only scholars to explain why the largest single category of primary evidence should be ignored. They haven't.

## Claim 2: Tigay's onomastic study is methodologically pioneering but must be expanded with iconographic data

**Authors' claim:** "Tigay produces a work that can be termed a pioneering effort, both in terms of his methodological premises, because he calls for supporting religio-historical theses with empirical data that take both chronology and geography into account, and in terms of his conclusions, because he raises serious doubts about the validity of the general consensus that Israel was supposedly polytheistic."

Tigay (1986, *You Shall Have No Other Gods*) studied personal names on Iron Age II inscriptions and found that only ~6% contained names of deities other than Yahweh. Most personal names were Yahwistic. This challenged the consensus that preexilic Israel was thoroughly polytheistic. Keel & Uehlinger see their work as "an expansion of Tigay's efforts" — same empirical rigor, but applied to the iconographic evidence Tigay only touched on in an appendix.

**Evidence presented:**
- Tigay's key finding: overwhelmingly Yahwistic onomasticon in Iron Age II
- Tigay included an "important appendix" on iconographic materials (1986, 91-96) — but this was peripheral to his main argument
- Keel & Uehlinger will critique Tigay "at times, in an effort to sharpen the conclusions still further"
- They note that inscriptional evidence "has dominated the discussion of the religious history of Palestine/Israel in recent years" — iconography has been "generally overlooked"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Tigay's onomastic data is strong, but Keel & Uehlinger will argue that the iconographic evidence tells a more complex story than Tigay's names-alone conclusion suggests. The value is in the methodological expansion.

**What's at stake:** If Tigay is right that the onomastic evidence shows widespread Yahwistic devotion, but the iconographic evidence shows persistent goddess worship and astral cults, then the two data sets reveal different dimensions of Israelite religion — what people *named* themselves vs. what they *did*. This foreshadows Dever's book-religion vs. folk-religion distinction.

**Who disagrees:** No one directly. Tigay is respected. But scholars who emphasize polytheism (Weippert, Lang) would argue that personal names don't necessarily reflect actual practice — you could name your kid after Yahweh and still burn incense to the Queen of Heaven.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data could be read as evidence that Yahwism was already dominant, supporting a more conservative position than Keel & Uehlinger's. The iconographic evidence Keel & Uehlinger will present might *undermine* rather than expand Tigay.

**My assessment:** This is a crucial methodological bridge. The onomastic data IS significant and many scholars ignore it. But names are cheap — iconography costs money and reflects genuine demand. The two data sets together are far more powerful than either alone. Keel & Uehlinger are building the most comprehensive empirical case in the field.

## Claim 3: Religion is a symbol system, not a text system — and iconography is the primary evidence for ancient symbol systems

**Authors' claim:** "A culture, or one aspect of a culture such as its religion, is made up of a limited number of visible and audible signs that form a distinct framework or network. The first task of those who study a particular culture is to describe these signs as precisely and comprehensively as possible."

Drawing on Geertz's anthropological definition of culture as a symbol system, Keel & Uehlinger argue that texts are only one kind of sign within the religious symbol system. Iconography — seals, figurines, amulets, cult stands, temple decorations — constitutes a parallel and often more revealing set of signs. The key insight: seal amulets survive "in relatively comparable quantities for all periods" and "can virtually serve as the standard by which religious history is documented, particularly because they are more or less public artifacts and can thus serve as a sensitive seismograph to detect subtle shifts in religious history."

**Evidence presented:**
- Dever's definition of religion as "belief through texts, cult through material culture" is "quite problematic" — belief and cult come from the same arena, not different ones
- The Einsiedeln Black Madonna case study: how religious symbols (Mary statues), economic activity (pilgrimage goods), and social status (monastic prestige) form an interdependent system; when tourism replaces pilgrimage, star symbols replace Mary medallions
- The Fribourg Biblical Institute's corpus of ~8,500 stamp seals from legal excavations in Palestine/Israel — the largest such catalog ever assembled
- Seals are more valuable than inscriptions because they appear in all periods consistently, unlike texts which cluster in certain eras

**Confidence:** HIGH. The symbol-system framework is standard in anthropology (Geertz) and Assyriology (Assmann's "constellations"). Applying it systematically to Palestinian archaeology is the innovation.

**What's at stake:** If religion is primarily a symbol system, then iconography is not supplementary to texts — it IS the primary evidence. Text-only scholars are doing something analogous to studying medieval Christianity without looking at cathedrals, paintings, and rosaries. This flips the burden of proof.

**Who disagrees:** Text-centric biblical scholars who see iconography as decorative rather than evidentiary. Also scholars who argue for a sharp text/image hierarchy in ancient Israel — that the "word" was primary and images secondary. Keel & Uehlinger's response: "Anyone who prefers to work exclusively with texts (e.g., to reconstruct 'Canaanite' religion using nothing but textual sources from Ugarit) ought to get little or no hearing."

**Alternative reading:** One could accept the symbol-system model but argue that in the specific case of Israel, the textual evidence IS the symbol system that mattered most — that Israelite religion was uniquely logocentric. This is the position Keel & Uehlinger are trying to test empirically.

**My assessment:** The Einsiedeln case study is elegant and persuasive. The question is whether ancient Israel is more like medieval Einsiedeln (integrated symbol system) or like Protestant Geneva (text over image). Keel & Uehlinger's empirical answer — 8,500 seals across all periods — strongly suggests the former. The book's value is in forcing text-centric scholars to account for data they've never had to face.

## Claim 4: Comparativism must be geographical and chronological — Ugarit is not Canaan

**Authors' claim:** "Conclusions drawn from an interpretation of Bronze Age texts discovered in northern Syria, and the religio-historical hypotheses developed from such evidence, cannot be used uncritically to explain the religious history of Canaan during the second millennium and, though it has happened repeatedly, certainly not to clarify what happened in Israel during the first millennium."

This is the strongest methodological objection to the Ugarit-centric comparative approach that dominates the field (Cross, Smith, Day, Lewis, etc.). Keel & Uehlinger argue that Ugarit is geographically outside Canaan and chronologically separated from Iron Age Israel by centuries. The proper comparison is: first, contemporary evidence from Canaan itself; only then, if there's positive correlation, to Iron Age Palestine.

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit lies far outside the borders of Canaan — Ugaritic texts treat "Canaanite" traders as foreigners (KTU 4.96 = UT 311, noted by Rainey 1963)
- Winter's *Frau und Göttin* (1983) relies too heavily on Bronze Age Syrian material (Lipiriski 1986 critique)
- H. Weippert (1990, 187) criticized the same tendency
- The proper primary context for Iron Age Israel is "iconographic and textual evidence recovered from those peoples who lived contemporaneously with and geographically close to Israel and Judah"
- Using Ebla, Mari, and Ugarit texts to explain first-millennium Israel "makes little sense"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The geographical point about Ugarit is factually correct. The chronological point is also sound — comparing 14th-century Ugaritic texts to 8th-century Israelite religion is like using 14th-century English texts to reconstruct Reformation Germany.

**What's at stake:** This challenges the entire Ugaritic-comparative tradition. If Cross, Smith, and Day are comparing the wrong texts from the wrong place at the wrong time, their entire reconstruction of Israelite polytheism needs recalibration. Keel & Uehlinger are not saying Ugarit is irrelevant — they're saying it must be used as a secondary comparison, not a primary template.

**Who disagrees:** Cross (1973), Smith (1990, 2001, 2002), Day (2000), and virtually every scholar in the Ugaritic-comparative tradition. Smith's *Origins* uses Ugaritic material as the primary lens for understanding Israelite religion.

**Alternative reading:** Ugaritic texts survive because of a scribal accident (the city burned with tablets in place). The absence of comparable texts from Canaan proper may be an accident of preservation, not evidence of different religion. The Ugaritic material may actually *be* the best window into the Canaanite religious world that both areas shared.

**My assessment:** This is the most provocative claim in the introduction, and I'm not fully convinced. The Ugaritic texts DO share divine names, epithets, and mythological patterns with the Hebrew Bible. That's not coincidence. But Keel & Uehlinger are right that the one-to-one mapping (Baal cycle → Israelite religion) is methodologically sloppy. The value of this book is precisely that it provides the contemporary, geographically-matched evidence they say is required.

## Claim 5: Assmann's "constellation" model explains why images are more stable evidence than texts

**Authors' claim:** "The myth owes its 'iconic constancy' to a few basic constellations... Significance and enduring character are not found in names that change and not in easily-forgotten narratives. Priority and the constancy of the motif is portrayed in the way icons depict these few basic constellations."

Drawing on the Heidelberg Egyptologist Jan Assmann, Keel & Uehlinger argue that myths are secondary narrativizations of more fundamental "constellations" — stable patterns of relationships between divine figures that persist across cultures, names, and narratives. The battle-against-the-dragon constellation appears with Baal-Yamm, Marduk-Tiamat, Yahweh-Leviathan, Perseus-Andromeda, and St. George — different names, different stories, same underlying icon.

**Evidence presented:**
- Assmann's concept of "constellations" as "pre-mythic complexes of ideas" that crystallize into myths over time
- The dragon-combat constellation: "imminent destruction of whatever threat some evil force poses to that which is good, resolved by the savior who must intervene"
- Mary-with-child as a nursing mother, the resurrected one as victor — icons that persist across centuries
- "Mythic texts are nothing other than icons (or else constellations) that have been presented in story form"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The concept is powerful and has broad application in art history and comparative religion. But it risks flattening genuine cultural differences under superficial iconographic similarities.

**What's at stake:** If Assmann's model is right, then iconography is not just supplementary evidence — it's the DEEPEST level of evidence, beneath the narrative surface of texts. This gives iconography priority over texts for detecting religious change.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who prioritize narrative and text over image. Also those who argue that the specific names and narratives MATTER — that Baal and Marduk and Yahweh are different gods, not just different names for the same constellation.

**Alternative reading:** Constellations may be too abstract to be useful for historical reconstruction. A seal showing a warrior-god smiting a serpent tells you that the smiting-warrior constellation existed — but not whether the god was Baal, Seth, Reshef, or Yahweh. The names matter for the specific question of Israelite religion.

**My assessment:** The constellation model is most useful for detecting cultural continuities the texts obscure. When Keel & Uehlinger find solar imagery absorbed into Yahwistic seals, the constellation model explains how this happened without textual evidence of a "Yahweh-solar syncretism" — the icon itself carried the meaning across cultural boundaries. This is genuinely powerful. But it needs to be paired with inscriptional evidence to ground the identifications.

## Claim 6: The periodization of Palestinian archaeology requires revision — Iron Age IIC begins with Assyrian domination, not architectural shifts

**Authors' claim:** "If one considers the artistic works, as will be done here, it will seem not only plausible but unavoidable that the break between Iron Age IIB and C must be dated to the second half of the eighth century, when Palestine came increasingly under the direct political influence of the great Assyrian superpower."

Keel & Uehlinger argue against H. Weippert's architectural-only periodization (which dates Iron Age IIB 925-850 BCE) and for a more nuanced model where Iron Age IIB extends to the Assyrian conquest (late 8th century). Their key evidence: small artifacts — seals, amulets, figurines — change decisively with Assyrian influence, marking the real cultural break.

**Evidence presented:**
- H. Weippert's model uses only architectural evidence and produces an Iron Age IIB ending ca. 850 — "almost no small artifacts survive from this clearly demarcated Iron Age IIB period"
- Small artifacts like seals come frequently from graves, which are still being found and documented
- Assyrian conquest caused massive political and administrative restructuring visible in "settlements and in the remains of the material culture, right down to the types of pottery"
- Before Assyrian domination: Israel oriented toward Phoenicia, Judah toward Egypt or internal Palestinian forces
- After: definite Assyrian (and thus Aramean) influence with "international scope"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is an intra-specialist debate about archaeological periodization. The evidence from small artifacts is legitimate but Weippert's architectural approach also has merit.

**What's at stake:** The dating of Iron Age IIB vs. IIC determines when the major iconographic shifts (solarization, astralization, aniconic turn) occurred. If Iron Age IIB ends in 850, the solar imagery of the 8th century falls in IIC and is an Assyrian-period phenomenon. If IIB extends to ~720, the solar imagery is pre-Assyrian and reflects internal Israelite/Judean development. This matters enormously for the Asherah/Kuntillet Ajrud/solar-Yahweh discussion.

**Who disagrees:** H. Weippert (1988) and scholars who follow her architectural periodization.

**Alternative reading:** The architectural evidence may be more secure because large structures are harder to misdate; small artifacts can be heirlooms or residual finds. Weippert's shorter IIB may be correct.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger's argument from small artifacts is persuasive because seals are mass-produced and consumed quickly — they're not heirlooms. If seal iconography changes with Assyrian influence, that's a genuine cultural shift. Their periodization makes more sense for religious history specifically, which is what they're studying.

## Claim 7: Middle Bronze Age IIB is the correct starting point because it's the beginning of a cultural continuum that extends to the Hebrew Bible

**Authors' claim:** "One can justify beginning our survey at this point, when the urban culture in Palestine begins to flourish, because from this point on we can deal with a cultural continuum in Palestine that extends all the way to the time of the emergence of the Hebrew Bible."

The authors begin their survey in MB IIB (1750-1550 BCE) rather than the Iron Age because this period establishes the iconographic vocabulary that persists throughout. The populations who inhabited the hill country during MB IIB did not disappear during the Late Bronze Age Egyptian colonization — they shifted to nomadism (per Finkelstein 1988) and resettled in Iron Age I.

**Evidence presented:**
- Early Bronze Age IV/MB I (2200-2000): minimal iconographic evidence, mostly from graves
- MB IIA (2000-1750): limited to Egyptian imports — scarabs with ornamental designs, hieroglyphs, king/official names
- MB IIB (1750-1550): "strong influences came from the north, specifically from Syria" — the beginning of a distinct Canaanite iconographic tradition
- The "Hyksos" period: Canaanite-dominated dynasty in Egypt (~1650)
- Finkelstein's (1988) argument that hill country populations didn't disappear during LB but shifted to nomadism
- "It is also likely that the same ethnic groups continued to maintain their cultural system from the Middle Bronze Age right into the Iron Age"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the cultural continuum argument. Finkelstein's nomadism thesis is widely but not universally accepted. The MB IIB starting point makes sense for an iconographic survey — you need to see what comes before to understand what changes.

**What's at stake:** If the cultural continuum argument is right, then Iron Age Israelite religion has deep roots in Middle Bronze Age Canaanite religion. The "Canaanite" elements in Israelite religion are not foreign borrowings but indigenous inheritances. This is a direct challenge to the biblical narrative of Israel as a people who entered Canaan from outside.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize the discontinuity between Bronze Age Canaan and Iron Age Israel (Albright school, some biblical maximalists). Also those who argue for a distinct Israelite ethnic identity emerging in Iron Age I.

**Alternative reading:** The resettlement thesis (nomadic populations returning) doesn't prove religious continuity — nomadic groups may have developed new religious traditions during the LB gap.

**My assessment:** The continuity argument is plausible but the evidence is population-level, not religious. It's possible for populations to be continuous while religion changes dramatically. The value of starting with MB IIB is heuristic: it establishes the iconographic baseline against which Iron Age innovations can be measured. That's methodologically sound regardless of whether full religious continuity can be proven.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Introduction Sections

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Polytheism must be tested against material evidence | HIGH | Finding that iconography and texts tell identical stories |
| 2. Tigay's onomastic study is pioneering but incomplete | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence that personal names perfectly track religious practice |
| 3. Religion is a symbol system; iconography is primary evidence | HIGH | Evidence of uniquely logocentric Israelite religion |
| 4. Ugarit is not Canaan — geographical/chronological critique | HIGH | Discovery of comparable texts in Canaan proper |
| 5. Assmann's constellation model gives iconography priority | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence that iconographic similarities are superficial |
| 6. Periodization needs revision based on small artifacts | MEDIUM | Better dating methods showing Weippert's model is correct |
| 7. MB IIB is the right starting point for cultural continuum | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence of genuine religious discontinuity between LB and Iron Age |

**Strongest section:** Claim 1 (material evidence over textual assumptions) and Claim 4 (Ugarit ≠ Canaan). Together they establish why this book is necessary and why the existing text-only reconstructions are insufficient. The critique of Smith's "missing puzzle pieces" metaphor — that he's ignoring "the largest portion" of the pieces — is devastating.

**Weakest section:** Claim 5 (Assmann's constellations). The concept is elegant but somewhat circular: if you define religion as a symbol system and images as the most stable symbols, then images are automatically the best evidence. The Einsiedeln case study is vivid but the leap from 20th-century Swiss Catholicism to Iron Age Israel is enormous.

**Key methodological contribution:** The Fribourg corpus — 8,500 stamp seals from dated, provenanced excavations — is the largest and most systematically collected iconographic dataset for ancient Israel. No text-based scholar has engaged with it at scale. This book is the first attempt. That alone justifies its centrality to the project.

**Cross-reference connections:**
- Dever's "cult through material culture" approach is explicitly engaged (and critiqued on the belief/cult dichotomy)
- Smith is named as a primary example of text-only reconstruction that's missing half the evidence
- Tigay's empirical rigor is the model, but his narrow focus on names is the limitation
- The book will provide the iconographic evidence Smith, Römer, Day, and Cross lack — this is the visual counterpart to their textual arguments
