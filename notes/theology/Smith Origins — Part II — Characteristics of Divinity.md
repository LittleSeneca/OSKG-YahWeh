# Smith — Part II: Characteristics of Divinity (Chapters 5-6)

---

## Chapter 5: The Traits of Deities

### Claim: ANE deities shared four essential traits — and Israel's God inherited all of them

**Smith claims that deities in Ugarit and Israel shared four fundamental characteristics:**

**1. Strength and Size:** "Gods were imagined as larger and stronger than humans... Baal's size is suggested by his weapons, which are enormous." Yahweh "measured the waters in the hollow of his hand" (Isaiah 40:12). Divine size = divine power. The bigger the god, the greater the power.

**2. Body and Gender:** Deities had sexed bodies. Male gods had beards, muscles, genitals. Female gods had breasts and wombs. Gender was not fluid (contra Sommer on some points) — it was binary and anatomical. "Yahweh's body... is described with male features."

**3. Holiness:** "Holiness is not a moral quality but a dangerous, radiant power." The *kavod* (glory) is the divine body's radiance — lethal to humans without protection. Holy objects, holy space, holy people share this dangerous quality through contact with the deity.

**4. Immortality:** Gods do not die — or rather, they CAN die but are restored. Baal dies and is resurrected. Yahweh "lives forever" (Deuteronomy 32:40). "Postscript: Divine and Human Life (Or, My Breakfast with Shulamit)" — Smith reflects personally on the scholar's relationship to the divine subjects she studies.

**Confidence:** HIGH on each trait. Smith catalogues the evidence exhaustively.

**What's at stake:** These traits show that Israel's God was a West Semitic deity like any other. He was not categorically different. His uniqueness is a matter of DEGREE (larger, holier, more immortal), not of KIND. Monotheism didn't change God's nature — it elevated one god above others while keeping his basic characteristics intact.

## Chapter 6: The Life and Death of Baal

### Claim: Baal's death-and-resurrection mythology provided the template for Israel's understanding of national catastrophe and restoration

**Smith's claim:** Baal dies and returns to life in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle. This is not a "dying and rising god" in Frazer's sense (vegetation deity whose death/rebirth mirrors the seasons). Rather, Baal's death represents the threat of cosmic chaos — what happens when the divine warrior is absent. His return represents the restoration of order. Israel adapted this mythology to interpret its own history: the destruction of Jerusalem was Yahweh's "death." The return from exile was his "resurrection."

**Evidence — Frazer's hypothesis criticized:**
- Frazer's category of "dying and rising gods" (Tammuz, Adonis, Osiris, Baal) assumed a universal vegetation-cult pattern
- Smith argues this "obscures more than it illuminates." Baal's death is not seasonal — it's a COSMIC crisis
- "Problems with the category" — there was no single "dying and rising god" type; each culture had its own understanding

**The conceptual ideology in Baal's life and death:**
- Baal dies not as a vegetation cycle but as the consequence of his conflict with Mot (Death)
- His death threatens the cosmic order — without the divine warrior, chaos returns
- His resurrection re-establishes order — Baal enthroned, temple built, kingship secured
- "The Mythology of Death and the God of Israel": Israel adapted this pattern

**Israel's adaptation:**
- The exile is Yahweh's "death" — the divine warrior defeated, the temple destroyed
- The return is Yahweh's "resurrection" — Second Isaiah proclaims a new exodus, a new creation
- "The Life and Death of Baal provides a conceptual template for understanding how Israel could experience the destruction of its national deity and yet affirm his ongoing power"

**Confidence:** HIGH on the Baal Cycle's content. MEDIUM-HIGH on Israel's adaptation of the death/return pattern (the parallels are real but indirect).

**What's at stake:** The death and resurrection of Jesus — the central claim of Christianity — has its conceptual roots not in unique divine revelation but in a West Semitic mythological pattern that predates Israel by centuries. The dying and rising god is a Canaanite idea, not an Israelite one.
