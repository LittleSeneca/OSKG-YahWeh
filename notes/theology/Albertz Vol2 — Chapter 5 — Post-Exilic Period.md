# Albertz — Chapter 5: The History of Israelite Religion in the Post-Exilic Period

Albertz's central thesis: the post-exilic period — especially 538-400 BCE — was one of the most productive eras in Israelite religious history, contra the Christian theological prejudice that it was a time of legalistic decline. The period was characterized by a dialectic of "splintering and integration": rival theological traditions (Deuteronomistic, Priestly, prophetic) competed while the formation of the Torah canon pulled toward unity. The decisive political fact was that Israel did NOT restore the monarchy — instead it developed a new form of community based on pre-state structures under Persian imperial oversight.

---

## Claim 1: The post-exilic period was extraordinarily productive, not a "late Judaism" decline — and it was characterized by a dialectic of splintering and integration

**Albertz's claim:** "More recent research... shows increasingly clearly that the post-exilic period, and especially the early post-exilic period (538-400), was one of the most productive eras in the history of Israelite religion." The reason for its neglect is "a deep-seated dogmatic Christian view, which sees the church as the goal of the history of Israelite religion, and therefore likes to go straight from the lofty heights of Deutero-Isaiah... to the New Testament."

**Evidence presented:**
- Foundation of a universally binding holy scripture (the Torah)
- The temple became the central symbol of religious life
- Eschatological expectation horizons developed beyond political history
- Multiple "theological currents" (Deuteronomistic, Priestly, Chronistic, wisdom, prophetic, psalmic) stood side by side and crossed
- Ploger's bipolar model (theocratic vs. eschatological) is "much too simple" — it ignores Deuteronomistic and Wisdom traditions
- Steck's "diagram of currents" does more justice to the evidence but fails to give sociological grounding
- Crüsemann's tripolar model (small farmers + Gola/priests; local aristocracy; prophetic conventicles) is better but still "too crude"
- Albertz's own model emphasizes: (a) rifts within the upper class, (b) rifts within the priesthood, (c) wisdom theology as a form of personal piety, not a separate "current" alongside Torah

**Confidence:** HIGH on the productivity claim (it's undeniable that the Pentateuch, most of the prophetic corpus, and much of the Writings were shaped in this period). MEDIUM on the specific sociological model (there's a lot of reconstruction from fragmentary evidence).

**What's at stake:** Albertz is fighting a long Christian theological tradition that dismisses "late Judaism" as legalistic degeneration. If he's right, this period is the creative matrix of both Judaism and Christianity — not a barren interlude between prophets and Jesus.

**Who disagrees:** The entire Christian supersessionist tradition. More specifically, scholars who locate key developments earlier (in the exile) or later (in the Hellenistic period). Wellhausen's model saw post-exilic Judaism as a decline from prophetic vitality to priestly ritualism.

**My assessment:** Albertz's polemic against Christian prejudice is well-taken and the evidence of literary productivity in this period is overwhelming. The more interesting question is his model of "splintering and integration." This dialectic is a powerful analytical framework that avoids both the "unified Judaism" picture and the "warring sects" picture.

## Claim 2: Post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy — it was a Persian province with limited self-administration borrowing from pre-state tribal structures

**Albertz's claim:** "At least for the Persian period there is no question of a dominant priestly rule. The priestly college was only one governing body alongside the council of elders, and while the temple was an important unifying symbol, temple power and the temple economy were not decisive factors." The community structure "clearly borrows from models from the period before the state."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Persian governor (pehah) headed the province, not the high priest — governors could be Jewish or Persian
- Two governing bodies: a "council of elders" (horim/seganim, lay nobility) and a "priestly college" under the high priest
- Below them: the popular assembly (qahal), summoned occasionally — but it "deteriorated into a purely executive organ" as richer families dominated
- The population was organized into artificial "clans" (bet 'abot) — "an attempt at an artificial restoration of the social structure of the pre-state period"
- We see the realization of "constitutional monarchy" ideals that reform groups had advocated since Absalom's rebellion
- The high priest assumed royal cultic functions (anointing, turban, breastplate) — filling the king's role in the cult — but was himself under intensified holiness restrictions
- The Persian religious policy was tolerant (supporting local cults) but the taxation policy was brutal — driving the wedge between rich and poor

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the institutional structure (it's attested in Ezra-Nehemiah). The "not a theocracy" claim is important and increasingly accepted. The "pre-state tribal structures" claim is more interpretive.

**What's at stake:** Calling post-exilic Judah a "theocracy" distorts our understanding of power dynamics. The priests didn't rule — they were one power bloc among others, and a Persian governor sat above them. This matters for understanding the conflicts in Ezra-Nehemiah and the later Hasmonean period.

**Who disagrees:** Josephus (who coined "theocracy"), and scholars who follow him. Blenkinsopp's "temple community" model. Weinberg's "citizen-temple community" model, which Albertz finds more appropriate but still needing refinement.

**Alternative reading:** Even if not formally a theocracy, the temple and priesthood exercised disproportionate influence because they controlled the central symbol of identity. De facto theocracy may be a better description than de jure.

**My assessment:** Albertz's correction is important and well-evidenced from the Nehemiah memorandum. The dual governing body (elders + priests) is clearly attested. However, the high priest DID accumulate power over time, and by the Hellenistic period the "theocracy" label becomes more accurate. Albertz is right for the Persian period but the trajectory matters.

## Claim 3: The temple rebuilding (520-515) sparked eschatological-nationalist hopes attached to Zerubbabel that Persian authorities crushed — marginalizing prophecy

**Albertz's claim:** "The building of the temple... sparked off glowing expectations of a revolution in world history and a national restoration which attached themselves to Zerubbabel of the house of David. The situation seemed so dangerous to the Persians that the satrap Tatnai paid a personal visit to Jerusalem... While he allowed the rebuilding... this was probably only on condition that Zerubbabel was withdrawn and that the dangerous prophetic movements were stopped."

**Evidence presented:**
- Haggai and Zechariah's oracles explicitly link Zerubbabel to Davidic restoration hopes (Hag 2.20-23; Zech 4.6-10; 6.9-15)
- The Persian satrap Tatnai's investigation (Ezra 5.3-17) indicates official alarm
- Zerubbabel disappears from the record after the temple's completion — Albertz infers Persian-ordered withdrawal
- The prophetic movements were "stopped or controlled effectively"
- Haggai and Zechariah's prophecies were "eschatologized" — their unfulfilled promises were reinterpreted for a distant future
- Ezra 4.6, 4.7-23 indicate ongoing suspicion and occasional violent suppression of nationalist-rebellious activity
- Nehemiah himself had to fend off prophets who wanted to proclaim him king (Neh 6.6-14)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The basic sequence (Zerubbabel → hopes → Persian intervention → Zerubbabel's disappearance) is well-supported. The claim that Zerubbabel was "withdrawn" by Persian order is inference from silence — we don't know what happened to him.

**What's at stake:** This episode established a pattern that shaped all of post-exilic religion: eschatological prophecy was politically dangerous and had to be marginalized. The pro-Persian coalition (priests concerned with cult autonomy + lay leaders seeking self-administration) learned that nationalist restoration hopes threatened the fragile arrangement with the empire.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Zerubbabel's disappearance as natural (death, retirement) rather than forced. Those who date Haggai/Zechariah differently or read their oracles as less politically charged.

**Cross-reference:** Römer (Invention of God, Ch 12) discusses the "end of prophecy" and the marginalization of prophetic voices in the post-exilic period. Albertz gives this a sharper political edge.

**My assessment:** Albertz's reconstruction makes excellent sense of the narrative gaps and tensions. The pattern of Persian tolerance combined with ruthless suppression of political-nationalist threats fits what we know of Achaemenid imperial practice. The "eschatologizing" of unfulfilled prophecy is a crucial concept for understanding the development of apocalyptic literature.

## Claim 4: The canonization of the Torah was a Persian-backed compromise between Deuteronomistic (lay) and Priestly parties

**Albertz's claim:** "The Torah, created in the fifth century probably under Persian pressure, is a compromise between the adherents of Deuteronomistic theology and those of priestly theology, which was to secure the religious identity and legal basis of the community of Judah along the lines of the two dominant groups."

**Evidence presented:**
- Ezra's mission (398 BCE in Albertz's preferred dating) involved Persian imperial authorization (Frei's "Reichsautorisation" model) — making Jewish law binding as imperial law for all Judaeans in Transeuphratene
- Parallel cases: the Xanthus stele, the Egyptian legal codification under Darius I, the Elephantine Passover Letter — all show Persian practice of authorizing local legal traditions
- The Pentateuch is literarily composite: KD (a lay/Deuteronomistic composition — Gen 12 through Deuteronomy, connected to the council of elders) was followed by KP (a priestly composition/revision — connected to the priestly college)
- Blum's traditio-historical model: the lay theologians wrote first, the priests responded, producing a "tense, agitated dialogue" that became the Torah
- "The juxtaposition, opposition and also interweaving of the two Pentateuchal compositions can be sufficiently explained from the juxtaposition of two rival groups of tradents who had different, indeed contrary, views of what could make up and preserve the identity of Israel"
- Ezra's role was not to INTRODUCE the Torah (it was already known, v.25) but to secure its PROPER IMPLEMENTATION with Persian state backing

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Frei's imperial authorization model is influential but not universally accepted. The dating of the Pentateuch's final form remains debated. The Blum model (KD → KP) is one of several. But the overall picture — Torah as compromise document, Persian-backed — is increasingly the consensus.

**What's at stake:** This completely reframes the origin of the Bible. The Torah wasn't a purely internal religious development — it was shaped by imperial politics. The Pentateuch's internal tensions (Deuteronomistic vs. Priestly) aren't accidents of redaction but the PRODUCT of the canonization process itself: a deliberately unresolved dialogue between rival theological parties, both of whom had to be included for the document to function as a unifying charter.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Ezra earlier (458) and see him as introducing a substantially new law. Those who reject the imperial authorization model. Minimalists who date the Pentateuch much later (Hellenistic period).

**Alternative reading:** The Pentateuch's composition was a purely internal Jewish development, and Persian involvement was limited to post-hoc recognition. Or: Ezra's law was a specific legal corpus (P, or the Holiness Code), not the full Pentateuch.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most important claims in the entire two-volume work. The "two-party compromise" model elegantly explains the Pentateuch's internal tensions without resorting to purely literary explanations. The Persian imperial authorization model gives a plausible political mechanism. However, the reconstruction is highly inferential — we have no direct evidence of the negotiation process. The strength of the claim is its explanatory power, not its direct attestation.

## Claim 5: The Pentateuch's most important theological decision was to center Israel's identity on the pre-state period — not on David, Zion, or the monarchy

**Albertz's claim:** "In concentrating here wholly on the historical experiences of the early period of Israel, they shifted the focus of the history which in DtrG had been on the saving institutions of the state (David, temple) right back to the experiences of salvation in the pre-state period. Israel had not first assumed its essential character under David and Solomon, but already under Moses."

**Evidence presented:**
- KD (the pre-priestly composition) begins with Abraham's call from Babylonia (Gen 12), NOT with creation — it's "a history of Israel"
- No creation story in KD; the primeval history was added by P
- The document deliberately excluded kingship theology — "kingship theology was largely excluded and prophecy was recognized only within the framework marked out by the law"
- The Sinai covenant, not the Davidic covenant, is the central theological event
- Social legislation (the "brotherhood ethic") is central, reflecting the lay theologians' concern for social solidarity
- The narrative parts (especially the patriarchal history) take account of the Gola's situation — Abraham's call to leave homeland becomes a model for return
- This theological decision "corresponds to the political decision which the majority party in the Jewish self-administrative bodies had taken after the failure of the restoration of the state"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The content of the Pentateuch clearly centers on pre-monarchic traditions. The linkage to the political decision against monarchic restoration is Albertz's distinctive contribution.

**What's at stake:** The Torah is not just a law code — it's a political-theological manifesto. By choosing to write a foundation history ending at Moses' death (not David, not the temple), the Pentateuch's framers made a deliberate statement: Israel's identity is constituted by liberation, covenant, and law — not by monarchy, temple, or territory. This made Judaism portable. It also made the Pentateuch acceptable to the Persians (no royal claims) and to both lay and priestly parties (both get representation).

**Who disagrees:** Those who see the Pentateuch's shape as driven by literary and theological considerations rather than political strategy. Scholars who argue the Pentateuch originally continued into Joshua (the "Hexateuch" theory) or even Kings (the "Enneateuch").

**My assessment:** Albertz's linkage of the Pentateuch's literary shape to the political situation is brilliant and persuasive. It explains the most puzzling feature of the Torah: why it ends where it does. Moses dies on Mount Nebo looking at the land he cannot enter — and that's it. No conquest, no monarchy, no temple. That's not an accident of transmission. That's a theological-political choice.

## Claim 6: The social crisis of the fifth century split the community, producing class-specific forms of personal piety — "theologized wisdom" for the pious upper class and "piety of the poor" for the lower class

**Albertz's claim:** "The social crisis of the fifth century... led to a far-reaching conflict of loyalty in the upper class and to a sharp opposition of fronts in society." This produced two distinct forms of personal piety: "theologized wisdom" among the pious upper class (Job, Prov 1-9) and the "piety of the poor" in lower-class religious circles (certain psalms).

**Evidence presented for "theologized wisdom":**
- The book of Job and Prov 1-9 represent a late development of wisdom tradition
- The "pious" upper class maintained solidarity with the poor (Job 29.12ff.; 31.13ff.) — unlike their aristocratic colleagues
- Their theological problem: how can a righteous God tolerate the prosperity of the wicked (aristocrats who abandoned solidarity) while the pious suffer?
- Job dramatizes this crisis — the friends represent traditional retribution theology, Job demands a hearing before God
- The resolution: not a rational answer but a theophany — God's freedom and majesty transcend human moral calculus
- "The fear of Yahweh" (Prov 1.7; 9.10) becomes the foundation of wisdom — a personal, trusting relationship with God replaces the pragmatic, observational wisdom of the older tradition

**Evidence presented for "piety of the poor":**
- Lower-class religious circles are traceable in Pss 9/10; 35; 69; 70; 109; 140 and prophetic liturgies Pss 12; 14; 75; 82
- Self-designations include 'anawim ("wretched"), 'ebyonim ("poor"), saddiqim ("righteous"), and those who "seek Yahweh"
- These groups saw themselves persecuted by "the wicked" — influential members of the community who "did not care what God would say" (Ps 10.4,11,13)
- Unlike the pious upper class, they had "no merits to which they could refer" — only their wretched existence
- Their hope was eschatological: God must intervene as judge and king to overthrow the wicked
- They developed their own worship apart from the temple cult — mutual pastoral care through laments and thanksgivings
- "Through the deliverance of the individual member, all Yahweh's followers are to have grounds for rejoicing (35.27f.)"

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The reconstruction of specific social groups from psalmic and wisdom texts is inherently speculative. We have texts, not sociological surveys. The "pious upper class" vs. "wicked aristocracy" distinction is plausible but difficult to verify independently.

**What's at stake:** This is Albertz's most ambitious sociological reconstruction. If correct, it means post-exilic Judaism wasn't just theologically diverse but sociologically fractured — and the fractures produced different theologies. The book of Job and the Psalms of the poor aren't just "literature" — they're class-specific responses to a real social crisis.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who interpret the "poor" in Psalms as a spiritualized/metaphorical category, not a social designation. Those who date Job earlier or later than the fifth-century crisis. Birkeland's caution about reading party designations into individual laments.

**Alternative reading:** The social conflict in Psalms reflects generic literary convention (the "righteous sufferer" motif) rather than specific historical groups. Job is a theological thought experiment, not a response to social crisis.

**My assessment:** Albertz makes a good case that SOMETHING real lies behind the convergence of social and religious language in these texts. The "pious poor" as a self-conscious group seems plausible. But I worry about circularity: he identifies the social crisis from Nehemiah 5, then reads Psalms and Job as responses to it, then uses that reading to confirm the social crisis. The evidence is real but the causal chain is looser than he presents it.

## Claim 7: The Samaritan schism was NOT an early theological break over syncretism — it was a gradual political and cultic split culminating in the building of the Gerizim temple

**Albertz's claim:** "The formation of the Samaritan community alongside the Jewish community was not the consequence of a theologically motivated split, but much more a political and cultic schism." The standard Deuteronomistic polemic in 2 Kings 17.24-41 does NOT refer to the Samaritans.

**Evidence presented:**
- 2 Kings 17.24-34a describes foreign settlers (not Israelites) worshipping their national gods alongside Yahweh at Bethel — it's a still-exilic warning to Judahites not to participate in the Bethel cult
- 2 Kings 17.34b-40 is a later addition that addresses Israelites who remained in the land, calling them to abandon syncretism and come to Jerusalem — this reflects early post-exilic outreach to northern Israelites
- The exclusion of "the people of Samaria" from temple rebuilding (Ezra 4.1-5), if historical at all, was political, not theological — the "enemies of Judah and Benjamin" were the foreign upper class
- The actual split developed gradually through: (a) political rivalry between Samaria (provincial capital) and Jerusalem (temple city), (b) competition between Sanballat's dynasty and the Jerusalem priesthood, (c) Nehemiah's expulsion of a high priest's son who married into Sanballat's family (Neh 13.28 — Josephus dates the Gerizim temple founding to this event)
- The real break came with the building of the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim (late 4th century under Alexander or early Hellenistic period) and the Samaritan adoption of their own Torah recension

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The reconstruction of 2 Kings 17's compositional history is detailed but contested. The dating of the Gerizim temple is disputed (Josephus vs. archaeology). But the overall model of political/cultic rather than theological schism is increasingly accepted.

**What's at stake:** If Albertz is right, the Jewish-Samaritan split wasn't about "pure vs. syncretistic Yahwism" — it was about political rivalry and cultic centralization. This matters for understanding Samaritan identity: Samaritans aren't "half-pagan" but represent a parallel development of Yahwism that didn't go through the Jerusalem-centered reshaping of the exile.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional readings that see 2 Kings 17 and Ezra 4 as describing theological exclusion of syncretistic Samarians. Scholars who date the Gerizim temple differently. Those who see deeper theological differences from the start.

**Cross-reference:** Römer (Chapter 10, Fall of Samaria) discusses the northern kingdom's religion and the later polemics. The Elephantine papyri (which show the Jewish community writing to BOTH Jerusalem and Samaria for support in rebuilding their temple — AP 30/31) suggest the split wasn't complete even in the late 5th century.

**My assessment:** Albertz's careful literary analysis of 2 Kings 17 is convincing — the text is layered and was written before "Samaritans" as a distinct group existed. The model of gradual political estrangement is more historically plausible than sudden theological rupture. However, I'd want more evidence on the dating of the Gerizim temple. Josephus's account is late and tendentious. Archaeological evidence from Gerizim suggests a temple existed by the Hellenistic period, which is consistent with Albertz's model but doesn't confirm all the details.

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Post-exilic period as extraordinarily productive | HIGH | None — textual evidence is overwhelming |
| 2. Not a theocracy; pre-state tribal model | MEDIUM-HIGH | De facto vs. de jure priestly power |
| 3. Zerubbabel episode and marginalization of prophecy | MEDIUM | Zerubbabel's disappearance is inference from silence |
| 4. Torah canonization as Persian-backed compromise | MEDIUM | Frei's imperial authorization model debated; dating of Pentateuch |
| 5. Pentateuch's pre-state centering as deliberate political-theological choice | MEDIUM-HIGH | Hexateuch/Enneateuch theories |
| 6. Class-specific piety (wisdom theology / piety of the poor) | LOW-MEDIUM | Circularity risk; alternative literary explanations |
| 7. Samaritan schism as gradual political/cultic split | MEDIUM | Gerizim temple dating; Josephus reliability |

**Strongest section:** 5.3 (struggle over identity / canonization of the Torah). Albertz's analysis of the Pentateuch as a two-party compromise document, produced under Persian imperial authorization, with the deliberate decision to center on pre-state history — this is original, well-argued, and has major implications.

**Weakest section:** 5.5 (split in personal piety). The reconstruction of class-specific religious groups from psalmic and wisdom texts, while suggestive, rests on a chain of inferences that's hard to verify independently.

## Claim 8 [ADDED IN RETROSPECTIVE]: The post-exilic priesthood transformed the sacrificial cult — expanding the tamid, creating the Day of Atonement, and making atonement theology central to public worship

**Albertz's claim:** The Day of Atonement "denotes a new element in post-exilic worship which permanently changed in comparison to pre-exilic worship, in both the public and the private spheres: an emphasis on the function of the cult in making atonement. It was the decisive means by which the reform priesthood reacted to the traumatic experience of a national catastrophe which had happened despite a functioning cult."

**Evidence presented:**
- The daily tamid was expanded: from one morning burnt offering + evening meal offering (2 Kings 16.15) to two burnt offerings each combined with food and drink offerings (Ex 29.38-42; Num 28.3-8), plus the high priest's daily meal offering (Lev 6.12-16)
- The Mishnah Tamid shows the morning tamid combined with Shema recitation, benediction, Decalogue, Aaronic blessing, Levitical choirs, and trumpet-signaled prostration — a developed liturgy involving the laity
- The festival calendar was expanded: the new year festival (1st day, 7th month) was added, possibly under Babylonian influence, and the Day of Atonement (10th day, 7th month) was inserted before Tabernacles (Lev 23.23-32)
- The Day of Atonement had two functions: (a) purify the sanctuary from impurities and sins of laity/priests, (b) make atonement for the high priest and people
- Two rituals were combined: the scapegoat ritual (sins transferred and eliminated from the community, Lev 16.7-9, 20-22) and the blood-sprinkling ritual in the holy of holies (atonement through encounter with divine holiness, 16.14f.)
- The financing shifted from royal provision to priestly self-administration through: drastically increased sacrificial portions (Num 18.9; Lev 2.3; 7.9), direct taxes (tithes to Levites, Num 18.20-32), money tax (1/3 shekel, later 1/2 shekel, Neh 10.33f.; Ex 30.11-16), and wood deliveries by clan rotation (Neh 10.35; 13.31)
- Total cultic obligations swelled to "around a third of the total income, with the state taxes on top of that"
- "The expense of the official sacrificial cult had hardly diminished since the time of the state cult: quite the contrary"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The textual evidence for the expanded cultic system is extensive (Leviticus, Numbers, Nehemiah, Mishnah). The theological interpretation (atonement as response to 587 trauma) is Albertz's own but plausible.

**What's at stake:** The post-exilic cult wasn't a diminished version of the First Temple cult — it was more elaborate and expensive. The Day of Atonement represents a permanent institutionalization of guilt processing: Israel could never again assume the cult automatically guaranteed divine favor. The entire system now presupposed the need for regular purification and atonement. This is the cultic counterpart to the historiographical "confession of sin" in DtrG.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Day of Atonement earlier or who see it as primarily a pre-exilic ritual adapted, not a post-exilic innovation. The claim about Babylonian influence on the new year festival is speculative.

**Alternative reading:** The expanded cult was primarily about priestly economic self-interest (more sacrifices = more priestly income), not theological innovation. The atonement emphasis is a rationalization, not a cause.

**My assessment:** This was a significant gap in my original Chapter 5 note. The cultic transformation is as important as the textual/legal transformation (Torah canonization) for understanding post-exilic religion. Albertz's linkage of atonement theology to the trauma of 587 (the cult failed once; it must never fail again) is persuasive. The economic data — "a third of total income" — is striking and explains why the temple became both the central unifying symbol AND a source of resentment.
