# Fleming — Chapter 4: The Old Poetry

This chapter tackles the strongest remaining evidence for the Midianite Hypothesis: the biblical poems that describe Yahweh coming from Seir, Edom, Sinai, Paran, and Teman. Fleming argues that this poetic motif describes Yahweh's theophanic movement from a distant divine home, not the historical location of his earliest worship. The Ugaritic Baal myth provides the key analogy: gods live on distant mountains and travel from them to act, without those mountains being evidence for the location of their first worshippers. Yahweh is no more "from" Seir than Baal is "from" Mount Sapan or Zeus from Olympus. The poetry is probably monarchic (9th-8th centuries), and the shared core between Judges 5 and Psalm 68 lacks southern geography altogether. The southern references are elaborations on "he of Sinai" — a divine dwelling, not a people's homeland.

---

## Claim 1: The old poetry does not describe Yahweh's origin — it describes his theophanic movement from a distant divine home, analogous to Baal's Mount Sapan

**Author's claim:** "Nothing in the old poetry suggests any sense that Yahweh was first worshiped in the southern desert by peoples known to traverse it or occupy its habitable margins... Yahweh is no more originally 'from' Sinai and Seir than Zeus is 'from' Olympus or Baal is from Sapan" (p. 181).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Ugaritic Baal myth shows gods traveling from distant mountain homes: Baal from Mount Sapan, El from Mount KS, Mot from Mount TRGZZ, the divine assembly at Mount LL (pp. 174-176)
- These divine residences are not evidence for where the gods were first worshipped — Sapan was associated with Baal because of storms, not because his first cult was there
- "The motif of Yahweh's march to war casts him in the same role as young god of battle, ready not just to fight but to journey to do so" (p. 178)
- The gods of Ugarit "live at distance from each other, detached from human abodes" (p. 177)
- Kothar-wa-Hasis is associated with Memphis and Kaphtor (Crete) because of skilled artisanship, not because his worshippers lived there (p. 176)
- The mountain of God in Exodus is "impossibly remote and mysterious, effectively inaccessible" — found only by divine appointment (Elijah's 40-day journey, 1 Kgs 19; p. 181)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Ugaritic analogy is the strongest single argument in this chapter. Fleming demonstrates that divine residence in distant, inaccessible locations is a standard feature of West Semitic mythology, not a historical memory of cult origins. The analogy has been underutilized in the Yahweh-origins debate.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh's southern home is mythological geography, not historical geography, the poetry cannot support the Midianite Hypothesis. The entire convergence of Egyptian + poetic + epigraphic evidence pointing south becomes a convergence of mythological tropes, not historical data.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (2012a, 2014, 2017) treats the poetry as preserving genuine memory of Yahweh's southern origin. Cross (1973) interpreted it as "ritual conquest." Römer (2015: ch. 2) gives the poetry substantial weight as geographical evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The Ugaritic analogy cuts both ways. Sapan WAS a real mountain visible from Ugarit. The gods' residences in the Baal myth are real places in a real landscape. If Yahweh's poetry points to Seir and Edom, those are real places too — and the convergence with Egyptian evidence for Shasu in the same region can't be mythological coincidence.

**My assessment:** Fleming's analogy is powerful but not decisive. The difference is that Sapan is ONE mountain with ONE divine resident, while Yahweh's poetry names FIVE different southern locations (Seir, Edom, Sinai, Paran, Teman) — suggesting a regional association rather than a specific sacred mountain. That regional association is harder to explain as purely mythological. Fleming's "distant home" model works for a single mountain; for a whole region, the origin hypothesis retains some force.

## Claim 2: The shared core of Judges 5:4-5 and Psalm 68:8-9 lacks southern geography — Seir and Edom are secondary elaborations

**Author's claim:** "The demonstrably oldest material should be what both have in common. This shared text would look something like the following: *Yahweh, when you went out, *when you walked, *the earth quivered, *the heavens dripped, *from before Yahweh, he of Sinai, *from before Yahweh, god of Israel" (p. 153). "Psalm 68:8 lacks Seir and Edom, and instead, it develops Yahweh's movement without reference to the south" (p. 153).

**Evidence presented:**
- The two texts share verbatim elements but diverge on geography
- Judges 5 has "from Seir... from the open country of Edom" while Psalm 68 has "from Seir" / "from the open country of Edom" replaced with "in the wasteland" (yĕšîmôn)
- The shared text includes "he of Sinai" and "god of Israel" but NOT Seir or Edom
- "It is likely that Seir and Edom were invoked specifically for the Song of Deborah introduction, and the psalm does not demonstrate the higher antiquity of a shared hymnic fragment" (p. 154)
- The "wasteland" in Psalm 68 can refer to Moab, the wilderness between Babylon and Judah, or just "the back country" (Deut 32:10) — no specific southern location

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The textual-critical argument is clean: what both texts share is older than what only one has. The shared core has "he of Sinai" (a divine dwelling) but not Seir/Edom. This is a genuinely important observation that hasn't received enough attention in the literature.

**What's at stake:** If Seir and Edom are secondary additions to a fragment that only mentioned Sinai, the oldest recoverable form of the tradition doesn't point to the historical territories of Seir and Edom at all. It points to "Sinai" as a divine dwelling — a sacred mountain, not a people or a political territory.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (2012a: 11) treats the differences as "ancient oral variants" — equally old alternative versions. Cross (1973: 101 n.35) called them "ancient oral variants."

**Alternative reading:** The differences between Judges 5 and Psalm 68 could reflect equally old oral variants of the same tradition. The "wasteland" in Psalm 68 may be a deliberate replacement of Seir/Edom for theological reasons (Jerusalem-focused), not evidence that Seir/Edom weren't original.

**My assessment:** Fleming's textual-critical approach is methodologically sounder than the "oral variants" approach. Shared = older is a standard principle. His reconstruction is elegant and persuasive. But the "Sinai" in the shared core is still a southern location, so this doesn't eliminate the southern connection — it just narrows it to Sinai specifically.

## Claim 3: The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions confirm Yahweh of Teman as an Israelite projection, not evidence of southern origin

**Author's claim:** "Rather, we have here evidence for devotion by Israelites to their own god Yahweh in identification with his southern presence" (p. 170). "In either case, Yahweh of Teman was a manifestation of Israelite worship, joining the perception that Yahweh somehow inhabited the wilderness far from Israel (and Judah) to the projection of Israelite, Samaria-based, presence into that very space" (p. 171).

**Evidence presented:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud (c. 800 BCE) has three invocations of "Yahweh of Teman" and one of "Yahweh of Samaria"
- The Samaria connection shows the site was Israelite (northern kingdom), not Judahite
- The site is a waystation on a road, not a pilgrimage destination — its purpose is travel-related, not cultic in the temple sense
- Spencer Allen (2015: 271): "no native Temanite community need be assumed"
- The plaster inscription (KA 4.2) contains poetry strikingly similar to Habakkuk 3 — "when El shines forth," mountains melt, earthquake, the Holy One
- The Kuntillet Ajrud poetry shows the same motifs as the biblical old poetry but with completely different wording — demonstrating creative variability, not fixed ancient transmission
- The site's alignment with Samaria means Israel was projecting power/religion southward, not receiving religion from the south

**Confidence:** HIGH. The archaeological evidence is clear: Kuntillet Ajrud was an Israelite site. The "Yahweh of Teman" inscriptions show Israelites worshipping their own god in his southern manifestation.

**What's at stake:** Kuntillet Ajrud has often been cited as evidence for Yahweh's southern origins. Fleming flips it: it's evidence for Israelite projection southward. This is a genuinely important reinterpretation.

**Who disagrees:** Meshel (the excavator) considered it a religious site. Some interpret "Yahweh of Teman" as evidence for a local Edomite cult of Yahweh that Israelites encountered.

**Alternative reading:** The presence of "Yahweh of Teman" alongside "Yahweh of Samaria" could indicate that two different Yahwehs (local manifestations) met at this crossroads site — one from the north (Samaria), one from the south (Teman).

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is more parsimonious. The site is clearly Israelite (Samaria connection). The simplest explanation is that Israelites brought their god with them southward, not that they encountered him there. But the possibility of bidirectional influence can't be ruled out entirely.

## Claim 4: The old poetry is probably monarchic (9th-8th centuries), not pre-monarchic — it served royal ideology

**Author's claim:** "It is important to recognize the possibility, or I would say likelihood, that all of the texts in view were composed to serve monarchic interests" (p. 179). "In this early first-millennium vision, Yahweh is a god who fights for Israel as a people under royal leadership. This poetry represents a tradition of monarchy" (p. 180).

**Evidence presented:**
- The "revision through introduction" in Judges 5 (the hymn, vv. 2-11) transforms a battle alliance into "Israel" — "most easily understood as an effort serving kings" (Stahl 2020, cited p. 180)
- Deuteronomy 33 presents "the tribes of Israel as one" gathered around divine kingship ("there was a king in Jeshurun," v. 5) — monarchic vocabulary (p. 156)
- Habakkuk 3 references "your anointed" (māšîaḥ, v. 13) — a king (p. 165)
- Psalm 68 is broadly considered a Jerusalem composition from the monarchic period
- The Mesha inscription (9th c.) shows the same pattern: Kamosh fights for Moab as Yahweh fights for Israel — national gods under royal patronage
- Even Pfeiffer (2017), who radically down-dates most old poetry, considers the Song of Deborah core to be 9th/8th century (p. 141, n.24)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The monarchic dating for the texts as we have them is well-supported. But whether the southern theophany motif itself is a monarchic invention or an older tradition appropriated by monarchic writers is harder to settle. Fleming acknowledges: "We cannot discern the origin of this poetic motif... it is important to recognize the possibility... that all of the texts in view were composed to serve monarchic interests" (p. 179).

**What's at stake:** If the poetry is monarchic, Yahweh's southern associations were promoted by kings for ideological reasons — projecting royal power into the wilderness, claiming the mysterious divine warrior for the state. This undermines the idea that the poetry preserves pre-monarchic "memory" of Yahweh's origins.

**Who disagrees:** Albright, Cross, Freedman, and their students treated the poetry as pre-monarchic (12th-11th centuries). Smith (2014) defends substantial antiquity while acknowledging the need for caution.

**Alternative reading:** The archaic linguistic features of Judges 5 (long hair for battle, "routes," "village militia" — all in the introductory hymn per Smith 2014: 219) suggest pre-monarchic composition, even if the texts were later adapted for monarchic use.

**My assessment:** Fleming shifts the burden of proof. The poetry as we have it looks monarchic. If you want to claim it preserves pre-monarchic tradition, you need to demonstrate that, not just assume it. The archaic features could be deliberate archaism or genuinely old vocabulary preserved in later compositions.

## Claim 5: The southern geography reflects a genuine tradition of Yahweh being "at home in the land of pastoralists" — but this is about affinity with pastoralist life, not historical origin

**Author's claim:** "This poetic motif, however ancient, makes Yahweh at home in the land of pastoralists, the very population that in still earlier times include a group called Yhwʒ" (p. 179). "The old poetry draws our eye not just to the south, but behind the geography, to a deep affinity for the pastoralists of the inland steppe" (p. 179).

**Evidence presented:**
- Seir and Edom in Judg 5:4 match the two Egyptian terms for Shasu regions in Papyrus Anastasi VI and Papyrus Harris I (p. 178)
- "Seir takes priority and Edom is open space (śādeh)" — not a kingdom but pastoralist terrain (p. 178)
- By the early first millennium, the inland east was occupied by Aramean Damascus, Ammon, and Moab — "the south was the terrain left open for life on the move, life in tents" (p. 179)
- This explains why the pastoralist association shifted southward over time
- The prose kinship traditions (Ch 3) and the poetic southern theophany (Ch 4) converge on the same insight: Israel had deep affinities with pastoralist populations

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is Fleming's most synthetic and original claim in the chapter. It's elegant but requires accepting multiple prior arguments (the Shasu reading from Ch 2, the kinship traditions from Ch 3). The claim that Seir/Edom in the 12th-century Egyptian texts are "pastoralist regions, not political entities" is well-supported. But whether the biblical poetry preserves this specific understanding is less clear.

**What's at stake:** This is the positive payoff of Fleming's deconstruction. He's not just saying the Midianite Hypothesis is wrong — he's offering an alternative explanation for why the southern geography matters. Yahweh was a god of pastoralists, and the poetry preserves that association even after the geography shifted.

**Who disagrees:** The Midianite Hypothesis camp would say Fleming is essentially restating their position in different terms: Yahweh came from southern pastoralists. The difference is that Fleming denies "came from" — Yahweh was always among pastoralist groups in the inland Levant, some of whom became Israel.

**Alternative reading:** The southern geography is a late theological development with no historical basis — the Berlin hypothesis position (Pfeiffer, Müller).

**My assessment:** This is where Fleming's argument becomes most constructive and most vulnerable. He's offering an alternative that explains the data without the Midianite Hypothesis, and it's genuinely attractive. But it's also the sketchiest part of the chapter — more a direction for future research than a settled conclusion.

## Chapter 4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Poetry = theophany, not origin | HIGH | Ugaritic analogy may not transfer perfectly |
| 2: Seir/Edom secondary in shared core | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Oral variants" alternative |
| 3: Kuntillet Ajrud = Israelite projection | HIGH | Bidirectional influence possibility |
| 4: Poetry is monarchic | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaic features may be pre-monarchic |
| 5: Pastoralist affinity, not southern origin | MEDIUM | Requires accepting Ch 2-3 framework |

**Strongest section:** The Ugaritic Baal myth analogy (pp. 174-178). This is genuinely illuminating and has been underutilized in the Yahweh-origins debate. Fleming's detailed survey of divine residences in the Baal cycle makes a compelling case that distant divine homes are mythological geography, not origin markers.

**Weakest section:** The concluding synthesis about pastoralist affinity (pp. 178-179) is suggestive but underdeveloped. Fleming connects the Egyptian Shasu texts, the poetry, and the prose kinship traditions into a single coherent picture, but the connections are asserted more than demonstrated.

**Overall:** This chapter completes the demolition of the Midianite Hypothesis's three pillars (Egyptian geography in Ch 2, prose in Ch 3, poetry in Ch 4). The poetry is the hardest target, and Fleming's arguments are more nuanced here — he's not claiming the southern references are meaningless, but that they mean something different than the Midianite Hypothesis assumes. The Ugaritic analogy is the strongest single contribution.
