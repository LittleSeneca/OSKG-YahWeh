# Keel & Uehlinger — Part VII: An Era Ends — Iron Age III

The shortest chapter of the book — a transitional survey of Iron Age III (ca. 586–450 BCE), spanning the Neo-Babylonian and early Achaemenid Persian periods. Keel & Uehlinger trace how the collapse of the Judean monarchy, the exile, and the return under Persian auspices reshaped the religious landscape of Palestine. The chapter's central argument: the Iron Age III material record reveals a **bifurcation** — the coastal Phoenician and southern Edomite-Arabian zones preserved and adapted "Canaanite" polytheistic traditions, while postexilic Judah consolidated an aniconic, exclusivist Yahwism that actively erased goddess worship and figurine production. The chapter covers four regions: Babylonian/Persian administrative glyptic (§217–218), the Phoenician coastal economy (§219), northern Arabian and Edomite forms (§220–221), and Judah's turn toward exclusivity (§222–227).

**This chapter provides the archaeological end-point for the entire book's narrative: the material evidence for when and how goddess worship was stamped out in Judah.**

## Synthesis: What Iron Age III Tells Us About the Larger Monotheism Story

Keel & Uehlinger's Iron Age III chapter is short but decisive. It provides the archaeological terminus for the book's 1,500-year survey:

1. **The goddess was not gradually forgotten — she was actively erased.** The Chronicler's literary erasure, Haggai's exclusion of the non-exiled, Zechariah's personification of evil as a woman — these are not neutral theological developments. They are the documentary record of a factional victory. The material record confirms the result: figurines stop appearing in Judah.

2. **Monotheism was one of three post-exilic Yahwisms, not the only one.** Elephantine (Anat-Yahu) and the Palestinian popular religion (continued figurine use, until suppressed) were real alternatives. The monotheism of Deutero-Isaiah won because its proponents won the political struggle — Persian imperial backing for the returning Gola was decisive.

3. **The battle wasn't about Baal — it was about the goddess.** The authors note this explicitly: the Chronicler cared about erasing Asherah, not Baal. The Phoenician coast showed that the goddess (as Isis-mother, as Tinnit, as Astarte/Atargatis/Aphrodite) was thriving. She was the real threat to exclusivist Yahwism because she offered something Yahweh alone couldn't: a female divine presence. The eventual solution — personified Wisdom — was a way to reclaim the goddess's heritage within monotheistic bounds.

4. **Yahweh's solar character persisted through the aniconic turn.** The lion with solar disk, the winged sun bulla from En Gedi, and the biblical language of Yahweh as light (Isa 60, Mal 3:20) show that the aniconic Yahweh of postexilic Judaism wasn't an abstract philosophical principle — he was a radiant solar deity whose imagery had been stripped of anthropomorphic form but not of luminous power.

5. **The south (Edom/Arabia) preserved what the north (Judah) suppressed.** Hurvat Qitmit's god-and-goddess pairing, ostrich imagery, and possible Qaus-Yahweh connection suggest that the "Canaanite" religious heritage survived longest not in Canaan but in the desert periphery — exactly where Yahweh himself may have originated.
