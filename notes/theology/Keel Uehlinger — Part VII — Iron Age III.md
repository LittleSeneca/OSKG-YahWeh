# Keel & Uehlinger — Part VII: An Era Ends — Iron Age III

The shortest chapter of the book — a transitional survey of Iron Age III (ca. 586–450 BCE), spanning the Neo-Babylonian and early Achaemenid Persian periods. Keel & Uehlinger trace how the collapse of the Judean monarchy, the exile, and the return under Persian auspices reshaped the religious landscape of Palestine. The chapter's central argument: the Iron Age III material record reveals a **bifurcation** — the coastal Phoenician and southern Edomite-Arabian zones preserved and adapted "Canaanite" polytheistic traditions, while postexilic Judah consolidated an aniconic, exclusivist Yahwism that actively erased goddess worship and figurine production. The chapter covers four regions: Babylonian/Persian administrative glyptic (§217–218), the Phoenician coastal economy (§219), northern Arabian and Edomite forms (§220–221), and Judah's turn toward exclusivity (§222–227).

**This chapter provides the archaeological end-point for the entire book's narrative: the material evidence for when and how goddess worship was stamped out in Judah.**

---

## Claim 1: Neo-Babylonian glyptic was supplanted by Achaemenid royal-hero imagery, but local populations adapted both traditions to express their own religious identity

**Authors' claim:** "As regards glyptic art, the cylinder seal form that had become widespread once again during the Assyrian Period fell into disuse during Iron Age II. Only a single Neo-Babylonian cylinder seal has been found in Palestine/Israel... More significant during Iron Age III are the more widely distributed conoids with facets, made of a hard stone (mostly agate or chalcedony). They are imports as well."

The shift from Assyrian to Babylonian to Persian domination is visible in seal iconography — but what locals *did* with those seals tells a more interesting story.

**Evidence presented:**

- **Neo-Babylonian conoids** (§217): Faceted hard-stone seals depicting a male worshiper before the cultic symbols of Marduk and Nabu (the spade and stylus), often under astral symbols (star, winged sun, crescent moon). A bulla from Tell el-Ajjul shows the worshiper before the cultic pedestal of the moon god Sin instead. These testify to "a certain durability of the astral cult concepts" among people in contact with Babylonian administration — but "nothing with comparable decoration has appeared thus far in the Judean heartland."
- **The `lpqd yhd` seal** (§217): A faceted conoid with the legend *lpqd yhd* — "Inspector of Judah/Yehud" — portrays a worshiper before the Aramean moon god in a crescent (illus. 358c). A *Persian* official of Judah used a moon-god seal.
- **Aniconic Hebrew adaptation** (§217): A faceted conoid from el-Jib has *only* a Yahwistic name inscription (l'*nyhw bn hryhw*, illus. 359) where the astral cultic scene would normally be. "It can hardly be by accident that the name of the female or male owner appears in place of the astral cultic scene on these and similar commercially acquired Hebrew name seals." Judeans bought imported seals and then *erased the images*, replacing them with names.
- **Achaemenid royal-hero imagery** (§218): Under the Persians (late 6th century), bullae from Samaria and Tell Keisan show the Persian king fighting hybrid creatures (lion-griffin, winged bull) or as "Lord of the Animals" subduing two beasts. A winged sun disk (not Ahuramazda — see §172) emphasizes divine legitimation. Syro-Phoenician carvers adapted the motif, replacing hybrid creatures with natural lions. On a scarab from Tell es-Safi, the hero "lacks any royal or Persian characteristics" — the motif has been localized.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The glyptic evidence is stratigraphically and stylistically well-dated. The contrast between imported cultic imagery and local aniconic adaptation is a strong independent witness to the direction of Judean religion.

**What's at stake:** This is a material-culture confirmation of the aniconic trajectory. Judeans weren't ignorant of Babylonian and Persian religious imagery — they deliberately chose *not* to reproduce it on their personal seals, in some cases actively erasing it. This parallels the literary evidence for aniconic polemic (Deutero-Isaiah's mockery of idols) but precedes it in some cases.

**Who disagrees:** Stern (1982) treats the material more as evidence of Persian administrative presence than of local theological choices. Keel & Uehlinger's reading is more sensitive to the *selection* and *adaptation* of motifs.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of Babylonian cultic seals in Judah could reflect simple lack of access or trade routes rather than theological rejection. But the el-Jib seal — where the image was explicitly omitted — argues against this.

**My assessment:** The el-Jib seal is the smoking gun. Someone bought a blank faceted conoid (the prestige form of the era) and had it inscribed with a Hebrew name instead of the standard Babylonian cultic scene. That's a choice. Combined with the broader pattern of aniconic Hebrew name seals already established in Iron Age IIC (§204–216), the trajectory is clear: Judean religious identity was being expressed through the *absence* of images.

## Claim 2: The Phoenician economy sustained "Canaanite" polytheism through Egyptian and Greek channels, with Isis-mother imagery dominating and Greek influence introducing a sharp gender-role separation

**Authors' claim:** "In the coastal plain, especially in the Sharon region and in the area around Acre, scarabs with Phoenician, Egyptian, and even Greek motifs dominate the stamp seal production of Iron Age III. As is well known, the Phoenicians and Punics are the actual heirs of the 'Canaanite' culture."

While Judah was stamping out goddess imagery, the Phoenician coast was doubling down on it — but with new sources and styles.

**Evidence presented:**

- **Isis lactans dominates** (§219): "Clearly the most commonly represented deity was Isis, nursing the infant Horus." Scarabs from 'Atlit, Tel Megadim, and Lohame Ha-Geta'ot all show this motif (illuss. 363–364). "She is the goddess par excellence on the scarabs. It is no accident that she appears almost exclusively in her role as mother, obviously corresponding to a trend of the time."
- **Terra-cotta shift toward motherhood** (§219): Naked-goddess-with-breasts types persist (illus. 377), but new types gain popularity: a seated pregnant woman (illus. 365) and a goddess with a child. "The great majority of the terra-cotta figures from Iron Age III come from *favissae*, meaning that they are discarded cultic and votive statuettes."
- **Tinnit sign** (§219): Figurines from a shipwreck near Shave Ziyyon are stamped with the Tinnit sign — identifying the Phoenician-Punic goddess by name.
- **Male imagery shifts to Zeus and Heracles** (§219): A bearded, enthroned Zeus figure (Greek-Cypriot influenced, possibly descendant of El/Baalshamem) appears. Heracles fighting a lion or as war hero enters the repertoire (illuss. 367a–c from 'Atlit), even appearing in the conservative terra-cotta industry (illus. 368 from Lachish). The nude gymnast (illus. 369) shows "with absolute clarity that the increasing Greek influence resulted in the spread of a value system that recognized a clear separation of gender roles. For males, it encouraged a high valuation of the body and physical-athletic power; for females, it deified motherhood."
- **Bes as "Lord of the Lions"** (§219): A pictorial constellation combining Egyptian and Asiatic themes (illuss. 366a–b from 'Atlit and Ashkelon).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Phoenician material is well-published and the iconographic shift is clear. The gender-role observation (warrior/athlete male vs. nursing mother female) is a significant interpretive insight.

**What's at stake:** This is the counter-narrative to Judah. The Phoenician coast preserves and evolves the Canaanite polytheistic heritage — but even here, the goddess is being reshaped. She's no longer the naked, sexually-presented Astarte of the Bronze Age. She's Isis the mother. The shift from erotic goddess to maternal goddess is a major religious development of Iron Age III that has implications for how we understand the eventual emergence of the Virgin Mary and the Wisdom traditions.

**Who disagrees:** Stern (1982) catalogs the material but doesn't draw strong conclusions about gender ideology. Some scholars might argue the Isis-mother emphasis is simply Egyptian influence rather than a genuine shift in conceptions of the goddess.

**Alternative reading:** The emphasis on motherhood could reflect changing social conditions (population recovery after deportations?) rather than theological development.

**My assessment:** The juxtaposition with Greek athletic male imagery is striking and probably deliberate. The authors are right to see a gender-role ideology being transmitted through imported imagery. This Phoenician gender binary — warrior male, mother female — is the exact opposite of what postexilic Judah was doing (where both the warrior goddess and the mother goddess were being erased, and Yahweh absorbed all functions).

## Claim 3: The Hurvat Qitmit Edomite shrine preserved a full "Canaanite" religion with god and goddess side-by-side into the 6th century, raising unresolved questions about the shared roots of Qaus, Yahweh of Teman, and the "Lord of the Ostriches"

**Authors' claim:** "In general, the discoveries at Hurvat Qitmit are convincing evidence of the inertia with respect to traditional 'Canaanite' religion that existed in a peripheral region like Edom. One could imagine oneself transported at this site right back into a long-past era of 'Canaanite' religion, with god and goddess visibly standing side-by-side as partners."

The Edomite shrine at Hurvat Qitmit (eastern Negev, ~25 km east of Beer-sheba, ~10 km south of Arad) — dated to the early 6th century (late Iron Age IIC / early Iron Age III) — provides the most vivid archaeological counterpoint to contemporary developments in Judah.

**Evidence presented:**

- **Architecture** (§221): Two buildings — Building A (three long parallel rooms opening to a courtyard) and Building B (square, tower-like with benches/podia and altar). Comparable structures at Kadesh Barnea, Idumean Lachish, and Nabatean temples suggest the three-part *adyton* "is most likely a characteristic feature of Edomite-Idumean sacral architecture."
- **Qaus inscription** (§221): A vessel fragment from Building B bears the incised inscription `...]blqwshp[...` — the element *qws* reproduces the Edomite national god Qaus. A massebah in Building B "very likely represents the god Qaus."
- **Warrior deity and three-horned goddess** (§221): Fragments from the courtyard include a hand holding a dagger, a dagger, and an arm holding a weapon — probably from a warrior deity statue (Qaus? or his female partner?). A three-horned head from a goddess statue (illus. 375) parallels Bronze Age metal statuettes of a goddess with three-horned helmet holding a dagger — "her right hand is raised to strike." This is an *aggressive* goddess, not a passive consort.
- **Naked goddess presenting breasts** (§221): A statuette "without horned helmet or any attributes of a deity" (illus. 377) — the same type as the Bronze/Iron Age nude female figurines.
- **Stylized tree** (§221): A terra-cotta in the shape of a stylized tree (illus. 376) raises the question "whether this might rather be an Edomite variation of Asherah."
- **Music and worshipers** (§221): A double-pipe player (illus. 378) and small anthropomorphic terra-cottas that "ought not be identified as deities, but are probably human male and female worshipers."
- **Ostrich and pig figurines** (§221): Numerous ostrich images recall the "Lord of the Ostriches." An image of a pig (illus. 379) is significant — pig offerings are mentioned scornfully in Isa 65:4; 66:3, 17; the dietary prohibitions of Lev 11:7 and Deut 14:8 "probably would have been formulated in exilic or early postexilic times," so a pig votive in 6th-century Edom is the kind of practice those prohibitions were targeting.
- **The shared-roots question** (§221): "Do 'Yahweh of Teman,' Qaus, and the 'Lord of the Ostriches' all have their roots finally in one and the same figure? If the answer is 'yes,' for how long were they understood to be identical to one another or at least related?" The authors connect this to Job 39:13–16 (Yahweh as Lord of the Ostriches) and Job's home in northwestern Arabia.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the archaeological description (published by Beit-Arieh and Beck). SPECULATIVE on the shared-roots question — the authors flag it as an open question, not a conclusion.

**What's at stake:** If Qaus, Yahweh of Teman, and the Lord of the Ostriches share a common root in the southern steppe/desert, then the Edomite religion preserved at Hurvat Qitmit might represent a *sibling* tradition to early Yahwism, not a foreign one. The aggressive three-horned goddess is also significant — she looks more like the Bronze Age warrior goddess (Anat?) than the maternal Isis of the Phoenician coast. Different regions preserved different aspects of the "Canaanite" heritage.

**Who disagrees:** The identification of the massebah with Qaus is plausible but not certain — the inscription is fragmentary. Some scholars might read the three-horned goddess as Anat rather than a local Edomite deity. The shared-roots hypothesis is acknowledged as speculative by the authors themselves.

**Alternative reading:** Hurvat Qitmit could represent an imported Edomite cult imposed on the region after Edomite expansion into the Negev following Judah's collapse, rather than an indigenous survival of "Canaanite" religion. The architectural parallels with Kadesh Barnea and Nabatean temples support this.

**My assessment:** The most arresting claim here is that Yahweh, Qaus, and the Lord of the Ostriches may have a common origin. This would mean that the god who became the sole deity of monotheistic Judaism once had *brother* gods in the same southern pantheon — and one of those brothers (Qaus) continued to be worshipped with a goddess partner centuries after Judah had begun purging goddess imagery. The Edomite-Judean break that the authors note ("from the perspective of biblical texts, Iron Age III is also the time when there was a definitive break between Judah and Edom") might have been as much a religious schism between sibling traditions as a political conflict.

## Claim 4: The exile produced three divergent Judean Yahwisms — Babylonian monotheism, Egyptian syncretism, and Palestinian popular religion — and the returning Babylonian elite imposed exclusivity by force, with goddess worship as the primary target

**Authors' claim:** "The complexity of the religious history of Judah during Iron Age III is increased by the need to take into account the religious traditions of the groups that were dislocated when they were exiled to Babylon and Egypt, as well as for those who remained in Judah. The three groups lived in different political, social, and cultural contexts, which affected the Judean-Jewish religious concepts in different ways."

This is the sociological engine behind Iron Age III religious change. The chapter maps three trajectories:

**Evidence presented — the three groups** (§222):

1. **Babylonian Diaspora — Monotheism**: "The credal tenet of monotheism (the monotheism of Deutero-Isaiah is hardly to be taken as independent from the Achaemenian worship of Ahuramazda), and the concomitant, mocking polemic against the production of so-called graven images... is stated explicitly for the first time." The exile gave the elite "an opportunity to collect and reformulate the religious traditions of the motherland" under "priestly" and "deuteronomistic" schools.

2. **Egyptian Diaspora (Elephantine) — Syncretism**: The Jewish military colony at Elephantine "demonstrated that their version of the religion had 'syncretistic' tendencies. Certainly they followed the Yahwistic El-traditions of Iron Age IIC, but, apparently following the lead of some colonists from Phoenicia concerning the partner/consort issue, they recognized and acknowledged alongside Yahweh a female deity, Anat." This is "merely a variant form of the 'Late-Canaanite' religion that was common throughout the Levant and that we encounter in Hurvat Qitmit. For this religion, there was a small pantheon, with a pair of deities at its peak."

3. **Those who remained in Judah — Popular Religion**: The returnees' exclusion of the non-exiled from temple restoration (Hag 2:10–14) and the conflicts over leadership "allow us to surmise that the Jewish women and men who had remained in Palestine had continued to follow the popular religious ideas and practices from Iron Age IIC after they emerged from under the pressure imposed by the priestly-deuteronomistic élite who had now been taken into exile."

**Evidence presented — the enforcement of orthodoxy** (§227):

- **Zechariah's seventh vision** (Zech 5:5–11): Personifies "everything 'evil' in the form of a woman(!)" transported in a closed vessel to Babylon — "lumps foreign cults and goddess worship together."
- **Haggai's exclusion** (Hag 2:10–14): The leadership around Zerubbabel and high priest Joshua excluded those who had not been exiled from helping restore the temple. "They particularly had their sights set on stopping all worship of the goddess."
- **The Chronicler's erasure** (§227): In the 4th century, the Chronicler "sought to eliminate all traces of the goddesses (Astarte and most especially Asherah)... from its own 'worldview.'" By silence or reinterpretation, "even the existence of the goddess was now virtually denied." Tellingly, the Chronicler was less zealous about Baal or Dagan — "the decisive question at the time of the Chronicler was no longer about foreign cults and gods. The dangerous issues concerned the existence of the goddess."
- **Wisdom as the goddess's vestige** (§227): "Only in postexilic theological speculation about wisdom was it possible, within a monotheistic-Yahwistic viewpoint about God, to think explicitly about personified wisdom as a way to show an explicitly female dimension of God. Patterned on contemporary worship of Isis, through the use of some metaphorical footprints, the heritage of the 'Canaanite' Asherah lived on."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The three-group framework is well-established in biblical scholarship (Albertz, Römer agree on the basic division). The Elephantine papyri are unambiguous about Anat-Yahu. The Haggai/Zechariah/Chronicler evidence for goddess-targeted polemic is textually clear.

**What's at stake:** This reframes the monotheism question. It wasn't a gradual evolution — it was a *political victory* by one faction (the Babylonian returnees) over two others (the Egyptian syncretists and the Palestinian popular religionists). Monotheism was enforced, not naturally emergent. And the *primary target* of that enforcement was the goddess. The authors explicitly note that Baal and Dagan were less threatening than Asherah/Astarte — the real battle was over whether Yahweh had a female counterpart.

**Cross-reference to Römer:** Römer (Chapter 12 + Conclusion) identifies Second Isaiah as the first unambiguous monotheism and the exile as the crucible. Keel & Uehlinger agree but add nuance: the exile produced *three* responses, and monotheism was only one of them. The Elephantine papyri show that 5th-century Jews in Egypt still worshipped Anat alongside Yahweh — monotheism was not inevitable or universal even a century after Deutero-Isaiah.

**Cross-reference to Albertz:** Albertz (Vol. 2, Chapter 4) emphasizes the differential impact of exile on official vs. family religion and the creative opportunity the crisis created. Keel & Uehlinger add the material-culture dimension: the disappearance of terra-cotta figurines from postexilic Judah is the archaeological correlate of the theological shift Albertz describes.

**Alternative reading:** Some scholars (e.g., Smith) emphasize Persian imperial authorization of local cults as the mechanism — the Torah was compiled under Persian auspices as the "law of the God of Heaven" for Yehud. In this reading, monotheism was as much Persian policy as Jewish theology. Keel & Uehlinger hint at this (the Ahuramazda parallel) but don't develop it.

**My assessment:** The three-group model is the single most important framework for understanding Iron Age III religion. It explains why the biblical texts are so internally conflicted about the goddess: they were written by the winners of a factional fight. The Elephantine evidence is especially valuable because it's extrabiblical — it shows what Yahwism looked like when the deuteronomistic editors *weren't* in control. Keel & Uehlinger's insight that the goddess (not Baal) was the primary target of the orthodox campaign is profound and underappreciated in the broader literature.

## Claim 5: Postexilic Judah consolidated aniconic solar-Yahwism — lion stamp seals potentially symbolize Yahweh, figurine production vanished, and sharp cultic boundaries now coincided with political boundaries

**Authors' claim:** "Generally, there are now 'clear cultural and cultic boundaries within Palestine that coincide with those political boundaries known from historical tradition' (H. Weippert 1988, 717f.). The contrast is striking between the discoveries on the coastal plain and in the Edomite-Arabic south (Hurvat Qitmit!)."

The material record of postexilic Judah shows what monotheism looked like on the ground: no figurines, no goddess imagery, and administrative seals that used animal symbols (lion) rather than anthropomorphic deities — possibly with solar-Yahwistic connotations.

**Evidence presented:**

- **Lion stamp seals** (§223): About 70 anepigraphic seal impressions on vessel handles from Jerusalem, Ramat Rahel, Gibeon, Tell en-Nasbeh, Jericho, and En Gedi (6th–5th centuries). The lion appears in two postures: standing on all fours (~60 times, illus. 380a–b) and standing on hind legs (3 times, illus. 381a–c). These parallel the royal stamps (8th century) and rosette stamps (7th century) as administrative instruments. The lion "is to be treated as a symbol for Judah (Gen 49:9; cf. Ezek 19:2–9)" — and would later be replaced after 450 BCE by aniconic *yh(w)d* seals. "Nor can we tell whether the royal animal represents Yahweh symbolically 'roaring from Zion'" — an incense stand next to the lion on some impressions (illus. 381) "suggests that the latter interpretation cannot be excluded."
- **Solar disk on lion** (§224): A vessel-handle impression from Ramat Rahel (illus. 382a–b) — often misinterpreted as a bull with solar disk — is more likely "an attacking lion, and the head of the lion... can be interpreted as having an open mouth." The solar disk "makes it probable that the aggressive animal... may represent a solar connotation of Yahweh of Zion." An En Gedi bulla (illus. 383) with a winged sun and Aramaic inscription *lmr* ("for the lord" or "belonging to the lord") "can be hardly any doubt that the winged sun represents the solar god of heaven." Isaiah 60:19f. and Malachi 3:20 show that even in postexilic times "the God of Jerusalem was conceived primarily in terms of a 'being of light,' related to the sun."
- **Disappearance of figurines** (§226): "There are hardly any terra-cotta or bronze figurines at all in postexilic Judah." Pillar figurines and horse-and-rider types vanish from Judah after the 5th century. Isolated statuettes in border areas like Lachish may indicate the settlement "did not belong to Judah/Yehud." "There was apparently no market for the inexpensive and once popular terra-cotta images in postexilic Judah, whether because religious development had advanced so far in the direction of monotheism that the need was no longer that great or because so-called 'orthodox' circles in postexilic Judah had effectively halted the production or import of these goods."
- **Aniconic name seals persist and intensify** (§225): Seventy bullae from an early postexilic Judean site show impressions that are "exclusively simple name seals." This contrasts with the 4th-century Wadi ed-Daliyeh archive in Samaria, whose bullae display "a colorful mixture of Greek and Persian images."
- **The solar dimension as constant** (§224): "In retrospect, this solar dimension should be seen as a constant element — even though flexible and adaptable — in Israelite and Judean worship of Yahweh." This connects back to the solarization theme of Iron Age IIB (Part V) and the winged sun disk on royal seals of Iron Age IIC.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the archaeological pattern. MEDIUM on the lion = Yahweh interpretation (the authors flag it as uncertain). HIGH on solar Yahwism as a persistent element — this is one of the book's central arguments.

**What's at stake:** If the lion stamps *do* represent Yahweh symbolically, then postexilic Yahwism wasn't fully aniconic — it used animal symbolism that later tradition would classify as idolatrous. The transition from lion stamps to aniconic *yh(w)d* stamps around 450 BCE marks the final step toward the imageless monotheism of rabbinic Judaism. The solar dimension is also significant: the Yahweh of postexilic Jerusalem was a *god of light*, not just a lawgiver.

**Who disagrees:** Aharoni and many others read the Ramat Rahel impression as a bull with solar disk (alluding to the golden calf/Bethel tradition). Keel & Uehlinger argue persuasively against this on iconographic grounds (bulls' tails hang down; lions' tails rise over the back). Stern (1982) reads the upright lion as wounded/agony from the Persian king-hunt motif, which Keel & Uehlinger also dispute.

**Alternative reading:** The lion stamps could be purely administrative symbols with no theological significance — just as the rosette stamps probably were. But the solar disk and incense stand make a purely secular reading harder to sustain.

**My assessment:** The disappearance of figurines from Judah is the single most important material fact in this chapter. It's not that figurines declined — they *vanished*. Meanwhile, Phoenicia and Edom continued producing them in quantity. This is a controlled experiment: same period, same region, but radically different archaeological profiles on either side of political borders. The orthodox campaign wasn't just literary polemic — it changed what people made, bought, and used. The solar-Yahwism theme connects the entire book's arc: from Egyptian solar imagery in Iron Age IIB, through the winged sun disk on royal seals in IIC, to the solar lion of postexilic Yehud. Yahweh absorbs the sun god's attributes.

## Synthesis: What Iron Age III Tells Us About the Larger Monotheism Story

Keel & Uehlinger's Iron Age III chapter is short but decisive. It provides the archaeological terminus for the book's 1,500-year survey:

1. **The goddess was not gradually forgotten — she was actively erased.** The Chronicler's literary erasure, Haggai's exclusion of the non-exiled, Zechariah's personification of evil as a woman — these are not neutral theological developments. They are the documentary record of a factional victory. The material record confirms the result: figurines stop appearing in Judah.

2. **Monotheism was one of three post-exilic Yahwisms, not the only one.** Elephantine (Anat-Yahu) and the Palestinian popular religion (continued figurine use, until suppressed) were real alternatives. The monotheism of Deutero-Isaiah won because its proponents won the political struggle — Persian imperial backing for the returning Gola was decisive.

3. **The battle wasn't about Baal — it was about the goddess.** The authors note this explicitly: the Chronicler cared about erasing Asherah, not Baal. The Phoenician coast showed that the goddess (as Isis-mother, as Tinnit, as Astarte/Atargatis/Aphrodite) was thriving. She was the real threat to exclusivist Yahwism because she offered something Yahweh alone couldn't: a female divine presence. The eventual solution — personified Wisdom — was a way to reclaim the goddess's heritage within monotheistic bounds.

4. **Yahweh's solar character persisted through the aniconic turn.** The lion with solar disk, the winged sun bulla from En Gedi, and the biblical language of Yahweh as light (Isa 60, Mal 3:20) show that the aniconic Yahweh of postexilic Judaism wasn't an abstract philosophical principle — he was a radiant solar deity whose imagery had been stripped of anthropomorphic form but not of luminous power.

5. **The south (Edom/Arabia) preserved what the north (Judah) suppressed.** Hurvat Qitmit's god-and-goddess pairing, ostrich imagery, and possible Qaus-Yahweh connection suggest that the "Canaanite" religious heritage survived longest not in Canaan but in the desert periphery — exactly where Yahweh himself may have originated.
