# Stavrakopoulou — Part I: Feet and Legs (Chapters 2-4)

---

## Chapter 2: Grounded

### Claim: God's feet marked his physical presence on earth — temples were his footprints
→ [[claim-god-feet-marked-physical-presence-temples-footprints]] | **stav-god-2-4.1** | Confidence: very-high
  God's feet render him LOCALLY present at specific sites. The 'Ain Dara temple footprints (a metre long, pointing inward with no exit prints) provide material-culture confirmation. Biblical evidence: Genesis 3:8 (footsteps in Eden), Exodus 24:9-10 (lapis lazuli pavement under God's feet), Ezekiel 43:7 ("place for the soles of my feet").
  Contradicted by: [[claim-genesis-1-26-anti-fluid-manifesto-image-without-embodiment]]

## Chapter 3: Footloose

### Claim: God walked — his locomotion was real, not metaphorical, and marked sacred space
→ [[claim-god-walked-real-locomotion-marked-sacred-space]] | **stav-god-2-4.2** | Confidence: high
  God walks, strides, treads, and stamps. Standing stones (*massebot*) marked where he stood — the stone IS the standing place. The word *maqom* ("place") derives from "standing place." Jacob's stone at Bethel becomes a massebah that God identifies with.
  Contradicted by: [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]]

## Chapter 4: Sensational Feet

### Claim: God used his feet as weapons — crushing enemies, stamping territory, marking ownership
→ [[claim-god-feet-weapons-crushing-enemies-property-markers]] | **stav-god-2-4.3** | Confidence: high
  Feet communicated ownership and identity throughout the ANE. God trampled enemies (Isaiah 63:1-6), made them a footstool (Psalm 110:1), and strode the earth in fury (Habakkuk 3:12-13). Mesopotamian legal rituals used foot-lifting to transfer land ownership.
  Contradicted by: [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]]

**Stavrakopoulou's strength in Part I:** Her ability to connect material culture (the 'Ain Dara footprints) to biblical texts (God's feet on the lapis lazuli pavement) to ANE social practice (feet as property markers). This is interdisciplinary scholarship at its best. She's not just reading texts — she's reconstructing an embodied world.

**Comparison to Sommer:** Where Sommer provides the philosophical framework (fluidity vs. non-fluidity), Stavrakopoulou provides the sensory, material, bodily experience. Sommer tells you God had bodies. Stavrakopoulou tells you what those bodies FELT LIKE — how the feet pressed into stone, how the ground became holy under God's tread, how the enemies were crushed underfoot.
