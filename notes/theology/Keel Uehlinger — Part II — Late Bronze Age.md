# Keel & Uehlinger — Part II: Egyptian Colonialism and the Prevalence of Political and Warrior Deities — Late Bronze Age

This Part covers §§30–60 (plus an Excursus) — the core of the Late Bronze Age (~1550–1150 BCE) iconographic evidence from four key Palestinian cities (Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, Beth-Shean), each representing a different regional subsystem. The central thesis: Egyptian colonialism reshaped Canaanite religion by suppressing the public, monumental representation of goddesses in favor of male warrior and political deities (Baal, Reshef, Seth-Baal, Amun, the Pharaoh), while goddess worship survived through cheap terra-cotta figurines in domestic settings — a split between "official" and "private" religion that anticipates Dever's book-religion vs. folk-religion distinction.

---

## Claim 1: The Middle-to-Late Bronze Age transition was gradual and regionally variable, not a clean break — and this matters for religious continuity

**Authors' claim:** "Much like the way the Early Bronze Age has been too neatly demarcated from the Middle Bronze Age, the traditional way of separating the Middle Bronze Age from the Late Bronze Age is too rigid." (§30) There was a transitional period of 100–150 years (LB I, ca. 1550–1450) between the golden age of MB culture and full Egyptian rule, and "this transition obviously did not take place in all parts of the country at the same time." Some regions (southern coastal plain, Beth-Shean) Egyptianized earlier and more intensively; others (Hazor) remained under northern Syrian influence throughout.

**Evidence presented:**
- Destruction of MB cities and hill-country encampments began gradually in the 16th century, not all at once
- The period comes to a "closure of sorts only when Tuthmosis III was victorious over a coalition of Canaanite princes near Megiddo in 1457"
- Hazor's four-phase Temple H (Area H) shows "a gentle and actually unbroken transition from the Middle Bronze Age to Late Bronze Age" with near-identical floor plans across rebuilds
- Middle Bronze Age votive offerings (engraved goddess figures with silver/bronze overlay) survived into LB strata at Hazor — reused across temple phases
- The transitional period produces distinctive new artifact types, notably the pear-shaped gold pendants of the vegetation goddess (replacing earlier silver/bronze leaf pendants)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The four-city comparative method itself validates this: Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, and Beth-Shean each tell a different story, which could only happen if the transition was genuinely non-uniform. Chronological anchors (Tuthmosis III, Ramesses II, Ramesses III) are well-established from Egyptian records.

**What's at stake:** If the LB transition was gradual and regionally variable, then religious change was too — there is no single "Canaanite religion" at any one moment, but multiple regional subsystems. This complicates any attempt to reconstruct a unified pre-Israelite pantheon from texts (as Smith and de Moor do). It also means that whatever "Israelite religion" emerged in Iron Age I did so against a background of regional diversity, not a monolithic Canaanite polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** Older scholarship tended to treat archaeological periods as clean breaks (EB→MB→LB→Iron). Albright's conquest model assumed sharp transitions. More recent archaeologists (Finkelstein, Dever, Mazar) generally agree on gradualism, but the religious implications are rarely drawn out.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue the four-city diversity is an artifact of preservation bias rather than genuine regional difference. But Keel & Uehlinger's whole method depends on taking the site-by-site evidence seriously, and the patterns they identify (northern vs. southern, Egyptian vs. indigenous) are robust enough to survive that critique.

**My assessment:** This is the book's methodological backbone. They're not just assembling a catalogue — they're showing that geographic and political context determines what gods appear and how. This four-city framework is a far more honest approach than synthesizing all LB evidence into a single "Canaanite pantheon." The transition matters for the debate about Yahweh's origins: if Hazor (north) and the southern cities had such different religious profiles in the LB, the religion of the central hill country in Iron Age I was likely its own regional subsystem, not simply a modified version of coastal Canaanite religion.

## Claim 2: Hazor maintained northern Syrian/Anatolian traditions throughout the LB, with male heavenly deities (moon god, weather god) eclipsing the goddess

**Authors' claim:** "Hazor, to name one site, remained very much under northern influence (northern Syria, southeastern Anatolia) during the entire Late Bronze Age." (§30) The dominant cults at Hazor were the weather god (on a bull) and the moon god — and "this is the first evidence of a piety in Palestine/Israel that is oriented toward heavenly beings, apparently having originated in northern Syria." (§33)

**Evidence presented:**
- **Architectural evidence:** Temple H (Area H) was a Syrian-style longroom temple with parallels at Ebla (Str. IIIA, ca. 2000/1900) and Alalakh (Str. VII, ca. 1720–1650). It was rebuilt three times with "almost the identical floor plan."
- **Basalt orthostats:** The wall-ornamentation technique is "a typical Anatolian technique" (Naumann 1955). The reclining lion (190 cm long) served an architectural function like one at Alaja Huyuk in central Anatolia.
- **The weather god on a bull:** A basalt bull fragment (barely 30 cm long) with human feet on its back, found in front of Temple H (Str. IB, 14th c.), shows "the same Anatolian sun or moon symbol, the disk with a cross, on its chest." Since the deity on a bull is the weather god in northern Syrian-Anatolian contexts, this is "a most interesting combination of a weather god and a sun or moon god." A small bronze bull from the Holy of Holies (Str. IA, 13th c.) confirms the weather god cult.
- **The moon god stele:** The stelae temple in Area C features a central stele with "a crescent moon with a disk above two outstretched arms" — the symbol of the Moon God of Haran in northern Syria (§33). A seated basalt figure wears a crescent moon breast pendant. Silver-covered bronze standards with crescent moons were found in another Area C shrine.
- **No anthropomorphic goddesses in later Temple H phases:** "Except for the three engraved figures with silver and bronze overlay, that are very likely from the Middle Bronze Age or from Late Bronze Age I, no anthropomorphic representations of female deities have been found in the later Late Bronze Age phases of Temple H." (§32)
- **Mitannian seals:** 18 cylinder seals in the "popular Mitannian style" were found, with sacral trees flanked by worshipers and animals (stags, caprids, fish). "Mitannian glyptic art is generally reserved when it comes to depicting deities anthropomorphically" — the artists "preferred to use a wide range of elements from the sphere of the goddess, such as the stylized tree, caprids, and birds" (§32).

**Confidence:** HIGH for the northern Syrian/Anatolian influence on Hazor — the basalt lion, orthostats, and moon god symbolism have clear parallels. MEDIUM for the claim that this represents "the first evidence of a piety oriented toward heavenly beings" — this depends on how one interprets the earlier astral symbolism from MB IIB (which Keel & Uehlinger acknowledge with the note "but see already illus. 41").

**What's at stake:** Hazor was the largest Canaanite city of the LB, and its religious profile is markedly different from southern sites. If Hazor was a major power center with a religion dominated by a weather god (Baal-Hadad) and a moon god — with goddesses absent from the temple iconography — this challenges the picture of LB Canaanite religion as goddess-centered. It also means that northern Israelite religion in Iron Age I may have inherited more from Hazor's traditions than from the coastal/southern traditions.

**Who disagrees:** Kempinski (1989a) argued for a triad of El, Baal, and a fertility goddess at the Megiddo "Fortified Temple" — a view Keel & Uehlinger explicitly reject (§37: "We see no reason to agree with Kempinski... The finds seem to point in another direction."). Yadin's original Hazor reports emphasized the MB-LB continuity; Keel & Uehlinger build on this but draw sharper conclusions about the shift away from goddesses.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of anthropomorphic goddess figurines from later Temple H phases at Hazor could reflect preservation bias or the temple's specific cultic function rather than a broader religious shift. The Mitannian seals with their stylized trees may *imply* the goddess without depicting her — which is not the same as her eclipse.

**My assessment:** The Hazor evidence is genuinely striking because it's the largest LB site and its temple was continuously used. The contrast with Middle Bronze Age Nahariyah (where doves, lions, and goddess figurines abounded — see Part I) is stark. However, Keel & Uehlinger may overstate the "absence of goddess" at Hazor. The sacral tree on Mitannian seals and painted pottery may indeed represent the goddess in aniconic form. This is the same interpretive move they'll make for Lachish (see Claim 4 below), and consistency would demand they acknowledge it for Hazor too.

## Claim 3: At Megiddo, the LB witnessed a decisive shift from the vegetation goddess to warrior deities (Baal, Reshef), mirrored on the human level by the replacement of erotic scenes with military and victory imagery

**Authors' claim:** "There was a decisive change in what was depicted on metal figures during the transition from the Middle Bronze Age to the Late Bronze Age. Warrior gods, such as Baal and Reshef, virtually replace the naked goddesses." (§37) On the human level: "Military themes replace those of loyalty and erotic contact between the sexes." (§38) And "the main concern of the prince is not his encounter with the goddess but rather a triumph over his enemies." (§39)

**Evidence presented:**
- **MB goddess pendants → LB gold pear-shaped pendants:** The earlier silver/bronze leaf pendants depicting the slender goddess are replaced by gold pendants in the shape of an inverted pear, with puncture holes marking head, breasts, navel, and female genitalia (§34). Platt (1976) interprets the overall shape as imitating female genitalia with pubic hair along the outside edge. These connect to the "Branch Goddess" iconography of MB IIB scarabs — a branch grows from navel/genitalia on some pieces (illus. 49). These gold pendants "were passed on by inheritance over long periods of time" but "none of these gold pendants survived beyond the Late Bronze Age to be found in an undisputed Iron Age context."
- **Cheap terra-cotta replaces expensive metal for goddesses:** "The Late Bronze Age witnessed the introduction of cheap, molded clay figures for the first time and these are found in great quantities." (§35) The dominant type shows a goddess holding/presenting her breasts — the same type that was most popular at the end of MB IIB, but now in clay instead of metal.
- **Enthroned youthful god (illus. 56):** A bronze figure (25+ cm high) covered in gold leaf, from the LB temple at Megiddo, shows "an enthroned youthful god" holding a blossom — iconographically connected to the weather god on MB IIB scarabs. The Taanach letters confirm that Baal-Hadad was "the dominant deity in the area surrounding the broad central plain of Jezreel during the Late Bronze Age" (§37).
- **Reshef figurine (illus. 57):** A bronze figure (13 cm high) from Grave 4 holds a handheld weapon and shield — "typical of portrayals of Reshef."
- **Warrior imagery on painted sherds (illuss. 58–59):** "Warriors with handheld weapons and small shields" appear on two painted sherds from the end of LB IIB, nearly identical to the Reshef bronze except that the human warriors wear beards and lack the high head covering.
- **The chariot as new iconographic motif:** Introduced from Egypt — the pharaoh in his chariot, triumphing over enemies with his bow, becomes "an important motif within the religious system of Egypt" and appears on Palestinian seal amulets (§38).
- **Victory celebration ivories (illus. 65):** The famous Megiddo ivory shows a prince returning victorious from battle, enthroned on a cherub throne, with a winged solar disk above — "gives the impression that the victor was blessed by the deity." Women are now shown "either as servants who stand before the enthroned prince or are absent altogether." (§39)
- **The feast scene parallel (illus. 67):** Another ivory shows only male celebrants. "Whereas women appear in depictions from the Middle Bronze Age both as sexual partners for men and with equal status, here they are shown either as servants... or are absent altogether."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The evidence is abundant and consistent across multiple media (metal figurines, ivories, painted pottery, seal amulets). The shift from MB goddess dominance to LB warrior dominance at Megiddo is one of the most clearly documented transitions in the book.

**What's at stake:** This shift — goddesses out, warrior gods in, women relegated to servant roles — is directly attributed to Egyptian colonialism and its military ideology. If this is correct, then the religion of pre-monarchic Israel (Iron Age I) inherited a landscape where male warrior gods dominated public religion and goddess worship had been pushed into cheap domestic terra-cottas. This makes sense of why the Hebrew Bible's polemics target goddess worship (Asherah, the Queen of Heaven) while Yahwism presents Yahweh as a warrior — both phenomena have LB roots.

**Who disagrees:** Kempinski (1989a) argued for a triad of El, Baal, and a fertility goddess at Megiddo's temple. Keel & Uehlinger reject this: "hardly any of the precious metal figures of the goddess were still being manufactured in the Late Bronze Age." They also note that letters from Taanach (Glock 1983) show Baal-Hadad was dominant, and the Amarna letters from Palestine show "the name Baal clearly dominated the scene in Canaan" among personal names (§37 n.5).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that the shift from goddess to warrior god at Megiddo reflects the bias of the surviving evidence — metal figurines and ivories were élite products, and the élite may have been more Egyptianized than the general population. The cheap terra-cotta goddesses found in large quantities suggest the goddess remained popular among common people. Keel & Uehlinger acknowledge this split (see Claim 6 below) but the Megiddo chapter's narrative emphasizes the warrior shift heavily.

**My assessment:** This is compellingly argued. The convergence of metal figurines, painted pottery, seal amulets, and carved ivories all telling the same story is hard to dismiss as preservation bias. The most significant detail is the gold pendants: their disappearance from undisputed Iron Age contexts suggests a genuine rupture, not just élite fashion. For the Truth Project, this matters because it shows that the "warrior Yahweh" of early Israelite poetry (Exodus 15, Judges 5, Deuteronomy 33) fits a LB pattern that was already well-established before Israel emerged — Yahweh didn't invent the warrior-god archetype, he stepped into a role that Baal and Reshef were already playing.

## Claim 4: Lachish and southern Palestine show the most intense Egyptian-Canaanite syncretism — the qudshu-type goddess, Baal-Seth as serpent-conqueror, and Hathor worship all represent fusions of Egyptian and indigenous traditions

**Authors' claim:** "Egyptian influence clearly predominates at Lachish and at other southern Palestinian sites." (§40) The qudshu-type goddess is "an Egyptian-style version of the Middle Bronze Age 'Branch Goddess'" (§41), and Baal-Seth represents a fusion where "the god who guaranteed the earth's fertility, when combined with Seth, has become a champion against all evil" (§45).

**Evidence presented:**
- **The qudshu-type goddess (illus. 69):** Naked female figurines with shoulder-length Hathor-style locks, holding papyrus or lotus stems in bent arms. "Iconographically, this is an Egyptian-style version of the Middle Bronze Age 'Branch Goddess'" (§41). Over 70 examples are known — primarily from sites south of Carmel. The term "qudshu" comes from Egyptian New Kingdom stelae where the inscription *qdš* ("holiness") appears beside a goddess standing on a lion, flanked by Reshef and Min. Keel & Uehlinger note: "*Qdš* is probably not the name of the goddess but an epithet indicating the dominant aspect of her sexual and erotic powers" (§41 n.7).
- **Goddess on a horse — warrior adaptation (illus. 71):** A gold-leaf piece from the temple on the tell at Lachish shows the goddess standing on a *horse* rather than a lion, wearing an atef-crown. "In the Late Bronze Age the horse was used to convey exclusively military connotations. The goddess, whose role had been connected primarily with vegetation and, to some extent, with the animals of the wilderness, is now drawn into the arena of war" (§41).
- **Hathor cult:** Six Hathor head pendants and a Hathor head amulet with cow's horns and solar disk from the Fosse Temple (13th c.); a gold Hathor sistrum amulet from Tell el-Ajjul; and the Timnah shrine (north of Elath) with Hathor capitals, a Ramesses III relief showing the pharaoh sacrificing to Hathor, and faience fragments with her title *nb.t mfk3.t* ("Mistress of the Turquoise") (§42).
- **The inscribed pitcher (illus. 81):** A pitcher from the Fosse Temple bears the inscription *mtn.šy [l'][rb]ty* — "Mattan [or: gift]: a present for my lady Elat [or: the goddess]." The inscription is placed directly over the caprid-flanked tree. "The venerated goddess is possibly to be identified as Asherah; in the mythological texts from Ugarit *rbt* 'lady' and *ilt* 'goddess' are epithets of Atirat/Asherah" (§44).
- **The Revadim/Aphek goddess (illus. 82):** A molded naked goddess figure with a palmetto tree flanked by caprids on each thigh near exposed genitalia, nursing children at each breast, wearing a Q-shaped womb-symbol pendant. "Within one individual deity, this goddess combines the secret powers of mother earth, giving birth to humans and animals, nourishing them, and causing them to flourish" (§44).
- **Baal-Seth as serpent-conqueror (illuss. 86–87):** An incised depiction from the Egyptian-Canaanite temple on the tell shows a god holding a great lance over his head — "a figure that combines the Canaanite Baal (who defeats the sea serpent *litanu*/Leviathan) and the Egyptian Seth (who conquers the Apophis serpent)" (§45). A scarab from Grave 120 at Lachish shows Baal-Seth with bull's horns striking a horned snake. "By means of the combination of Baal and Seth as serpent conquerors, the serpent... became a symbol of danger in general. The god who could defeat such a creature is treated as a savior, pure and simple."
- **Reshef:** A cylinder seal from the Fosse Temple shows a god with shield and hand weapon — the attributes typical of Reshef (illus. 85a). Multiple scarabs confirm Reshef's presence at Lachish.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the syncretism pattern — the evidence is dense and converges across media. MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific identification of the Lachish pitcher's goddess as Asherah (the Ugaritic parallel for *rbt* is strong, but *ilt* can function as a generic designation, not always Atirat specifically — as Keel & Uehlinger note in n.12).

**What's at stake:** The Lachish evidence shows that southern Canaanite religion in the LB was neither purely Egyptian nor purely Canaanite — it was a genuine fusion. The qudshu goddess is a perfect example: her nakedness, frontal pose, and vegetation associations are Canaanite; her Hathor locks, lotus/papyrus stems, and lion-mount are Egyptian. This syncretism is the religious context in which early Israel emerged in the southern hill country. It also suggests that Asherah worship in Iron Age Israel/Judah had deep LB roots in the very region where Judah would later develop. The Baal-Seth "savior" figure is especially relevant: it shows a LB precedent for a god who conquers cosmic chaos, which Yahweh would later absorb.

**Who disagrees:** Cross (1973), Maier (1986), and Hadley (1989) identify the qudshu-type goddess with Atirat/Asherah — Keel & Uehlinger are more cautious, noting that *qdš* is an epithet for her sexual/erotic powers, not necessarily her name. Day (1986) and Smith (1990) differ on the Asherah identification. Ussishkin (1978) published the Lachish temple incised god but interpretations vary.

**Alternative reading:** The syncretism could be interpreted as Egyptian religious imperialism rather than genuine fusion — Egyptians imposing their iconographic norms on indigenous deities. Keel & Uehlinger seem to lean toward genuine fusion, noting that the qudshu goddess retains her Canaanite frontal nudity even when Egyptianized.

**My assessment:** The Lachish chapter provides the most direct pre-Israelite context for what would become Judah. The inscribed pitcher calling the tree-goddess "my lady Elat" is one of the closest material links between LB iconography and the Asherah polemics of the Hebrew Bible. The Baal-Seth fusion as "savior" from cosmic danger anticipates Yahweh's Chaoskampf mythology (Isaiah 27:1, Psalm 74:13–14, Job 26:12–13) and supports the view that Yahweh absorbed Baal's functions, not just El's. This aligns with Smith's argument in *The Early History of God* (Chapter 2) that Yahweh and Baal were initially in competition precisely because they were so similar.

## Claim 5: Beth-Shean shows the most extreme case of Egyptian state religion — the Amun cult, pharaoh ideology, and the near-total exclusion of goddesses from monumental art

**Authors' claim:** At Beth-Shean, "the indigenous tradition survives in the later strata only on inexpensive terra-cotta images, contrasting with the monumental art and image-bearing artifacts made with precious materials" which are "purely Egyptian, both in style and theme." (§48) On the Ramesses II monuments: "No goddess is to be seen on any of these royal monuments. It is equally remarkable how much the royal god Amun is emphasized at the time of Ramesses II." (§53)

**Evidence presented:**
- **Strategic location:** Beth-Shean controls the eastern entrance of the Jezreel Valley at the crossroads of major north-south and east-west routes — "this important strategic location was probably what made Beth-Shean an important Egyptian center from the fifteenth century on" (§48).
- **Mekal stele (illus. 102):** The enthroned "Mekal, the god of Beth-Shean" and the Egyptian architect Amen-em-Opet with his family — "fashioned in a pure Egyptian style." The local city god's image was made to "adapt very quickly to the new circumstances" under Egyptian pressure (§48).
- **Goddess in costly vs. cheap media:** Gold pendants and stelae (illuss. 106–108) show a goddess in Egyptian style — clothed, holding was-scepter and ankh, wearing atef-crown. Identified as "Anat, Mistress of Heaven, Mistress of the Gods" on one stele (illus. 108). But terra-cotta goddesses continue in indigenous Canaanite form: naked, holding breasts or children (§49). "The nakedness of the goddess on the terra-cottas is a typically Canaanite feature. The Canaanite nursing goddess is an indigenous form, not just a local adaptation of an Egyptian theme" (§49).
- **Cultic stands with goddess imagery (illuss. 104–105):** From the 12th century south temple at Beth-Shean — a goddess in a doorway holding doves, with a snake below; another fragmentary piece with naked seated figure, doves, lions, and snakes. "Snakes and doves are also found on cylindrical cultic stands... while doves and lions, as noted already at Middle Bronze Age Nahariyah and in the Late Bronze Age Fosse Temple of Lachish, continue to be made as terra-cotta figures" (§49).
- **Anat as warrior goddess (illus. 109–110):** A cylinder seal from Bethel shows Anat holding a lance, facing Baal-Seth. A Jerusalem-purchased scarab shows Anat on horseback, wearing atef-crown, swinging a scimitar over a fallen enemy. "Anat, who enjoyed a special popularity in Beth-Shean during the Late Bronze Age, was a warrior goddess from the outset" (§50).
- **Royal stelae of Sety I and Ramesses II:** The Sety I stele (2.45 m high) shows the pharaoh worshiping Re-harakhty; his fragmentary stele likely showed Ptah. The Ramesses II stele (2.67 m high) shows Amun presenting the scimitar of victory to the pharaoh — "the royal god Amun faces toward the right and presents to the pharaoh the scimitar, signifying divinely bestowed power for victory" (§51).
- **Ramesses II cylinder seal (illus. 113):** An Asiatic god (possibly Reshef-Mekal) presents the scimitar to the pharaoh — a rare fusion of Egyptian royal ideology with a local deity.
- **Amun dominance:** During the Eighteenth Dynasty, 22 of 38 double-sided plaques (58%) name Amun explicitly or cryptographically. In the Nineteenth Dynasty, Amun appears anthropomorphically, as a ram-headed god, or as a ram sphinx. A clay loaf of bread (ca. 10 cm diameter) stamped repeatedly with *Ymn-R'* suggests "a particularly strong Amun cult in Beth-Shean" (§53).
- **Official loyalty monuments:** A Megiddo plaque shows a vizier of Ramesses II revering Amun (illus. 118); a Megiddo ivory shows an official of Ramesses III kneeling before Amun (illus. 119). Door lintels from Beth-Shean show officials revering the king. A nearly complete seated statue of Ramesses III was found at Beth-Shean (§54).

**Confidence:** HIGH. Beth-Shean is the most extensively excavated Egyptian administrative center in Canaan, and the monumental evidence is unambiguous. MEDIUM on the Anat identification — Helck (1962) argued against identifying the unarmed goddess on the Beth-Shean stele as Anat (since she holds no weapons), but Keel & Uehlinger note that even on Ramesses II's monuments at Tanis, Anat appears without weapons.

**What's at stake:** Beth-Shean is a laboratory for what Egyptian colonialism did to indigenous religion. The split between monumental (Egyptian, male-dominated) and terra-cotta (indigenous, goddess-inclusive) religious expression is starkest here. If this split was typical of Egyptian-controlled cities, then the "official religion" of Late Bronze Age Canaan was essentially the Egyptian state cult — Amun, Re-harakhty, Ptah, the divine pharaoh — while the indigenous gods and goddesses survived in domestic, private, and cheaper forms. This is the archaeological precedent for Dever's argument that "book religion" (official, Yahwistic, aniconic) and "folk religion" (domestic, goddess-inclusive, iconic) coexisted throughout the monarchy.

**Who disagrees:** Helck (1962) disputes the Anat identification on the stele, arguing she's a local Beth-Shean goddess. Thompson (1970) studied Mekal as the local city god. Rowe's original excavation reports (1930, 1940) interpreted the finds differently, with less emphasis on the Egyptian-colonial framework.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of goddesses from royal monuments could reflect Egyptian royal ideology (which was always male-focused) rather than a deliberate suppression of goddess worship. The pharaoh's relationship with Amun and Re-harakhty was a theological-political program that didn't necessarily displace local cults — it just didn't depict them on royal monuments. The fact that the Anat stele and goddess cultic stands *do* exist at Beth-Shean supports this: goddess worship wasn't suppressed, it just wasn't "state religion."

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger's argument for a split between official and private religion is strongest at Beth-Shean. The contrast between the monumental stelae (all male gods and pharaoh) and the terra-cotta cultic stands (goddess with doves, lions, snakes) is so stark that it can't be accidental. However, the Anat stele complicates the picture — if a goddess *could* be depicted on a stele in Egyptian style, why is she absent from the royal monuments? The answer may be that the royal monuments are specifically about the pharaoh's relationship with the gods who legitimate his rule (Amun, Re-harakhty, Ptah), while Anat belonged to a different sphere (warfare, local protection). For the Truth Project, the key takeaway is that the gender of divinity in official/public religion was already heavily masculine in the LB — well before Yahwism's emergence. The Hebrew Bible's male monotheism didn't invent the suppression of goddesses; it continued a LB trajectory.

## Claim 6 (Excursus): The so-called "Astarte Plaques" are depictions of a goddess, not Egyptian "concubine" figures or mortal women on beds — and they represent the survival of indigenous goddess worship in domestic, affordable, female-centered piety

**Authors' claim:** "Decisive help is provided by the iconographic context in which one finds similar images of the naked figures that are depicted on terra-cotta... The 'naked woman' on the 'Astarte plaques' is a 'naked goddess.'" (§57) And: "The modern question about whether a figure represents a goddess or a woman, posing strict alternatives, is too short-sighted. The plaques portray the goddess as a woman, and conversely they identify the female worshipers above the plaques with the goddess." (§60)

**Evidence presented:**
- **Tadmor's "concubine" thesis (1982):** Miriam Tadmor argued that these plaques represent women lying on beds (rectangular shape = bed), are analogous to Egyptian "concubine" figurines, and belong to funerary/mortuary contexts. Helga Weippert (1988) and others (Lipinski 1986, Hadley 1989) have followed her.
- **Rebuttal (a) — Typology:** Most plaques have a "long, extended oval shape" — interpreting these as beds is "improbable." Neither the flat-pressed back side nor the oval lower end "prove conclusively that these did not originally stand on end, but must have lain flat." The qudshu plaques (which Tadmor and Weippert *accept* as goddesses) come in the same shape. "One might just as easily argue that the naked woman on the 'Astarte plaques' is portrayed standing, not lying."
- **Rebuttal (b) — Archaeological context:** "The vast majority of the published plaques were found in living quarters." At Gezer, at most 4 of 26+ LB plaques came from graves. "Approximately 80% of the Late Bronze Age graves contained no plaques at all." At Lachish, with dozens of LB graves (some impressive family graves with multiple burials), only two plaques were found. "It is thus hardly in keeping with the facts to designate the plaques 'chiefly as objects associated with mortuary beliefs and funerary practices.'"
- **Rebuttal (c) — Iconographic context:** The Tell Qasile shrine plaque (illus. 124, Iron Age IB, late 11th c.) is decisive: a rectangular terra-cotta (36.6 × 25 cm) found on the temple floor imitates a shrine facade with pilasters and cornice. Two standing female figures (naked legs extant, feet pointing forward naturally) are positioned frontally inside. "To represent naked female figures either in front of or actually inside a sanctuary shows clearly that they are to be interpreted as (twin?) goddesses associated with this shrine." Later Iron Age II parallels from Egypt and southern Palestine (Mazar 1985b) show one or two naked women with arms on thighs, feet forward, positioned in front of shrine facades. "The architectural elements clearly argue against" the woman-on-couch interpretation.
- **Shrine models and cultic stands:** Naked female figures appear in duplicate or multiples on LB and early Iron Age shrine models, clay towers, and cultic stands from both Syria and Palestine. The Pella cultic stand (illus. 126, transitional Iron I/IIA) shows two mold-made figures with arms extended down, "standing on feline heads" flanking a window — combining the "Astarte plaque" posture with the lion attribute of the qudshu type, showing they "cannot be treated as completely separate entities."
- **Gravidae flasks (illuss. 127–128):** Small pitchers shaped like the female body, filled with oil to prevent stretch marks during pregnancy, found from LB II through Iron IIA. A medicinal vessel from Tell Qasile Str. XI has holes in both breasts through which liquid could be poured. "This renders more plausible the possibility... that the presentation of the breasts on the plaques could be understood as an offering of, or the pressing out of, the mother's milk" (§59).
- **Gender association:** On shrine plaque parallels with a smaller figure alongside the goddess, the smaller figure is always female — "probably a worshiper or donor." "No evidence of males in this posture has been found, showing that the goddess was first and foremost an identification figure for women" (§60). A mold from Tall Munbaqa in northern Syria is inscribed on the back with *sinništu(m)* "woman" — "probably to be understood as identifying the goddess as an exemplary woman" (§60).

**Confidence:** HIGH that the plaques represent goddesses, not mortal women or concubines. The typological, archaeological, and iconographic arguments form a convergent case. MEDIUM on the specific identity of the goddess (Asherah? Anat? Astarte?) — Keel & Uehlinger are appropriately cautious: "it is not possible to associate her with any one particular name" though they suggest Asherah, Anat, or Astarte are all possible (§58).

**What's at stake:** This Excursus is directly relevant to one of the central debates in the Truth Project: the interpretation of Judean Pillar Figurines (JPFs). Dever argues that JPFs from 8th–6th century Judah represent Asherah and are evidence of persistent goddess worship in domestic settings despite official Yahwistic monolatry. Keel & Uehlinger's Excursus provides the *prehistory* of these figurines — showing that the "naked goddess" tradition runs continuously from MB IIB metal figurines, through LB "Astarte plaques," into Iron Age I cultic stands and shrine models, and eventually into Iron Age II JPFs. If the LB plaques are goddesses (not concubines), the typological continuity to JPFs is much stronger.

Furthermore, the Excursus anticipates Dever's domestic-vs-official religion framework by nearly a decade (the German original appeared in 1992). Keel & Uehlinger argue that the shift from expensive metal goddess figurines to cheap terra-cotta plaques represents a shift from public/temple religion to "private piety" and "the little cultic centers that were set up in each home" (§56). This is precisely Dever's argument about JPFs, and Keel & Uehlinger provide the LB and Iron I background that Dever's Iron II-focused book lacks.

**Who disagrees:** Tadmor (1982) argued for the "concubine" interpretation and mortuary function. Helga Weippert (1988, 1990) follows Tadmor. Hadley (1989, 224–233) accepted Tadmor's interpretation. Pritchard (1943) coined the term "Astarte plaques" but his corpus is outdated. Winter (1983) provided the most comprehensive typology but interpreted the "Naked Woman" in the context of Old Syrian glyptic art.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the plaques are goddesses rather than concubines, the specific *function* could vary. Some may have been votive offerings, some may have been used in healing/fertility rituals (as the Gravidae flasks suggest), and some may have had mortuary functions (the minority found in graves). The interpretation doesn't need to be all-or-nothing. Tadmor's mortuary thesis fails for the majority of finds but may apply to a subset. The more important point is that these are divine, not human, figures.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger's rebuttal of Tadmor is devastating and largely persuasive. The three-pronged argument (typology, context, iconographic parallels) leaves little room for the "concubine" thesis. The Tell Qasile shrine plaque is the smoking gun: naked female figures inside a shrine — if these aren't goddesses, what are they? The modern either/or question is indeed "too short-sighted" — the plaques *both* depict the goddess and identify the female worshiper with her.

This Excursus is essential reading alongside Dever's Chapter V. Dever argues that JPFs represent Asherah and that the textual scholars (like Smith) have ignored this evidence. Keel & Uehlinger provide the deep history showing that Dever's JPFs are not a late Iron Age innovation but the latest phase of a tradition stretching back 700+ years. The key difference between Keel & Uehlinger and Dever is that Keel & Uehlinger are more cautious about naming the goddess (she could be Asherah, Anat, or Astarte — or the plural "Astartes" as a generic category), while Dever is more confident in the Asherah identification.

For the Truth Project, this Excursus has two major implications:
1. **Continuity argument:** If the naked goddess tradition runs unbroken from MB IIB through the Iron Age, then Asherah worship in monarchic Judah wasn't a late pagan corruption of a previously pure Yahwism — it was the indigenous default that Yahwism had to struggle against from the beginning.
2. **Gender argument:** The goddess was "first and foremost an identification figure for women" (§60). The suppression of goddess worship in official religion (as seen at Beth-Shean and Megiddo) and the eventual eradication of goddess imagery in Yahwistic orthodoxy can be read as a suppression of women's religious agency — a theme Keel & Uehlinger only hint at but Dever develops more fully.

## Claim 7: The LB witnessed a radical split between "official" religion (male warrior/political deities in costly media) and "private" religion (the naked goddess in cheap terra-cotta) — a split driven by Egyptian colonialism that would shape Israelite religion

**Authors' claim:** "During this era, which witnessed the increasing suppression of the female god(s) within Late Bronze Age 'official' religion, as represented in the great temples, in the Egyptian city-state and colonial power structure, and in products manufactured in the workshops that were to spread these ideas, there was concurrently a huge increase in the production of inexpensive terra-cotta plaques depicting a 'naked goddess,' clearly showing that the traditions connected with the Middle Bronze Age goddess continued to hold sway." (§56)

**Evidence presented (summary of entire Part II):**
- Costly metal figurines of goddesses virtually cease production; metal is now used for warrior and enthroned male deities (Baal, Reshef, the pharaoh)
- Monumental art (stelae, temple decoration) is dominated by Egyptian royal theology: Amun, Re-harakhty, Ptah, the divine pharaoh in battle/victory scenes
- Seal amulets (the most common image-bearing artifacts) are "almost exclusively male" — goddesses appear "only in the form of a fetish (Hathor) or as part of a king's name (Ma'at)" (§46)
- Meanwhile, "inexpensive terra-cotta plaques depicting a 'naked goddess'" proliferate — produced by the thousands, found overwhelmingly in domestic contexts
- Male deities "are hardly represented in terra-cotta" (§56)
- The goddess's iconography shifts: she is "shown as the mistress of the city (Beth-Shean) or as a warrior deity... in a role that corresponds more closely to the 'masculine' attributes that would be associated with a power-woman" (§55)
- "Surprisingly, male deities are hardly represented in terra-cotta" — terra-cotta is the medium of the goddess
- "The change from expensive to easily affordable material carried with it the assumption that the goddess had now entered the realm of what some call a 'private piety' and that the goddess was more approachable than ever before in the little cultic centers that were set up in each home" (§56)

**Confidence:** HIGH that a split existed between costly/monumental and cheap/domestic media and that the former is male-dominated, the latter goddess-dominated. MEDIUM on whether this represents a deliberate "suppression" vs. a natural consequence of Egyptian colonial administration (which simply didn't care about local domestic cults so long as public monuments displayed loyalty).

**What's at stake:** This is the archaeological precedent for everything that follows. The split between official Yahwism (male, aniconic, Jerusalem-centered, text-based) and popular religion (goddess-inclusive, iconic, domestic, practice-based) that Dever documents for Iron Age II Judah has its roots in the LB colonial situation. Egyptian colonialism didn't just suppress goddesses — it created the very category of "official religion" as distinct from "popular religion" by imposing a state cult that was indifferent to domestic practice.

Furthermore, if this split was already in place during the LB, then the emergence of Israel in Iron Age I did not create the conditions for goddess suppression — it inherited them. Yahweh's rise to exclusive worship in the Iron Age occurred in a religious landscape where the goddess had already been relegated to the domestic sphere for centuries. The Deuteronomic reformers, Josiah, and the biblical prophets were attacking a goddess tradition that had been pushed underground long before Israel existed.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize textual evidence (Smith 1990, de Moor 1990) tend to treat LB Canaanite religion as a unified pantheon. The Amarna letters and Ugaritic texts give a picture of El, Baal, Asherah, Anat, and Astarte as an integrated divine family — but Keel & Uehlinger's material evidence shows that at ground level in Palestine, what was actually practiced varied enormously by city, region, and social class. Text-based reconstructions can flatten this diversity.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that Keel & Uehlinger overstate the split. The Anat stele at Beth-Shean shows a goddess in costly monumental form. The qudshu goddess appears on gold leaf and Egyptian-style stelae. The goddess wasn't *only* in terra-cotta — she just predominated there. Conversely, the Mitannian seals at Hazor and Megiddo show that celestial/astral themes entered through costly glyptic. The reality may be more of a spectrum than a binary split.

**My assessment:** The split is real and important, but Keel & Uehlinger's own evidence shows it's not absolute. The Beth-Shean Anat stele and the Lachish gold-leaf goddess-on-horse show that goddesses could still appear in costly media when they adopted "masculine" traits (warfare, city-mistress roles). This suggests the split was partly about *what kind* of goddess was acceptable in official contexts, not just whether goddesses were permitted at all. The nurturing, naked, fertility-focused goddess was pushed into domestic terra-cotta; the warrior goddess (Anat) and the Egyptianized city-mistress could still appear on monuments. This nuance matters for understanding Asherah in the Iron Age: she appears in the Hebrew Bible as both a fertility symbol (the *asherim* poles) and a divine consort ("Yahweh and his Asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud), suggesting she too straddled the official/private divide.

### Key takeaways for the Truth Project:

1. **Yahweh emerged in a landscape already dominated by male warrior gods.** Baal, Reshef, Seth-Baal, Amun, and the divine pharaoh all competed for supremacy in LB Palestine. Yahweh's character as a divine warrior (Exodus 15, Deuteronomy 33) was not an innovation — it was Yahweh's entry into a role already occupied by multiple competitors.

2. **The goddess was already marginalized in official/public religion before Israel existed.** This challenges any narrative that presents pre-Yahwistic Canaan as a goddess-centered paradise. The material evidence shows that Egyptian colonialism had already driven the nurturing goddess into domestic, cheap, "private" forms by the 13th century.

3. **Continuity, not rupture, characterizes the MB→LB→Iron Age transition in goddess worship.** The same naked-goddess-with-breasts type appears in MB metal, LB terra-cotta, and Iron II Judean Pillar Figurines. Dever's JPFs are the latest phase of a 700+ year tradition. This makes it harder to argue that Asherah worship was a late "pagan corruption" of Yahwism — it was the indigenous default.

4. **Regional diversity is essential.** Hazor (northern Syrian), Megiddo (mixed), Lachish (Egyptian-Canaanite fusion), and Beth-Shean (Egyptian state cult) each represent different religious subsystems. The hill country where Israel emerged was likely its own subsystem, not a direct continuation of any one of these.

5. **The name of the goddess matters less than her function.** Keel & Uehlinger's caution about naming the "Astarte plaque" goddess (she could be Asherah, Anat, or Astarte — or all three) is methodologically sound. The iconographic evidence reveals what the goddess *did* (fertility, protection, childbirth, nursing, erotic blessing) more reliably than it reveals what she was *called* in any particular place and time.

6. **The methodological integration of iconography and text is still incomplete.** Keel & Uehlinger provide the comprehensive iconographic evidence that Smith, de Moor, and other text-based scholars lack. But they also acknowledge that iconography and texts "belong to completely different functional spheres of religion" (§58 n.49). The Truth Project needs both — iconography for what people actually did, texts for what the literate élite thought should be done.
