# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 2-3

---

## Chapter 2: The Geographic Origin of Yhwh

### Claim: Yhwh originated in the southern deserts, not in Canaan

**Römer's claim:** "The oldest attested occurrences of Yhwh are from the south of Palestine, the territory of Edom and Araba." This is supported by Egyptian epigraphic evidence, biblical poetry, and archaeological context. Yhwh was a southern desert/war god before he was the god of Israel.

**Evidence presented — the four proposed origins Römer evaluates:**

| Site | Evidence | Römer's Verdict |
|------|----------|----------------|
| **Ebla** (Syria, 3rd millennium) | Personal names ending in *-ya* | **False.** The *-ya* is a hypocoristic (diminutive) ending, not a divine name. No god Yhwh appears in Ebla offering lists. |
| **Ugarit** (Syria, 13th c.) | Fragmentary text (KTU 1.1) mentioning "YW" | **Inconclusive.** Could be Yhwh, could be Yam (Sea god), could be a scribal error. "We cannot therefore definitively exclude a link between Yw and Yhwh, but this passage is too fragmentary and unclear." |
| **Mari** (Euphrates, 2nd millennium) | Proper names like *Yaḫwi-ilum* | **False.** The root means "to manifest oneself" — a verb, not a divine name. "Cannot be linked with Yhwh." |
| **Egypt/Soleb** (Sudan, 14th c. BCE) | Inscriptions of Amenophis III and Ramses II listing "the Shasu of Yhw(h)" | **The strongest evidence.** "Yhwʒ seems to be a geographic term (referring to a mountain?) and perhaps also a divine name." |

**The Soleb evidence — the smoking gun:**
- Amenophis III (c. 1370 BCE) at Soleb in Sudan: a list of Shasu nomad territories includes "The country of the Shasu of Yhw(h)"
- Same designation repeated in Ramses II's temple at West Amara
- The Shasu are located in the Negev, Seir, Edom — "the south"
- Papyrus Anastasi VI mentions "Shasu of Edom" being allowed to sojourn in Egypt with their herds
- Papyrus Harris I (c. 1150): Pharaoh Ramses IV boasts "I destroyed the people of Seir among the Shasu tribes"
- Relief at Karnak (Seti I, c. 1290-1280): depicts Shasu with distinctive goatees and hairbands
- The Shasu were involved in copper mining in the Araba valley (Timna) — this was a real economic context for these people

> This is the earliest plausible attestation of Yhwh anywhere. 14th century BCE. Southern Transjordan. A god associated with nomadic Shasu tribes.

**The biblical evidence — four poems describing Yhwh "coming from the south":**

1. **Deuteronomy 33:2:** "Yhwh came from Sinai, he shone forth from Seir, he was resplendent from Mount Paran"
2. **Judges 5:4-5:** "Yhwh, when you came forth out of Seir, when you advanced from the land of Edom, the earth trembled"
3. **Psalm 68:8-9, 18:** "O God, when you came forth at the head of your people, when you advanced over the arid land... the earth trembled"
4. **Habakkuk 3:3:** "God comes from Teman, the Holy One comes from Mount Paran"

Römer notes these are all WAR poetry — Yhwh is described as a warrior god advancing from his southern home to fight for Israel. The texts of Judges 5 and Psalm 68 are so close they appear to be the same poem in two versions. Römer argues Judges 5 is the older version and Psalm 68 reworks it.

**The strange phrase *zeh sînay*:** This appears in both Judges 5:5 and Psalm 68:9. The grammar is odd — literally "this Sinai." Römer discusses various interpretations: Is Sinai a divine name (a god, not just a mountain)? Is it a scribal gloss? He doesn't resolve it definitively but notes it's one more piece of the puzzle.

**The dating problem:** Römer acknowledges that Judges 5's date "varies from the twelfth century to the Hellenistic era" among scholars. The Hebrew is either very archaic or consciously archaizing. There were "several stages in its composition and its final revision." This is the same dating problem that haunts Smith — but Römer is more willing to accept an early date for the core of the poem.

**The copper connection:** Römer notes that Timna (in the Araba valley) was a major copper mining center in the 14th-12th centuries. The Shasu were involved in this industry. This provides an economic context for why a god from this region might travel — trade routes, not just nomads.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the Soleb inscriptions are the earliest attestation of Yhwh. MEDIUM-HIGH that the biblical poems preserve a genuine tradition of southern origin. MEDIUM on the copper/trade connection as mechanism of transmission.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Yhwh originated in Edom/Midian, then the God of Israel is geographically foreign to Israel. The Bible's own story (God reveals himself in Egypt and the wilderness) preserves a distorted memory of this foreign origin. But the theological implication is sharper than Smith made it: Yhwh wasn't just one god among many in Canaan. He wasn't FROM Canaan at all. He was an import — a desert god adopted by highland tribes who then rewrote their own history to make him seem indigenous.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a Canaanite origin of Yhwh. The Ugaritic fragment (YW) is their best evidence, but Römer considers it too fragmentary. Scholars who date Judges 5 to the Hellenistic period would say the "southern origin" poems are late theological constructs, not historical memory.

**Alternative reading:** The southern origin poems could be late theological retrojections — scribes in the Persian period creating a mythic origin story for a god who was actually indigenous to Canaan. The Soleb inscriptions prove Yhwh was KNOWN in the south, not that he ORIGINATED there. He might have been a Canaanite god whose cult spread south and was later re-imported.

**My assessment:** Römer's chapter is a model of accessible scholarship. He evaluates each piece of evidence honestly, discards the weak claims (Ebla, Mari), flags the uncertain (Ugarit), and builds the case around the strongest evidence (Soleb + biblical poetry). His conclusion is appropriately cautious: "The archaeological, epigraphic, and iconographic evidence all place the Shasu in the territory of Edom or Seir... and among these Shasu there might have been a group whose tutelary god was called 'Yhw.'" The word "might" is doing honest work.

## Chapter 3: Moses and the Midianites

### Claim: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is the best explanation for how Yhwh moved from the southern deserts to Israel

**Römer's claim:** Moses' connection to Midian (his father-in-law Jethro was a Midianite priest) preserves a historical memory of how Yhwh worship was transmitted to proto-Israel. The Midianites/Kenites were the bridge between the southern Yhwh of the Shasu and the Israelite Yhwh.

**Evidence presented:**

**On Moses — a constructed figure with historical kernels:**
- "Moses is a figure of tradition, of whom, however, there are no historical traces" (quoting Assmann). "The only thing we know about the historical Moses is that he died."
- But his NAME is Egyptian: derived from Egyptian *msj* ("to engender, beget"), as in "Ramses" = "Ra has begot him." The Hebrew rendering uses *šin* rather than *sāmek*, suggesting an early transcription — "the name Moses takes us perhaps back to the second millennium."
- Two possible Egyptian historical parallels: Beya (a Canaanite chancellor under Seti II who led a revolt of Asiatics, possibly executed) and Ben-Ozen (a Semite mediator between Shasu laborers and Egyptian overseers under Ramses II). Neither is a perfect match. Moses is "a 'construct' in which memory traces of various different historical events and figures are combined."

**On the Midianites — who were they?**
- The name "Midian" may mean "extended territory" — long valleys, suitable for nomads
- 1 Kings 11 mentions Midian as a country between Edom and Paran, accessible from the south
- The Midianites were a nomadic or semi-nomadic group in northwestern Arabia/southern Transjordan
- They appear in the biblical narrative as both allies and enemies of Israel
- Archaeological evidence for Midian is sparse — they were nomads who left few permanent settlements

**The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis:**
- Moses flees Egypt, marries Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, "priest of Midian" (Exodus 2-3)
- Jethro already worships Yhwh and offers sacrifice to him (Exodus 18)
- This would mean Yhwh was ALREADY worshipped by Midianites before Moses encountered him
- The Kenites (a Midianite subgroup) appear as metalworkers associated with Israel (Judges 1:16, 4:11)
- The hypothesis: Yhwh was a Midianite/Kenite god → Moses encountered him through his Midianite father-in-law → Moses brought Yhwh worship to the Israelites

**Römer's assessment of the hypothesis:**
- The biblical narrative preserves a "historical recollection of the importance of the Midianites and of some kind of close contact between them and Moses"
- The hypothesis "provides the best explanation currently available of the relevant literary and archaeological data" (citing Blenkinsopp)
- BUT: the biblical narratives about Moses and Midian are "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance" — they're literary constructions, not straightforward history

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The hypothesis elegantly connects the Soleb evidence (Chapter 2) with the Moses narrative (Chapter 3). But it depends entirely on the biblical text preserving a genuine memory, which Römer himself acknowledges is "very difficult to reconstruct." The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is plausible, elegant, and underdetermined by the evidence.

**What's at stake for faith:** If this is correct, Moses didn't receive a revelation from a previously unknown God. He encountered a god his Midianite father-in-law already worshipped. Yhwh wasn't revealed to Israel through Moses — Moses brought Yhwh TO Israel from Midian. "Revelation" becomes "cultural transmission."

**Who disagrees:** Critics of the Kenite hypothesis (Tebes, 2021, for archaeological reasons). Scholars who see the Jethro narrative as a late literary construct with no historical value. Scholars who argue Yhwh was indigenous to Canaan.

**Alternative reading:** The Jethro/Midian connection is a LITERARY device — the biblical author uses Midian to explain how a foreign god (Yhwh) became Israel's god. The story preserves the memory of foreign origin while domesticating it ("Moses' father-in-law was a Midianite priest, therefore Yhwh is in the family"). The story explains a problem, not a history.

**My assessment:** This is where Römer's "traces of memory" methodology is most contested. The Kenite hypothesis is elegant but fragile. If Schmid is right that the Pentateuch is Persian-period, then the Jethro narrative tells us nothing about the 13th century BCE. Römer's willingness to use these texts as historical sources is what distinguishes him from the minimalist camp — but it's also his greatest vulnerability.

## Cross-Cutting: Römer's Narrative Arc Through Chapter 3

Römer is building a geographic narrative:
1. Yhwh originates in the southern deserts (Ch. 2) — Soleb, Shasu, Seir/Edom
2. Moses encounters Yhwh through Midianite intermediaries (Ch. 3) — Jethro, the Kenites
3. Yhwh is brought north to Israel (Ch. 4, upcoming) — the mechanism of transmission

This is a cleaner story than Smith's more cautious, evidence-by-evidence approach. Römer tells you what probably happened. Smith tells you what the evidence allows you to say. Both approaches have value — Römer is more readable; Smith is more methodologically honest about uncertainty.
