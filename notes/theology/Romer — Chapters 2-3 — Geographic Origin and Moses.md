# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 2-3

---

## Chapter 2: The Geographic Origin of Yhwh

### Claim: Yhwh originated in the southern deserts, not in Canaan
→ [[claim-yhwh-southern-desert-origin-soleb-poetry-synthesis]] | **romer-inv-2-3.1** | Confidence: high
  Romer evaluates four proposed origins (Ebla, Ugarit, Mari, Egypt/Soleb) and eliminates the first three. The Soleb inscriptions (14th c. BCE) listing "Shasu of Yhw(h)" in Seir/Edom are the strongest evidence. Four biblical war poems (Deut 33:2, Judg 5:4-5, Ps 68, Hab 3:3) independently preserve a southern origin tradition. Yhwh was a southern desert god adopted by Israel.
  Key edges: Depends on [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]] and [[claim-four-biblical-poems-preserve-southern-origin-tradition]]. Supports [[claim-kenite-midianite-transmission-bridge-synthesis]], [[claim-yahweh-originated-south-midian-edom]], [[claim-yahweh-southern-storm-warrior-origin]].

## Chapter 3: Moses and the Midianites

### Claim: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is the best explanation for how Yhwh moved from the southern deserts to Israel
→ [[claim-kenite-midianite-transmission-bridge-synthesis]] | **romer-inv-2-3.2** | Confidence: medium
  Moses' connection to Midian (marrying Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, a Midianite priest who already worshipped Yhwh) preserves a historical memory of Yhwh's transmission. The Midianites/Kenites were the bridge between the southern Shasu Yhwh and Israelite Yhwh. Elegant, plausible, underdetermined.
  Key edges: Depends on [[claim-yhwh-southern-desert-origin-soleb-poetry-synthesis]], [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]], [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]]. Supports [[claim-bible-acknowledges-yhwh-foreign-origin]].

## Cross-Cutting: Römer's Narrative Arc Through Chapter 3

Römer is building a geographic narrative:
1. Yhwh originates in the southern deserts (Ch. 2) — Soleb, Shasu, Seir/Edom
2. Moses encounters Yhwh through Midianite intermediaries (Ch. 3) — Jethro, the Kenites
3. Yhwh is brought north to Israel (Ch. 4, upcoming) — the mechanism of transmission

This is a cleaner story than Smith's more cautious, evidence-by-evidence approach. Römer tells you what probably happened. Smith tells you what the evidence allows you to say. Both approaches have value — Römer is more readable; Smith is more methodologically honest about uncertainty.
