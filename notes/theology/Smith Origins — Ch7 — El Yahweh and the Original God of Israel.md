# Smith — Ch7: El, Yahweh, and the Original God of Israel

This is THE pivotal chapter of the book — the hinge between Part II (Characteristics of Deities) and Part III (Origins of Monotheism). Smith makes the case that El, not Yahweh, was the original god of Israel, that Yahweh was a southern (Edomite/Midianite) warrior-storm god who was secondarily assimilated into El's identity, and that this "convergence" paradigm replaced the older Near Eastern "succession" paradigm (son replaces father). The chapter also raises — and largely endorses — the explosive possibility that El, not Yahweh, was the original god of the Exodus. Seven sections: El in the Bronze Age, Iron Age Phoenicia, Iron Age Aram/Transjordan, Iron Age Israel, whether El was Israel's original god, Yahweh's original character, and El as god of the Exodus.

---

## Claim 1: El's cult persisted into the Iron Age across the Levant — van der Toorn's "deus otiosus" thesis is overstated

**Author's claim:** The demise of El among Israel's neighbors has been claimed most recently by K. van der Toorn ("El is a common Northwest Semitic god to whom the devotion is largely rhetorical in the first millennium BCE. Having turned into a deus otiosus, his place was gradually taken by Baal-shamem"), but the evidence is more ambiguous than this narrative allows. B. A. Levine and Cross defend the position that El's cult perdured.

**Evidence presented — Phoenicia:**
- Karatepe inscription: title 'l qn 'rṣ ("El creator of the earth"), KAI 26A III:18
- Neo-Punic inscription KAI 129:1 with same title
- Hellenistic period Umm el-'Awamid inscription with name El
- Philo of Byblos attests to Phoenician El — Ouranos's children included Elos (West Semitic El), Baetylos, Dagon, Atlas
- Ezekiel 28 describes Tyrian El in terms similar to Ugaritic descriptions of El's abode; wisdom ascribed to Tyrian El recalls El in Ugaritic texts
- M. L. Barré: Phoenician Bethel = hypostasis of El (though van der Toorn challenges this, arguing Bethel is Aramean)
- Baal Hamon may be a title of El (Landsberger, Cross, Olyan). Iconography comports with El at Ugarit. Cross sees ḥmn as Mount Amanus (El's home). But this identification is disputed — Day notes Kronos was equated with both El AND Baal; iconographic similarity is no guarantee. Lipiński calls it "sheer fantasy." Yet: Phoenician seal p'rhmn (8th century) likely = Pagras in the Amanus, lending credence to ḥmn = Amanus.
- Roman emperor Elegabalus (203-222 CE) — Stager takes this as Latinized 'El Jebel ("El of the mountain"), an identification Smith finds "viable but not necessary."

**Evidence presented — Aram/Transjordan:**
- Panammu of Samal lists El among deities (KAI 214:1, 2, 11, 18)
- Sefire treaty includes El as divine witness (KAI 222A:11)
- Deir 'Alla inscriptions — Levine argues they show an El cult separate from Yahweh in the Iron Age. But genre and date are disputed: if these are later copies of older texts, they can't serve as Iron II evidence.
- Papyrus Amherst 63: El appears in a prayer with parallels to Psalm 22 — this implies a cult, "perhaps even in Israel."
- Edomite and Ammonite onomastica dominated by 'l theophoric element (Cross, Tigay, Aufrecht) — suggests El's cult.
- South Arabian epigraphic texts also attest to El — "might warrant caution against dismissing first-millennium Levantine evidence."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The evidence is genuinely sparse and scattered, and Smith is appropriately cautious. But the cumulative weight — across Phoenicia, Aram, Transjordan, Edom, Ammon, and possibly South Arabia — makes van der Toorn's sweeping dismissal harder to sustain.

**What's at stake:** If El's cult was alive and well in the Iron Age Levant outside Israel, the biblical absorption of El into Yahweh was not the natural end of a dying god but an active theological choice made within Israel. El was a live religious option that Israel deliberately incorporated.

**Who disagrees:** Van der Toorn (El as deus otiosus), Lipiński (Baal Hamon ≠ El), Day (Kronos = both El and Baal, so Philo is ambiguous).

**Alternative reading:** Van der Toorn could be largely right for the Phoenician/Punic city-states where Baal-shamem DID replace El as chief deity. Smith's evidence may show El was KNOWN but not necessarily actively WORSHIPED as a living cult. Name survival ≠ cult survival.

**My assessment:** Smith walks a careful line — he doesn't claim El's cult was thriving, only that the evidence is too ambiguous to declare it dead. The papyrus Amherst 63 prayer is the strongest single piece of evidence because it places El in a cultic context near or within Israel. The onomastic evidence (Edomite/Ammonite) is suggestive but not probative. Smith's caution is appropriate: "the difficulty of evidence hardly settles the issue."

## Claim 2: El was the original deity at major Israelite sanctuaries — Shiloh, Shechem, and Jerusalem — later absorbed into Yahwistic cult

**Author's claim:** "At a variety of sites, Yahweh was incorporated into the older figure of El, who belonged to Israel's original West Semitic religious heritage." Specific sites show El language and characteristics that predate Yahwistic appropriation.

**Evidence presented — Shiloh:**
- C. L. Seow: El language and characteristics reflected in the cult of Shiloh
- Tent tradition at Shiloh (Psalm 78:60; Joshua 18:1; 1 Samuel 2:22) conforms to Ugaritic descriptions of El's abode as a tent
- Samuel's incubation-dream — divine appearance in dreams is an El characteristic
- Hannah's gift of a child — El as granter of progeny (Kirta parallel)
- Elqanah's name — suggests an El worshipper
- Shiloh called bêt 'ĕlōhîm ("house of God/El") in Judges 18:31
- Psalm 78 repeatedly uses El names and epithets in describing the rise and fall of the Shiloh sanctuary

**Evidence presented — Shechem:**
- Local god was 'ēl bĕrît ("El of the covenant") — Judges 9:46; cf. 8:33, 9:4
- The word 'ilbrt apparently appears as a Late Bronze Age title for El (CAT 1.128.14–15)
- In patriarchal narratives, the god of Shechem is called 'ĕlōhê yiśrā'ēl ("the god of Israel") and presumed to be Yahweh
- Process of reinterpretation: when Shechem's cult became Yahwistic, it continued El traditions; Yahweh received the title 'ēl bĕrît, the old title of El

**Evidence presented — Jerusalem:**
- Possible connection of El Elyon and El "creator of the earth" in Genesis 14:8–22 to Jerusalem
- "Israelite knowledge of the religious traditions about other deities did not only reflect contact between Israel and her Phoenician neighbors in the Iron Age... the old religious lore of El was inherited by the priesthood in Israel"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Shiloh evidence is particularly strong — the tent tradition, incubation-dream, and Elqanah's name form a coherent cluster. The Shechem case is also solid because the title 'ilbrt is attested at Late Bronze Age Ugarit.

**What's at stake:** If El was the original god at Israel's major sanctuaries, then Yahweh's presence there is secondary — a southern import that took over existing El cult sites. This inverts the traditional picture: it wasn't that El was "added" to Yahwism but that Yahweh was "added" to El worship.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain Yahweh was always the god of Israel from Sinai onward (Albright, de Moor, Propp). Those who read the patriarchal narratives as straightforward history rather than layered tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The El characteristics at these sites could reflect a shared West Semitic divine typology that Israelites applied to Yahweh FROM THE START — not evidence of an earlier El cult later Yahwized. The tent and dream traditions could be Israelite innovations borrowing from common stock.

**My assessment:** Smith's cumulative case is persuasive. The convergence of (1) Ugaritic parallels, (2) site-specific traditions, and (3) the name Israel itself (Claim 3) makes the "Yahweh was always there" position increasingly difficult to maintain. The Shiloh evidence is the strongest — Seow's study shows too many El-specific traits in one place for coincidence.

## Claim 3: The name "Israel" (yiśrā-'ēl) proves El was the original god — if Yahweh had been original, we'd expect *yiśrâ-yahweh or *yiśrâ-yāh

**Author's claim:** "If Yahweh had been the original god of Israel, then its name might have been *yiśrâ-yahweh, or perhaps better *yiśrâ-yāh in accordance with other Hebrew proper names containing the divine name. This fact would suggest that El not Yahweh was the original chief god of the group named Israel."

**Evidence presented:**
- The divine element in "Israel" is *'ēl — unambiguous
- Israel is a very old name, apparently known both at Ebla and Ugarit — thus predates the historical phenomenon of the Iron I highlands people
- "When the name began to refer to the historical phenomenon of a people in the Iron I highlands, perhaps it no longer referred to the god to whom it was devoted" — Smith acknowledges the name may have become detached from its original referent, but its very form is evidence of origins
- The distribution of El vs. Yahweh in personal names in early poems "likewise points in this direction"
- Smith concedes proper names "pose difficulties when used to reconstruct religious history, yet when used in conjunction with other evidence, proper names offer admissible evidence"
- Israelite inscriptions: 557 names with Yahweh, 77 with *'l — Tigay argues 'l is a title for Yahweh, not a separate deity. Smith doesn't deny this for the Iron II period but argues the NAME "Israel" preserves an earlier stage.

**Confidence:** HIGH as a suggestive datum, MEDIUM as a stand-alone proof. The name is a powerful piece of the cumulative case but not by itself dispositive.

**What's at stake:** This is the simplest, most elegant evidence in Smith's entire argument. You don't need Ugaritic, epigraphy, or source criticism — just look at the name. It's the kind of argument that makes you wonder why it isn't foregrounded more often in discussions of Israelite origins.

**Who disagrees:** Those who argue the name could be a sentence name with El as an appellative ("God strives") rather than the proper name of the deity El. Those who date the name "Israel" to a period AFTER El = Yahweh identification. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE) attests the name — but this doesn't tell us which god was referenced.

**Alternative reading:** The name "Israel" could have been coined when El and Yahweh were already identified — i.e., the name-bearers thought of 'ēl as a title for Yahweh. The etymology doesn't have to preserve the earliest stage of religion.

**My assessment:** Smith uses the name as one piece of a cumulative argument, not as a silver bullet. He explicitly notes that "when the name began to refer to the historical phenomenon of a people in the Iron I highlands, perhaps it no longer referred to the god to whom it was devoted." This is honest. But combined with the sanctuary evidence (Claim 2) and Deut 32:8-9 (Claim 4), the name becomes part of a triangulation that's hard to dismiss.

## Claim 4: Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (LXX/4QDeutj) preserves an older polytheistic theology — Yahweh was one of the "sons of El" who received Israel as his allotment

**Author's claim:** The Septuagint and Dead Sea Scroll (4QDeutj) versions of Deuteronomy 32:8-9 "cast Yahweh in the role of one of the divine sons, understood as fathered by El, called Elyon in the first line." The MT changed "sons of El" to "sons of Israel" — reflecting discomfort with the polytheistic implications.

**Evidence presented — the reconstructed text:**
> When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance,
> When He divided up humanity,
> He fixed the boundaries for peoples,
> According to the number of the divine sons:
> For Yahweh's portion is his people,
> Jacob His own inheritance.

- MT reads bĕnê yiśrā'ēl ("sons of Israel"); LXX reads ἀγγέλων θεοῦ ("angels of God"); 4QDeutj reads bny 'lwhm / bny 'l ("sons of God" / "sons of El")
- The MT reading makes no sense in context: how would the number of Israel's sons determine the boundaries of ALL peoples?
- The LXX/4Q reading aligns with a known ANE concept: the high god assigns nations to lesser gods
- "This passage...presents a cosmic order in which each deity received its own nation. Israel was the nation which Yahweh received, yet El was the head of this pantheon and Yahweh only one of its members."
- "This reading points to an old phase of Israel's religion when El held a pre-eminent position apart from the status of Yahweh."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is one of the most important text-critical discoveries for understanding early Israelite religion. The MT change is a classic example of theological correction. The LXX/4Q reading is clearly superior on text-critical grounds, and the theology it preserves aligns with what we know of West Semitic pantheon structures.

**What's at stake:** This single passage provides a snapshot of an early Israelite theology in which Yahweh was NOT the supreme god — El/Elyon was. It's as close to a smoking gun as we get in Hebrew Bible studies. If this reading is correct, Israelite monotheism was not an original feature but an innovation that required suppressing older traditions.

**Who disagrees:** Evangelical/conservative scholars who defend the MT reading. Those who argue the LXX translator misunderstood the Hebrew (though 4QDeutj now makes this harder to argue). Scholars who read the passage as poetic parallelism where Elyon and Yahweh are the same deity.

**Alternative reading:** The passage could be read with Elyon = Yahweh from the start — i.e., "the Most High" IS Yahweh, and the "divine sons" are his subordinate angels. The MT change would then be a clarification, not a correction. But this reading struggles with the structure: if Elyon = Yahweh, why does the text say Elyon allotted nations and Yahweh received Israel as HIS portion? If they're the same, the structure is redundant.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading is the consensus position in critical scholarship and for good reason. The text-critical evidence (4QDeutj) combined with the contextual logic (how do "sons of Israel" determine the boundaries of all peoples?) makes the MT reading indefensible as original. This passage is the keystone of Smith's argument that early Israel knew a distinction between El and Yahweh.

## Claim 5: A three-stage model accounts for the El-Yahweh relationship — convergence replaced succession

**Author's claim:** "We may posit three hypothetical stages (not necessarily discrete in time or geography)": (1) El as original god of early Israel, (2) El as head of pantheon with Yahweh as warrior-god member, (3) El and Yahweh identified as a single god. This "convergence" paradigm represents a departure from the older Near Eastern pattern of divine succession.

**Evidence presented — Stage 1 (El as original god):**
- The name Israel (Claim 3)
- References to El as a separate figure (Genesis 49, Psalm 82)
- El epithets in patriarchal narratives (El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam, El Bethel, El Roi)

**Stage 2 (El + warrior Yahweh in pantheon):**
- Texts mentioning both El and Yahweh but not as the same figure: Genesis 49:24-25 (El epithets separate from Yahweh in v.18); Numbers 23-24; Psalm 82
- Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (Claim 4) — Yahweh as one of the divine sons under Elyon
- If Psalm 82 reflects early Israelite polytheistic assembly: El as head, warrior Yahweh as member of second tier; other gods called "sons of the Most High" — Yahweh "might have been earlier understood as one of these sons"

**Stage 3 (Identification):**
- Merger predates the Song of Deborah (Judges 5), at least for the region where it was composed — Yahweh the divine warrior from the south is attributed victory in the central highlands
- "The merger probably took place at different rates in different parts of Israel"
- "Many scholars place the poem in the pre-monarchic period, and perhaps the cult of Yahweh spread further into the highlands of Israel in the pre-monarchic period infiltrating cult sites of El and accommodating to their El theologies"
- References to El in Numbers 23-24 and Job suggest "survival of El's cult in Transjordan" — i.e., the merger was incomplete in some regions

**The paradigm shift from succession to convergence:**
- Normal ANE pattern: son replaces father (Ea → Marduk in Enuma Elish; Baal overshadows El at Ugarit)
- Israel's innovation: son BECOMES the father — "Yahweh in ancient Israel and Baal at Ugarit were both outsider warrior gods who stood second in rank to El, but they eventually overshadowed him in power. Yet Yahweh's development went further. He was identified with El: here the son replaced and became the father whose name only serves as a title for the son."
- "The erasure of the father, with his transformation into the son, was a requisite condition for the monotheistic identity of the son."
- "With a distinct father-god erased, the son's identity as son was also erased. And then there was only One."
- When El and Yahweh are no longer distinguished, "there was no longer a triangle of relations, pitting the head god and his beloved monstrous enemies against the young warrior-god... El becomes Yahweh (or vice versa), and what stands at the end of the biblical convergence of divinity is one head god."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the broad outline, MEDIUM for the specific three stages. Smith acknowledges these are "hypothetical" and "not necessarily discrete in time or geography." The framework is useful and plausible, but the evidence for distinct sequential stages (rather than overlapping processes) is thin.

**What's at stake:** The convergence paradigm explains HOW monotheism emerged from within polytheism — not by denying other gods' existence (that comes later, in Part III) but by collapsing the distinction between father-god and son-god. Monotheism isn't a rejection of polytheism; it's a concentration of it.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Yahweh as El from the start (Albright, Cross, de Moor). Those who think the convergence happened much earlier (pre-Israelite) or much later (exilic). Scholars who argue Baal, not El, was the primary model for Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** The three-stage model may be too neat. The evidence could reflect regional variation (some tribes had El, others Yahweh) rather than temporal stages. The convergence could have been a one-time event rather than a process. Or the direction could be: Yahweh was original, and El characteristics were added to him (the inverse of Smith's model).

**My assessment:** The convergence paradigm is Smith's most creative contribution in this chapter. The contrast with Baal — who overshadowed El but was never IDENTIFIED with him — is crucial. Yahweh did what Baal never did: he absorbed the father's identity. That's the distinctive Israelite move. Smith's language about "erasure" of the father being a "requisite condition for monotheistic identity" is genuinely insightful and has implications for how we understand the psychological/cultural dynamics of monotheism's emergence.

## Claim 6: Yahweh originated as a southern storm/warrior god (Edom/Midian/Teman/Seir/Sinai) — NOT as a title or manifestation of El

**Author's claim:** "According to many scholars, Yahweh originated at the southern sites of Seir/Edom/Teman/Sinai... located by many scholars today in the northwestern Arabian peninsula east of the Red Sea." This southern origin argues against identifying Yahweh as originally a title of El (contra Albright, Cross, Freedman, de Moor, Dijkstra, Wyatt).

**Evidence presented — biblical:**
- Deuteronomy 33:2: "Yahweh came from Sinai, and dawned from Seir upon us; he shone forth from Mount Paran"
- Judges 5:4-5: "Yahweh, when you went out from Seir, when you marched from the region of Edom..."
- Psalm 68:9, 18
- Habakkuk 3:3: "God came from Teman, and the Holy One from Mount Paran"
- These passages form a consistent geographical litany: Seir/Edom/Teman/Paran/Sinai = the far south

**Evidence presented — epigraphic:**
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscriptions attest to "Yahweh of Teman" — a southern shrine preserving inscriptions by visiting northerners

**Evidence presented — historical mechanism:**
- Judges 5:6 mentions trade as part of the problem leading to the conflict; verses 4-5 provide the litany of Yahweh's southern origins
- Judges 5:14: "From Ephraim came they whose roots are in Amalek" — a positive indication of kinship between Ephraim and Amalek (a southern group associated with Edom). "Such a neutral reference to Amalekites in Judges 5:14 has a ring of authenticity."
- Judges 12:15 places a "hill of the Amalekites" in Ephraim territory
- Judges 5:24 mentions Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite — Kenites placed by biblical tradition south of Judah, "precisely the area of ancient Yahwistic cult"
- "Perhaps trade, enhanced by some kinship ties, provided the mechanism by which a far southern tradition of the deity in Seir/Edom/Sinai/Teman/Midian came to be celebrated originally at northern sites such as Shiloh and Bethel."

**What was Yahweh's original character?**
- Battle and precipitation were likely original features — but the southern region has low rainfall, not propitious for a storm-god profile like Baal
- Judges 5:4-5 reflects a god that provides rains — but "does this rain necessarily reflect the standard repertoire of a coastal storm-god, or does the passage reflect the storm and flash floods of desert areas?"
- Smith suggests:"Yahweh's original character approximated the profile of Athtar, a warrior- and precipitation-producing god associated with mostly inland desert sites with less rainfall."
- "Perhaps this profile was rendered secondarily in the highlands in the local language and imagery associated with the coastal storm-god."
- Candid admission: "Part of the original profile of Yahweh may be permanently lost, especially if the earliest biblical sources reflect secondary developments in the history of this deity's profile."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the southern origin, MEDIUM for the Athtar comparison. The convergence of biblical and epigraphic evidence for a southern Yahwistic cult is strong. The Athtar suggestion is speculative but provocative.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh is a southern import, the entire "Yahweh as original god of all Israel" narrative collapses. Israel's national god wasn't indigenous to the highlands — he was an outsider who arrived through trade/kinship networks and took over existing El sanctuaries. This has major implications for how we understand Israelite ethnogenesis.

**Who disagrees:** Cross, Freedman, de Moor, Dijkstra, Wyatt — all argue Yahweh was originally a title or manifestation of El. Albright saw Yahweh as a form of El from the start. Mettinger accepts the southern origin.

**Alternative reading:** The southern origin texts could be literary-theological constructs rather than historical memory — i.e., Israel LATER associated Yahweh with the south for ideological reasons (the south as locus of divine revelation). The Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscription could reflect this same later tradition. The Kenite hypothesis (Midianite origins of Yahwism) remains contested.

**My assessment:** Smith's reconstruction of the trade/kinship mechanism is speculative but responsible — he's not claiming certainty, just pointing to suggestive data. The Athtar comparison is intriguing: it provides a model for how a southern desert deity with storm-warrior features could exist outside the Baal/El binary. But Smith's honesty about the limits of our knowledge ("part of the original profile of Yahweh may be permanently lost") is refreshing and should be a model for the field.

## Claim 7: El, not Yahweh, was likely the original god of the Exodus — Numbers 23-24 preserves this older tradition

**Author's claim:** "El may have been the original god connected with the Exodus from Egypt and...this event was secondarily associated with Yahweh when the two gods coalesced." The primary evidence comes from the Balaam oracles in Numbers 23-24.

**Evidence presented — Numbers 23-24:**
- Numbers 23:22 and 24:8 associate the Exodus NOT with Yahweh but with El: "El who freed them from Egypt has horns like a wild ox"
- The description "horns like a wild ox" evokes El's attribute animal at Ugarit — the ox, reflected in his title "Bull El"
- Name distribution in Numbers 23-24: Yahweh appears (23:8, 21; 24:6) but El appears nearly three times as often (23:8, 19, 22, 23; 24:4, 8, 16, 23)
- B. A. Levine: these poems "preserve an old repertoire of El tradition, now synthesized with references to Yahweh"

**Evidence presented — Egyptian names in Shilohite priesthood:**
- Moses, Phinehas, Hopni, and Merari — Egyptian names in Shilohite lineage
- J. M. Powis Smith (1918): "The name of 'Phinehas,' the son of Eli, priest at Shiloh, is Egyptian and points to some Egyptian connections with the Levitical priesthood of Yahweh at Shiloh, the northern shrine"
- Exodus 2 and Judges 17 share parallel etiologies: Moses names his son Gershom because Moses was a gēr ("sojourner") — the original context may have been the Levite as a gēr attached to sanctuaries, not specifically in Egypt
- "Because of Seow's study and the Egyptian names, one could claim that the god of the putative figure Moses and the Levitical priesthood in Shiloh was El"

**Evidence presented — Exodus 6:2-3:**
- "I am Yahweh. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them."
- This Priestly passage explicitly acknowledges that the patriarchs knew El Shadday, not Yahweh
- "This passage could represent an attempt to reconcile the discrepancy by appealing to their identification"

**The family-god profile of El fits the Exodus better than the divine warrior:**
- "The divine profile manifest in the Exodus may have looked originally more like the presence of the deity in the patriarchal narratives, the family god or 'god of the fathers' who accompanies the family on its journeys"
- "It is perhaps no accident that El names and titles proliferate in the older patriarchal narratives"
- "The divine warrior profile in the Exodus narrative may reflect not an original description of the god involved but a secondary application (albeit an early one) of Yahweh's identity as a divine warrior"
- "The old profile of the god of the Exodus may have been then the family god, a characterization that fits El eminently well"

**Smith quotes T. J. Lewis's hypothetical:**
> "If, for the sake of argument, one assumes that El was the original god of the exodus, one then wonders how Yahweh was welcomed into the fold. What function would Yahweh have provided if worshippers look to El to fight their battles?... Perhaps just as [in the Ugaritic texts] Baal comes to the fore and El recedes when it comes to a dynastic deity who fights for the nation, so the traditions of Yahweh as divine warrior come to the fore over those of El as the needs of the society changed from those of a small family group to those of a nation."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is Smith's most provocative claim and the evidential base is thinner than for the other claims. The Balaam oracles are suggestive but ambiguous; the Egyptian names in Shilohite priesthood are indirect; Exodus 6:2-3 could be a late Priestly harmonization rather than preserving authentic tradition.

**What's at stake:** If El was the god of the Exodus, then the central event of Israel's salvation history — the foundation of the covenant — was originally associated with a different god than the one Israel eventually worshiped. The theological implications are seismic. It would mean that the Exodus narrative as we have it is a synthesis of two originally independent traditions: an El-led family migration and a Yahweh divine-warrior theophany.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional scholars who maintain Mosaic Yahwism. Scholars who argue the Exodus traditions are entirely late literary constructs (minimalists) — for them the question is moot. Scholars who think the Balaam oracles are late compositions that happen to use 'ēl as an appellative, not a proper name.

**Alternative reading:** The Numbers 23-24 use of 'ēl could be poetic parallelism (El = Yahweh), not evidence of different gods. The Balaam oracles could date to a period when El and Yahweh were already identified, and the poet simply varied divine names for stylistic reasons. Exodus 6:2-3 reflects a Priestly theological schema (progressive revelation of divine names), not historical memory.

**My assessment:** This is the most speculative claim in the chapter, and Smith presents it as such — note the hedging language ("may have been," "could claim," "if the general line of interpretation is correct"). The Numbers 23-24 evidence is genuinely striking (the 3:1 ratio of El to Yahweh, the "horns like a wild ox" = Bull El), but it could also reflect an archaizing poetic style rather than an older theological stratum. The Egyptian names argument is ingenious but indirect. I find the claim plausible but not proven. The convergence of evidence — Name Israel, sanctuary data, Deut 32:8-9, Exodus 6:2-3, Numbers 23-24 — creates a cumulative case that El preceded Yahweh in Israel, but whether El was specifically the Exodus god is a further step that requires more evidence than we have.

## Ch7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: El's cult persisted into Iron Age | MEDIUM-HIGH | Sparse evidence; name survival ≠ cult survival |
| 2: El at Israelite sanctuaries (Shiloh, Shechem, Jerusalem) | HIGH | Shared West Semitic typology vs. specific El cult |
| 3: Name "Israel" = El as original god | HIGH | Name coinage after El=Yahweh identification |
| 4: Deut 32:8-9 (LXX/4Q) = Yahweh as son of Elyon | VERY HIGH | Poetic parallelism (Elyon = Yahweh) reading |
| 5: Three-stage convergence model | MEDIUM-HIGH | Regional variation vs. temporal stages; neatness |
| 6: Yahweh's southern origins (Edom/Midian) | HIGH | Literary construct vs. historical memory; Athtar comparison is speculative |
| 7: El as original god of Exodus | MEDIUM | Poetic archaism vs. older tradition; thin evidence |

**Strongest sections:** Section 4 (El in Iron Age Israel — the sanctuary evidence), Section 5 (the convergence model and the "son becomes father" paradigm shift), and the treatment of Deuteronomy 32:8-9. The convergence paradigm, especially the comparison with Baal (who overshadowed El but was never identified with him), is Smith's most original and generative contribution.

**Weakest section:** Section 7 (El as god of the Exodus). The evidence is thinner, the argument depends on inferences from the Balaam oracles whose dating and interpretation are contested, and the Egyptian-names argument, while clever, is indirect. Smith acknowledges this with more hedging language here than anywhere else in the chapter.

**Tension note:** Smith's argument in Section 6 that Yahweh's original profile may be "permanently lost" sits in tension with his reconstruction of that profile (Athtar-like warrior/precipitation god). If the earliest sources are secondary developments, how can we reconstruct the original? Smith's honesty about the limits of knowledge is admirable but creates an internal tension: he's reconstructing a profile he simultaneously says may be unrecoverable.

**Cross-reference to Ch6:** The convergence model here (Yahweh absorbs El) parallels the argument in Ch6 about Baal's relationship to El at Ugarit. In both cases, a younger warrior-god overshadows the patriarchal high god. But the crucial difference — Yahweh's identification WITH El — is what makes Israelite religion unique. This is the chapter that explains why Israel got monotheism and Ugarit didn't.

**Cross-reference to Römer:** Römer's "The Invention of God" also argues for Yahweh's southern origins and the secondary identification with El. Smith provides the detailed West Semitic/Ugaritic background that Römer assumes. The two books complement each other: Smith gives the conceptual prehistory, Römer gives the historical narrative of how it played out.

**Cross-reference to Cross:** Cross argued that Yahweh was originally a title of El ("Yahweh" as a cultic name of El). Smith's chapter is effectively a sustained counter-argument. The southern origin data, Deut 32:8-9, and the three-stage convergence model all point toward two originally distinct deities. Smith honors Cross's contribution while fundamentally revising his central thesis.
