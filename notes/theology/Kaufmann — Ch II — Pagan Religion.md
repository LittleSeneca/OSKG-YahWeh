# Kaufmann — Chapter II: Pagan Religion

This is the theoretical foundation of Kaufmann's entire project. He defines paganism by its "fundamental idea" — the existence of a metadivine realm above and beyond the gods, to which even the gods are subject. If this definition holds, Israelite religion (which lacks this realm) is different in KIND, not degree. If it fails, the whole Kaufmannian edifice collapses. This chapter is therefore the most important in the book — and also the most vulnerable.

---

## Claim 1: The "fundamental idea" of all pagan religion is the metadivine realm — a primordial domain of power prior to and above the gods

**Author's claim:** "Yet all these embodiments involve one idea which is the distinguishing mark of pagan thought: the idea that there exists a realm of being prior to the gods and above them, upon which the gods depend, and whose decrees they must obey. Deity belongs to, and is derived from, a primordial realm." (lines 22-26) "The gods are not the source of all that is, nor do they transcend the universe. They are, rather, part of a realm precedent to and independent of them." (lines 35-37)

**Evidence presented:**
- This realm appears across cultures in various guises: darkness, water, spirit, earth, sky — "always as the womb in which the seeds of all being are contained" (lines 26-28)
- Cosmogonies universally begin with a primordial substance (Nammu in Sumer, Apsu-Tiamat in Babylon, Nun in Egypt, Chaos in Greece, primeval waters in India, Ginnungagap in Germanic myth, Tao in China)
- The gods are BORN out of this realm through natural, involuntary procreation — never created by sovereign will (lines 54-67)
- The multiplicity of gods stems from the "manifold powers and 'seeds' of the primordial realm, each of which is conceived as a self-contained divine entity" (lines 70-72)
- Even "primal gods" are no more than fathers who engender offspring with no more control than a human father (lines 59-63)
- The ruling gods are typically second or third generation — the son who dethrones or murders his father is a "standing feature of pagan mythologies" (lines 64-67)

**Confidence:** This claim is Kaufmann's entire thesis, and it is breathtakingly ambitious. On the descriptive level — that pagan religions share cosmogonic patterns of primordial realms, theogonies, and divine lineages — he is largely correct. The question is whether this COMMON PATTERN amounts to a single "fundamental idea" or whether he's imposing a unified framework on enormously diverse phenomena.

**What's at stake:** Everything. If Kaufmann's "fundamental idea" accurately captures what all non-Israelite religions share, then Israelite religion's lack of theogony, metadivine realm, and self-operating magical forces makes it genuinely different in kind. If the definition is tendentious — if it's designed precisely to exclude Israel by defining paganism as "what Israel is not" — then Kaufmann has built a circular argument disguised as a phenomenological observation.

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical tradition, but in different ways. Cross and Smith don't deny that ANE religions share patterns — they argue that ISRAEL shared many of them too, at least initially. Kaufmann's move is to define paganism so that it excludes Israel by definition. Smith's evidence that early Israel HAD a divine family (Ch 2-3), HAD astral deities (Ch 3), and HAD a pantheon structure (Ch 2) directly contradicts Kaufmann's claim that Israelite religion was "absolutely different" from the beginning. Cross's argument that Yahweh began as an epithet of El places Israel squarely within the West Semitic religious world Kaufmann claims Israel transcended.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann HAS identified a real pattern — ANE religions DO share cosmogonic structures, theogonic narratives, and a sense that gods operate within a cosmic order they didn't create. His error is not in identifying the pattern but in (a) claiming it's universal across ALL non-Israelite religions (lumping Australian aboriginal religion with Greek philosophy and Chinese Taoism), (b) insisting Israel was entirely free of it, and (c) treating it as an "idea" rather than a shared cultural grammar that expressed itself differently in different contexts. The critical consensus can accept Kaufmann's phenomenology as a useful ideal type without accepting its totalizing exclusivity.

**My assessment:** This is a magnificent piece of synthetic scholarship — Kaufmann's command of the comparative data from Sumer to Japan is staggering, especially for a book written in the 1930s-50s before the full Ugaritic corpus was widely assimilated. But the claim is overdetermined. Kaufmann needs paganism to be ONE thing so that Israel can be absolutely OTHER. The evidence he marshals shows shared PATTERNS, not a shared IDEA. The difference matters: patterns can be transformed, adapted, partially retained, or gradually abandoned. An "idea" is an all-or-nothing proposition — you either have it or you don't. Kaufmann's insistence on "idea" rather than "pattern" is what allows him to draw the absolute boundary between Israel and everyone else. But it's precisely this absolutism that makes his thesis vulnerable to evidence of continuity.

## Claim 2: Theogony — the birth of gods through natural procreation — is essential to paganism and absent from Israel

**Author's claim:** "The limitation of divine powers finds its source in the theogonies that are part of every mythology. The gods emerge out of the primordial substance, having been generated by its boundless fertility. It is not the gods and their will that exist at first, but the primordial realm with its inherent forces." (lines 53-57) "What is decisive is that the gods are born out of it by the natural, involuntary process of procreation." (lines 58-59)

**Evidence presented:**
- Sumerian: Nammu "the mother who gave birth to all the gods" — cosmic mountain (An + Ki) proceeds from her, then Enlil, Nanna, Utu by successive generation (lines 157-165)
- Babylonian: Apsu and Tiamat "mingled their waters" and begot successive generations; Ea begot Marduk (lines 166-182)
- Egyptian: Atum formed within Nun, engendered Shu and Tefnet upon himself; Re fathers Nut who gives birth to him daily — "all these stories speak in terms of birth and procreation" (lines 183-197)
- Canaanite: primeval spirit + chaos → Mot → all creatures; Ras Shamra deities are "a community of husbands and wives, fathers and sons" — "their pagan mark is that they are born" (lines 198-218)
- Greek: Hesiod's Chaos → Gaea → Uranus → Cronus → Zeus — "the world and the gods who rule it come into being by a process of natural (sexual) reproduction" (lines 296-310)
- Indian: Purusha divided himself, became husband and wife, engendered humankind; "from him came the gods also" (lines 219-265)
- All others surveyed: Zoroastrian (Zervan fathers Ormazd and Ahriman), Germanic (Ymir → Buri → Bur → Odin), Japanese (Izanagi and Izanami), Gnostic (emanations, male/female pairs), Chinese (P'an Ku from chaos) — all have theogonic structures

Kaufmann also argues that "primitive monotheism" claimants (Lang, Schmidt) fail precisely because their "high gods" have mythological traits: families, needs, births, magical dependencies. Mungan ngaua has a son Tundun; Baiame feeds on emu birds; Puluga makes himself a wife. These are theogonic residues, not monotheistic survivals (lines 354-455).

**Confidence:** HIGH that theogony is near-universal in the religions Kaufmann surveys. Kaufmann's catalogue is genuinely impressive. The question is whether Israel truly lacked theogony, or whether the Bible's silence on Yahweh's origins reflects later censorship rather than original absence.

**What's at stake:** The absence of theogony in Israel is one of Kaufmann's strongest comparative points. The Bible NEVER tells a story of Yahweh's birth, parentage, or emergence from primordial substance. When Yahweh appears, he already IS. This IS genuinely different from every ANE religion Kaufmann surveys. But the absence of theogony in the BIBLICAL TEXT doesn't prove its absence in EARLY ISRAELITE RELIGION. The texts we have are the product of centuries of theological development. Smith and Cross argue that Yahweh's identification with El (who HAS no theogony in Ugaritic texts either — El just IS, as "Father of Years," the eternal one) is HOW Israel avoided theogony: by absorbing a god who already lacked birth narratives. If that's true, the absence of theogony is the RESULT of convergence (El + Yahweh), not evidence of absolute difference from the beginning.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (Ch 3) argues that El himself lacks a clear theogony at Ugarit — El is 'ab šnm ("Father of Years"), the eternal patriarch. If El had no birth story, and Yahweh absorbed El's identity, then the absence of theogony is El's legacy, not Israel's innovation. Cross sees El-Yahweh identification as the mechanism. Day notes Yahweh originated in the south (Midian/Edom) — his origins were simply unknown to Canaanite scribes, so no theogony developed. Kaufmann's claim that theogony is "part of every mythology" may be true for ACTIVE deities (Baal, Marduk, Zeus) but less true for HIGH GODS (El, Anu, Brahman) who tend to simply exist. Israel chose the high god model.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann may be right about the FACT (Israel lacks theogony) but wrong about the EXPLANATION (absolute difference from the beginning). The absence could be a secondary development. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (LXX/DSS) preserves a theology where Elyon apportions nations to his divine sons — including Yahweh. This IMPLIES a divine parent-child relationship (Elyon as father, Yahweh as son) without narrating a birth. Israel may have HAD a theogonic framework (El as father of Yahweh) that was suppressed as monotheism developed, leaving only the vestige of "sons of God" language.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's strongest empirical point. The absence of theogony in the Hebrew Bible IS a genuine comparative anomaly that needs explanation. Kaufmann's explanation (Israel was different from the beginning) is one possibility. Smith's explanation (El had no theogony; Yahweh absorbed El) is another. The evidence from Deut 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 (where Yahweh stands IN El's assembly, implicitly as a son) favors Smith: Israel HAD a theogonic framework but progressively suppressed it. Kaufmann's theogony argument is the best evidence for his thesis — and even it is inconclusive.

## Claim 3: Mythology and magic are twin expressions of the same fundamental idea — the metadivine realm produces both

**Author's claim:** "This is the radical dichotomy of paganism; from it spring both mythology and magic." (lines 43-44) "Just as the fundamental idea of paganism found poetic expression in myth, so it found practical expression in magic." (lines 98-100) "Because of the mythological nature of its gods, because of their subjection to a primordial realm, paganism was necessarily and essentially magical as well." (lines 126-129)

**Evidence presented:**
- Myth depicts gods not only as actors but as ACTED UPON — there is always "tension between the gods and other forces that shape their destinies" (lines 46-50)
- "Fate... apportions lots to the gods as well as to men" — myth narrates the subjection of gods to independent forces (lines 50-52)
- Magic is the practical counterpart: since the metadivine realm contains forces transcending the gods, pagan man "employs magic also, hoping thereby to activate the forces of the metadivine" (lines 121-123)
- Even the gods call upon metadivine forces to surmount their limitations (lines 102-103)
- "Pagan man feels himself subject to and in need of both realms" — prayer addresses the gods; magic addresses the metadivine (lines 104-105)
- The gods themselves are magicians: Marduk is the "great magician of the gods"; Thoth recites magical books over Re; Isis uses sorcery to learn Re's secret name (lines 870-888)
- Babylonian witches "can weary heaven, bind up the mouths of the gods, tie the knees of goddesses" (lines 916-918)
- Magical objects confer divine power: "Tablets of Destiny," Ishtar's girdle, Aphrodite's aphrodisiacal girdle, Hermes' wand — all are "a fundamental symbol of paganism, bespeaking the idea that there is no supreme divine will that governs all" (lines 484-500)

**Confidence:** HIGH that mythology and magic are linked in the religions Kaufmann surveys. MEDIUM that the link is specifically through the metadivine idea (rather than through other mechanisms). LOW that Israel was genuinely free of both.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's later argument (Ch III-IV) that Israelite religion lacks magic and myth is crucial to his case for Israel's uniqueness. If he can show that myth and magic are structurally linked to the metadivine realm, then Israel's alleged freedom from myth and magic becomes evidence for its freedom from the metadivine realm. But this is double-edged: if we find myth and magic IN Israel (and we do — the ark as magical weapon in 1 Samuel 4-6, the bronze serpent in Numbers 21, the Urim and Thummim, the testing ordeal of Numbers 5), then Kaufmann's structural link IMPLIES Israel had a metadivine realm too. He can't have it both ways.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars find plenty of magic in Israelite religion. The ark narrative in 1 Samuel 4-6 depicts the ark as an object of intrinsic power that afflicts the Philistines regardless of Yahweh's "will." The priestly ritual of the red heifer (Numbers 19) has automatic efficacy. The Urim and Thummim are divinatory objects. Kaufmann will need to argue that these are NOT genuinely magical — a move that requires redefining magic so narrowly that it excludes Israelite practice by definition. This is the same circularity problem at the micro level.

**Alternative reading:** The distinction between "religion" (petitioning the gods) and "magic" (manipulating impersonal forces) is a modern Western category that ancient people wouldn't have recognized. Kaufmann's claim that paganism is "necessarily and essentially magical" imposes a dichotomy that ancient practitioners experienced as a unified whole. Israelite religion may have INTEGRATED magical and religious elements differently — not eliminated magic but subordinated it to divine will in its theological self-presentation.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's structural analysis of the myth-magic link is insightful. He's right that the two belong together in the religions he surveys. But his argument cuts both ways. If myth + magic = metadivine realm, and we find myth + magic in Israel, then Israel had a metadivine realm. Kaufmann's escape route is to argue that Israel's "magic" isn't REAL magic — it's symbolic, or subordinated to divine will, or a degenerate popular practice. I'll need to watch how he handles the Israelite evidence in later chapters. The danger of special pleading is acute.

## Claim 4: Pagan gods are subject to necessity (fate, ananke, rita, karma) — even the highest gods cannot escape predestined decrees

**Author's claim:** "Necessity dominates the universe and the gods who are part of it. Birth, procreation, growth, youth, age, death, and the like — are innate properties of the world-stuff." (lines 501-504) "The fabulous wonderland of myth and magic is bound to necessity; even the gods must bow to the inexorable decrees of fate." (lines 506-507)

**Evidence presented:**
- Babylonian astrology: the stars follow fixed laws that determine even divine actions — "immutable laws are operative" alongside the gods as living beings (lines 1143-1159)
- Hindu rita: the world order, principle of pattern and regularity. "The gods are sometimes called the lords of rita, but they are also its servants, guardians, members of its household" (lines 510-514)
- Persian asha: the cosmic order to which even Ormazd is subject — he doesn't set the 9,000-year timeline for his battle with Ahriman; "he merely has knowledge of what has been foreordained" (lines 285-293)
- Greek ananke/moira: Zeus doesn't decide Achilles' fate — "Zeus merely weighs the fates of the two in the balance to learn what is destined for them" (lines 519-520). Zeus must swallow Metis to forestall the birth of a son who would depose him — "even Zeus may not avoid what is destined" (lines 525-530)
- Roman fatum: "similar notions are found throughout the pagan world" (lines 530-532)
- Fixed cosmic cycles: "the world is destined to pass through various predetermined stages before its destruction or renewal. The gods have no control over this 'natural' process" (lines 537-539)
- Buddhist karma: "the self-operating law of moral causality and retribution which binds both gods and men" (lines 760-761)

**Confidence:** HIGH that Kaufmann has identified a real cross-cultural pattern. The subjection of even high gods to impersonal cosmic order is genuinely widespread. But the question again is: does Israel escape it?

**What's at stake:** If Israelite religion subjects Yahweh to NO higher order — no fate, no necessity, no cosmic law that Yahweh didn't create — then this IS a genuine difference. But is it true? The Bible frequently depicts Yahweh acting within constraints: he "repents" (Gen 6:6, Exod 32:14), he can be persuaded by human argument (Abraham in Gen 18, Moses in Exod 32), he is bound by his own covenant promises, and his "name" or "glory" seems to have quasi-independent existence. None of these are exactly "fate" in the pagan sense, but they complicate the claim of absolute, unconstrained sovereignty. Kaufmann needs to show that these are qualitatively different from pagan necessity — that Yahweh's constraints are self-imposed while pagan necessity is external. The distinction may be real, but Kaufmann's case needs more theological precision than he provides here.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see continuity might point to texts like Isaiah 45:7 ("I form light and create darkness, I make peace and create evil") as evidence that Israel DID develop a radical doctrine of Yahweh's sovereignty over ALL forces — including what pagans would call "fate." The question is when this development occurred. If it's late (Deutero-Isaiah, 6th century), then early Israel MAY have shared pagan assumptions about cosmic order.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's silence about fate/necessity could reflect not theological innovation but genre. Israelite literature is historical narrative and prophecy — not theogonic myth or philosophical speculation. Different genres produce different conceptual vocabularies. Greek tragedy explores fate because tragedy requires it; Hebrew narrative explores covenant faithfulness because covenant narrative requires it. The absence of "fate" language may be a literary phenomenon, not a theological one.

**My assessment:** This is a real and important comparative observation. Pagan religions DO consistently subordinate gods to impersonal cosmic order. Israel DOES consistently present Yahweh as the source of order rather than its subject. Whether this reflects actual Israelite belief from the beginning or the theological achievement of later scribes is the question. Kaufmann assumes the former; the evidence of texts like Deut 32:8-9 (where Elyon, not Yahweh, apportions nations) suggests the latter.

## Claim 5: Divine wisdom in paganism is external to the gods — it's knowledge OF the world order, not self-knowledge, and must be acquired

**Author's claim:** "The wisdom of a deity does not consist in self-awareness, in knowledge of his will and its effect on a world dependent upon it, but rather in a knowledge of the world and its mysterious properties. Its object lies outside the gods; it is knowledge of an order of which they are but a part." (lines 544-551) "The god acquires his wisdom along with his other divine powers from the seed out of which he was born, or by some magical means." (lines 551-553)

**Evidence presented:**
- Wisdom is a specific property of certain gods, not a universal divine attribute: Thoth (Egypt), Ea (Babylon), Metis (Greece) are "lords of wisdom" — but not necessarily the heads of their pantheons (lines 555-557)
- Connection of wisdom with primeval waters: Ea is god of waters AND lord of wisdom; Thetis, Nereus, Proteus are water deities who are wise and oracular (lines 564-570)
- Gods ACQUIRE wisdom: Marduk learns medicine from Ea; Cronus learns tricks from Gaea; Apollo learns divination from Pan; Odin learns runes by falling from the world tree; Izanami and Izanagi learn copulation from a bird (lines 571-577)
- Mortals or lesser gods can surpass chief gods in wisdom: Lugalbanda solves the Zu bird problem when the gods cannot; Kothar-wa-hasis builds Baal's palace; Hephaestus crafts Hercules' shield; "Zeus is astounded" (lines 578-582)
- Zeus swallows Metis because she is "wiser than all the gods and men" — "a striking expression of the pagan concept that wisdom is something external to the gods and has to be acquired by them" (lines 586-589)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the descriptive observation. The pattern of wisdom being a specific, acquirable, external attribute IS widely attested. But Kaufmann is making a stronger claim: that this pattern reflects the metadivine realm concept. That's an interpretive move that could be challenged.

**What's at stake:** If pagan wisdom is external and acquired while Israelite wisdom is internal (God's wisdom is his own, inseparable from his being), this is another axis of difference. But Kaufmann may be overstating the Israelite side. Proverbs 8 depicts Wisdom as a created being who was "beside" Yahweh "as a master workman" — Wisdom as quasi-independent entity. This looks remarkably like the external-wisdom pattern Kaufmann attributes to paganism.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israelite wisdom literature as heavily indebted to ANE wisdom traditions (Egyptian, Mesopotamian). The book of Proverbs shares substantial material with the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope. If Israelite wisdom is borrowed from "pagan" sources, the absolute distinction Kaufmann draws looks artificial.

**Alternative reading:** The distinction Kaufmann identifies may be more about LITERARY GENRE than theology. Pagan myths narrate how gods acquired wisdom; Israelite texts attribute wisdom to Yahweh as an inherent attribute. But these are different KINDS of texts making different KINDS of claims. The comparison may be apples to oranges.

**My assessment:** The "wisdom as external and acquired" pattern is one of Kaufmann's most subtle and insightful observations. The Metis story (Zeus swallows Wisdom to possess it) really IS a vivid expression of the idea. But Kaufmann's claim that Israelite divine wisdom is fundamentally different needs more evidence. The Wisdom of Solomon tradition, the role of Lady Wisdom in Proverbs 8, and the apocalyptic literature's treatment of hidden knowledge all complicate the picture.

## Claim 6: The pagan cult is intrinsically and automatically efficacious — it operates on the gods AND the metadivine realm, and the gods themselves use it

**Author's claim:** "The characteristic mark of the pagan cult is not its plurality of worshipped beings, but its view of ritual as automatically efficient and intrinsically significant. The cult is not ordained by the supreme, free will of the deity; its end is not merely to express and embody man's adoration. It is rather a system of rites capable in themselves of working good and evil, whose potency derives from the realm above the gods." (lines 1383-1392)

**Evidence presented:**
- The cult serves the gods' NEEDS — it "not only invokes the blessings of the gods, it also supports them and strengthens them through its rites" (lines 1396-1398)
- Sacrifice provides food for the gods: Mesopotamian gods "cluster around the sacrifice of Utnapishtim 'like flies'" after the Flood; daily temple rites include "arraying of banquet tables before the images of the gods" (lines 1435-1442)
- The cult's higher form: ritual brings "mysterious powers to bear on the cosmos" — man is "a partner of mighty gods, with whom his destiny is linked" (lines 1444-1447)
- Aztecs revitalize aging gods through festival; rites of death and resurrection of gods; slaying the god to keep him from aging (lines 1448-1455)
- Hinduism offers the "most striking case": sacrifice is the "navel of the world," "above the gods," through which "the gods live, create, and govern" (lines 1463-1472)
- The battle of good and evil is fought THROUGH the cult — Zoroastrianism: man who keeps purity "strengthens Ormazd" in his struggle with Ahriman (lines 1537-1553)
- **Gods as priests** — the ultimate expression: Marduk is "priest of the gods"; Ea invents temple-building; Ishtar performs lament for the slain bull of heaven; Egyptian ritual "is an imitation of what Horus, Isis, Nephthys, and Anubis did to the dead Osiris"; Hindu gods "sacrifice, use incantations, practice ascetic rites"; Zervan "made libations one thousand years"; Greek gods purify themselves after murder and sexual relations (lines 1554-1615)
- "In no other realm is the subservience of the pagan gods so evident as here. Wisdom, magic, divination, prophecy are all expressions of divine potency and art. But the cult is necessarily an expression of dependence." (lines 1611-1615)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the descriptive evidence. The notion that gods receive sustenance from sacrifice, that rituals have automatic efficacy, and that gods themselves perform cultic acts is genuinely widespread. MEDIUM that Israel is fundamentally different — Israelite sacrifice also has automatic features (the scapegoat in Lev 16, the red heifer in Num 19, the jealous husband's ordeal in Num 5).

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's later claim that Israelite cult is fundamentally different (Ch III-IV) depends on this characterization. If Israelite sacrifice is petitionary (asking God) while pagan sacrifice is manipulative (feeding God or activating metadivine forces), the contrast holds. But Israelite sacrifice has BOTH elements. Psalm 50:12-13 explicitly REJECTS the idea that God needs to eat sacrifices — "If I were hungry, I would not tell you" — which suggests the idea was present enough to need refutation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who note that Israelite sacrifice and ritual share substantial features with ANE practice. The scapegoat ritual (Leviticus 16) sends sin into the wilderness to Azazel — an act with automatic, quasi-magical efficacy. The priestly consecration (Leviticus 8-9) involves blood manipulation that sanctifies through contact. Israelite cultic practice may have been less explicitly "magical" in its theological self-presentation while being functionally similar at the ritual level.

**Alternative reading:** The difference between pagan and Israelite cult may be more about THEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION than ritual practice. Similar acts (sacrifice, purification, divination) were reinterpreted within a monotheistic framework. The acts didn't change; the meaning did. Kaufmann mistakes theological interpretation for ritual reality.

**My assessment:** The "gods as priests" section (lines 1554-1615) is Kaufmann at his most brilliant — the image of deities dependent on cult for purification, strength, and sustenance is a genuinely powerful contrast to the biblical picture of a God who needs nothing from human hands. But the power of the contrast doesn't make it historically accurate for early Israel. The question is whether the Bible's "God needs nothing" theology was present from the beginning or developed over time. Kaufmann assumes the former; the critical consensus (possibly correctly) sees it as a prophetic/priestly achievement.

## Claim 7: The pagan "way to salvation" is self-salvation — man saves himself through knowledge, cult, or philosophy; the gods are at best helpers, not saviors

**Author's claim:** "The most advanced manifestations of paganism show a tendency to regard man as able to save himself by his own devices. The cult rises above the commonplace concerns of rain, produce, fertility, and victory to the vision of salvation. At this level, man may be viewed as the ally of the gods in their struggle with evil — that is, at bottom, as co-savior with the gods (Zoroastrianism). Or the tendency may be toward the magical, with the cult regarded as a system of rites capable of exalting man to divine rank and thus saving him from evil. Salvation, however, is his own concern, not the gods'; at most, they but help him find the hidden way (Brahmanism)." (lines 1632-1641)

**Evidence presented:**
- Buddhism: "it is not the gods who redeem, but knowledge of the secrets of existence"; the Buddha is "teacher" of gods and men alike; "before the Buddha even the gods prostrate themselves" (lines 820-830)
- Brahmanism: the gods themselves "search, meditate, practice yoga" seeking the cosmic secret (lines 821-822)
- Gnosticism: "man liberates himself through his mind and spirit from the prison of the body" — knowledge IS salvation (lines 1642-1645)
- Plato: "man how to redeem himself through attachment to the realm of ideas" (lines 1646-1647)
- Hellenistic mysteries: apotheosis through rites — the participant "becomes Osiris" through magical rites; Orphic rites liberate the soul from matter (lines 686-700)
- The pagan ideal: Apollonius of Tyana, who attains "a rank a little less than the gods" through philosophical discipline — "he who attains it is on a par with Apollo" (lines 1375-1381)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the pattern. There IS a recurrent theme in "higher" paganism of self-directed salvation, whether through knowledge (gnosis, Buddhist enlightenment), cult (mysteries), or philosophy (Platonism). The gods are often obstacles to be transcended rather than saviors to be trusted. But MEDIUM that Israel's "way to salvation" was fundamentally different from the beginning — the prophetic tradition's emphasis on divine grace develops over time.

**What's at stake:** This is the theological heart of Kaufmann's contrast. Pagan man saves himself; Israelite man is saved by God. The distinction is real — but again, is it original or developmental?

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who note that Israelite religion ALSO has elements of self-directed righteousness. The Deuteronomic theology ("if you obey... you will live long in the land") makes salvation conditional on human action. Wisdom literature (Proverbs) teaches that the wise man prospers by his wisdom. The prophetic critique of empty ritual (Isaiah 1, Amos 5) implies that SOME Israelites were treating the cult as automatically efficacious — exactly the pagan pattern Kaufmann describes.

**Alternative reading:** The grace-works tension exists in BOTH Israelite and pagan religion. Kaufmann is comparing the BEST of Israelite religion (prophetic grace theology) with a reductionist reading of pagan religion. A fairer comparison would acknowledge that pagan religion also knows divine grace (Ishtar's love for Tammuz, Marduk's mercy, the personal piety of Babylonian penitential psalms) and that Israelite religion also knows self-directed righteousness (Deuteronomic blessing-and-curse, wisdom's act-consequence nexus).

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's "way to salvation" contrast is theologically powerful but historically vulnerable. It works as a characterization of IDEAL TYPES — prophetic Israelite religion vs. philosophical pagan religion — but breaks down when applied to the full range of actual practice in both cultures. Babylonian penitential prayers cry out for divine mercy; Israelite wisdom literature advises self-disciplined path to flourishing. The boundary is messier than Kaufmann allows.

## Claim 8: The Bible's misrepresentation of paganism as fetishism is EVIDENCE of Israel's absolute difference — Israelites genuinely didn't understand pagan religion

**Author's claim:** [This claim is developed in the Introduction but prepared here.] Kaufmann argues that the biblical writers consistently mock idols as mere wood and stone, never engaging with actual pagan theology (the myths, the theogonies, the metadivine realm). He takes this as evidence that Israelites GENUINELY didn't understand paganism — not that they deliberately distorted it for polemical purposes. The Israelites were, in a sense, innocently ignorant of what paganism really was.

**Evidence presented in this chapter (by implication):** The entire chapter demonstrates what REAL paganism is — a sophisticated system of myth, magic, divination, and cult grounded in the metadivine realm. The biblical polemic against idolatry (mocked as fetishism, "they have mouths but do not speak") completely misses this sophisticated reality. Kaufmann's implicit argument: if the biblical writers KNEW real paganism and were merely polemicizing, they'd engage the actual theology. Their failure to do so suggests they genuinely didn't understand it — which in turn suggests Israelite religion was so fundamentally different that paganism was genuinely incomprehensible to Israelites.

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Kaufmann's most creative but least defensible move. The alternative explanation — that biblical polemic DELIBERATELY caricatured pagan religion as a rhetorical strategy — is far more parsimonious and is standard practice in religious polemic across cultures.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right, the biblical caricature of paganism is positive evidence for Israel's uniqueness. If he's wrong, the caricature is just good polemic and tells us nothing about what Israelites actually believed or understood.

**Who disagrees:** Everyone. The prophetic mockery of idols (Isaiah 44:9-20, Psalm 115:4-8, Jeremiah 10:1-16) is universally understood as deliberate polemical caricature. The prophets KNEW pagans believed their idols were more than wood and stone — they chose to mock them as mere matter to make a rhetorical point. Kaufmann's reading requires us to believe that Isaiah, who lived in Jerusalem during the Assyrian crisis and certainly encountered Assyrian religion firsthand, genuinely thought Assyrians worshipped inert blocks of wood. This strains credulity.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical caricature of paganism as fetishism is exactly what we'd expect from a monotheistic polemic against polytheism — it's a standard rhetorical move that tells us about the POLEMICISTS' theological commitments, not about their ignorance. The more interesting question is why THIS particular polemic (fetishism rather than, say, immorality of the gods) became the standard biblical trope. Kaufman's observation that the polemic is consistent is valuable; his explanation for that consistency is not.

**My assessment:** This is the weak link in Kaufmann's chain. He needs the biblical caricature to be evidence of Israelite innocence, not Israelite polemic. But the simpler explanation — that monotheists mock polytheists by reducing their gods to inert matter — accounts for the data without requiring us to believe that Israelites were uniquely ignorant of their neighbors' religions despite centuries of coexistence, trade, intermarriage, and political interaction. Kaufmann's argument here is a classic case of treating a theological claim as a historical one.

## Claim 9: Kaufmann's definition of paganism is circular — he defines paganism as "what Israel is not," then concludes Israel is not pagan

**This is not Kaufmann's claim but my critical assessment of his method.**

**The circularity:** Kaufmann's procedure is:
1. Survey pagan religions and identify their "fundamental idea" (metadivine realm + theogony + magic)
2. Note that Israelite religion lacks these features
3. Conclude that Israelite religion is fundamentally different

The problem is step 1 already presupposes step 3. By DEFINING paganism in terms of features Israel lacks, Kaufmann guarantees that Israel will appear unique. A fair procedure would be:
1. Identify features common to ANE religions (including early Israel)
2. Note which features Israel SHARES and which it LACKS
3. Assess whether the differences are of kind or degree

**Evidence of the circularity:** Kaufmann EXCLUDES Israelite religion from his survey of paganism at the OUTSET: "We designate as pagan all the religions of mankind... excepting Israelite religion and its derivatives, Christianity and Islam" (lines 4-6). He then surveys paganism to find what ALL pagan religions share. But since he excluded Israel (and its derivatives) at the start, whatever he finds will, by definition, not apply to Israel. The conclusion is built into the premise.

**What's at stake:** The entire Kaufmannian project. If his definition of paganism is circular, his difference-in-kind thesis is unfalsifiable. No evidence of Israelite continuity with ANE religion could ever count against it, because any shared feature is simply NOT part of the "fundamental idea" by definition.

**Who disagrees:** Kaufmann would presumably argue that he's not defining paganism by exclusion but by positive identification of a real pattern — the metadivine realm really IS the common denominator of non-Israelite religions, discovered inductively through the survey. But the inductive claim is weakened by the prior exclusion: how do we know Israel doesn't share this pattern if we exclude Israel from the data set we're using to identify the pattern?

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann could restructure his argument non-circularly: (1) Survey ALL ANE religions INCLUDING Israel to identify common features; (2) Note that Israel and its neighbors share X, Y, Z but Israel lacks A, B, C; (3) Argue that A, B, C (theogony, metadivine realm, magic) are more fundamental than X, Y, Z. This would be defensible even if one disagreed with the weighting. But Kaufmann doesn't do this — he treats Israel as sui generis from line one.

**My assessment:** The circularity is real and it's the most serious methodological problem with Kaufmann's entire project. However, it doesn't automatically make him wrong — circular arguments can sometimes be restructured as non-circular ones if the excluded data genuinely doesn't share the pattern. The question is whether Israelite religion, when examined WITHOUT the prior assumption of uniqueness, DOES share the pattern. And on the evidence we have (Kuntillet Ajrud, the figurines, Deut 32:8-9, the divine council texts), it shares more than Kaufmann acknowledges. The circularity isn't just a formal problem — it enabled Kaufmann to miss evidence that was available even in his day.

## Claim 10: Kaufmann's treatment of "primitive monotheism" (Schmidt) is devastating — but his own alternative has mirror-image problems

**Author's claim (implicit):** The "high gods" of primitive tribes are NOT monotheistic survivals but pagan deities defined by their subjection to metadivine forces — they have families, needs, magical dependencies, and operate within a universe they didn't create.

**Evidence presented (lines 354-455):**
- Baiame created "all" yet other creation stories with other actors exist; he feeds on emu birds, his legs turn to stone in old age, his son reigns for him (lines 381-403)
- Puluga is "eternal" but makes himself a wife, eats, drinks, sleeps, descends to provision himself — omniscient "but only during the daytime" (lines 403-407)
- Nurrundere is a fisherman, wounded in battle, grows deaf and feeble in old age (lines 399-401)
- Mungan ngaua (Schmidt's star witness) has a son Tundun; Schmidt interprets sonship "in a moral sense" — which Kaufmann ridicules; Mungan ngaua is "neither a creator, nor originally a sky-god" (lines 421-424)
- The Kurnai also know "an evil sky-god, Brewin, who has a wife and son" — Mungan ngaua is "but one of several powers" (lines 429-431)
- Schmidt's claim that California tribes believed in creation ex nihilo is exposed: the accounts have a creator "descending" onto pre-existent sea and sky, or a fox "congealed in a cloud in the primeval sky" — "In these stories Schmidt sees creation ex nihilo" (lines 442-447)
- "There is then no reason to regard the mythological element in these religions as a result of degeneration. Its ultimate ground is the idea that each god represents only one embodiment of the forces of a universe filled with divine powers." (lines 448-451)

**Confidence:** HIGH that Kaufmann successfully refutes Schmidt's primitive monotheism thesis. The high gods of tribal religions really do exhibit mythological features. MEDIUM that this refutation supports Kaufmann's own thesis rather than simply dismantling Schmidt's.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann uses the failure of "primitive monotheism" to clear the ground for his own claim: Israelite monotheism is unique, not a survival of primitive purity. But refuting Schmidt doesn't prove Kaufmann right — it just shows that one alternative to the evolutionary model (devolution from primitive monotheism) fails. The evolutionary model (development from polytheism to monotheism) is untouched by this argument.

**Who disagrees:** Schmidt's followers, but they're a minor voice in contemporary scholarship. More importantly, scholars who accept Kaufmann's critique of Schmidt but draw the OPPOSITE conclusion: that Israelite monotheism developed FROM a pagan matrix (as evolutionary models claim) rather than being absolutely different from the beginning.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's critique of Schmidt is excellent — but it works just as well for the evolutionary model as for Kaufmann's. The high gods DON'T represent primitive monotheism → therefore monotheism is NOT primitive → therefore monotheism DEVELOPED. That's the evolutionary conclusion, not Kaufmann's. Kaufmann wants to say: high gods aren't monotheistic → but Israel IS → Israel must be sui generis. The logic doesn't follow without Kaufmann's prior exclusion of Israel from the comparative data.

**My assessment:** This section (lines 354-455) is Kaufmann at his best — sharp, witty, devastating to Schmidt's thesis. The detail about Schmidt seeing creation ex nihilo in a story about a fox congealing in a cloud is genuinely funny and analytically precise. But Kaufmann's own alternative faces a mirror-image problem: just as Schmidt read monotheism INTO tribal religion, Kaufmann reads metadivine-realm-paganism INTO tribal religion. Both are imposing a unified framework on data that may be more complex than either framework allows. The difference is that Kaufmann's framework is more sophisticated and took the evidence more seriously — but it's still a framework, not a neutral description.

**Strongest section:** "The Gods and Necessity" (lines 501-542) and "Gods as Priests" (lines 1554-1615). The former demonstrates real cross-cultural patterns persuasively; the latter is Kaufmann's most original and vivid contribution — the image of gods dependent on cult for their own survival is genuinely powerful and contrasts sharply with biblical theology.

**Weakest section:** The methodological framing (lines 1-44). Kaufmann defines paganism by exclusion, then "discovers" that Israel is excluded. The rest of the chapter is an elaborate unpacking of a premise assumed at the start.

**Most important for the Truth project:** This chapter IS the fulcrum of Kaufmann's entire argument. If the metadivine realm definition stands, Israelite religion is different in kind — and the evolutionary model (monotheism developed gradually from polytheism) must be false. If it falls, Kaufmann's project becomes an interesting but ultimately unsuccessful attempt to defend a theological position with historical methods.

**Honest assessment of Kaufmann's achievement:** Kaufmann has identified real patterns that genuinely differentiate Israelite religion FROM ITS NEIGHBORS in significant ways. The absence of theogony, the rejection of a metadivine realm, the subordination of magic to divine will — these ARE distinctive features of the BIBLICAL portrayal of Israelite religion. Kaufmann's error is not in identifying these features but in claiming they were present "from the very beginning" and that Israel was "absolutely different" from the start. The evidence from Smith, Cross, Day, Römer, and Dever suggests these were ACHIEVEMENTS — the result of centuries of theological development, conflict, and eventual consolidation — not given from the outset. Kaufmann reads the Bible's theological endpoint back into Israel's historical beginning. This is a powerful theological reading but a problematic historical one.

**What I'm watching for in subsequent chapters:**
- How Kaufmann handles the archaeological evidence (figurines, Kuntillet Ajrud, standing stones) that suggests popular Israelite religion was more continuous with Canaanite practice
- Whether he can distinguish "genuine paganism" from "Israelite deviance" without special pleading
- Whether his dating of P as pre-prophetic can survive scrutiny given the linguistic evidence
- How he accounts for texts like Deut 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 that seem to preserve earlier polytheistic frameworks

**Methodological note:** Kaufmann writes like a theologian of religions doing history, not a historian doing theology. His method is to identify the "fundamental idea" of a religious system and then interpret all data through that lens. This produces elegant, internally coherent readings — but it's vulnerable to the charge that the "fundamental idea" is imposed rather than discovered. The critical historian's method — start with the data, identify patterns, allow for messiness and development — produces less elegant but more reliable results. The Truth project needs to decide which methodological approach is more likely to yield truth about the origins of Yahweh and the emergence of monotheism. I currently lean toward the critical-historical approach — but Kaufmann's challenge forces me to articulate WHY, which is itself valuable.

## Chapter Summary

Kaufmann defines paganism by its "fundamental idea": the existence of a metadivine realm — a primordial domain of power above and beyond the gods. This realm is the source of the gods (through theogony), limits them (through necessity/fate), and can be accessed independently of them (through magic). From this single idea, Kaufmann derives all the features of pagan religion: mythology (narratives of gods subject to forces beyond them), magic (practical activation of metadivine forces), divination (discovery of cosmic secrets, not just divine will), cult (rites of automatic efficacy that gods themselves need), and salvation (self-directed through knowledge or ritual, not divine grace). The chapter surveys cosmogonies, divine attributes, magical practices, divinatory techniques, and cultic systems from Sumer to Japan, arguing they all instantiate the same fundamental pattern. It concludes with a devastating critique of Schmidt's "primitive monotheism" thesis, showing that tribal high gods exhibit the same mythological-pagan features. The entire argument sets up Kaufmann's core thesis: Israelite religion, which lacks the metadivine realm, is absolutely different in KIND from everything else in the ancient world. The question the reader must ask is whether this "fundamental idea" was genuinely discovered through inductive comparison or was assumed from the start by defining paganism as "what Israel is not."
