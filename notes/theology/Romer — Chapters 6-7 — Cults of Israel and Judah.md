# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 6-7

---

## Chapter 6: The Cult of Yhwh in Israel

### Claim: The biblical picture of pure Yahwism in Israel is a Deuteronomistic fiction
→ [[claim-israel-pure-yahwism-deuteronomistic-fiction-synthesis]] | **romer-inv-6-7.1** | Confidence: high
  The Deuteronomists retrojected 6th-century theology onto the 9th-8th century north. The real cult was diverse and regional, operating on three socio-religious levels (family, local, national). The golden calves at Dan and Bethel were legitimate Yhwh symbols — El's bull iconography transferred to Yhwh. Exodus 32 was written to delegitimize northern worship retroactively.

## Chapter 7: The Cult of Yhwh in Judah

### Claim: Jerusalem was not originally Yhwh's exclusive home
→ [[claim-yhwh-gradual-rise-jerusalem-two-kingdoms-synthesis]] | **romer-inv-6-7.2** | Confidence: high
  Multiple Yahwistic sanctuaries existed in Judah (Arad, Lachish, Beer-Sheba). Yhwh competed with Jerusalem's solar deity and gradually absorbed El Elyon. The south won the theological war by literary survival — after 722, Judean scribes wrote the north as apostasy. One capital, one temple, one god was a political project, not original religion.

## Cross-Cutting: Chapters 6-7 and the "Two Kingdoms" Problem

Römer's picture of the two kingdoms complicates the biblical narrative:

| | Israel (North) | Judah (South) |
|---|---|---|
| **Political status** | Dominant, wealthy, fertile | Small, poor, vassal to Israel |
| **Yhwh worship** | Bull iconography, multiple sanctuaries (Dan, Bethel, Samaria) | Multiple sanctuaries (Arad, Lachish, Beer-Sheba), solar competition in Jerusalem |
| **Biblical portrayal** | Apostate from the beginning | Sometimes faithful, sometimes not |
| **Historical reality** | Legitimate Yahwism with older El imagery | Gradual Yhwh centralization, late monopoly |

The south WON the theological war because it survived. After 722 (fall of Israel), Judean scribes wrote the history of the north as apostasy. This is the classic case of history being written by the winners — except Judah wasn't a military winner (it fell in 587). It was a literary winner. Its scribes outlasted the northern kingdom and shaped the story.

**Römer's consistent narrative:** Yhwh worship was always diverse, regional, and inclusive of other deities. The drive toward exclusivity, centralization, and monotheism was a LATE development — driven by political centralization, the trauma of Israel's fall, and the theological innovations of Judean scribes. This aligns with Smith's differentiation model but tells it as political history rather than textual analysis.
