# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapter 8

## The Statue of Yhwh in Judah

---

## Claim 1: Yhwh was represented by standing stones (maṣṣēbôt) — these were divine images, not memorials

**Römer's claim:** The traditional view that Israelite religion was aniconic from the beginning is wrong. Standing stones (*maṣṣēbôt*) functioned as representations of Yhwh. "The masseba was a way of representing the god Yhwh." The prohibition on images (Exodus 20:4) is late, not original.

**Evidence presented:**
- *Maṣṣēbôt* had four biblical functions: funerary markers, commemorative memorials, contract witnesses, and **divine representations**
- Genesis 28: Jacob anoints a *masseba* at Bethel and calls it "a house of God" (*bêt 'ĕlōhîm*)
- The Arad temple had two steles in its holy of holies — "representing Yhwh and another deity" (probably Asherah)
- At Mari (2nd millennium), betyles and statues COEXIST — "it would be a mistake to oppose aniconism and iconism sharply in this period"
- Some steles at Mari were sculpted with human features and painted. The Arad steles had traces of paint — "possibly covered with painted images of the gods who were worshipped there"
- Deuteronomy 16:22 and Leviticus 26:1 prohibit BOTH statues AND standing stones — proving they were considered equivalent. "In this prohibition the terms *pesel* and *maṣṣēbāh* refer to the cult of Yhwh"

**The two-stone problem at Arad:** Two steles in the holy of holies — one certainly representing Yhwh. The other? "Yhwh and his Asherah" is the most natural reading. This is material evidence for a divine couple.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The prohibition texts lumping *massebot* with idols are the strongest evidence — you don't ban something unless people are doing it.

---

## Claim 2: Seals and coins from Judah depict Yhwh in anthropomorphic form

**Römer's claim:** Despite the later aniconic ideal, some Israelites depicted Yhwh visually. A 7th-century seal shows a god enthroned; Persian-era coinage shows Yhwh on a winged wheel.

**Evidence presented:**
- A Hebrew seal inscribed "Elishama, son of Gedalyahu" shows a god seated on a throne flanked by two trees of life
- Two 7th-century seals may show Yhwh with lunar attributes — "the Yahwistic names of the owners of these seals would suggest rather an identification of the seated god with Yhwh"
- A Persian-era coin (c. 380 BCE) from Judah shows a deity seated on a winged wheel with the inscription "Yehud" (Judah) — almost certainly Yhwh as sky god, using Mesopotamian iconographic conventions

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the seals (can't definitively prove the figure is Yhwh without an identifying inscription). MEDIUM-HIGH on the coin (the Yehud inscription makes the identification much more likely).

---

## Claim 3: The prohibition on divine images is late — 6th century BCE at the earliest

**Römer's claim:** The Decalogue's prohibition on graven images is not Mosaic. It was formulated during the exile (6th century) to distinguish Yahwism from Babylonian idolatry, then projected backward into Israel's foundational narrative.

**Evidence:**
- "Already in the second millennium at Mari, betyles and statues of gods coexist" — there was no ancient Near Eastern tradition of aniconism
- The golden calf story in Exodus 32 was composed to condemn northern bull worship retroactively
- Isaiah 19:19 (c. 350-300 BCE) still envisions a *masseba* for Yhwh in Egypt — the prohibition was not absolute even in the Persian period
- The aniconic ideal triumphed only gradually

**Confidence:** HIGH on the lateness of the prohibition (well-established in critical scholarship). MEDIUM on the specific 6th-century exile dating.

**What's at stake for faith:** The second commandment — "you shall not make for yourself a graven image" — was not given to Moses on Sinai. It was invented by priests in Babylon who wanted to distinguish their religion from the statues they saw around them. The invisible, transcendent God is a late construction — earlier Israelites could SEE their God, in stone and on seals and coins.
