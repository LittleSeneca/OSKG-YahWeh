# Keel & Uehlinger — Excursus: The Late Bronze and Early Iron Age So-Called "Astarte Plaques"

This is the book's systematic treatment of the naked female terra-cotta plaques that dominate scholarly discussion of goddess worship in Late Bronze and Iron Age I Palestine. The excursus makes two moves that fundamentally complicate the "Astarte plaques = goddess figurines = popular Asherah worship" equation that Dever and others rely on. First: these ARE goddesses, not mortuary "concubines" (contra Tadmor). Second: they're goddesses of a very specific type — non-royal, non-mythological, everyday protectresses associated primarily with WOMEN'S piety and the female life-cycle — and they're NOT found in early Israelite hill country settlements.

---

## Claim 1: The "Astarte plaques" depict goddesses, not mortal women or "concubine" figures — the Tadmor thesis is wrong

**Author's claim:** "Three mutually complementary paths of argumentation... suggest that the nude woman on the 'Astarte plaques' is a goddess" (lines 63-65). They explicitly reject M. Tadmor's argument that the plaques are Egyptian-style "concubine" figures related to funerary practice.

**Evidence presented:**
- **(a) Typological:** Tadmor's argument depends on rectangular plaques resembling beds. But "only a few of the terra-cottas are shaped in this way." Most are "long, extended oval" — interpreting these as beds "is improbable" (lines 74-75). The same oval shape is used for qudshu-type plaques that Tadmor ACCEPTS as goddesses. The figures should be understood as STANDING, not reclining.
- **(b) Archaeological context:** The plaques are NOT primarily funerary. The "vast majority" were found in LIVING QUARTERS, not graves. At Gezer: 26 LB plaques, at most 4 from graves. "Approximately 80% of the Late Bronze Age graves contained no plaques at all" (lines 102-103). At Lachish: "only two plaques were found" in dozens of LB graves. "Their main function, and the setting in which their use made sense, was in the family living quarters" (lines 111-112).
- **(c) Iconographic context:** Shrine models from Tell Qasile (Iron I, 11th c.) show two standing naked female figures INSIDE a shrine with pilasters and cornice. The feet point forward, not sideways — same as the plaques. "To represent naked female figures either in front of or actually inside a sanctuary shows clearly that they are to be interpreted as (twin?) goddesses associated with this shrine" (lines 157-159). A cultic stand from Pella (Iron I/IIA transition) shows the same figures standing on lion heads.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The three lines of evidence converge. The shrine-model evidence (c) is particularly decisive — you don't put mortal women inside shrine facades flanked by pilasters.

**What's at stake:** If Tadmor were right, the "Astarte plaques" would be evidence for funerary practice, not goddess worship — removing a major piece of evidence from the goddess-religion argument. Keel & Uehlinger RESTORE the plaques as goddess evidence, but with crucial qualifications (Claims 2-4 below) that limit what they can tell us about ISRAELITE goddess worship specifically.

**Who disagrees:** Tadmor (1982), followed by H. Weippert (1988), Lipinski (1986), and Hadley (1989). They read the lack of divine attributes as indicating mortal status.

**Alternative reading:** Tadmor could be right about SOME of the rectangular plaques (which might be beds with reclining women/concubines) even if the oval plaques are goddesses. The corpus may be mixed.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger win this argument decisively. The shrine model from Tell Qasile is the smoking gun — you don't build a shrine facade around a concubine.

## Claim 2: The plaques depict a NON-ROYAL, every day goddess — not a great goddess of the pantheon, and not identifiable with a specific divine name (Anat, Astarte, or Asherah)

**Author's claim:** "The combination of variable iconography and the spread of this form by means of many inexpensive plaques gives one reason to think that this image is not of a great, distant, royal deity (note the lack of a crown and the fact that the nakedness contradicts a Late Bronze Age tendency to clothe deities). Instead, it portrays a goddess who takes care of the everyday needs of her worshipers" (lines 263-269).

**Evidence presented:**
- No crown — unusual for a major deity in the LB period
- Nakedness — in an era when deities are increasingly shown clothed, nakedness signals something other than royal status
- The goddess "never appears in relation to other identifiable deities" (unlike the qudshu type, which appears with Reshef, Seth, etc.) — she has no place in the pantheon's relational network
- "It is not possible to associate her with any one particular name (Anat, Astarte, Asherah)" (lines 273-274). The doubled images might suggest twin goddesses (Anat AND Astarte), but this is speculative.
- The term "Astartes" (plural) in Hebrew and Akkadian "is not to be understood as a plural proper name (analogous in English to 'Marys'), but as a generic indicator and is to be translated as '(protecting) goddesses'" (lines 282-284)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The absence of crown, the nakedness-in-an-era-of-clothing, and the lack of relational iconography are all well-documented. MEDIUM on the specific name question — the evidence genuinely doesn't permit a specific identification, which is itself an important finding.

**What's at stake:** If these figurines can't be assigned a specific divine name, then calling them "Astarte plaques" (as the scholarly convention does) is misleading. They're "naked goddess plaques" — and which goddess they represented probably varied by time and place. This complicates Dever's tendency to treat ALL female figurines as "Asherah."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who identify the plaques specifically with Astarte (Pritchard 1943), Asherah (Dever), or Anat. Winter (1983) leans toward multiple possible identifications.

**Alternative reading:** The lack of specific attributes doesn't mean the goddess HAD no name — it means the NAME wasn't iconographically marked. Worshipers knew which goddess they were addressing even if we can't tell from the image alone.

**My assessment:** Their agnosticism about the name is methodologically honest. The important point is that these are goddesses of a specific TYPE — personal, accessible, female-identified — not that they can be matched to a specific name from the Ugaritic pantheon.

## Claim 3: The goddess represented in the plaques was primarily a figure for WOMEN — associated with pregnancy, childbirth, and female life-cycle rituals

**Author's claim:** "The relationship of the 'Astarte plaques' to the Gravidae flasks, with their emphasis on female sexuality leading up to motherhood, permits us to assume that the 'Astarte plaques' played a special role in the piety of women" (lines 353-355). "No evidence of males in this posture has been found, showing that the goddess was first and foremost an identification figure for women" (lines 379-380).

**Evidence presented:**
- Gravidae flasks: small pitchers in the shape of the female body, filled with oil "to help prevent torn tissue and stretch marks during advanced pregnancy" (lines 336-337). Found in LB II through Iron IIA contexts.
- A vessel from Tell Qasile with holes in the breasts "through which the liquid from the container may be poured" — suggesting "the presentation of the breasts... could be understood as an offering of, or the pressing out of, the mother's milk" (lines 347-350).
- The hand-on-belly gesture on many plaques emphasizes the lower stomach region — pregnancy and childbirth concerns.
- On shrine plaques, smaller female figures appear alongside the goddess — "probably a worshiper or donor" — always female. "No evidence of males in this posture has been found."
- A mold from Tall Munbaqa (northern Syria) is inscribed on the back with the cuneiform sign for "woman" (sinnistu) — "probably to be understood as identifying the goddess as an exemplary woman" (lines 386-387).
- Where grave contexts are documented, "the dead individuals next to which the plaques were placed were female" (W. Helck, cited at line 362).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Gravidae flask connection is strong. The exclusively-female grave associations are suggestive but the sample size isn't specified. The "woman" inscription on the mold is a single piece of evidence but striking.

**What's at stake:** This radically reframes the figurine discussion. The "Astarte plaques" aren't evidence for a generic "goddess worship" that included men and women equally. They're evidence for a specifically FEMALE religious practice centered on the female body and its life-cycle. The goddess here isn't a consort or a cosmic power — she's a protectress of pregnancy, a model of womanhood, a figure with whom women identified.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the plaques as evidence for a broadly popular goddess cult that included male worshipers. Also scholars who connect them to "sacral prostitution" (which Keel & Uehlinger mention as a possible "initiation rite" but don't endorse — lines 395-402).

**Alternative reading:** The plaques could have been used by men ON BEHALF OF women (husbands seeking protection for pregnant wives), which would broaden the cult's social base beyond the interpretation offered here.

**My assessment:** The female-specific reading is well-supported by the comparative evidence (Gravidae flasks, female grave associations). But it's also a reading that fits neatly with the broader Keel & Uehlinger thesis — that goddess worship in the Iron Age was becoming increasingly compartmentalized into "women's sphere" rather than being a central element of the shared religious symbol system.

## Claim 4: The modern question "goddess or woman?" is a false binary — the plaques portray the goddess AS a woman and the woman AS the goddess

**Author's claim:** "The modern question about whether a figure represents a goddess or a woman, posing strict alternatives, is too short-sighted. The plaques portray the goddess as a woman, and conversely they identify the female worshipers above the plaques with the goddess" (lines 388-393).

**Evidence presented:**
- The goddess lacks the attributes that distinguish major deities (crown, relational iconography) — she looks like an ordinary woman
- The cuneiform inscription "woman" (sinnistu) on the mold is used "as an epithet for Ishtar and other goddesses" in Akkadian texts — so the word bridges divine and human
- The smaller female figures on shrine plaques (worshipers/donors) mirror the goddess's posture and appearance
- "It may be that the women pay for the cost of the plaque as a votive gift or they might keep this image in the home in hopes of a blessing"
- The goddess IS "an exemplary woman" — not a distant cosmic power but a model of embodied female experience

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is an interpretive claim about ancient mentality that can't be proven from artifacts alone. But it's consistent with the evidence and avoids the anachronistic either/or thinking that plagues much figurine scholarship.

**What's at stake:** This dissolves the debate between those who say "these are goddesses" (Pritchard, Winter, Dever) and those who say "these are mortal women/votive figures" (Tadmor, H. Weippert). They're BOTH — the goddess embodied in female form, the woman identifying with the goddess. The boundary between divine and human is porous in a way that modern categories struggle to capture.

**Who disagrees:** Both sides of the goddess-vs-mortal debate. Tadmor's camp would say they ARE mortal. Dever's camp would say they're clearly goddesses. Keel & Uehlinger are saying the question is badly framed.

**Alternative reading:** The "both/and" reading might be too clever by half — a way to avoid choosing between interpretations that have real scholarly consequences. If you can't tell a goddess from a worshiper, then the figurines can't be used as evidence for EITHER.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most sophisticated moves in the book. It connects to the "transparency" concept from Ch VII — the idea that symbols can carry divine presence without depicting it anthropomorphically. Here, the goddess is present IN the woman's body, not distinct FROM it. This is a fundamentally different model of divine presence than the "great god on throne" model of official religion.

## Claim 5: The "Astarte plaques" are a CITY phenomenon — they're not found in early Israelite hill country settlements, which means they're not evidence for Israelite folk religion

**Author's claim:** "It is especially noteworthy that all the plaques that have been found to date come only from the cities and their environs; none have been found in the small, early Iron Age settlements in the hill country" (lines 37-39).

**Evidence presented:**
- Representative pieces come from Tel Zeror, Tell Beit Mirsim, Gezer, Lachish, Taanach — all urban sites
- The early Iron Age hill country settlements (proto-Israelite) have produced NO Astarte plaques
- This distribution contrasts with the qudshu-type plaques, which have a wider distribution

**Confidence:** HIGH. The distribution data is archaeological fact. The absence from hill country sites could theoretically be overturned by new finds, but the pattern is currently clear.

**What's at stake:** THIS IS THE CRITICAL FINDING FOR THE TRUTH PROJECT. If the "Astarte plaques" are a Canaanite city phenomenon and NOT found in proto-Israelite settlements, then they cannot be used as evidence for popular Israelite goddess worship. Dever's argument that goddess figurines represent widespread folk religion suppressed by Jerusalem orthodoxy depends on continuity between these LB/Iron I plaques and later Iron II figurines. Keel & Uehlinger show that continuity is broken — the plaques disappear from the archaeological record precisely in the areas and periods where Israelite settlement emerges.

**Who disagrees:** Dever implicitly, by treating the figurine evidence as continuous from LB through Iron II. Scholars who assume cultural continuity between Canaanite cities and Israelite villages.

**Alternative reading:** The absence from hill country sites could reflect different deposition practices (hill country villagers might have discarded plaques differently) rather than absence of use. Or the plaques might have been used in hill country but in perishable materials (wood, cloth) that didn't survive.

**My assessment:** The absence is striking and significant. Combined with the finding that the plaques are primarily a women's piety phenomenon (Claim 3) and that they're non-royal, every day goddesses (Claim 2), the picture that emerges is: these figurines represent a specifically Canaanite URBAN women's religious practice that did NOT transfer to the Israelite hill country. The goddess worship that Dever documents in Iron II (Judean Pillar Figurines) is a DIFFERENT phenomenon — later, different type, different distribution — not a survival of the Astarte plaque tradition.

## Excursus Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Plaques = goddesses, not concubines | VERY HIGH | Shrine model evidence is decisive |
| 2: Non-royal, unnameable everyday goddess | HIGH | New inscribed finds with divine names |
| 3: Primarily women's piety | MEDIUM-HIGH | Male-associated finds undiscovered |
| 4: "Goddess or woman?" is false binary | MEDIUM | Anachronism objection — maybe they DID distinguish |
| 5: City phenomenon, absent from hill country | HIGH | New hill country finds, but pattern is robust |

**Strongest section:** §57(c) on shrine models. The Tell Qasile shrine facade with TWO naked goddesses inside is the single most decisive piece of evidence in the excursus.

**Weakest section:** §60 on initiation rites and "sacral prostitution." This is speculative and underdeveloped — they raise it as a question rather than an argument.

**Most consequential finding for Dever's figurine argument:** The city/hill-country distribution gap. If Astarte plaques aren't found in proto-Israelite settlements, then Dever's narrative of continuous folk goddess worship from Canaanite through Israelite periods has a missing link. The Iron II Judean Pillar Figurines that Dever relies on (Ch VIII of Keel & Uehlinger) are a SEPARATE phenomenon with a different iconography, different distribution, and likely different function.

**What this means for the big picture:** The excursus shows that Keel & Uehlinger are not "denying the goddess" — they're being PRECISE about WHICH goddess, WHERE, WHEN, and for WHOM. The Astarte plaques are real goddess worship. But it's Canaanite city-dwelling women's religion, not Israelite folk religion. And it doesn't survive into the Iron II period in the same form. The "Great Goddess" narrative that Dever and feminist theologians find attractive flattens these distinctions into a single story. The iconographic evidence won't support that flattening.
