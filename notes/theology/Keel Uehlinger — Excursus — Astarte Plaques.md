38|# Keel & Uehlinger — Excursus: The Late Bronze and Early Iron Age So-Called "Astarte Plaques"
39|
40|This is the book's systematic treatment of the naked female terra-cotta plaques that dominate scholarly discussion of goddess worship in Late Bronze and Iron Age I Palestine. The excursus makes two moves that fundamentally complicate the "Astarte plaques = goddess figurines = popular Asherah worship" equation that Dever and others rely on. First: these ARE goddesses, not mortuary "concubines" (contra Tadmor). Second: they're goddesses of a very specific type — non-royal, non-mythological, everyday protectresses associated primarily with WOMEN'S piety and the female life-cycle — and they're NOT found in early Israelite hill country settlements.

44|## Claim 1: The "Astarte plaques" depict goddesses, not mortal women
→ [[claim-astarte-plaques-depict-goddesses-not-concubines]] | **keel-ggi-excursus.1** | Confidence: very-high
  Three mutually complementary paths of argumentation (typology, archaeological context, iconographic context) converge. The Tell Qasile shrine model with naked female figures inside a shrine facade is decisive — you don't put mortal women inside shrine facades.
  Contradicts: Tadmor (1982), H. Weippert (1988)
  
## Claim 2: Non-royal, every day goddess — not identifiable with a specific name
→ [[claim-astarte-plaques-non-royal-everyday-goddess-unnamed]] | **keel-ggi-excursus.2** | Confidence: high
  No crown, nakedness-in-an-era-of-clothing, no relational iconography, no place in the pantheon's network. The term "Astartes" is a generic indicator meaning "(protecting) goddesses." This complicates Dever's tendency to call all female figurines "Asherah."
  Contradicts: Pritchard (1943 = Astarte), Dever (= Asherah)

## Claim 3: Primarily women's piety — pregnancy, childbirth, female life-cycle
→ [[claim-astarte-plaques-womens-piety-pregnancy-childbirth]] | **keel-ggi-excursus.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  Gravidae flasks, nursing vessels, hand-on-belly gesture, exclusively female worshipers on shrine plaques, "woman" inscription on mold. Not a broadly popular cult including men — specifically female religious practice.
  Contradicts: Scholars reading plaques as broadly popular cult with male worshipers

## Claim 4: "Goddess or woman?" is a false binary
→ [[claim-goddess-or-woman-false-binary-identification]] | **keel-ggi-excursus.4** | Confidence: medium
  The plaques portray the goddess AS a woman and the woman AS the goddess. The cuneiform "woman" inscription bridges divine and human. The boundary between divine and human is porous in ways modern categories struggle to capture.
  Contradicts: Both sides of the goddess-vs-mortal debate

## Claim 5: City phenomenon — absent from early Israelite hill country
→ [[claim-astarte-plaques-city-phenomenon-absent-hill-country]] | **keel-ggi-excursus.5** | Confidence: high
  All findspots are urban (Tel Zeror, Tell Beit Mirsim, Gezer, Lachish, Taanach). Zero examples from proto-Israelite hill country settlements. This breaks the continuity Dever's figurine argument depends on — the plaques can't be evidence for Israelite folk religion.
  Contradicts: Dever (implicitly, by assuming LB→Iron II figurine continuity)

166|**Strongest section:** §57(c) on shrine models. The Tell Qasile shrine facade with TWO naked goddesses inside is the single most decisive piece of evidence in the excursus.
167|
168|**Weakest section:** §60 on initiation rites and "sacral prostitution." This is speculative and underdeveloped — they raise it as a question rather than an argument.
169|
170|**Most consequential finding for Dever's figurine argument:** The city/hill-country distribution gap. If Astarte plaques aren't found in proto-Israelite settlements, then Dever's narrative of continuous folk goddess worship from Canaanite through Israelite periods has a missing link. The Iron II Judean Pillar Figurines that Dever relies on (Ch VIII of Keel & Uehlinger) are a SEPARATE phenomenon with a different iconography, different distribution, and likely different function.
171|
172|**What this means for the big picture:** The excursus shows that Keel & Uehlinger are not "denying the goddess" — they're being PRECISE about WHICH goddess, WHERE, WHEN, and for WHOM. The Astarte plaques are real goddess worship. But it's Canaanite city-dwelling women's religion, not Israelite folk religion. And it doesn't survive into the Iron II period in the same form. The "Great Goddess" narrative that Dever and feminist theologians find attractive flattens these distinctions into a single story. The iconographic evidence won't support that flattening.
