# Day — Chapter 2: Yahweh and Asherah

Day argues that Asherah was genuinely worshipped as a goddess in ancient Israel — not just as a cult object — and that she functioned as Yahweh's consort in syncretistic circles, an appropriation from El's consort Athirat that the Deuteronomists eventually rejected and the Chronicler had forgotten entirely.

---

## Claim 1: Asherah appears as a GODDESS (not just a cult object) in several OT passages

**Author's claim:** "There can therefore be no doubt that there are several places in the Old Testament where the word 'Asherah' denotes the goddess herself, and not simply the cult symbol of Asherah" (p. 46). The key passages are Judg 3:7; 1 Kgs 15:13; 18:19; 2 Kgs 21:7; 23:4.

**Evidence presented (five passages examined):**

1. **2 Kgs 23:4:** Josiah removes "vessels made for Baal, for Asherah, and for all the host of heaven." Asherah sits between Baal and the host of heaven — both divinities. "It would be extremely forced not to understand Asherah here as the name of a deity" (p. 43). Mark Smith and Judith Hadley read it as a cult object here; Day says their reading is strained.

2. **2 Kgs 21:7:** Manasseh's "graven image of Asherah" (*pesel haʼašerah*). Day argues this means "image of the goddess Asherah" — not "image of the Asherah cult object," which would be "an image of a symbol... an image of what was in effect already a kind of image" (p. 44). The redundancy argument is strong.

3. **1 Kgs 15:13:** Asa removes Maacah "because she had a horrid thing made for Asherah." If Asherah here meant the cult object, "that would imply an idolatrous object made for another idolatrous object, which does not seem very plausible" (p. 44).

4. **1 Kgs 18:19:** Elijah summons "prophets of Baal and prophets of Asherah." The parallelism "can mean only that a divine name is intended" (p. 44). Day concedes this is likely a post-exilic addition (Hexaplaric asterisk, no role in subsequent contest), but "whoever added the reference... must have understood Asherah to be a divine name, which implies continued awareness of this goddess amongst the Jews at this relatively late date" (p. 45).

5. **Judg 3:7:** "Serving the Baals and the Asheroth." The plural parallelism with "the Baals" makes divine reference "undeniable" (p. 45). Some would emend to "Ashtaroth" (Peshitta, Vulgate, two Hebrew mss), but "Asheroth" is *lectio difficilior* — easier to explain a corruption *to* Ashtaroth than *from* it (p. 46).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The cumulative case from multiple passages, using different arguments in each (parallelism, redundancy, context), is convincing. Day directly engages the counter-arguments from Smith and Hadley.

**What's at stake:** If Day is right, the OT itself attests that Asherah was worshipped as a goddess — not just a harmless wooden pole. The Deuteronomists weren't just cutting down trees; they were suppressing a goddess cult. This changes how we read every "Asherah" reference in the OT.

**Who disagrees:** Mark S. Smith (*Early History of God*, pp. 88-94) — rejects goddess interpretation, reads "Asherah" as cult object in all cases. Judith Hadley (*The Cult of Asherah*, pp. 62-63) — argues "by the time of dtr... the term had ceased to be used with any knowledge of the goddess." Steve Wiggins emends Judg 3:7 to Ashtaroth.

**Alternative reading:** Smith's position: all OT references are to the cult object. The goddess had been so thoroughly demythologized that even Deuteronomistic authors didn't perceive a divine referent. Day's counter: 1 Kgs 18:19 (even if post-exilic addition) proves awareness of the goddess persisted.

**My assessment:** Day's strongest argument is the redundancy point (image of a symbol) in 2 Kgs 21:7 and 1 Kgs 15:13. The parallelism arguments are also strong. Smith's counter-position feels like motivated reasoning — he needs "Asherah" to be a cult object to support his "convergence and differentiation" model where Yahweh absorbed attributes without a consort. Day's reading is more natural.

## Claim 2: OT Asherah = Ugaritic Athirat (El's consort) — the equation is secure

**Author's claim:** The equation of Asherah with Ugaritic Athirat is "the natural assumption" — phonetic equivalence is clear, and "it would be very odd if these equivalent forms did not denote the same deity" (p. 48).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Phonetic equivalence:** Ugaritic *ʼaṯrt* → Hebrew *ʼăšērâ* via intermediate form *ʼšrt* (attested at Ekron, and the base for "his Asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud/Khirbet el-Qom) (p. 48)
2. **Structural expectation:** Every other major Ugaritic deity appears in the OT. It would be bizarre for Athirat to be absent and for a different unknown goddess to appear instead
3. **Theological logic:** Yahweh was equated with El. Athirat was El's wife. It's "entirely expected for Yahweh to have appropriated El's wife" (p. 48)
4. **Mother of the gods:** Athirat was mother of the gods at Ugarit. Asherah is mentioned four times alongside "the host of heaven" (2 Kgs 17:16; 21:3; 23:4, 6-7) — and the host of heaven corresponds to the sons of God/El (cf. Job 38:7; KTU² 1.10.I.3-4)
5. **Shared tree symbolism:** Both Athirat and Asherah were symbolized by a stylized tree (see Claim 4 below)

**Replies to Bernhardt's critique** (the most detailed challenge to the equation):
- **Objection 1:** Athirat is mother of gods at Ugarit, but Asherah is a fertility goddess in the OT. Day: Athirat is called Qudshu at Ugarit, and Egyptian Qudshu depictions (contemporary with Ugaritic texts) show her as an erotic fertility goddess (p. 49, citing ANEP plates 470-474)
- **Objection 2:** Athirat is El's consort, but Asherah is paired with Baal in the OT. Day: This is a Deuteronomistic polemical move — guilt by association — not an authentic tradition (p. 49, developed later in chapter)
- **Objection 3:** Athirat is "Lady of the Sea" but Asherah has no sea connection. Day: "weak argument from silence" (p. 49)

**Confidence:** HIGH. Day's structural argument (every other major Ugaritic deity appears in the OT) is particularly elegant. Bernhardt's objections are each individually weak and collectively insufficient.

**What's at stake:** If Athirat = Asherah, we have a continuous goddess tradition from Late Bronze Age Ugarit through monarchic Israel into the Persian period. The goddess didn't appear from nowhere in Israel — she was inherited Canaanite religious furniture.

**Who disagrees:** A. Caquot ("Athirat and Asherah have nothing in common but their names"), T. Binger ("insufficient evidence"), K.-H. Bernhardt (three detailed objections). All are minority positions.

**Alternative reading:** None that's credible. Day's structural argument is close to decisive.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most secure equations in the entire field. Day dispatches the critics efficiently. The Ekron inscriptions (ʼšrt) provide the missing link in the phonetic chain between Ugaritic *ʼaṯrt* and Hebrew *ʼăšērâ*. The mother-of-gods / host-of-heaven connection is the clincher.

## Claim 3: "Yahweh and his Asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud/Khirbet el-Qom refers to the CULT OBJECT, not the goddess directly — BUT the cult object symbolized the goddess and implied consort relationship

**Author's claim:** The translation "Yahweh and his Asherah" is correct, and the Asherah here is the wooden cult symbol (not the goddess directly). BUT this cult symbol "was clearly regarded as a symbol of the goddess Asherah" (p. 60), and the relationship between Yahweh and Asherah "comes near to being a certainty" as god-and-consort (p. 61).

**Evidence presented (cult object, not goddess):**
1. **Pronominal suffix problem:** "In ancient Hebrew idiom personal names do not take pronominal suffixes" — so Asherah "could appropriately be 'the Asherah' but not simply the goddess 'Asherah'" (p. 52). The suffix *-h* on *ʼšrth* means "his," which can't attach to a personal name in Hebrew
2. **Analogies for cult object as source of blessing:** Neo-Assyrian letters: "May Uruk and Eanna bless my lord"; Byblos inscription: "To our Lord and the image of Baal... May they bless"; Elephantine: swearing "by the temple and by Anat-Yahu" (AP 44.3); rabbinic: swearing by temple and altar; NT: swearing by gold of the temple (Mt 23:16-22) (p. 53)
3. **The drawings are Bes, not Yahweh/Asherah:** P. Beck showed the two similar figures on pithos A are the Egyptian god Bes — "they have nothing to do with the inscription" (p. 51-52). The stylized tree flanked by ibexes on the same pithos probably depicts the Asherah cult object mentioned in the inscription

**Evidence presented (consort relationship):**
1. The Asherah cult object "stood in a special relationship with Yahweh" — it was in Yahweh's sanctuary, paired with the *maṣṣēbâ* (male deity symbol) in Deut 16:21-22 (p. 60-61)
2. The cult object symbolized the goddess. If the symbol was Yahweh's, so was the goddess
3. In Canaanite religion, Asherah was El's consort. Yahweh was equated with El. Ergo: Asherah was Yahweh's consort (p. 61)
4. The pairing of Asherah with Baal in the OT is a Deuteronomistic polemical move (Olyan's theory): "guilt by association" to discredit her (p. 61-62). This is never attested in any extra-biblical text

**Confidence:** HIGH for the cult object reading of the inscriptions; MEDIUM-HIGH for the consort conclusion. Day's own position on the grammar question is widely shared (Emerton, Tigay, Olyan, Keel/Uehlinger, Wiggins, Hadley). The consort conclusion follows logically but requires the chain: cult object → symbolized goddess → goddess was El's consort → Yahweh = El → therefore Yahweh's consort. Each link is individually plausible but the chain has four steps.

**What's at stake:** This is the single most explosive question in the field — did Yahweh have a wife? Day says yes, the evidence "comes near to being a certainty." If true, ancient Israelite religion was more polytheistic (or at least dytheistic) than the biblical text admits.

**Who disagrees:** Those reading the goddess directly (Coogan, Dietrich/Loretz, Xella — arguing pronominal suffixes on personal names are attested in other Semitic languages, just not Hebrew). Those denying the consort relationship entirely — they would argue the cult object was just a traditional fertility symbol without implying divine marriage. Mark Smith is characteristically cautious.

**Alternative reading:** The Asherah cult object absorbed attributes of the goddess but was functionally a Yahwistic symbol — like the bronze serpent (Nehushtan) — rather than evidence of a consort goddess. This preserves the archaeological data without the theological claim.

**My assessment:** Day's grammar argument (pronominal suffix can't attach to personal names in Hebrew) is the mainstream position and well-supported. The analogies for cult objects as sources of blessing are effective. The consort conclusion is harder to evade than Day's critics admit — if the symbol of Asherah was paired with Yahweh in his sanctuary and the symbol represented the goddess, it's hard to see how the goddess herself wasn't being worshipped alongside him. Dever's archaeological evidence (3000+ female figurines) adds independent weight Day doesn't fully leverage.

## Claim 4: The Asherah was a stylized tree, not a living tree, grove, shrine, or simple image

**Author's claim:** "There has been growing evidence in recent years that enables us to be more precise and specify that the Asherah was not simply a bare pole but more specifically a stylized tree" (p. 56).

**Evidence presented (what it was NOT):**
1. **NOT living trees** (LXX "grove," Mishnah trees): OT speaks of "making" (*ʻāśâ*) Asherim, "building" (*bānâ*), "erecting" (*nāṣab*) — verbs you don't use for living trees. Jer 17:2 mentions Asherim "beside every luxuriant tree" — odd if Asherim were themselves living trees (p. 54-55)
2. **NOT groves or shrines** (Lipinski): Both meanings would have to coexist in identical contexts, which makes no sense. "Cutting down" a shrine is incoherent (p. 55)
3. **NOT simply images of the goddess** (Reed): Asherim are listed alongside *pesîlîm* "graven images" as distinct objects in Deut 7:5, 12:3, Mic 5:12-13, 2 Chron 33:7, 19, 34:3-4, 7 (p. 55). However, anthropomorphic images of Asherah DID exist separately: *pesel hāʼăšērâ* in 2 Kgs 21:7
4. **NOT the pillar figurines** (Engle): The verbs indicate wooden objects (not pottery), the Asherim were public/syncretistic (figurines mostly in private houses), and Asherim are distinguished from images per se (p. 56)

**Evidence presented (stylized tree):**
1. **Kuntillet Ajrud pithos A:** Stylized tree flanked by two ibexes above a lion — Hestrin and Hadley argue this depicts the Asherah mentioned in the inscription (p. 57)
2. **Lachish ewer** (13th c. BCE): Tree flanked by ibexes with inscription *mtn. šy l[rb]ty ʼlt* "Mattan. An offering to my Lady Elat" — AND Elat (= Asherah) appears directly above the tree drawing (p. 57)
3. **Lachish goblet:** Four times depicts ibexes flanking a pubic triangle instead of a tree — "further strengthening the view that the tree symbolizes a fertility goddess" (p. 57)
4. **Late Bronze pendants:** Naked female with tree/branch above pubic triangle, sometimes with Hathor headdress characteristic of Qudshu (= Asherah) (p. 57)
5. **Taanach cult stand** (10th c. BCE): Four tiers — second tier has sacred tree between ibexes, fourth tier has naked female between lions. Same flanking lions = same deity, so tree = Asherah's symbol (p. 57-58)
6. **Pella cult stands:** Nude goddesses on lion's head; stylized trees on sides (p. 58)
7. **Hos 14:9 (ET 8):** Yahweh IS a "luxuriant cypress" — uniquely compared to a tree, in a context condemning idols. Day argues this is Hosea appropriating Asherah's tree symbolism for Yahweh: "It is I who answer and look after him" (*ʼănî ʻānîtî waʼăšûrennû*) = wordplay on Anat and Asherah (p. 58-59). The "Isaiah apocalypse" writer (Isa 27:9) detected this and polemicized against it

**Confidence:** HIGH for stylized tree identification. The iconographic convergence (Lachish ewer + Kuntillet Ajrud + Taanach + Pella + pendants + goblet) is extensive. MEDIUM for the Hosea wordplay.

**What's at stake:** The Asherah was a recognizable religious symbol with consistent iconography across centuries and sites. This wasn't folk improvisation — it was an established cultic tradition with a specific visual vocabulary.

**Who disagrees:** Lemaire (living trees — but the "making" verbs are decisive against this). Lipinski (grove/shrine — but the cutting-down language kills shrine, and "under every luxuriant tree" kills grove). Olyan and Frevel think Hos 14:9 doesn't necessarily reference Asherah (Day concedes the Isaiah apocalypse dependence doesn't *prove* the Hosea reading was correct, but believes it was).

**My assessment:** The iconographic evidence Day marshals is impressive and multi-site. The Lachish ewer is the closest thing to a labeled depiction — "my Lady Elat" above the tree. The Taanach stand's parallel tiers make the tree-goddess equation explicit. Day's weakest link is the Hosea 14 wordplay (it IS clever and characteristic of Hosea, but the double wordplay on both Anat AND Asherah in a single verb is a lot to hang on one word).

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Asherah as goddess in OT (not just cult object) | HIGH | Smith/Hadley reading all references as cult object |
| OT Asherah = Ugaritic Athirat | HIGH | Bernhardt's objections (individually weak, collectively insufficient) |
| "His Asherah" = cult object at Kuntillet Ajrud | HIGH | Other Semitic languages allow pronominal suffix on personal names |
| Consort relationship with Yahweh | MEDIUM-HIGH | Alternative: cult object as Yahwistic symbol without divine marriage |
| Asherah was a stylized tree | HIGH | Lemaire's living tree theory (verb usage decisive against it) |

**Strongest section:** The rebuttal of "living tree" and "grove/shrine" theories. Day systematically eliminates wrong answers before presenting his positive case. The verb usage point (ʻāśâ, bānâ, nāṣab) is simple and devastating.

**Weakest section:** The Hosea 14 wordplay. It's creative and consistent with Hosea's methods, but a double wordplay (Anat + Asherah from a single verb *ʼăšûrennû*) is a lot to ask of a consonantal text with no vowels. The Isaiah apocalypse dependence is a better argument than the wordplay itself.

**Most important methodological move:** Day's treatment of the Baal-Asherah pairing as Deuteronomistic polemic (following Olyan). This is crucial — if the Baal-Asherah pairing is authentic tradition rather than polemic, the El-Asherah → Yahweh-Asherah chain weakens. Day's argument that no extra-biblical text pairs Baal with Asherah is a strong point.

**Cross-reference notes:** Smith (*Early History*, Ch 3) agrees on many details but disagrees on the goddess question and is far more cautious about the consort relationship. Dever (Ch V-VI) provides archaeological evidence Day barely touches — 3000+ pillar figurines, multiple shrines. Römer (Ch 9) largely agrees with Day. The Kuntillet Ajrud debate has evolved since Day wrote: Smoak and Schniedewind (2019) now argue the Bes identification definitively rules out the drawings as Yahweh/Asherah, confirming Day's already-stated position.
