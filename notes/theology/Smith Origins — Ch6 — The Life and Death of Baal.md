# Smith — Chapter 6: The Life and Death of Baal

Smith demolishes Frazer's category of "dying and rising gods" through a detailed critique of its intellectual origins and a case-by-case examination of the figures involved. His constructive argument: Baal's death in the Baal Cycle was modeled not on agricultural cycles but on royal funerary ritual (specifically CAT 1.161). Baal embodies the death of the old king and the life of the new — his kingship is cosmic, not seasonal. Israel's lack of a Yahweh-vs-Death mythology is explained by the fact that the Baal-Mot conflict was a specifically Ugaritic literary innovation, not a pan-West Semitic tradition.

---

## Claim 1: Frazer's "dying and rising gods" is a modern myth
→ [[claim-frazer-dying-rising-gods-modern-myth]] | **smith-obm-6.1** | Confidence: very-high
  Frazer's category was shaped by his classical education and unstated interest in explaining Jesus' resurrection — not from primary evidence. His "patternism" abstracted features from cultural contexts, producing categories that never existed.
  Supports: [[claim-immortality-norm-divine-death-narrative-polemic]]. Contradicts: Frazer's paradigm and interpretations dependent on it (Mettinger, Day on Hosea 5-6, Gaster).

---

## Claim 2: The "dying and rising gods" are fundamentally different
→ [[claim-dying-rising-gods-fundamentally-different]] | **smith-obm-6.2** | Confidence: high
  Osiris (does NOT resurrect — becomes king of netherworld), Dumuzi (deified king conflated with vegetation deity), Melqart (late classical evidence only), Adonis (thin indigenous evidence), Baal (royal funerary connection). Each reflects different cultural logic.
  Depends on: [[claim-frazer-dying-rising-gods-modern-myth]]. Contradicts: Frazer's unified category.

---

## Claim 3: CAT 1.161 provides the interpretive key to Baal's death
→ [[claim-cat-1-161-royal-funerary-key-baal-death]] | **smith-obm-6.3** | Confidence: very-high
  Four verbatim parallels between the royal funerary liturgy (CAT 1.161) and the Baal Cycle (1.5-1.6): mourning gestures, the descent formula ('atr b'l + 'arṣ), Shapshu's role, and offerings sequence. Baal's death was modeled on royal funerary ritual, not agricultural cycles.
  Depends on: [[claim-immortality-norm-divine-death-narrative-polemic]], [[claim-frazer-dying-rising-gods-modern-myth]]. Supports: [[claim-baal-kingship-three-levels-divine-human-natural]]. Contradicts: Frazer's agricultural-cycle reading.

---

## Claim 4: Baal's kingship operates on three levels
→ [[claim-baal-kingship-three-levels-divine-human-natural]] | **smith-obm-6.4** | Confidence: high
  Divine (order vs. conflict), human (king's life and death), natural (abundance vs. desiccation). Baal is WEAK compared to Marduk and Yahweh — his kingship is fragile, reflecting Ugarit's political position as a small kingdom. His death encodes dynastic vulnerability, not seasonal cycles.
  Depends on: [[claim-cat-1-161-royal-funerary-key-baal-death]], [[claim-baals-local-manifestations-one-deity]]. Contradicts: seasonal-agricultural readings of the Baal Cycle.

---

## Claim 5: Israel lacked a Yahweh-vs-Death mythology because it was a Ugaritic innovation
→ [[claim-israel-lacked-death-conflict-ugaritic-innovation]] | **smith-obm-6.5** | Confidence: medium
  The Baal-Mot conflict was modeled on the earlier Baal-Yamm section under the influence of royal mortuary cult — it was a specifically Ugaritic literary innovation, not pan-West Semitic. Israel inherited Baal's storm-battle imagery (Leviathan, Sea, Sapan=Zion) but NOT the Mot conflict because there was nothing to inherit.
  Depends on: [[claim-cat-1-161-royal-funerary-key-baal-death]], [[claim-baal-kingship-three-levels-divine-human-natural]]. Supports: [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]]. Challenged by: the censorship model (priestly suppression, not original absence).

**Strongest section:** Section 4 (CAT 1.161 comparison). The four verbatim parallels between the ritual and the myth are the strongest empirical argument in the entire book. This is philological detective work at its best.

**Weakest section:** Section 1 (Frazer's biography/intellectual history). Interesting but overly long — the critique of Frazer has been made before, and Smith spends more time on Frazer's intellectual genealogy than on the constructive argument.

**Most important implication for the book's thesis:** If Baal's death was a specifically Ugaritic literary production tied to royal mortuary cult, then the "dying and rising god" was never part of Israel's West Semitic heritage. Israel didn't REJECT a dying-and-rising Yahweh — it never had one to reject. Monotheism's elimination of divine death was not a break with tradition but an extrapolation from a tradition that had already dissociated the chief god from death.

**Cross-reference:** This chapter is the pivot from Part II (traits of deities) to Part III (origins of monotheism). The argument that the Baal-Mot narrative was a Ugaritic literary innovation explains why Israel's monotheism could develop without confronting a death-mythology for Yahweh. Ch 5 established that immortality is a divine trait; Ch 6 explains why the EXCEPTION (Baal's death) doesn't apply to Israel's tradition. J.Z. Smith's earlier work on dying and rising gods is the major dialogue partner; Mark Smith (no relation) builds on it constructively.
