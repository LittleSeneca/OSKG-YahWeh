# Day — Chapter 7: Yahweh and the Underworld Deities (Mot, Resheph, Molech, and the Rephaim)

Day covers four categories of netherworld beings in Israelite and Canaanite religion. Mot provides language for Sheol. Resheph is a plague-demon archer. Molech is a genuine Canaanite underworld god who received child sacrifice and was NOT identified with Yahweh. The Rephaim are deified royal dead at Ugarit whose name was transferred to both underworld shades and legendary giant inhabitants of Canaan.

---

## Claim 1: Sheol language in the OT derives from Canaanite Mot imagery, but Sheol is not a fully personified deity

**Author's claim:** "Although language suggestive of personification is used of Sheol, this does not mean that Sheol is actually a person in the way that Mot was" (p. 186). The OT uses Mot's imagery (swallowing throat, insatiable appetite) but demythologized it.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Swallowing imagery:** Mot has "jaws reaching to earth, lips to heaven and a tongue to the stars" (KTU² 1.5.II.1-6). Sheol "has enlarged its throat and opened its mouth beyond measure" (Isa 5:14). The same word *nepeš* "throat" is used of both (p. 186)
2. **Insatiable appetite:** Hab 2:5: "His throat is as wide as Sheol; like death he has never enough." Prov 27:20; 30:15b-16. Mot's appetite is similarly boundless in Ugaritic texts (p. 186-187)
3. **Shepherd of death:** Ps 49:15 (ET 14): "Death shall be their shepherd" — recalls Mot making Baal "as lamb in my mouth" (KTU² 1.6.II.21-23) (p. 187)
4. **Irreverent irony in Isa 25:8:** "He will swallow up death forever." "There is an evident irony here, for... it is elsewhere Death or Mot that does the swallowing, but here it is the swallower that is to be swallowed up" (p. 187). Day argues this originally referred to exile, not literal abolition of death — the context speaks of "the reproach of his people" (exile) and Isa 26:19 (parallel passage) is about national restoration (p. 187-188)
5. **Azmaweth = "Mot is strong":** The place/personal name *ʻazmāwet* (2 Sam 23:31; Ezra 2:24; Neh 7:28) originally meant "strong as Mot [the god]" not just "strong as death." Ugaritic attests *mt ʻz* "Mot was strong" as a stereotyped phrase in the Baal-Mot combat (KTU² 1.6.VI.17-22). And Cant 8:6: "love is strong as death" (*ʻazzâ kammāwet*) preserves awareness of the mythological background — love's arrows (*rešāpeyhā*) derive from Resheph, the underworld god (p. 188-189)
6. **Jer 9:20 (ET 21) — possible but uncertain Mot allusion:** "Death has come up into our windows." Some (Cassuto) connected this to Baal's fear of Mot entering through a palace window (KTU² 1.4.V.58-VI.15) — but Day shows the Ugaritic text actually fears YAM, not Mot. "There are therefore no grounds for believing that this passage lies behind the imagery in Jer 9:20" (p. 190)

**Confidence:** HIGH for Mot-to-Sheol language transfer. MEDIUM for the specific Isa 25:8 interpretation (exile metaphor rather than literal).

**What's at stake:** The personification of Death in the OT is borrowed furniture from Canaanite mythology, but Israel demythologized it — Mot never becomes an independent rival to Yahweh the way he is Baal's opponent at Ugarit. The imagery survived; the theology didn't.

**My assessment:** Day's corrective to Cassuto on the Jer 9:20 window passage is a good example of his philological precision — he checks the Ugaritic text himself and finds the consensus attribution to Mot is wrong (it's Yam). The Azmaweth argument is clever and convincing.

## Claim 2: Resheph is a Canaanite underworld plague god whose arrows bring pestilence, and the OT preserves him as a demonic personification

**Author's claim:** Resheph "was an underworld god, whose arrows brought plague and pestilence" (p. 199). In the OT, *rešep* often retains personalized demonic force rather than being fully demythologized.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Ugaritic identification:** Resheph equated with Nergal (Mesopotamian underworld/plague god) at Ugarit (KTU² 1.47.27; 1.118.26). As sun goddess's gatekeeper (KTU² 1.78.2-4), he guarded the netherworld entrance. Epithets: "the archer Resheph" (*bʻl ḥẓ ršp*) in Ugaritic, "Resheph of the arrow" (*ršp ḥṣ*) in Phoenician (p. 199-200)
2. **Hab 3:5:** "Before him went Pestilence (*deber*) and Plague (*rešep*) followed close behind" — part of Yahweh's storm theophany. This mirrors Ugaritic KTU² 1.82 where "the archer Resheph shot his kidneys and his heart." The pattern of a leading god with two bodyguards is attested elsewhere (Adad with Shullat and Hanish in Gilgamesh) (p. 200-201)
3. **Ps 78:48-49:** "He gave over their cattle to the pestilence and their flocks to the plague (*rešāpîm*)." Day defends the emendation of MT *labbārād* "to the hail" to *laddeber* "to the pestilence" — because Ps 78 parallels the J plague narrative in Exodus and this reading completes all seven J plagues. The "destroying angels" in v. 49 likely allude to the *rešāpîm* (p. 201-202)
4. **Job 5:7:** "The sons of Resheph fly upwards." Day favors the mythological reading (demonic beings bringing pestilence from the underworld) over "sparks" (no certain meaning in Hebrew) or "birds" (the ancient versions' misunderstanding). This coheres with Job 5:6 — *ʻāpār* "dust" can denote the underworld (cf. Job 17:16). "Just as 'the firstborn of death' brings pestilence in Job 18:13... so here in Job 5:7 'the sons of [the underworld demon] Resheph' bring pestilence" (p. 203-204)
5. **Deut 32:24:** "fought by plague (*rešep*) and poisonous pestilence." The verb *lḥm* "fight" used with *rešep* echoes "the mythological archer god Resheph" — not just impersonal plague (p. 204)
6. **Cant 8:6 — demythologized but aware:** "Its arrows (*rešāpeyhā*) are arrows of (*rišpê*) fire." Love's arrows derive from Resheph's pestilential arrows. The juxtaposition with "love is strong as death" (*ʻazzâ kammāwet*) shows the poet was aware of the mythological background (Mot + Resheph both underworld deities) (p. 205)

**Confidence:** HIGH for Resheph as plague demon in OT. The convergence of Ugaritic (Nergal equation, archer epithet), Phoenician (Resheph of the arrow), and OT (parallel to *deber*, flying imagery, fighting verb) is strong.

**What's at stake:** The OT doesn't just have "angels" — it preserves the names, imagery, and functions of specific Canaanite underworld demons. Resheph is the clearest case of a Canaanite demon surviving with his identity intact in biblical literature.

**Who disagrees:** Those translating *rešep* as "sparks" or "thunderbolts" in Job 5:7 (KJV, RSV, NRSV). The ancient versions that read "birds." Day's mythological reading is more consistent with comparative evidence.

**My assessment:** Day's case is robust. The Hab 3:5 parallel to Ugaritic KTU² 1.82 is particularly impressive — Resheph as divine bodyguard/attendant in both texts. The Ps 78 emendation argument is well-reasoned and solves a genuine textual problem (the missing J plague).

## Claim 3: Molech was a genuine Canaanite underworld deity (not a type of sacrifice), received child sacrifice, and was NOT equated with Yahweh

**Author's claim:** Eissfeldt's 1935 theory that *mōlek* is a sacrificial term (like Punic *mlk*) rather than a divine name is "certainly incorrect" (p. 210). Molech is the Canaanite god *mlk*, an underworld deity, and child sacrifice was real. Furthermore, Molech was NOT equated with Yahweh by his worshippers.

**Evidence presented (Molech IS a god):**
1. Lev 20:5: "those who follow him in playing the harlot after *mōlek*" — the OT speaks of "playing the harlot after pagan deities" but NEVER "after a sacrifice" (p. 210)
2. Verbs used with Molech (*heʻĕbîr*, *nātan*, *śārap*) are elsewhere attested with *lĕ* + divine name, NEVER with *lĕ* + sacrificial term alone (p. 210-211)
3. Ugaritic serpent charms mention the god *mlk* (KTU² 1.100.41; 1.107.17) associated with Ashtaroth in Transjordan (p. 215)

**Evidence presented (child sacrifice was real):**
1. OT explicitly speaks of burning (Jer 7:31; 19:5; 32:35). Isa 30:33 takes imagery from Molech cult (oven/pyre prepared for "the king" *lammelek*) — "makes sense only on the sacrificial interpretation" (p. 212)
2. *Heʻĕbîr* "passed through the fire" = actual sacrifice, not harmless dedication: Exod 13:12-13 equates *hʻbyr* with *zbḥ* "sacrifice"; Ezek 20:26 confirms *hʻbyr* of firstborn was "terrifying" (p. 212)
3. Independent evidence: Classical sources, Punic inscriptions, archaeological evidence from Carthage, Egyptian depictions of human sacrifice ritual in Syria-Palestine (p. 212-213)

**Evidence presented (Molech = underworld deity):**
1. *Malik* equated with Nergal (Mesopotamian underworld god) in two separate god-lists (Old Babylonian and Late Assyrian) (p. 215)
2. Ugaritic text RS 1986.2235 pairs *Mlk* and Resheph (another underworld god) together — "fifteen jars for the horses of Resheph, fifteen jars for the horses of Mlk of Ashtaroth" (p. 215-216)
3. Isa 57:9: "You journeyed to Molech... and sent down even to Sheol" — locates Molech's dwelling in Sheol (p. 216)
4. Gehenna = valley of Hinnom where Molech's fiery rites occurred. "If Molech was an underworld god it would help explain why Gehenna became a term specifically for hell" (p. 216) — better than the popular "burning rubbish dump" theory (first attested only ~1200 CE in Kimhi!)

**Evidence presented (NOT equated with Yahweh):**
1. Molech sacrifices did NOT take place in Yahweh's temple — they were in the valley of Hinnom, "a little distance away" (p. 217)
2. Yahweh was "notably separated from the underworld through long periods of Israel's history" — Molech was an underworld deity (p. 217)
3. Children offered to Molech: boys AND girls (Jer 7:31; 32:35). Children offered to Yahweh: ONLY firstborn males (Exod 13:12-15). Numbers don't match (p. 217)
4. Jeremiah's repeated "which I did not command" in Molech passages (Jer 7:31; 19:5; 32:35) means "which I forbade" — same phrase used for astral worship in Deut 17:3 (p. 216-217)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH for Molech as god (against Eissfeldt). HIGH for child sacrifice and underworld character. MEDIUM-HIGH for non-equation with Yahweh (the firstborn-only vs. all-children distinction is the strongest argument).

**What's at stake:** Molech is the single most morally disturbing deity in the OT. If child sacrifice to Molech was real (and Day argues it was), and if some Israelites practiced it, the ethical stakes are enormous. The question of whether Molech was equated with Yahweh determines whether this was foreign apostasy or a horrific form of Yahwism.

**Who disagrees:** Eissfeldt, Albright (partially), Weinfeld, Stager/Wolff — Molech as sacrificial term. Snaith, Plataroti — no child sacrifice, only dedication. Weinfeld — OT accounts are unreliable polemic. Those equating Molech with Milkom (Ammonite), Adad-Milki, Mot, or Baal. Levenson and others — Molech sacrifices were originally to Yahweh.

**My assessment:** Day's 1989 monograph on Molech is the definitive treatment, and this chapter distills its key arguments. The refutation of Eissfeldt on two simple grammatical points (harlotry after a god, verbs with lĕ + divine name) is clean and definitive. The Gehenna etymology argument is characteristically sharp — the "burning rubbish dump" theory is a medieval invention that needs to die. The non-equation with Yahweh is important: child sacrifice to Molech was NOT a distorted form of Yahwism.

## Claim 4: The OT Rephaim (underworld shades) derive from Ugaritic *rpʼum* (deified royal dead), and the ethnic Rephaim (giants) developed from the underworld concept

**Author's claim:** "There is a direct relationship" between the underworld and ethnic Rephaim, and "the ethnic Rephaim derive from the underworld Rephaim" (p. 224). The connection is proven by the shared location Ashtaroth/Edrei.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Ugaritic *rpʼum* = deified dead:** KTU² 1.161 invokes "the ancient *rpʼum*" — clearly dead Ugaritic kings. KTU² 1.6.VI.46-48 parallels *rpʼim* with *ʼilnym* "gods" and *mtm* "the dead" (p. 218-219)
2. **OT Rephaim = shades of the dead:** Isa 14:9; 26:14, 19; Ps 88:11; Job 26:5; Prov 2:18; 9:18; 21:16 (p. 218)
3. **Divinization of dead in OT:** 1 Sam 28:13 — medium of Endor calls Samuel's shade "a god (*ʼĕlōhîm*) coming up out of the earth." Isa 8:19 — "should not a people consult their gods (*ʼĕlōhîm*), the dead on behalf of the living?" The OT preserves terminology from when the dead were considered divine (p. 219-220)
4. **Etymology:** Root is *rpʼ* "to heal" — *rpʼum* = "healers," possibly related to fertility provision (like Osiris). But in the OT, "all thought of the Rephaim as 'healers' or providers of fertility is absent" — we have "a demythologization of the original Canaanite concept" (p. 221-222)
5. **The CRITICAL LINK — Ashtaroth/Edrei:** The ethnic Rephaim are associated with Ashtaroth/Ashteroth-Karnaim in Transjordan (Gen 14:5; Josh 12:4; 13:12). At Ugarit, Rpʼu "dwells in Ashtaroth, the god who judges at Edrei" (KTU² 1.108.2-3). And Josh 12:4 says "Og, king of Bashan, one of the remnant of the Rephaim, who dwelt at Ashtaroth and at Edrei" — virtually identical language! (p. 224-225)
6. **Molech connection:** The god *Mlk* (Molech) also dwelt at Ashtaroth in Ugaritic texts (KTU² 1.100.41; 1.107.42). And in Jerusalem, the valley of Hinnom (Molech's cult site) joins the valley of Rephaim (Josh 18:16). "All this suggests that there is indeed a relationship between the underworld Rephaim and the ethnic Rephaim" (p. 225)
7. **Analogous patterns:** Nephilim = offspring of sons of God + daughters of men (Gen 6:1-4). Samson partly derives from sun god. Nimrod from Ninurta. The pattern of divine beings generating legendary human figures is cross-cultural (p. 225)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Ashtaroth/Edrei connection is Day's strongest original contribution here — the language of KTU² 1.108 ("dwells in Ashtaroth, judges at Edrei") and Josh 12:4 ("dwelt at Ashtaroth and at Edrei") is too close for coincidence.

**What's at stake:** The legendary "giants in the land" (Rephaim, Nephilim) are not garbled memories of tall people — they're demythologized Canaanite divine beings. The OT writers transformed underworld deities into ethnic predecessors of the Conquest.

**Who disagrees:** S. Talmon — no relationship between the two usages. J.R. Bartlett — the Transjordan association is late/Deuteronomistic invention. Schmidt — *rpʼum* were living figures, not dead kings.

**My assessment:** This is elegant comparative work. The Ashtaroth/Edrei parallel between Ugaritic Rpʼu and biblical Og is Day's smoking gun. The Molech connection (same location in Ugaritic, adjacent valleys in Jerusalem) adds independent corroboration. Day's conclusion that the ethnic concept is derivative from the underworld concept is well-supported by both the Ugaritic chronology (second millennium rpʼum predate the OT ethnic usage) and the cross-cultural pattern of divine beings generating legendary human figures.

## Chapter 7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Sheol language from Mot imagery (demythologized) | HIGH | Personification degree subjective — some passages may be more personified than Day allows |
| Resheph = Canaanite plague demon preserved in OT | HIGH | "Sparks" reading in Job 5:7 (RSV/NRSV) still has defenders |
| Molech = god (not sacrifice term) with real child sacrifice | VERY HIGH (god); HIGH (child sacrifice) | Weinfeld's polemic-as-fiction position (weak) |
| Molech NOT equated with Yahweh | MEDIUM-HIGH | Levenson's counter-argument from Philo of Byblos |
| Ethnic Rephaim derive from underworld Rephaim | HIGH | Ashtaroth/Edrei parallel is the clincher |

**Strongest section:** The Molech analysis. Day's 1989 monograph expertise shows — the refutation of Eissfeldt is methodical and devastating, and the underworld identification via Nergal equation and RS 1986.2235 (Mlk + Resheph pairing) is original and convincing.

**Weakest section:** The discussion of whether Anat's treatment of Mot lies behind the golden calf destruction (Exod 32:20). Day says no, but this is a minor point and doesn't affect the chapter's main arguments.

**Most important cross-reference:** The Rephaim section ties the entire chapter together — Mot, Resheph, and Molech are all underworld deities, and the Rephaim (shades) provide the conceptual framework for understanding Israel's netherworld. The Ashtaroth location connects Rephaim + Molech + the ethnic giants. Day's ability to trace a single location through Ugaritic, Phoenician, and biblical texts is exemplary comparative method.
