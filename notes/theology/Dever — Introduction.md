# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Introduction

---

## Claim 1: Dever's personal journey gives him a unique vantage point — he knows what's at stake

**Dever's claim:** His biography matters for understanding his book. He was raised fundamentalist ("My father was a fire-breathing fundamentalist preacher, sometime tent evangelist"), became a liberal Protestant minister, studied at Harvard under G. Ernest Wright, lost his faith through archaeology, converted to Reform Judaism, and now describes himself as "not a theist... essentially a secular humanist." He knows the Bible from the inside and knows what it costs to lose faith in it.

**Evidence — in his own words:**
- "I was reared in a deeply religious family in small towns in the South and Midwest. My father was a fire-breathing fundamentalist preacher, sometime tent evangelist, for a while a missionary in Jamaica, from whom I inherited a lifelong love of the Bible."
- "Finally I went to Harvard to study Old Testament theology... only to discover that while I had the necessary dogmatic temperament, I really had no talent for that discipline, and little patience. Indeed, theology by now seemed to me a dead end. What more could be learned from endless reinterpretation of the same texts? So I turned to archaeology."
- "The Hebrew Bible finally became real for me, indeed more 'credible,' because I dealt constantly with the tangible evidence."
- "I became a nominal Jew. Today I am somewhat active in the Reform community, but I am not observant, in fact not a theist."
- "To my parents, long gone, I am grateful for inculcating in me a deep respect for the Bible and an awareness of the awesome power of religion (even though they would be horrified to see how I have turned out)."

**Confidence:** HIGH that this is his genuine self-description. The significance for evaluating his work is that he is not a neutral observer — but neither is anyone else. Dever is explicit about his biases in a way that Smith and Römer are not.

**What's at stake for faith:** Dever's personal trajectory mirrors the faith deconstruction process itself. He started where many believers start, discovered that the evidence didn't support what he'd been taught, and reconstructed a different relationship to the tradition. His book is, in part, an account of what he found in the ground that made belief impossible — and what he found that still had value.

**Who would challenge this:** Conservatives might argue Dever's loss of faith predetermines his conclusions. Dever would respond that his conclusions drove his loss of faith, not the reverse — and that he's being more honest about his biases than scholars who pretend neutrality.

**My assessment:** Dever's candor about his personal journey is one of the book's strengths. He doesn't pretend to objectivity. He tells you where he stands and lets you evaluate his evidence accordingly. This is methodologically superior to the "objective scholar" pose.

## Claim 2: Ancient Israel had TWO religions — "Book religion" and "folk religion"

**Dever's claim:** The religion described in the Hebrew Bible ("Book religion") was the theology of a tiny elite — priests, scribes, reformers. It was monotheistic, aniconic, centralized in Jerusalem, and focused on obedience to written law. The religion actually practiced by most Israelites ("folk religion") was polytheistic, image-rich, domestic, and focused on fertility, healing, and survival. These two religions coexisted, overlapped, and eventually the Book religion won — but only at the very end of the monarchy, and only on paper.

**Evidence presented — Dever's chart of contrasts:**

| Book Religion | Folk Religion |
|--------------|---------------|
| Idealism / theology | "Real life" practice |
| National / centralized | Local / family-centered |
| Priests, prophets, sages | Ordinary people, especially women |
| Jerusalem Temple | Local shrines, high places, homes |
| Male deity alone | Female deity (Asherah) as consort |
| Abstract principles / morality | "Care and feeding of the gods" |
| Written / literate | Oral / popular |
| Orthodoxy (right belief) | Orthopraxy (right practice) |
| "Intellectual" | Emotional / experiential |
| Exclusionary | Inclusionary |
| Elitist | Populist |

**Dever's definition of "Book religion"** (following Karel van der Toorn): The canonical religion of the literary tradition preserved in the Hebrew Bible. It represents "their final crystallization" of themes long in development — but it was never the religion of most Israelites.

**Dever's definition of "folk religion":** What ordinary people actually practiced — at home, at local shrines, with figurines and standing stones and asherah poles. "Folk religion was, in fact, everything that Josiah and his reformist colleagues wanted to put a stop to."

**Terminology survey:** Dever reviews how other scholars have named this distinction — Holladay ("established/State" vs. "distributed/local"), Ackerman ("popular religion"), Albertz ("official religion" vs. "family religion"), Berlinerblau ("official" vs. "popular"), Keel/Uehlinger (family/local/national levels), Miller (State vs. family), Smith ("popular religion"), Zevit (pluralism). He settles on "Book religion" vs. "folk religion."

**Confidence:** HIGH that such a distinction existed (every scholar in the field recognizes some version of it). MEDIUM on how sharp the distinction was in practice (the categories are heuristic, not absolute — Dever acknowledges this).

**What's at stake for faith:** If the religion of the Bible was never the religion of most Israelites, then what the Bible condemns as "idolatry" and "apostasy" was actually the normal, legitimate religion of God's people. The prophets weren't calling Israel back to its roots — they were calling Israel to abandon its roots for something new. The religion you inherited as "biblical" is the innovation, not the original.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives who argue the biblical religion WAS the original and folk religion represents later corruption. Minimalists who say we can't reconstruct "folk religion" at all from the available evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The two-religions distinction may be too binary. There was probably a spectrum — from fully Yahwistic monotheism at one end to fully polytheistic practice at the other, with most people somewhere in between, mixing elements of both depending on context.

**My assessment:** Dever's binary is a useful heuristic but probably oversimplifies. He acknowledges this himself: "These categories are not rigid, of course. And they are somewhat artificial, since the ancients would not have recognized such distinctions." The two-religions thesis is a lens, not a fact. Used carefully, it illuminates. Used dogmatically, it distorts.

## Claim 3: Religion in ancient Israel was about survival — "the care and feeding of the gods"

**Dever's claim:** Ancient Israelite religion was not about theology, morality, or "spirituality" in the modern sense. It was about survival. In a marginal economy with 30-year life expectancy, unpredictable rainfall, and constant threat of famine, religion addressed the most basic human needs: food, health, fertility, protection from disaster. "The 'care and feeding of the gods' — sacrifices, offerings, rituals — was the practical strategy."

**Evidence presented:**

**The three ultimate concerns:**
1. **Survival:** "They faced the ultimate threat: extinction... Israelites could not comfort themselves with the much later (and non-Semitic) notion of the 'immortality of the soul.' When the body died, that was the end. Religion thus had first of all to deal with the problem of survival, in the most brutal, elementary sense."
2. **Aligning with the universe:** "It was essential to 'personalize' the numinous powers that ultimately ruled the universe and to 'get on their side.'" Even the gods could kill you for no reason. "Living in antiquity was being 'religious.'"
3. **Placating the deities:** Sacrifices as gifts — "give back to the gods a token portion of what they had graciously given." This was the practical mechanism. It "worked — or so it appeared when things were going well."

**Dever on the brutality of ancient religion:**
> "Ancient religion was, as the anthropologist Eilberg-Schwartz puts it, rather messy. It was in fact 'savage' — a brutal, often bloody, life-and-death struggle, the outcome of which was by no means certain. The modern, idealistic, romantic portrait of ancient Israelite religion is a comfortable delusion, but one that obscures the reality."

> "Such a pragmatic definition of religion may seem to us primitive, even debased, as though religion were simply 'magic.' But that's precisely what religion is, or at least was, however much that may offend modern sensibilities."

**Dever's working definition** (following Hans Penner): "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." Simple, functional, focused on PRACTICE.

**Confidence:** HIGH that ancient religion was pragmatic and survival-oriented (this is the anthropological consensus). MEDIUM that Dever's characterization captures ISRAELITE religion specifically (the Bible does contain moral and theological dimensions that Dever minimizes for rhetorical effect).

**What's at stake for faith:** If ancient Israelite religion was primarily about survival — making it rain, having children, not dying of disease — then the Bible's moral and theological content was the concern of a tiny literate elite, not the lived religion of most people. The God of the Bible was, for most Israelites, a god you placated with sacrifices to keep your family alive, not a god you loved with all your heart and soul.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible itself contains both strands — the pragmatic/sacrificial and the moral/theological. Dever emphasizes the former because it's what archaeology reveals; the latter is what texts reveal. Both are real; the question is which was more representative of MOST people's experience.

**My assessment:** Dever overstates for effect. The Bible contains genuine moral and theological reflection alongside the sacrificial system. But he's right that the material evidence — figurines, altars, cult stands — points toward pragmatic, survival-oriented religion. This is a useful corrective to the overly theological readings of Israelite religion that dominate biblical scholarship.

## Claim 4: Ancient Israel was a marginal, impoverished backwater — this context is essential for understanding its religion

**Dever's claim:** "Ancient Israel was a truly marginal economy and society. The country was always poor compared to its prosperous neighbors; always powerless compared to their might; always on the verge of extinction (as finally happened). It may be unsettling to some readers, but the fact is that ancient Israel was an obscure cultural and historical backwater of the ancient Near East. It would have been long forgotten except for its one memorable contribution to civilization: the Hebrew Bible."

**Evidence presented:**

| Demographic/Geographic Fact | Implication |
|---------------------------|-------------|
| Total area: size of New Jersey | "Israel looms 'larger than life' in our Western imagination because of its spiritual significance, but it was a tiny country" |
| Total population: never exceeded ~150,000 | Most villages had a few hundred people |
| Jerusalem at its height: ~5,000 people | The "city of God" was a small town |
| 80-90% of population rural | Most Israelites never saw Jerusalem or the Temple |
| Life expectancy: ~30 years | "A man of 50 was an 'elder,' respected for his remarkable longevity" |
| Literacy: below 5% | "Ordinary people could not have read the Bible if they had had it" |
| No natural resources | No gold, silver, precious stones, or useful minerals |
| Unpredictable rainfall | Drought every few years; crops often failed |
| "Fractured geography" | Made political unity nearly impossible |

**On Israel's vulnerability:**
> "Because of its exposed position as a 'land bridge' between Africa and Asia, Palestine and later Israel were frequently trampled over, subjugated, and occupied for long periods by foreign powers. Think of the long succession of peoples who have overrun this general area... Amorites, Hittites, Hurrians, Egyptians, Philistines, Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Muslims, European Crusaders, Turks."

> "No single political entity ever ruled the area in antiquity for more than about 400 years, and that was ironically the rather feeble Israelite-Judean monarchy."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the demographic and geographic facts (archaeological data is solid). HIGH that this context shaped religion (this is basic anthropological reasoning).

**What's at stake for faith:** The "chosen people" lived in a marginal, impoverished, frequently conquered territory the size of New Jersey. Their capital city had 5,000 people. Their God was the patron of an obscure hill-country chiefdom, not a world empire. The grandeur of biblical theology — God as creator of the universe, Lord of history — was a theological achievement that far outstripped Israel's actual political and economic status. This doesn't make the theology false, but it makes it remarkable.

## Claim 5: Dever directly criticizes Mark Smith for ignoring archaeological evidence

**Dever's claim:** Smith's work is "not informed by any archaeological data that would change his almost exclusively text-based approach in any essential way." This is Smith's blind spot — and it leads him to wrong conclusions, particularly on the Asherah question.

**Evidence — Dever's critique:**
> "Despite his critique of the inadequacies of many other scholars and his acknowledgment that 'synthetic archaeological research has reached a new level of sophistication'... I regret to say that I find Smith's work is not informed by any archaeological data that would change his almost exclusively text-based approach in any essential way. Thus he says of the crucial question of understanding the term 'A/asherah' that despite the new evidence since 1990 he is still not inclined to accept the likelihood that it names a goddess."

**Dever on Smith's resistance:** He attributes Smith's position to psychology — Smith "rejects the observation of some critics that his interpretation is 'psychologically unprepared to deal with the opposite outlook,' countering tentatively with the claim that the 'Zeitgeist (world view) of our age psychologically preconditions advocates to desire a goddess in ancient Israel.'"

**Confidence:** HIGH that Dever genuinely disagrees with Smith on this. MEDIUM on whether Smith's position is psychologically motivated (Dever's claim) vs. methodologically motivated (Smith's claim).

**What's at stake:** This is the central scholarly disagreement in the field. Smith says the evidence is insufficient. Dever says the evidence is overwhelming and Smith is ignoring it. The reader has to decide who's right based on the evidence Dever presents in the rest of the book.

## Claim 6: Dever's methodology — archaeology as primary source, "descriptive" not "prescriptive"

**Dever's claim:** "As an archaeologist and an anthropologist, and thus a historian, I shall argue that philosophy and theology are distractions. These disciplines may well be legitimate and interesting in their own right; but they get us nowhere in the inquiry into ancient folk religion." Text-based scholars treat the Bible as the primary source and archaeology as supplementary. Dever reverses this: for folk religion, archaeology is primary and texts are secondary.

**His eight-point phenomenological method:**
1. Observing society directly, in action rather than theory
2. Using typical case studies, not necessarily "reconstruction"
3. Understanding society "from within" — what people say about themselves
4. Emphasizing individual creativity over large-scale typologies
5. Aiming at Geertz's "thick description," not necessarily explanation
6. Using organic models (social systems like biological systems)
7. Inductive methods: particular → general, not deductive
8. Not eschewing ideology, but inferring the irretrievable from the observable

**Confidence:** HIGH that this is Dever's method. MEDIUM on whether archaeology CAN deliver what Dever claims for it (especially on religion, where practice is observable but belief is inferred).

**What's at stake:** If Dever is right about archaeology's primacy, then text-based scholars (Smith, Römer) are missing most of the evidence. The debate about Israelite religion can't be settled from texts alone — you need the figurines, the cult stands, the inscriptions from the ground. This is Dever's core methodological argument.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Dever's personal journey shapes his approach | HIGH | He could be overstating his objectivity |
| Two-religions thesis (Book vs. folk) | HIGH (distinction exists), MEDIUM (sharpness) | Categories are heuristic, not absolute |
| Religion as "care and feeding of the gods" | HIGH (pragmatic dimension) | Dever understates theological/moral dimensions |
| Israel as marginal backwater | HIGH | Demographic facts are solid |
| Smith ignores archaeology | Debatable — Smith's defenders would push back | Depends on what counts as "enough" archaeology |
| Archaeology as primary source for folk religion | MEDIUM-HIGH | Artifacts don't interpret themselves |

**Dever's distinctive voice:** Unlike Smith (cautious text critic) and Römer (narrative historian), Dever is a polemicist. He's writing to persuade, not just to inform. His book is an argument — against text-only scholarship, against theological readings of the Bible, against the erasure of women's religion. This makes him the most engaging of the three authors and the one whose conclusions require the most careful scrutiny. When Dever says "the evidence is overwhelming," you need to ask: "Is it, or is he just saying it is?"
