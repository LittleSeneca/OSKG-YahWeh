# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Introduction

---

## Claim 1: Dever's personal journey gives him a unique vantage point — he knows what's at stake
→ [[claim-dever-personal-journey-biographical-vantage-point]] | **dever-dghw-intro.1** | Confidence: high
  Dever's candor about his personal journey (fundamentalist → minister → archaeologist → secular humanist Jew) is one of the book's strengths. He tells you where he stands and lets you evaluate evidence accordingly — methodologically superior to the "objective scholar" pose.
  Supports: [[claim-historian-of-religion-vs-theologian-distinction]] (Dever embodies Lewis's historian-vs-theologian distinction)

---

## Claim 2: Ancient Israel had TWO religions — "Book religion" and "folk religion"
→ [[claim-two-religions-book-vs-folk-israelite-religion]] | **dever-dghw-intro.2** | Confidence: medium-high
  The religion of the Bible was the theology of a tiny elite — it was never the religion of most Israelites. Folk religion was polytheistic, image-rich, domestic, and focused on fertility and survival. The Book religion won only at the very end of the monarchy.
  Contradicts: [[claim-israelite-religion-absolutely-different-paganism-from-beginning]] (Kaufmann) | Depends on: [[claim-internal-religious-pluralism-official-vs-personal-piety]] (Albertz), [[claim-family-piety-uninfluenced-official-yahweh-religion]] (Albertz)

---

## Claim 3: Religion in ancient Israel was about survival — "the care and feeding of the gods"
→ [[claim-religion-as-survival-care-feeding-of-gods]] | **dever-dghw-intro.3** | Confidence: high
  Ancient religion was pragmatic and survival-oriented: placating the gods with sacrifices to keep your family alive, make it rain, have children. The Bible's moral and theological content was the concern of a tiny literate elite, not the lived religion of most people.
  Contradicts: [[claim-israelite-religion-absolutely-different-paganism-from-beginning]] (Kaufmann), [[claim-priestly-cult-non-pagan-silent-non-magical-awe-of-holiness]] (Kaufmann) | Supports: [[claim-family-piety-pre-cultic-pre-political-pre-moral-unconditional]] (Albertz)



## Claim 4: Ancient Israel was a marginal, impoverished backwater — this context is essential for understanding its religion
→ [[claim-israel-marginal-impoverished-backwater-demographic-context]] | **dever-dghw-intro.4** | Confidence: high
  Size of New Jersey, ~150K population, Jerusalem ~5K, literacy <5%, 80-90% rural, 30-year life expectancy. The "chosen people" were an obscure hill-country chiefdom — biblical theology far outstripped Israel's actual status.
  Supports: [[claim-two-religions-book-vs-folk-israelite-religion]] (demographic reality is the foundation), [[claim-religion-as-survival-care-feeding-of-gods]] (harsh conditions explain survival-oriented religion)

## Claim 5: Dever directly criticizes Mark Smith for ignoring archaeological evidence
→ [[claim-dever-criticizes-smith-ignoring-archaeological-evidence]] | **dever-dghw-intro.5** | Confidence: debatable
  The central scholarly disagreement in the field. Smith says evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess. Dever says evidence is overwhelming and Smith's text-only approach is his blind spot.
  Contradicts: [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]], [[claim-his-asherah-cultic-symbol-stylized-tree-not-goddess-consort]] | Supports: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]]

## Claim 6: Dever's methodology — archaeology as primary source, "descriptive" not "prescriptive"
→ [[claim-archaeology-primary-source-folk-religion-descriptive-method]] | **dever-dghw-intro.6** | Confidence: medium-high
  For folk religion, archaeology is the primary source: texts represent the elite, artifacts reveal what ordinary people did. Eight-point phenomenological method focused on "thick description" of practice, not prescriptions. Dever reverses the text-primary assumption of Smith and Römer.
  Supports: [[claim-two-religions-book-vs-folk-israelite-religion]], [[claim-dever-criticizes-smith-ignoring-archaeological-evidence]]

**Dever's distinctive voice:** Unlike Smith (cautious text critic) and Römer (narrative historian), Dever is a polemicist. He's writing to persuade, not just to inform. His book is an argument — against text-only scholarship, against theological readings of the Bible, against the erasure of women's religion. This makes him the most engaging of the three authors and the one whose conclusions require the most careful scrutiny. When Dever says "the evidence is overwhelming," you need to ask: "Is it, or is he just saying it is?"
