# Albertz — §3.3–3.4: The Main State Cults of South and North

These sections describe how the monarchy created a new type of cult — the royal state sanctuary — fundamentally different from the decentralized local shrines of the pre-state period. Albertz's key insight: Jerusalem and Bethel represent two different responses to the same problem of reconciling monarchy with pre-state Yahwism. Jerusalem (South) was syncretistic, imperial, and priest-controlled — incorporating Jebusite/Canaanite traditions. Bethel (North) was conservative, populist, and Exodus-oriented — self-consciously archaizing against Jerusalem's innovations. The tragedy: Jerusalem's tightly controlled syncretism survived; Bethel's populist El-Yahwism slid into Baalization and was destroyed.

---

## §3.3 The Main State Cult in the South (Jerusalem)

### Claim 1: The monarchy created a fundamentally new type of cult — the royal state sanctuary — which fused throne and altar and began the centralization that would culminate in Deuteronomy

**Albertz's claim:** The monarchy was "an important turning point for the history of the main cult." Royal sanctuaries emerged that were "the property and the private chapel of the king" while claiming to be the public sanctuary of the kingdom. This "set in motion a centralization of the main Israelite cult which later... was to issue in the Deuteronomic requirement of the abolition of all local cults outside Jerusalem." It led to a "close institutional fusion of political power and the cult."

**Evidence:**
- Amos 7:13 calls Bethel "house of the kingdom" (bêt mamlākâ) and "sanctuary of the king" (miqdāš hammelek)
- State financing transformed the cult: the temple had unprecedented buildings, vessels, personnel, and festival scale — "could only be financed and organized by a system of state taxation"
- The Jerusalem temple and palace formed a single complex (1 Kings 7:12; Ezek 43:8)
- Priests were "royal officials" and sometimes members of the royal family (2 Sam 8:17-18)
- The king exercised priestly functions: sacrifice, blessing, intercession

**Confidence:** HIGH. The fusion of throne and altar in the Jerusalem temple is well-documented.

---

### Claim 2: David's religious policy was a deliberate syncretism between Israelite and Jebusite traditions; Solomon tipped the balance toward the Jebusite/Canaanite side

**Albertz's claim:** David appointed TWO priests: Abiathar (from the old Israelite Shiloh priesthood) and Zadok (the former Jebusite priest of Jerusalem). This was a deliberate balancing act. Solomon exiled Abiathar and gave the priestly monopoly to the Zadokides. "Solomon evidently already saw himself as so much a ruler analogous to the Canaanite sacral monarchy that he no longer felt it necessary to take into account any institutional links... with the pre-state traditions of Yahweh." Consequently, "the official Yahweh religion of the Jerusalem temple shows hardly any awareness of the liberation traditions of the early period."

**Evidence:**
- 2 Sam 8:17; 20:25: two priests appointed — Abiathar (Eli lineage from Shiloh) and Zadok (Jebusite)
- 1 Kings 2:26f.: Solomon banishes Abiathar to Anathoth; Zadokites gain monopoly (1 Kings 4:2)
- The tradition deliberately obscures the pre-Israelite sanctuary: claims David kept the ark in a tent for 40 years, that the temple site was a threshing floor (2 Sam 24:16ff.), that no sanctuary existed before
- But 2 Sam 12:20 incidentally mentions David visiting "the temple of Yahweh" (bêt YHWH) — proving a pre-existing sanctuary
- "The report of the building of a 'Solomonic temple' in 1 Kings 6 suggests more a rebuilding than a complete new building; it was a former Jebusite temple which Solomon had renovated"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The two-priest tradition and the Abiathar-Zadok conflict are well-attested. The claim that the Solomonic temple was a Jebusite renovation is more speculative but well-argued from the text's own inconsistencies.

**What's at stake:** If the Jerusalem temple was built on a Jebusite foundation with a Jebusite priesthood, then the central institution of Israelite religion was fundamentally Canaanite in origin. The "Yahweh" worshipped there was substantially fused with El-Elyon and other Canaanite deities from the beginning.

---

### Claim 3: Jerusalem temple theology created a new kind of Yahweh — king of the gods, creator, enthroned on Zion, identified with imperial power — fundamentally alien to pre-state liberation Yahwism

**Albertz's claim:** Jerusalem theology revolved around two poles: "the kingship of Yahweh and Jerusalem as the city of God." It combined El's static kingship (eternal, head of divine assembly) with Baal's dynamic kingship (victory over chaos), then "transposed into the sphere of political history" — Yahweh's victories over Israel's neighbors proved his universal kingship. "Yahweh changed from being liberator of the oppressed to being promoter of his own policy of expansion and oppression."

**Evidence:**
- **Yahweh Sabaoth**: "enthroned on the cherubim" — a heavenly court of divine beings implementing his rule. Isaiah's vision (Isa 6) shows how central this was
- **Divine kingship**: "king over all gods" (Ps 95:3), enthroned above the flood (Ps 29:10), ruling the nations (Ps 47:3-9)
- **Zion theology**: Yahweh made his dwelling on Zion (Ps 76:3); "Yahweh is in her midst" (Ps 46:5f.); "the holiest of Elyon's dwellings" — "almost complete identification of Yahweh with the capital"
- Zion was identified with "the peak of Zaphon" (Ps 48:3) — the mythical mountain of the gods in northern Syria — "not with Sinai in the south!"
- **Creator theology**: "creator of heaven and earth" (qōnē šāmayim wā'āreṣ, Gen 14:19), taken over from El-Elyon
- **Other deities**: Asherah (1 Kings 15:13; 2 Kings 23:7), Shemesh the sun-god (Ps 19:5-7; 2 Kings 23:11; Ezek 8:16ff.), Zedek ("righteousness" as a hypostasis)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the existence of these theological elements (the Psalms are unambiguous). MEDIUM on the interpretation that this represents a betrayal of pre-state Yahwism — that's Albertz's normative judgment.

**What's at stake:** Albertz is arguing that Jerusalem theology was not a development OF Yahwism but a replacement OF Yahwism — substituting El/Baal kingship theology and Canaanite sanctuary traditions for the Exodus-liberation core. If true, the prophetic critique of Jerusalem (Isaiah, Jeremiah) was not innovation but restoration.

## §3.4 The Main State Cult in the North (Bethel)

### Claim 4: Jeroboam's revolt was a social liberation movement explicitly fought under the banner of the Exodus tradition — the Exodus narrative may have been shaped by it

**Albertz's claim:** "It is highly probable that the battle against Solomon's forced labour by Jeroboam and the northern tribes was fought with an appeal to the liberation of their forefathers from Egyptian forced labour, and that these old religious reminiscences took their first narrative form from the contemporary experiences of Jeroboam's revolt."

**Evidence:**
- Striking parallels between the Moses story (Ex 2:11-15; 5*) and Jeroboam's revolt (1 Kings 11-12): both are men from royal circles who show solidarity with oppressed countrymen; both strike an overseer and flee abroad; both return after the king's death; both negotiate with the successor, whose demands are heightened; both achieve liberation by departure from the oppressor's sphere of power
- Ex 5:16: the Israelite overseers accuse Pharaoh of having "sinned against his own people" — polemically identifying Solomon/Rehoboam with Pharaoh
- "The narrative Ex.5...3,4*,6-19 was once a propaganda text from the struggle against the forced labour imposed by Solomon or Rehoboam"
- Jeroboam's movement "was the first instance in which an individual prophet with no institutional ties intervened to destabilize an existing political rule" — Ahijah of Shiloh's oracle (1 Kings 11:29-39) supported the revolt

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The parallels are real but could be literary convention rather than historical causation. The claim that Ex 5* was Jeroboam-era propaganda is creative but speculative.

**What's at stake:** This is a major claim about the formation of the Exodus tradition itself — that it was shaped (perhaps even created) by 10th-century anti-Solomonic politics. If true, the Exodus narrative is not ancient memory but political propaganda from the early monarchy. This would be a significant contribution to Pentateuchal source criticism.

---

### Claim 5: Jeroboam's Bethel cult was a deliberately conservative, archaizing alternative to Jerusalem — the bull image was a pedestal for Yahweh (like Jerusalem's cherubim), not an idol, and the cult cry explicitly invoked the Exodus

**Albertz's claim:** "Jeroboam, who had been guided by the Yahweh traditions from the time before the state, also wanted to develop an archaizing alternative to the state cult of Jerusalem." Bethel had "old tribal legitimation" (unlike Jerusalem). The bull was a pedestal for the invisible Yahweh — parallel to the cherubim throne in Jerusalem, not a divine image. The cult cry "Behold your god, Israel, who brought you out of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28) was "a regular cultic cry with which the cultic image was presented to the assembled community."

**Evidence:**
- Bethel had pre-Israelite El-worship (El-Bethel, Gen 31:13; 35:7); the bull symbolism was inherited from El ("Bull El" is a Ugaritic epithet)
- A bronze bull figurine from Iron Age I was found in the Samarian hill country — evidence of pre-monarchic bull iconography in the North
- The Deuteronomistic description is systematically polemical: plural "calves" to charge polytheism; "calf/heifer" ('ēgel) to discredit; interpretation as "divine images" to charge idolatry
- Near Eastern parallels: the storm god Hadad stands on a bull — the bull is a pedestal, not the deity
- The cult cry was in the singular (contra the MT's polemical plural) — "This is your god... who brought you out of Egypt"
- Unlike Jerusalem (hidden in the holy of holies), Bethel's cult symbol was visible to the community — people "blew kisses to it" (Hos 10:5; 13:2)
- Jeroboam refused to build a permanent residence, maintained multiple sanctuaries (Dan, Shechem, Penuel), didn't intervene in local priesthoods — all "continuity with conditions existing before the state"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Deuteronomistic polemic is clearly tendentious, and Albertz's reconstruction (bull as pedestal, singular, Exodus-oriented) is standard critical scholarship. The populist character of the Bethel cult is well-supported by Hosea's descriptions.

---

### Claim 6: Both state cults were syncretistic, but Jerusalem's succeeded (controlled by the state) while Bethel's failed (close to the people, slid into Baalization)

**Albertz's claim:** "Whereas the El syncretism of Jerusalem, more strictly controlled by the state, succeeded, the El syncretism of Bethel, which was close to the people, ended in complete disaster." The bull's ambiguous symbolism — power AND fertility — made it vulnerable. "At least in the view of the people... the theological differentiation between Yahweh and his cultic pedestal was lost and the bull image was identified directly with Yahweh." Hosea saw it as "a 'Baalized' Yahweh cult, which had forgotten its historical origins."

**Evidence:**
- Jerusalem's syncretism was controlled: Yahweh's identity was maintained through theology (Sabaoth, creator, king), and the cult was hidden behind walls and priestly mediation
- Bethel's syncretism was populist: the image was visible, accessible, and the bull's fertility associations (Baal, El's sexual potency) overwhelmed its Exodus associations
- Hosea's polemic (Hos 8:6; 10:5; 13:2) shows the bull had become identified with Yahweh directly
- Hosea's verdict provided the legitimation for Josiah's destruction of Bethel (2 Kings 23:15-18)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The contrast between state-controlled and populist syncretism is analytically sharp. But was Bethel really more "Baalized" than Jerusalem, or did the Deuteronomistic polemic (which was Jerusalem-centered) simply succeed in discrediting the Northern cult while protecting its own?

**Alternative reading:** The "failure" of Bethel may be an artifact of the southern/Jerusalem perspective that dominates the OT. From a Northern perspective, Bethel was the legitimate Yahwistic sanctuary preserving Exodus traditions, and Jerusalem was the syncretistic innovation. The difference is that Jerusalem's party won the editorial war.

## §3.3–3.4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Royal sanctuaries = new type of cult; fusion of throne and altar | HIGH | — |
| 2. David's syncretistic balancing act; Solomon tipped to Jebusite side | MEDIUM-HIGH | The "Solomonic temple = Jebusite renovation" is speculative |
| 3. Jerusalem theology = betrayal of liberation Yahwism | HIGH (theological elements); MEDIUM (normative judgment) | Whether this is betrayal or legitimate development |
| 4. Jeroboam's revolt shaped the Exodus narrative | LOW-MEDIUM | Literary parallels may be conventional, not causal |
| 5. Bethel cult was conservative, archaizing; bull = pedestal, not idol | MEDIUM-HIGH | Standard critical reading; depends on reading against Deuteronomistic polemic |
| 6. Jerusalem syncretism succeeded; Bethel's populism led to Baalization | MEDIUM | May be artifact of Jerusalem's editorial victory |

**Strongest section:** The interpretation of the Bethel bull as pedestal and the cult as Exodus-oriented conservatism (Claim 5). Albertz reads against the Deuteronomistic polemic convincingly and recovers a coherent Northern theology.

**Weakest section:** The claim that Jeroboam's revolt shaped the Exodus narrative (Claim 4). The parallels are interesting but the causal direction could run either way (Exodus narrative shaped Jeroboam's self-presentation; or later redactors shaped Jeroboam's story to match the Exodus pattern).

**Cross-reference:** Römer's *Invention of God* Ch 6-7 covers the cults of Israel and Judah with similar conclusions. Smith's *Early History of God* Ch 2 covers Yahweh and Baal, relevant to the Bethel bull's ambiguity. This section is where Albertz most closely parallels the standard critical account of the two kingdoms' divergent religious trajectories.
