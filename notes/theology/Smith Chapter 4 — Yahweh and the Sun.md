# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 4

## Yahweh and the Sun

---

## Claim 1: Yahweh was described in solar language, and this developed under royal influence
→ [[claim-yahweh-solar-language-royal-development]] | **smith-ehg-4.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Solar imagery for Yahweh developed in Judah under royal influence — solarized Yahwism, not a separate sun cult. The third deity profile in Smith's convergence trilogy (after Baal's storm in Ch2 and Asherah in Ch3).
  Key edges: Depends on [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]] (convergence pattern template). Supports [[claim-solar-storm-combination-theological-transcendence]].

---

## Claim 2: Solar and storm imagery were combined to express Yahweh's transcendence over nature
→ [[claim-solar-storm-combination-theological-transcendence]] | **smith-ehg-4.2** | Confidence: medium
  Combining storm and solar imagery was a theological innovation — not just syncretism but a way of saying "God is bigger than any one metaphor." Smith at his most sympathetic to the biblical theologians.
  Key edges: Depends on [[claim-yahweh-solar-language-royal-development]] and [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]] (both imagery sets required).

---

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 4

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Solar language for Yahweh was real and royally-driven (smith-ehg-4.1) | MEDIUM-HIGH | Solar language could be purely metaphorical |
| Combination of storm+solar = theological transcendence (smith-ehg-4.2) | MEDIUM | Hard to prove deliberate strategy vs. accumulated tradition |

**Strongest evidence:** Ezekiel 8:16 (priests worshipping sun in temple) and the horse figurines with sun disks (archaeological confirmation of solar practice).

**Weakest:** The claim that solar language was specifically a Judean royal development. The evidence is circumstantial.

**Relationship to larger argument:** Chapter 4 extends the convergence pattern to a fourth category (after El, Baal, Asherah). The cumulative weight matters more than any single chapter.
