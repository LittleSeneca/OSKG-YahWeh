# Lewis — Chapter 10: Yahweh as the Holy One

Lewis examines holiness as the "deepest and innermost nature" of the Hebrew Bible's God (quoting Sellin). The chapter traces holiness from its earliest non-cultic meanings (awesome power, military might) through the Ark narrative's lethal holiness to the cultic management of holiness in P, H, and Ezekiel — where holiness becomes an ideological tool for constructing priestly hierarchy and marking sacred space/personnel/objects. It concludes with the Holiness Code's radical expansion of holiness to the entire community.

---

## Claim 1: The earliest Israelite understanding of divine holiness was about power — especially military power — not cultic purity

**Lewis's claim:** "Many of these traditions are surprisingly non-cultic in nature... The notion of Yahweh being holy is found in our earliest biblical traditions." Exodus 15:11 — "Who is like you among the gods, Yahweh? Who is like you, feared in holiness?" — is embedded in a divine warrior hymn. Habakkuk 3:3: "the Holy One (qādôš) from Mt. Paran" marches in military theophany. "The earliest passages of the Hebrew Bible on holy divinity resonate with what we see in Late Bronze Age Levantine antecedents... to say that Yahweh is holy is to say that he is powerful."

**Evidence:** Exodus 15 (one of the oldest poems) mentions holiness three times in a military context. Habakkuk 3 presents the Holy One as a divine warrior with pestilence and plague in his retinue. Deut 33:2 has Yahweh coming with "myriads of holy ones" as combatants. Psalm 68, Psalm 89, and the Kuntillet ʿAjrud plaster fragment KA 4.2 all connect holiness with divine power and theophany. The Northwest Semitic background: Ugaritic ʾIlu is called "the Holy One" (qdš); the gods collectively are "sons of the Holy One" (bn qdš); the Arslan Tash incantation mentions "the assembly of all the Holy Ones" (dr kl qdšn). At this early stage, "there is no hint of what comes to define holiness in priestly and Deuteronomic traditions (e.g., sacerdotal rank, cultic purity, social and ethical behaviors, and being withdrawn from common use)."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is one of Lewis's most original contributions. The early military/power meaning of qdš has been underappreciated because later priestly traditions dominate our understanding of holiness.

**What's at stake:** Whether holiness is primarily about separation/purity (the standard theological definition) or about power. Lewis argues the latter is chronologically prior. This has implications for how we read EVERY mention of holiness in the Hebrew Bible.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who define holiness exclusively through the priestly lens (separation, purity, distinctness). The standard theological dictionaries (e.g., TDOT) emphasize separation as the core meaning.

**My assessment:** This is a genuinely important insight. It explains why the Ark narrative (Uzzah struck dead for touching it) makes sense: the Ark is not "pure" in a ritual sense; it's DANGEROUSLY POWERFUL. Holiness as power also explains the Nadab and Abihu deaths (Lev 10) better than ritual infraction alone.

---

## Claim 2: The cultic management of holiness — especially in P — was an ideological tool for constructing and reinforcing priestly hierarchy, rank, and privilege

**Lewis's claim:** "It is easy to see how the concept of holiness is used ideologically to construct and reinforce rank, power, and privilege. This is especially true in the promotion of Moses, Aaron, and the Aaronid priests." Holiness "served as an indicator of rank among cultic personnel." P adamantly asserts that ONLY Aaronid priests (kōhănîm) are holy — in contrast to non-Aaronid Levites.

**Evidence:** P's gradations of holiness map directly onto social hierarchy: the high priest > Aaronid priests > Levites > clean lay Israelites > unclean Israelites. The ritual clothing of Aaron (Lev 8) is "an act of investiture that formally bestows and confirms the authority due his rank as high priest. Vestments are used to mark both his subservience before God and his elevated status before humans." The anointing ritual (oil poured on Aaron's head vs. sprinkled on ordinary priests) marks sacerdotal rank. Moses is the preeminent authority but "the verb qdš is never used of Moses throughout the Hebrew Bible apart from a single (late) reference" — and Moses is never explicitly called a priest. Lewis suggests this is because P needed to elevate AARON as the priestly ancestor, not Moses.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the ideological function of holiness in P. MEDIUM on the Moses omission being deliberate (it could be an accident of textual preservation).

**What's at stake:** Whether priestly religion is revealed ritual or constructed ideology. Lewis leans heavily toward the latter — holiness language is a tool of power, not a description of divine nature.

**Who disagrees:** Milgrom and confessional scholars who see P's holiness system as divinely revealed. Even critical scholars might argue Lewis overstates the ideological reading — some gradations of holiness may reflect genuine functional distinctions rather than power plays.

**My assessment:** Lewis is persuasive. The Aaronid-only-holiness claim in P is so explicit and so polemical that it virtually announces itself as a contested claim. Someone was arguing that Levites were holy too — and P is pushing back.

---

## Claim 3: The Holiness Code (H) represents a radical counter-tradition — holiness is extended to the ENTIRE community, not just priests

**Lewis's claim:** "Even more striking is the Holiness Code's (H's) comprehensive notion of holiness that includes the entire community (not only priests) and covering a wide array of cultic, economic, judicial, moral, and social parameters." H's command "You shall be holy, for I Yahweh your God am holy" (Lev 19:2) is addressed to "all the congregation of the Israelites" (Lev 19:2) — not to priests.

**Evidence:** H expands holiness beyond the sanctuary to everyday life: harvesting practices (leaving gleanings for the poor), labor relations (paying workers promptly), justice (impartial judgment), social ethics (love of neighbor, love of the ger/foreigner), sexual ethics, and economic justice (Jubilee). This is a DEMOCRATIZATION of holiness. P restricts holiness to Aaronids; H extends it to everyone. The theological basis: imitatio Dei — "to be holy as he is holy."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. H's expansion of holiness is well-documented in the scholarship (Knohl, Milgrom, Schwartz).

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite religion is fundamentally hierarchical (priests mediate holiness) or democratic (everyone participates in holiness). The answer is both — P and H represent competing visions preserved within the same canon.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see H as an organic extension of P rather than a counter-tradition. The debate is about the relationship between P and H, not about H's content.

**Alternative reading:** H's democratization may be rhetorical rather than practical — everyone is CALLED to be holy, but priests still manage the cult.

**My assessment:** Lewis's treatment of the P vs. H tension is excellent. It shows that the Hebrew Bible preserves genuine debate about the nature of holiness, not a single uniform doctrine. The democratization of holiness in H is one of the most ethically significant developments in Israelite religion — and it grows directly from the concept of imitatio Dei.

## Chapter 10 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Early holiness = power, not purity | VERY HIGH | Some early texts may also imply separation |
| P's holiness = ideological hierarchy tool | HIGH | Moses omission could be accidental |
| H democratizes holiness to entire community | VERY HIGH | Practical vs. rhetorical democratization debated |
| No cultic prostitution in ancient Israel | VERY HIGH | Scholarly consensus now; older translations persist |
| Yahweh manifests holiness through military power | HIGH | Ezekiel's usage is exceptional (power in exile context) |

**Strongest sections:** The early holiness = power argument (genuinely original synthesis). The P vs. H contrast (clear and well-argued). The cultic prostitution debunking (necessary and well-sourced).

**Weakest section:** The extensive treatment of priestly ritual details (clothing, anointing, blood manipulation) can become a catalogue. Necessary for completeness but less analytically sharp than the earlier sections.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Holiness has a diachronic development: power → cultic danger → priestly hierarchy → democratized ethics
- P and H represent competing visions of holiness preserved together
- The Hebrew Bible's holiness traditions are remarkably diverse — and this diversity is the point
- Holiness was "the deepest and innermost nature" of Yahweh not because of abstract theology but because it structured Israelite society at every level

**Cross-references:** Sommer's Chapter 4 covers sacred space in a complementary way. Heiser's Part 5 covers holiness in the context of holy war (complementing Lewis's early holiness = power claim). The P vs. H tension is an important corrective to readings that flatten Israelite religion into a single system.
