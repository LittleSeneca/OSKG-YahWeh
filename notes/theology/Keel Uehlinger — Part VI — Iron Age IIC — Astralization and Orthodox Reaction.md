# Keel & Uehlinger — Part VI: Iron Age IIC

## The Astralization of the Heavenly Powers, the Revival of the Goddess, and the Orthodox Reaction

Iron Age IIC (ca. 720-600 BCE) is the period of Assyrian domination, the reign of Manasseh, Josiah's reform, and the decades leading to the Babylonian exile. It sees three apparently contradictory developments: (1) the astralization of the divine realm under Assyrian/Aramean influence, (2) a massive revival of goddess imagery in the form of Judean Pillar Figurines, and (3) the emergence of an aniconic, text-centered orthodoxy among the Jerusalem elite. Keel & Uehlinger's iconographic evidence reveals these not as sequential stages but as simultaneous, competing tendencies within a single religious symbol system under imperial pressure.

**This is the chapter that gives Dever's figurine argument its proper chronological and interpretive context. It is also the chapter where the pathway to biblical monotheism becomes visible in the material record.**

---

## Claim 1: Astralization replaces solarization as the dominant religious iconography — the Assyrian conquest brings a shift from sun to stars

**Authors' claim:** "The chief characteristic of the glyptic art of Iron Age IIC is not the generally modest presence of Assyrian deities but the general tendency toward the astralization of the religious symbol system. During the seventh century, preference was given to the divine powers being accepted and worshiped in their astral form, with emphasis on their nocturnal appearance in the starry heavens."

The Egyptian solar motifs that dominated Iron Age IIB (sun child in lotus, winged solar disks) give way to Mesopotamian astral motifs: the eight-rayed Venus star (Ishtar), the crescent moon (Sin), and the Pleiades. This is not just fashion — it represents a fundamental shift in how the divine was conceptualized: from the constant, life-giving sun to the remote, mysterious stars of the night sky.

**Evidence presented:**
- Cylinder seals reappear after centuries of absence — "not even three dozen" from 8th-7th centuries in Palestine, found almost exclusively at Assyrian administrative centers (Megiddo, Dor, Samaria, Gezer, Beth-Shean)
- The most frequent cylinder seal motif: the Assyrian king as bareheaded worshiper before astral symbols (crescent moon standard, eight-rayed star, Pleiades) — "the emphasis in the illustration is on the king... loyalty shown by the king to the heavenly powers effects, or ought to effect, the growth and prosperity of the fertile land"
- Assyrian cylinder seal from Gezer: winged genius fighting a bovine creature, with Venus star, crescent moon, and Pleiades in the upper field
- Only four Assyrian deities appear anthropomorphically: Ninurta (warrior god vs. dragon), Adad (weather god with bow), Ishtar (goddess in star nimbus), and occasionally other figures
- Most Assyrian gods appear only as cultic symbols: the crescent moon standard of the Moon God of Haran, the stylus of Nabu, the spade of Marduk
- "Mediating creatures, such as winged, hybrid creatures, no longer play a significant role in Iron Age IIC iconography" — the cherubs and sphinxes of Iron IIB recede, replaced by astral symbols and (male) worshipers

**Confidence:** HIGH. The shift from solar to astral iconography is documented across hundreds of seals and is one of the clearest period markers in the archaeological record.

**What's at stake:** The solarization-to-astralization shift tracks the broader geopolitical shift from Egyptian to Mesopotamian cultural dominance. More importantly, it shows that Israelite religion was not developing in isolation toward monotheism — it was adapting to imperial religious languages. The "astral cults" condemned in 2 Kings 23:5 (Josiah deposed priests "who burned incense to Baal, to the sun and moon, to the constellations and to all the host of heaven") are not an invention of the deuteronomistic editor — they're describing a real 7th-century religious landscape that the iconographic evidence confirms.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who minimize Assyrian influence and see astral cults as indigenous Canaanite revivals (McKay 1973). Cogan (1974) argues the astral cults are Syrian/Aramean, not Assyrian.

**Alternative reading:** The astral motifs could represent the religious practice of Assyrian officials stationed in Palestine, not the local population. Keel & Uehlinger note this possibility but argue the local adoption is visible in provincial seal production.

**My assessment:** The astralization is unequivocally real. The question is how deeply it penetrated local Israelite/Judean religion vs. remaining an elite/official phenomenon. Keel & Uehlinger's evidence from locally produced seals suggests significant penetration — but the aniconic reaction (Claims 6-7) shows there was also resistance.

## Claim 2: The Moon God of Haran was identified with the local El — and Yahweh absorbed lunar characteristics

**Authors' claim:** "From this evidence, one can draw the conclusion that in Palestine, during Iron Age IIC, the Moon God of Haran (who was also venerated by the Assyrians as the highest god in the western provinces) and the local El were identified with each other, at least in certain circles of the population, as two manifestations of the same 'Most High God.'"

The crescent moon standard — the cultic emblem of Sin, the Moon God of Haran — appears on local Palestinian seals alongside the enthroned El figure. The two were identified. Yahweh, already identified with El, absorbed lunar characteristics through this channel.

**Evidence presented:**
- The crescent moon standard appears on Aramean and locally produced seals throughout Palestine (illuss. 291, 295-301)
- A local seal group shows an enthroned, bearded deity (El type) with lunar features — "in the act of blessing, as an anthropomorphic moon god" (illuss. 303-307)
- The Beth-Shean cylinder seal (illus. 308): an "iconographic anthology" showing the enthroned god (El) with "his asherah" (stylized tree) in the lower register — independent confirmation of the Iron IIB reading
- "Anthropomorphic representations and cultic symbols appear to be interchangeable entities, used according to regional preference"
- Two lunar-name seals with El/moon god imagery from Judah: the owner could seal documents with the "orthodox" name side while wearing the deity image as an amulet on the reverse
- The stylized tree (asherah) appears next to the crescent moon standard on clay amulets from Tell Keisan (illuss. 309a-b) and on a conoid from the Amuq region — "emblem and a stylized tree also decorate two sides of a squared stone"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The identification of the enthroned figure as El is solid. The lunar characteristics are clear. The step to "this is Yahweh" is inferential but well-supported by the Yahwistic names on these seals and the broader identification of Yahweh with El.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh absorbed lunar characteristics during Iron IIC, this adds a crucial piece to Smith's convergence thesis: Yahweh absorbed not just solar traits (Iron IIB) but also lunar traits (Iron IIC) from El through the Moon God of Haran identification. The "Most High God" (El Elyon) becomes increasingly celestial and abstract — a god of the heavens broadly, not just the sun. This is the theological pathway to the universal God of Second Isaiah.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Yahweh's lunar traits as original (the "Shasu-Yahweh" originating from a moon-worshiping region). Also those who minimize El-Yahweh identification.

**Alternative reading:** The lunar motifs on Judean seals could be passive cultural borrowing without theological significance — people bought seals with moon motifs because they were fashionable Assyrian/Aramean designs.

**My assessment:** The dual-sided seals where the owner separates the image from the name strongly suggest conscious theological management, not passive borrowing. The owner knew the lunar image was religiously significant and chose to keep it — but on the reverse, not alongside his name. This is exactly the kind of religious negotiation you'd expect in a culture under imperial pressure.

## Claim 3: The Judean Pillar Figurines represent a REVIVAL of the goddess in anthropomorphic form — not a continuous tradition

**Authors' claim:** "Notwithstanding this 'continuity of intent'... the hiatus in the local production of small terra-cottas with representations of the goddess... is too clear for one to assume an unbroken transition from one type to another in Israel or Judah."

This is the critical finding for the Asherah debate. The pillar figurines that Dever cites as evidence of widespread goddess worship are an Iron Age IIC phenomenon — they appear at the END of the 8th century, flourish in the 7th, and represent a REVIVAL after a hiatus during Iron IIB. They are not evidence of continuous goddess worship since the Bronze Age.

**Evidence presented:**
- "More than eight hundred pillar figurines from Judah have been found" — the most common terra-cotta type of Iron IIC
- Upper body: anthropomorphic breasts, arms, female head (often with elaborate "tear drop" curl hairstyles from molds)
- Lower body: hand-formed pillar pedestal (hence the name), sometimes wheel-turned and hollow
- At least one-third have crude "pinched" faces ("birdhead" appearance) — cheaper, locally produced versions
- Dating: "Pillar figurines, or fragments of such, are first found in contexts that can be dated with confidence... toward the end of the eighth century" — NOT in Iron IIB, NOT in the 10th century as Engle claimed
- The hiatus: Iron IIB has very few goddess figurines in Israel/Judah. The pillar figurines represent a new phenomenon, not an unbroken chain from LB "Astarte plaques"
- "More can be said in favor of this revival of the 'Canaanite' tradition being a harmonization linking indigenous forces with a new impulse that came 'from the outside'"
- The possible outside impulse: Phoenician influence (Tadmor, Holladay), perhaps indirectly related to Assyrian invasions and changed market structures
- Context: found in private homes and tombs — usually ONE per household. "The figurines represent a kind of 'household icon'"
- "The motherly goddess, whose image emphasizes especially the face (accessibility) and full breasts (blessing), appears to be none other than the (repersonalized) Asherah"

**Confidence:** HIGH for the dating and the revival interpretation. MEDIUM for the Phoenician/Assyrian trigger mechanism.

**What's at stake:** Dever's entire figurine argument depends on these being evidence of continuous, widespread goddess worship. Keel & Uehlinger agree they're evidence of goddess worship — but in the 7th CENTURY, NOT the 9th-8th centuries of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions. The pillar figurines are not proof that "Yahweh had a wife" during Iron IIB. They're proof that the goddess made a dramatic COMEBACK in Iron IIC under Assyrian imperial conditions. This is a very different historical narrative.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (2005, who dates the figurines earlier and sees continuity), Engle (1979, who dated them to the 9th century). Hadley (1989) is closer to Keel & Uehlinger's dating.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the pillar figurines appear first in IIC, they could represent the SURFACING of a folk religion that had always existed but wasn't recorded in official glyptic art during IIB. The "hiatus" might be an artifact of what media survive (seals vs. cheap terra-cottas).

**My assessment:** The hiatus argument is strong. During Iron IIB, not only are pillar figurines absent — ALL goddess imagery is minimal across ALL media (seals, ivories, terra-cottas). The revival in IIC is dramatic and visible across multiple media. Something changed in the late 8th century that made goddess imagery popular again. The Assyrian disruption of traditional social structures is the most plausible trigger — in times of imperial stress, people turn to mother goddesses for protection and fertility. Dever's error is chronological, not interpretive: the pillar figurines ARE Asherah — but they're Asherah in her 7th-century come-back, not evidence of her 8th-century consort status.

## Claim 4: "Even now, we have no clear evidence for a strict partner/consort relationship between Yahweh and Asherah" — the goddess revival doesn't prove Yahweh had a wife

**Authors' claim:** "Was the female form of Asherah in seventh-century Jerusalem the partner/consort of Yahweh? As attractive as such a conclusion might be, it is difficult to support. Even now, we have no clear evidence for a strict partner/consort relationship between Yahweh and Asherah."

Despite the massive increase in goddess imagery during Iron IIC — the pillar figurines, the "Queen of Heaven" cult, Manasseh's reported installation of an asherah image in the Temple (2 Kings 21:7) — Keel & Uehlinger maintain their position: the asherah was a mediating entity, not a consort. The goddess revival produced lots of goddesses. It did NOT produce divine couples.

**Evidence presented:**
- "There are no pictures of a pair of deities in Judah" — unlike Bronze Age seals showing divine couples (see Part I), Iron IIC Judahite iconography never pairs a male and female deity
- "The rider terra-cottas and other male terra-cottas are not in a partner relationship with the goddess" — the horse-and-rider statuettes (the "Host of Heaven" in domestic form) are found alongside pillar figurines but never grouped as couples
- "Neither biblical texts nor Iron Age IIC inscriptions offer any evidence for such a relationship"
- The seal in illus. 331b — the only clear male-female divine pair — "is not of Israelite-Judahite provenance" and "can be related only indirectly — if at all — to Yahweh and Asherah"
- "If Yahweh did at some time have a partner, it would probably have been at the time of Manasseh. Nevertheless, we have not been able so far to find any solid evidence"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The absence of divine couple imagery in Judah is a significant finding. But the "Queen of Heaven" cult described in Jeremiah 7 and 44 — where women bake cakes for the goddess and pour out libations — sure sounds like independent goddess worship, regardless of whether she was officially "married" to Yahweh.

**What's at stake:** The popular narrative — "Yahweh had a wife named Asherah!" — collapses under this evidence. There WAS a powerful female divine presence in 7th-century Judah. But she was not depicted as Yahweh's consort. She was worshiped independently (as "Queen of Heaven") or as a mediating entity ("his asherah"). The relationship was hierarchical (she's subordinate to him in his asherah form) or parallel (she's the Queen of Heaven, he's the Lord of Hosts), but not conjugal. This is a more complex picture than either the "divine couple" enthusiasts or the orthodox monotheists allow.

**Who disagrees:** Dever, Römer, and most scholars who read the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions as evidence of a consort relationship. Also Manasseh's installation of an asherah in the Temple — the deuteronomistic editor clearly thought this was about a goddess, not just a cultic symbol.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of divine couple imagery could mean the couple was SO obvious it didn't need depiction — or that the relationship was depicted in media that didn't survive (textiles, wood). The biblical texts that pair Yahweh with the asherah polemically suggest the deuteronomists at least perceived the relationship as dangerously close.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger are methodologically rigorous: if the evidence doesn't show divine couples, don't claim divine couples. But I think they may be holding themselves to an excessively strict standard. The pillar figurines and the "Queen of Heaven" cult represent a genuine goddess presence in Judahite religion. Whether she was "married" to Yahweh or not may be the wrong question — she was worshiped, she mattered, and she was eventually suppressed. The "consort" language may be an unhelpful imposition of human marriage categories onto divine relationships that were more fluid.

## Claim 5: The aniconic turn — Judahite name seals become overwhelmingly image-free during the 7th century

**Authors' claim:** "The Judahite name seals of Iron Age IIC show a clear tendency toward aniconicity, shown in the way that iconographic decorations are limited to vegetal and ornamental elements or are avoided altogether."

This is the material correlate of the "orthodox reaction." While pillar figurines flourish in domestic contexts, the administrative elite — the people using seals — abandon figurative imagery. Over 300 bullae from 7th-6th century Jerusalem show ZERO anthropomorphic deities and virtually no astral symbols.

**Evidence presented:**
- Over 80 impressions of private seals on vessel handles from late 8th/early 7th centuries — two-line name inscriptions without iconographic elements
- "Both the site distribution and dating of the context for each find show that this is a typical phenomenon in Judah, one that began at the time of Hezekiah"
- The Ophel archive: 55 bullae, only 4 with iconic decorations — and those are minor (a bird, a griffin, a ladder motif, one crescent moon standard)
- The "Burnt Archive": 255 bullae from 211 different seals — "only 13 have substantive iconographic motifs"
- "To date, neither an anthropomorphic image of a deity nor an astral symbol has appeared on even one single bulla of the more than 300 examples from the seventh and sixth centuries"
- Hebrew name seals are approximately 70% aniconic — compared to ~30% for Ammonite seals, an even more dramatic contrast when considering the total numbers
- Two-sided seals show the pattern consciously: the owner's name on one side (for sealing documents "orthodoxly"), a deity image on the reverse (for personal amulet use)
- On aniconic seals, decoration is limited to: double lines separating name registers, ladder-shaped bands, rosettes with buds — all ornamental/vegetal, never figural

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The statistical pattern across multiple archives and hundreds of seals is overwhelming. This is not a fluke of preservation.

**What's at stake:** This is the archaeological footprint of the Deuteronomistic reform BEFORE Deuteronomy was written. The aniconic impulse was not a post-exilic theological invention — it was an elite cultural practice in 7th-century Jerusalem, visible in the most mundane administrative artifacts. This connects directly to Römer's argument about the missing statue of Yahweh: the aniconic trend among the elite meant that even if there WAS a statue in the Temple (Manasseh may have put one there), the administrative class had already shifted to text-based, image-free religious expression.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the aniconic turn to the exile or later. H. Weippert attributed the aniconic seals to "growing literacy" — which Keel & Uehlinger reject as insufficient explanation, given that neighboring cultures with comparable literacy did NOT show the same aniconic tendency.

**Alternative reading:** The aniconic seals could reflect practical bureaucratic standardization rather than theological conviction — administrators used simpler seals because they were faster to produce and easier to read.

**My assessment:** The bureaucratic explanation fails because (a) neighboring bureaucracies didn't go aniconic, (b) the aniconic trend appears alongside other evidence of religious reform (Ketef Hinnom amulets, Josiah's reforms), and (c) the two-sided seals show the owners were consciously managing the tension between amulet function (image) and sealing function (name-only). This was a deliberate religious choice, not just administrative convenience.

## Claim 6: The Ketef Hinnom silver amulets — the earliest biblical text — represent "early deuteronomistic" orthodoxy where text replaces image

**Authors' claim:** "Not Asherah, and not an 'angel of Yahweh,' but Yahweh himself — mediated by the presence of a text! — accompanies the deceased, with his blessing and protection, into the cold darkness of the grave."

The two silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom (Jerusalem, ca. 600 BCE) contain the priestly blessing from Numbers 6:24-26 — the earliest surviving biblical text, predating the Dead Sea Scrolls by centuries. In the context of Keel & Uehlinger's survey, they represent the culmination of the aniconic tendency: the WORD replaces the IMAGE as the medium of divine presence.

**Evidence presented:**
- The amulets were found in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom, Jerusalem, dating to the end of Iron Age IIC (early 6th century)
- Text: "May Yahweh bless you and keep you; may Yahweh make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you; may Yahweh lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace" (Num 6:24-26)
- "The two silver amulets present early evidence for the practice prescribed in Exod 13:9; Deut 6:8; 11:18, which calls for wearing the words from the Torah on the body"
- "In contrast to the evidence discussed in §§189ff., it is worth noting that there are no terra-cottas of doves, goddesses, or riders in the graves of Ketef Hinnom" — COMPLETE ABSENCE of the domestic piety artifacts found everywhere else
- "The motherly Asherah is far removed from the piety documented in this grave"
- The tomb ALSO contained a Phoenician Bes head amulet and a Bastet pendant — showing the family still used small non-Judahite protective amulets alongside their Yahwistic texts
- "The combination that links Deut 7:9 and Num 6:24f. points to a link between priestly and 'early deuteronomistic' thinking"
- The metaphor of Yahweh's "shining countenance" (ya'er panaw) may derive from solar concepts (Korpel 1989) or lunar concepts — in either case, the anthropomorphic divine face replaces the asherah as the source of blessing

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH for the dating and text identification. MEDIUM for the interpretation that this represents a conscious rejection of goddess piety (the absence of figurines could be specific to this family rather than programmatic).

**What's at stake:** These amulets show that by 600 BCE, at least some Jerusalemites had made the transition from image-based to text-based religious practice. The divine presence is no longer mediated by a figurine or a cultic symbol — it's mediated by WORDS worn on the body. This is the birth of the "religion of the book" — not in the Persian period, but in the last decades of the Judean monarchy. And it coexisted with the pillar figurine piety — the two systems were contemporary, not sequential.

**Who disagrees:** Barkay (the excavator) and most scholars accept the dating. Disagreement focuses on whether these amulets represent a widespread movement or an isolated elite practice.

**Alternative reading:** The combination of Yahwistic text amulets WITH Bes and Bastet pendants in the same tomb suggests eclecticism, not orthodoxy. This family was hedging its bets — Yahweh's blessing AND Egyptian protective magic. Not a "reform" but a both/and approach.

**My assessment:** The both/and reading is actually MORE historically plausible than the "orthodox reform" reading. Real religious change is messy. The Ketef Hinnom family had Yahwistic texts AND Egyptian amulets. They represent a transitional moment — the text is gaining importance but the old protective magic hasn't been abandoned. This is exactly what you'd expect in a period of genuine religious transformation: people don't flip switches, they accumulate.

## Claim 7: The Assyrian "Queen of Heaven" cult was not imposed by force — it was a local synthesis of Assyrian Ishtar and indigenous Asherah

**Authors' claim:** "Unless the relation between the 'Queen of Heaven' cult and the Assyrian Ishtar can be explained by means of some intermediary factors, then there is no evidence that the Assyrian administration made a deliberate attempt to propagate this cult through force."

The "Queen of Heaven" condemned in Jeremiah 7 and 44 — where women bake cakes for the goddess, pour out libations, and burn incense — was not an Assyrian imperial imposition. It was a local Judean synthesis: the Assyrian Ishtar (known from seal amulets found in Palestine) merged with the indigenous Asherah to create a new, popular, astral-tinged goddess figure.

**Evidence presented:**
- Ishtar appears on Assyrian stamp seals found in Palestine — "she is found most frequently among the deities represented anthropomorphically"
- But "iconographic evidence does not support her being accepted in Judah or Jerusalem" — the Assyrian Ishtar imagery doesn't penetrate Judean glyptic art the way the Moon God of Haran does
- The "Queen of Heaven" cult described in Jeremiah 7 and 44 is focused on family piety, especially women's concerns — baking, libations, incense burning for food, health, security (Jer 44:17f.)
- "The aim of this cult was the transmission of blessing in the form of sufficient food, health, and security" — the EXACT same function as "Yahweh's asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud
- "The Judahite 'Queen of Heaven' was probably none other than a resurrected form of the female human form of Asherah" — with the difference that she functioned as an INDEPENDENT entity, not as Yahweh's subordinate mediating symbol
- The iconographic evidence "documents both extremes": Assyrian Ishtar in glyptic art, Judean Asherah in terra-cotta production — "the two were never merged into one divine form"
- No Assyrian religious coercion: "there is no evidence in the country during the time of Assyrian occupation for the ostensibly Assyrian cult of the 'Queen of Heaven.' That cult appears only long after the collapse of the Assyrian superpower, in the sixth century"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The synthesis hypothesis is plausible but speculative. The lack of inscriptions naming the "Queen of Heaven" in 7th-century Judah is frustrating — we have only the biblical polemic.

**What's at stake:** If the "Queen of Heaven" is an indigenous revival rather than an Assyrian import, then Israelite religion's "Canaanite" elements are not foreign corruptions — they're internal developments that re-emerge under changing social conditions.

**Who disagrees:** Olyan (1987a — identifies Queen of Heaven as Astarte), Koch (1988 — identifies her as Asherah). Spieckermann (1982) argues for Assyrian religious coercion.

**Alternative reading:** The timing problem (Queen of Heaven cult appears AFTER Assyrian collapse) could mean it was always there but only became visible in the sources at that point — or that the collapse of Assyrian authority removed whatever was suppressing local cults.

**My assessment:** The timing IS suspicious — why does the Queen of Heaven cult surface in Jeremiah only in the early 6th century? Keel & Uehlinger's answer (it was a delayed synthesis that took time to develop) is plausible but not fully satisfying. I suspect the cult existed throughout the 7th century and Jeremiah's focus on it reflects the crisis of the Babylonian siege, not the cult's novelty.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Iron Age IIC

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Astralization replaces solarization | HIGH | Evidence of strong solar continuity through IIC |
| 2. Moon God of Haran = El = lunar Yahweh | MEDIUM-HIGH | Better evidence distinguishing El from Yahweh in this period |
| 3. JPFs are a revival, not continuity; dated to IIC | HIGH | Finds of securely-dated IIB pillar figurines in Judah |
| 4. No evidence for consort relationship | MEDIUM | Discovery of divine couple imagery in Judahite context |
| 5. Aniconic turn in 7th-century Judahite seals | VERY HIGH | The pattern is overwhelming across hundreds of artifacts |
| 6. Ketef Hinnom = early deuteronomistic text-centered piety | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence this was an isolated family practice, not a movement |
| 7. Queen of Heaven = local Ishtar-Asherah synthesis | MEDIUM | Inscriptions naming the goddess in 7th-century Judah |

**Strongest section:** Claims 3 and 5 together. The dating of pillar figurines to IIC (not IIB) + the aniconic turn in seals = the most important chronological correction in the entire Asherah debate. Dever's figurines are real and important — but they belong to the 7th century, not the period of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions.

**Weakest section:** Claim 7 (Queen of Heaven identification). Without inscriptional evidence from Judah naming the goddess, we're left matching Jeremiah's polemic against an iconographic record that shows two related but never merged goddess figures (Assyrian Ishtar vs. local Asherah). The identification is plausible but not proven.

**Most important finding for the project:** The simultaneity of three contradictory trends in Iron IIC: (1) astralization under imperial influence, (2) goddess revival in domestic piety, (3) aniconic text-centered orthodoxy among the elite. This is NOT a story of linear progress toward monotheism. It's a three-way contest, and "orthodoxy" is the UNDERDOG that eventually wins — not because it was always dominant, but because its adherents survived the exile and wrote the history.

## Chapter-Level Assessment

**Overall quality of argument:** HIGH. Part VI is as methodologically rigorous as Part V, with the added complexity of tracking three simultaneous and contradictory trends. The integration of biblical texts (Jeremiah, 2 Kings), epigraphy (Ketef Hinnom amulets), and iconography (seals, bullae, figurines) is a model of how ancient Israelite religion should be studied.

**What shifts for the project:**
- **Dever's figurine argument is chronologically corrected but substantively validated.** The 3,000+ pillar figurines ARE evidence of goddess worship. But they're 7th-century evidence, not 8th-century. Dever is right that Smith ignores them. He's wrong about what period they illuminate.
- **The aniconic turn is dated earlier than most scholars think** — it begins with Hezekiah in the late 8th century, accelerates through the 7th, and is visible in administrative seals long before the exile. This supports Römer's argument that the missing statue of Yahweh was not an absence but an achievement.
- **The goddess revival in Iron IIC is the crucial counter-narrative to the orthodox story.** The Bible tells us about Josiah's reform. The figurines tell us about everyone who ignored it. The two stories are both true — they just describe different populations.
- **The Ketef Hinnom amulets are the material bridge between Iron Age religion and biblical religion.** The transition from image to text, from figurine to benediction, is right there in a 6th-century Jerusalem tomb.
