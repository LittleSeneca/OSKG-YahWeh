# Kaufmann — Chapter XIII: The Prophecy of the Fall: Jeremiah and Ezekiel

Kaufmann's climactic argument: the prophets of the fall were not innovators or universalists breaking with a pagan popular religion. They were products of the Torah literature, rooted in Deuteronomy and the Priestly Code, calling Israel back to a monotheism it had always possessed. The fall of Jerusalem was not caused by exceptional moral decay or pagan syncretism — it was the cumulative judgment on Manasseh's unique idolatrous eruption, filtered through the Torah's warning literature.

## Claim 1: The fall of Jerusalem was not caused by exceptional moral decay
→ [[claim-fall-jerusalem-not-exceptional-moral-decay]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.1** | Confidence: medium
  Genuinely interesting case — Jeremiah survived his enemies. But understates biblical evidence for social dissolution.
  Depends on: [[claim-no-fundamental-religious-rift-prophets-people]]
  Supports: [[claim-josiah-reforms-endured-no-pagan-revival]], [[claim-evil-explained-human-rebellion-history-not-myth]]
  Challenged by: [[claim-amos-innovation-social-morality-national-destiny]]

**What's at stake:** Whether the exile was a punishment for genuine national sin (the biblical view) or a geopolitical event retrospectively theologized. If Kaufmann is right, the entire prophetic enterprise was not about moral reform of a uniquely corrupt nation but about interpreting geopolitical catastrophe through Torah categories.

**Who disagrees:** The prophets themselves. Jeremiah's indictment (Jer 5-7) is comprehensive. Römer reads the prophetic denunciations as evidence of real social crisis. Albertz ties prophetic critique to specific social changes under the monarchy.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that Judah wasn't uniquely corrupt by ancient Near Eastern standards, and right that theodicy drives the biblical interpretation. But he overcorrects — there WAS real social disintegration under the late monarchy that the prophets responded to, even if their theological framing amplified it.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's strongest point is the treatment of Jeremiah by his enemies — if Jerusalem were truly a den of wickedness, the prophet who calls for surrender wouldn't have survived. This is a genuinely sharp observation. But his claim that morality actually IMPROVED since the 8th century prophets is speculative and contradicts the trajectory suggested by the biblical texts themselves.

## Claim 2: No fundamental religious rift existed between prophets and people
→ [[claim-no-fundamental-religious-rift-prophets-people]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.2** | Confidence: medium
  Distinction between polytheistic BELIEF vs. cultic PRACTICE is genuinely important. But conflating "no polytheistic belief" with "no syncretism" is problematic.
  Depends on: [[claim-israelite-idolatry-vestigial-fetishism-not-genuine-polytheism]], [[claim-biblical-idolatry-not-genuine-mythological-polytheism]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-baal-yahweh-coexisted-judges-period-no-conflict]], [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]], [[claim-bible-preserves-polytheistic-traces]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann is right that the prophets don't accuse Israel of polytheistic BELIEF — they accuse them of cultic practices. This distinction IS genuinely important. But he conflates "the people didn't believe in other gods" with "the people's religion wasn't syncretistic," which are different claims.

**What's at stake:** The entire critical narrative of Israelite religion. If there was no pagan popular religion, the prophets weren't battling polytheism — they were battling cultic deviance within a monotheistic framework. This makes the religion of Israel continuous rather than revolutionary.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus. Smith (Origins Ch 8-10) argues that monotheistic rhetoric emerges precisely in the exilic period as a NEW development. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism through the prophetic corpus. Dever's archaeological evidence of popular polytheistic practice directly challenges Kaufmann's claim.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the prophets' polemic focuses on practice, not theology. But this could be because the theology WASN'T the issue — nobody denied YHWH's ultimate supremacy. The issue was that people ALSO worshipped other deities (Asherah, the Queen of Heaven, Baal) alongside YHWH. That's not polytheism in the sense of denying YHWH; it's polytheism in the sense of acknowledging other divine beings. Kaufmann's definition of "genuine polytheism" requires mythological belief, which lets him dismiss cultic practice as "vestigial."

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most original and most problematic argument. He defines polytheism so narrowly (requiring theogony, mythology, metadivine realm) that Israelite practice can never qualify. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions bless "Yahweh and his Asherah" — that's not mythology, but it IS recognition of another deity alongside YHWH. Kaufmann would say this is "vestigial fetishism" rather than genuine polytheistic belief. But that's a distinction without a difference — if people think Asherah can bless them, she's a deity in their practical religious life, regardless of whether they have a theogony for her.

## Claim 3: Josiah's reforms ENDURED — there was no pagan revival under Jehoiakim or Zedekiah
→ [[claim-josiah-reforms-endured-no-pagan-revival]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  Kaufmann's strongest historical argument. Jeremiah's silence about temple idolatry vs. Ezekiel's vision is genuinely striking.
  Depends on: [[claim-ezekiel-visions-fantasy-not-historical-sources]], [[claim-jeremiah-prophet-of-deuteronomy-torah-derived]]
  Supports: [[claim-josianic-reform-organic-israelite-development-not-priestly-power-grab]], [[claim-exile-extinction-idolatry-proves-popular-monotheism]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-josiah-reform-invented-exclusive-yahwism]], [[claim-josiah-reform-removed-asherah]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is Kaufmann's strongest historical argument in the chapter. The contrast between Ezekiel's visionary temple abominations and Jeremiah's total silence about them in his actual temple ministry IS genuinely striking and demands explanation.

**What's at stake:** Whether the exile was punishment for ongoing, royally-sponsored idolatry (the critical view) or for the cumulative weight of Manasseh's unique apostasy (Kaufmann's view). If Kaufmann is right, the popular religion was more faithful than scholars suppose.

**Who disagrees:** The critical reading of Kings and Jeremiah typically assumes Manasseh's cults were restored. Ezekiel 8 is usually taken as evidence. Römer reads Ezekiel 8 as reflecting actual cultic practice.

**Alternative reading:** The Kings author's silence could be theological framing (Manasseh as the ultimate villain whose sin couldn't be undone), not historical reportage. And Jeremiah's relative silence about temple idolatry could reflect different rhetorical priorities, not absence of the practices.

**My assessment:** This is persuasive on its own terms. Kaufmann catches something real: if Ezekiel 8 describes actual temple practice in Zedekiah's time, Jeremiah's silence is inexplicable. Ezekiel's visions are better understood as retrospective — the ghosts of Manasseh's abominations haunting the prophet's imagination. The one genuinely idolatrous cult Jeremiah DOES attack (the Queen of Heaven in Jer 7 and 44) is described as a private, impoverished, old-women's practice without temple, altar, or clergy. Kaufmann's reading of the evidence here is tighter than the critical alternative.

## Claim 4: Jeremiah was the prophet of Deuteronomy — his entire worldview is Torah-derived
→ [[claim-jeremiah-prophet-of-deuteronomy-torah-derived]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.4** | Confidence: high
  Literary dependence widely recognized. Memory error in slave law citation (7 years for 6) is genuinely clever evidence.
  Depends on: [[claim-torah-pre-prophetic-earliest-stage-israelite-religion]], [[claim-torah-sealed-canonized-before-prophecy]]
  Supports: [[claim-prophets-inherited-monotheism-did-not-invent]], [[claim-josiah-reforms-endured-no-pagan-revival]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-deuteronomy-written-josiah-reform]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]]

**Confidence:** HIGH. The literary dependence of Jeremiah on Deuteronomy is widely recognized even in critical scholarship. Kaufmann's specific arguments about Jeremiah's education on D before its "discovery" are plausible.

**What's at stake:** The direction of influence. Kaufmann argues Jeremiah learned monotheism FROM the Torah, not vice versa. This reverses the critical sequence (prophets → Torah). If Jeremiah's monotheism is Deuteronomic, and Deuteronomy is pre-prophetic, then monotheism predates classical prophecy.

**Who disagrees:** The critical dating of D. If D was composed in Josiah's time (the standard critical view), then Jeremiah and D are contemporaries — Jeremiah could be influencing D's formulation or vice versa. Kaufmann's claim requires D to substantially predate Jeremiah's education.

**Alternative reading:** The dense Deuteronomistic language in Jeremiah may reflect redaction — the Deuteronomistic editors who shaped Jeremiah's book inserted their language. This would explain the phraseological overlap without requiring Jeremiah to have studied D in his youth.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's argument about Jeremiah's memory error in citing the slave law (seven years instead of six) is genuinely clever — a later glossator wouldn't make that mistake. The sheer density of Deuteronomic language in Jeremiah is hard to explain purely by redaction. But the relationship could be bidirectional: Jeremiah was shaped by Deuteronomic circles AND his book was later edited by them. The inaugural vision predating the book's "discovery" is Kaufmann's strongest point for Jeremiah's early education in D.

## Claim 5: The prophets did not innovate monotheism — they inherited it
→ [[claim-jeremiah-ezekiel-inherited-monotheism-not-innovators]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.5** | Confidence: medium
  Persuasive that Jeremiah/Ezekiel worked within an existing monotheistic framework. But claiming POPULAR religion was monotheistic goes beyond evidence.
  Depends on: [[claim-jeremiah-prophet-of-deuteronomy-torah-derived]], [[claim-prophets-inherited-monotheism-did-not-invent]], [[claim-classical-prophecy-inherited-monotheism-popular-religion]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-romer-narrative-arc-desert-god-to-only-god]], [[claim-true-monotheism-emerged-babylonian-exile]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]]
  Challenged by: [[claim-bible-preserves-polytheistic-traces]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann is persuasive that Jeremiah and Ezekiel worked within an existing monotheistic framework rather than creating one. But his claim that the POPULAR religion was monotheistic goes beyond the evidence for the Torah literature's monotheism.

**What's at stake:** The entire developmental model. If the prophets didn't create monotheism, then monotheism predates them — which means it's earlier than critical scholarship allows. This is Kaufmann's most important historical claim.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus that monotheism emerged THROUGH the prophetic movement. Smith (Origins Ch 10) traces monotheistic language developing within Isaiah 40-55. Römer sees the exile as the crucible in which monotheism crystallized.

**Alternative reading:** The Torah literature IS monotheistic, but it reached that form LATE — after the prophets. The monotheism Jeremiah and Ezekiel "inherit" from the Torah is actually the monotheism the Torah learned from them (or their tradition). Kaufmann mistakes the final form of the literature for its original form.

**My assessment:** This is the core methodological issue. Kaufmann treats the Torah literature as a fixed, early corpus that the prophets drew upon. Critical scholarship treats it as a fluid tradition that reached its monotheistic form in dialogue with — and partly after — the prophets. The evidence of Jeremiah's dependence on Deuteronomy is real, but it doesn't prove Deuteronomy is pre-prophetic in its FINAL form. It could prove that the Deuteronomic school and the prophetic movement developed together. Kaufmann's conclusion only follows if you accept his dating of the Torah literature (Chapter V), which I haven't evaluated yet.

## Claim 6: Ezekiel's visions are fantasy — not historical sources for Israelite paganism
→ [[claim-ezekiel-visions-fantasy-not-historical-sources]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.6** | Confidence: medium-high
  Jeremiah-Ezekiel contradiction is genuinely strong — if Ezekiel 8 describes actual temple practice, Jeremiah's silence is inexplicable.
  Depends on: [[claim-josiah-reforms-endured-no-pagan-revival]]
  Supports: [[claim-no-fundamental-religious-rift-prophets-people]], [[claim-popular-religion-non-mythological-not-genuine-polytheism]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-bible-preserves-polytheistic-traces]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on Ezekiel 8-11 as visionary rather than journalistic; MEDIUM on the historical surveys. Kaufmann's argument that Ezekiel's temple visions cannot be reconciled with Jeremiah's testimony is genuinely strong. His reading of the historical surveys as hyperbolic allegory rather than reportage is plausible but not certain.

**What's at stake:** A significant portion of the critical evidence for Israelite syncretism comes from Ezekiel's descriptions. If those are fantasy, the case for popular polytheism loses a major witness.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who use Ezekiel as a source for Israelite religious practice. Römer, Smith, and Dever all draw on Ezekiel to reconstruct popular religion, though none treat it uncritically.

**Alternative reading:** Ezekiel's visions are exaggerated and theologized, but they reflect real practices he knew about. The visionary frame doesn't make the CONTENT fictional — it's a literary device. Prophets regularly use visionary settings to comment on real conditions.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's best argument is the Jeremiah-Ezekiel contradiction. If Ezekiel 8 describes actual temple practice, Jeremiah's silence is indeed inexplicable. But Kaufmann overstates: Ezekiel's visions could reflect real practices that Jeremiah simply didn't emphasize (different rhetorical purposes) or that occurred in spaces Jeremiah didn't access. The logical impossibility of the walled-up chamber is actually a good point — it signals the visionary nature of the entire scene. I think Kaufmann is right that Ezekiel 8 shouldn't be read as journalistic reportage, but wrong that this means there was NO pagan practice in the temple. The visions may be Manasseh-haunted memory rather than current reality — which is Kaufmann's own position.

## Claim 7: Ezekiel is more priest than prophet — his ideology is pre-prophetic Torah religion
→ [[claim-ezekiel-priestly-pre-prophetic-torah-religion]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.7** | Confidence: medium-high
  Contrast with 8th-century prophets is real and well-observed. But P could be contemporary with Ezekiel, not earlier.
  Depends on: [[claim-p-priestly-source-earliest-not-latest]], [[claim-torah-pre-prophetic-earliest-stage-israelite-religion]]
  Supports: [[claim-jeremiah-prophet-of-deuteronomy-torah-derived]], [[claim-prophetic-hierarchy-morality-godlike-cult-symbolic]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The contrast between Ezekiel's categories and those of the 8th-century prophets is real and well-observed. Ezekiel IS different — more ritual, more priestly, less focused on social justice. Kaufmann's explanation (Ezekiel is drawing on P) is plausible but not the only possible one.

**What's at stake:** Whether Ezekiel represents a DEVELOPMENT beyond classical prophecy (the critical view) or a RETURN to pre-prophetic Torah religion (Kaufmann's view). If Kaufmann is right, Ezekiel isn't a late evolution but an archaic survival.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholarship typically sees Ezekiel as reflecting exilic or post-exilic priestly concerns. The dependence on P is read as evidence that P already existed in some form by Ezekiel's time (exilic), not that P is pre-prophetic.

**Alternative reading:** Ezekiel's priestly character reflects his social location (Zadokite priest) and his exilic context (where ritual/identity markers become more important), not the antiquity of his ideology. He draws on P because P is the literature of his priestly milieu — but P itself may be roughly contemporary with him.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observation that Ezekiel differs from the 8th-century prophets in systematic ways is sound. His explanation (Ezekiel = pre-prophetic Torah religion) requires P to be substantially earlier than Ezekiel, which is the dating question Chapter V must answer. The evidence here is consistent with Kaufmann's thesis but doesn't independently prove it — Ezekiel's use of P is equally compatible with P being roughly contemporary (standard critical dating).

## Claim 8: The exiles were monotheists who never considered worshipping Babylonian gods
→ [[claim-exiles-monotheists-never-considered-babylonian-gods]] | **kaufmann-ri-13.8** | Confidence: medium
  Ezekiel 20: the exiles say "let us serve wood and stone," not "let us accept Marduk." But argument from silence; survivorship bias.
  Depends on: [[claim-jeremiah-ezekiel-inherited-monotheism-not-innovators]], [[claim-exile-extinction-idolatry-proves-popular-monotheism]]
  Contradicted by: [[claim-true-monotheism-emerged-babylonian-exile]], [[claim-imperial-crisis-demanded-super-national-god]], [[claim-monotheism-as-exilic-rhetoric-not-religious-revolution]]
  Challenged by: [[claim-romer-narrative-arc-desert-god-to-only-god]]

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is a genuinely interesting reading of Ezekiel 20, and the observation about the absence of polemic against Babylonian mythology is striking. But it's an argument from silence, and the archaeological/epigraphic evidence for syncretism at Elephantine and elsewhere complicates the picture.

**What's at stake:** Whether the exile was a theological crisis (Marduk defeated YHWH?) or a crisis of faith in the covenant (why did YHWH abandon us?). Kaufmann says it was the latter — the exiles never doubted YHWH's power, only his favor.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the exile as generating genuine theological crisis. Römer argues the exile forced monotheism to develop in response to the apparent defeat of the national god. Smith sees Second Isaiah's monotheism as a creative response to this crisis.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of explicit polemic against Babylonian gods could reflect the editors' priorities, not the exiles' beliefs. And the exiles who DID assimilate wouldn't appear in the biblical record — survivorship bias. Kaufmann's reading of Ezekiel 20 is perceptive but the text could also be interpreted as Ezekiel's polemical CARICATURE of the exiles' position.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reading of Ezekiel 20:32-44 is genuinely illuminating. The exiles don't say "Marduk is stronger" — they say "let's be like everyone else and worship wood and stone." That IS a different thing. But I suspect the reality was messier than either Kaufmann or the critical consensus allows: some exiles assimilated fully (and vanished from the record), some maintained YHWH worship alongside Babylonian practice, and some (the ones who produced the biblical texts) maintained exclusive YHWH worship. Kaufmann takes the last group as representative of the whole, which is the same move he makes throughout the book.

## Chapter XIII Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Fall not caused by moral decay | MEDIUM | Jeremiah's own oracles describe real corruption |
| 2: No rift between prophets and people | MEDIUM | Defines polytheism so narrowly it excludes Israelite practice by definition |
| 3: Josiah's reforms endured | MEDIUM-HIGH | Kings' silence could be theological, not historical |
| 4: Jeremiah = prophet of Deuteronomy | HIGH | Redaction could explain some Deuteronomic language |
| 5: Prophets didn't innovate monotheism | MEDIUM | Depends on Ch V's dating of Torah literature |
| 6: Ezekiel's visions are fantasy | MEDIUM-HIGH | Jeremiah-Ezekiel contradiction is genuinely strong |
| 7: Ezekiel = pre-prophetic priestly ideology | MEDIUM-HIGH | P could be contemporary with Ezekiel, not earlier |
| 8: Exiles were monotheists at bottom | MEDIUM | Survivorship bias in the biblical record |

**Strongest section:** The Jeremiah-Ezekiel contradiction (Claims 3, 6). Kaufmann's demonstration that Ezekiel's temple visions cannot be squared with Jeremiah's eyewitness testimony is the most empirically grounded argument in the chapter. If Ezekiel 8 describes actual temple practice in Zedekiah's time, Jeremiah would have seen it and said something. The silence is deafening.

**Weakest section:** Claim 2's extension to popular religion. Kaufmann demonstrates that the prophets don't accuse Israel of polytheistic THEOLOGY. But he then leaps to the conclusion that popular religion was monotheistic. The distinction between "mythological polytheism" and "cultic deviance" is doing immense work here — and it may be a distinction the practitioners themselves wouldn't have recognized.

**Methodological note — Kaufmann's procedure in this chapter:** Kaufmann is doing something genuinely clever. He takes the biblical texts at face value (the prophets rebuke Israel for idolatry) but then reinterprets what "idolatry" means (not genuine polytheistic belief, but vestigial fetishistic practice). This lets him accept the biblical testimony while rejecting the critical conclusion that Israel was polytheistic. The move works IF his definition of "genuine polytheism" (requiring theogony, mythology, metadivine realm) is correct. If polytheism can exist without a developed mythology — if people can worship multiple deities without having creation myths about them — then Kaufmann's distinction collapses, and Israelite "idolatry" WAS polytheism, just not the philosophically elaborated kind he's defined.

**The unanswered question:** Where did the Torah literature's monotheism come from, if not from the prophets? Kaufmann has argued that the prophets inherited it from the Torah, not vice versa. But he hasn't yet explained where the TORAH got it. That's Chapter VI (The Origins of Israelite Religion), which I haven't read yet. This chapter's arguments are only complete when paired with Kaufmann's account of monotheism's origins.
