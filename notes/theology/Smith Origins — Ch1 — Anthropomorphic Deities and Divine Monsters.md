# Smith — Chapter 1: Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters

Smith lays out the most basic structural division in Ugaritic divinity: the distinction between anthropomorphic/benevolent deities (center/sown/cult) and monstrous/destructive divine creatures (periphery/outback/no cult). This spatial mapping, drawn from Wiggermann's Mesopotamian typology and Schloen's center-periphery model of Ugaritic society, becomes the foundation for understanding Israel's "monotheistic poetics" — how biblical texts progressively tame, depersonalize, and deny the divine status of cosmic enemies.

---

## Claim 1: Ugaritic divinity is organized by a center/periphery spatial distinction — benevolent deities inhabit the center, monstrous divine forces the periphery and beyond

**Author's claim:** "The main distinction between anthropomorphic deities and monstrous divine creatures... The division of divinity between deities and demons, so to speak, corresponds to the mapping of divine space." The center is "home" — the sown, the cultivated, the realm of cult. The periphery is the "outback" (mdbr), a transitional zone of danger. Beyond the periphery are the underworld (Mot) and cosmic waters (Yamm, thmtm). This is based on Wiggermann's Mesopotamian typology and Schloen's model of Ugaritic society.

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.23.65-69 explicitly contrasts the "sown" (mdr') with the "outback, steppe" (mdbr). The sown contains food, wine, and royal cultic devotion (1.23.70-76; 4.149.14-16 refers to "sacrifice of the queen in the sown").
- Benevolent deities receive cult at sacred mountains: El at mount ks, Baal at Sapan, Anat at 'inbb, Dagan at Tuttul, Resheph at bbt, Athtart at Mari (1.100). Cosmic enemies do not have holy mountains.
- The Baal Cycle places Mot's confrontation with Baal in the mdbr at the edge of the underworld (1.6 II 20; cf. 1.5 VI 6, 29; 1.6 I 8-14).
- El's home is at "the channels of the Double-Deeps" ('apq thmtm, 1.4 IV 21-22), at "the meeting-place of the Double-Deeps" — positioned at the edge between near and far.
- Center has a subdivision: HOME (local cultivation, home deities) vs. FOREIGN (Egypt/Crete, foreign deities like Kothar). Periphery subdivides into what humans experience (outback, near surface waters) and what lies beyond (underworld, netherworld, cosmic waters).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The textual evidence for spatial mapping of divinity across Ugaritic sources is strong and multi-generic (myths, incantations, administrative texts, letters). Wiggermann's typology was developed for Mesopotamia; Smith's extension to Ugarit is well-supported textually.

**What's at stake:** If the center/periphery distinction is fundamental to West Semitic divinity, then Israelite monotheism's treatment of cosmic enemies (taming, depersonalizing, denying divinity) represents a radical reorganization of cosmic space, not just a change in belief about how many gods exist. Monotheism becomes a cosmic re-mapping — what was divine periphery gets pulled into the created order.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the center/periphery model as an etic imposition rather than an emic category. The specific Ugaritic terminology (mdr' vs. mdbr) supports Smith, but the three-zone model (center/periphery/beyond) may be Smith's systematization rather than the ancients' own framework.

**Alternative reading:** The spatial mapping could be a literary convention of the Baal Cycle rather than a general Ugaritic conceptual structure. Administrative texts do use mdr' but not necessarily as part of a cosmological scheme. The snake-bite incantation's listing of deities with mountains may simply reflect ritual geography, not a systematic cosmic map.

**My assessment:** The evidence is convincing that spatial categories mattered for organizing divinity. The strongest data point is the across-genre attestation (myth, letter, incantation, administrative text). But Smith may be pressing the three-zone scheme harder than the texts warrant — especially the "center vs. periphery vs. beyond" tripartition. The Baal Cycle uses space narratively, not systematically.

## Claim 2: Deities receive cult at sacred mountains; cosmic enemies do not — and this is a fundamental marker of the benevolent/destructive distinction

**Author's claim:** "Within the center or area of human cultivation and civilization, deities are accorded sacred mountains or cult sites, but cosmic enemies are not." The pantheon as a whole meets on Mount ll (1.2 I 20), locating the divine family within "near" space. The one exception (Mot's mountain knkny in 1.5 V 13) proves the rule: Mot's mountain is the ENTRANCE to the underworld, not a cult site on top.

**Evidence presented:**
- Snake-bite incantation (1.100) assigns mountains/cult-sites to El, Baal, Anat, Athtart, Dagan, Resheph, Athtart, Yarih, ẒẒ and KMT, and Mlk — all benevolent deities.
- Kothar wa-Hasis has no mountain; he dwells in Memphis and Caphtor (1.100.46), reflecting foreign center/trade networks.
- Baal's Mount Sapan is the center point: heading of deity list as "the gods of Sapan" ('il ṣpn, 1.47.1), ritual text "the feast of Sapan" (dbḥ ṣpn, 1.148.1).
- Yamm, Mot, tnn, and other cosmic enemies do not have holy mountains.
- Exceptions: Mot has knkny (1.5 V 13) but it's the entrance to the underworld, not a dwelling on top. Athirat has no divine mountain (she shares El's?). Astral deities Shahar and Shalim are in the heavens (šmmh, 1.100.52).
- Baal's realm is sky, Yamm's is sea, Mot's is underworld — realms mark cosmic competition among second-tier gods.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The pattern is clear and the exceptions are explicable within the framework.

**What's at stake:** This establishes a criterion for "who counts" in the divine world that Israelite religion would eventually invert — Yahweh absorbs all realms (sky, sea, underworld, foreign nations) into one deity's domain, eliminating the spatial logic that justified multiple deities.

**Who disagrees:** Probably few. The data is straightforward. Disagreement would center on whether Smith OVER-reads the absence of cult for cosmic enemies — some may have received apotropaic rites that don't survive in the textual record.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of cult for cosmic enemies may reflect the accidents of preservation rather than a systematic distinction. Apotropaic rituals against destructive forces are well-attested in Mesopotamia; their absence at Ugarit may be a gap in the corpus.

**My assessment:** The pattern holds, but the argument from absence of cult is inherently weaker than positive evidence. I'd be more cautious about claiming cosmic enemies NEVER received cult. The personal name ym'il ("Yamm is god," 4.75 V 14) at least suggests Yamm was considered divine enough to appear in personal names, which implies SOME devotional recognition.

## Claim 3: Home deities are anthropomorphic or associated with domesticated animals; destructive divinities are monstrous and associated with undomesticated species

**Author's claim:** "Benevolent deities are often rendered anthropomorphically, whereas destructive divinities appear as monstrous in character... Whereas cosmic enemies are monstrous or undomesticated, the animals associated with benevolent deities ('attribute animals') lie within the orbit of cultural domestication."

**Evidence presented:**
- El's title: "Bull" (tr) — a domesticated species, appearing ~30 times in the Baal Cycle. Personal name 'iltr, "El is Bull" (4.607.32).
- Baal is presented as a bull-calf (1.5 V 17-21; 1.10 II-III, esp. III 33-37). Amiet: the bull is the storm-god's "attribute animal" in Syrian glyptic.
- Anat is presented as a bird — explicit title "flyer of flyers, she who soars" (d'i d'it rḥpt, 1.108.8); she hovers among a flock of birds over Aqhat (1.18 IV). A drinking mug from Ugarit depicts Anat as a bird behind El's throne.
- Cosmic enemies are snake-dragons: Tunnanu described as "the Twisty Serpent, the Potentate with seven Heads" (1.3 III 40-42). Litan/Leviathan identically described (1.5 I 1-3). Ugaritic glyptic shows warrior-god with snake in each hand.
- The bull/calf imagery behind Exodus 32 and Jeroboam's bulls at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12) — Smith notes debate over whether this is El's iconography (Bethel = "house of El," Genesis 28:10-22) or Baal's.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the general pattern. MEDIUM for the specific claim that bull iconography in Israel maps to El specifically (Smith hedges: "many scholars are inclined to see El's rather than Baal's iconography... but this iconography has been traced back to Baal as well").

**What's at stake:** If the domesticated/monstrous distinction is fundamental, then biblical polemics against "idolatry" involving animal imagery (golden calf, bulls) are targeting legitimate Israelite tradition, not foreign importation. The calves at Bethel and Dan weren't "Canaanite corruption" — they were old El/Baal iconography within Israel's own heritage.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who attribute the bull imagery exclusively to Baal rather than El. The Bethel-as-El-sanctuary argument is strong but not universally accepted. Cross would see El traditions as indigenous, Baal traditions as imported.

**Alternative reading:** The domesticated/undomesticated distinction may be less about divinity's nature and more about literary genre — myths about cosmic conflict require monstrous antagonists. The form follows the narrative function, not a theological taxonomy.

**My assessment:** The pattern is real and well-demonstrated. The ambiguity about whether Israel's bull iconography is El's or Baal's is actually productive — it shows exactly how Israelite religion absorbed and merged traditions from both deities into Yahwism, which is a core Smith argument from *The Early History of God*.

## Claim 4: El has a special, affectionate relationship with the cosmic monsters — they are his "beloved," "calf," "dog," and "daughter" — terms denoting legal selection and vassalage

**Author's claim:** "The Ugaritic material is especially rich in terms of endearment between El and the cosmic enemies... These epithets mark not only El's preference for them over the other gods but also their status as his publicly designated successors."

**Evidence presented:**
- Anat's speech listing defeated cosmic enemies (1.3 III 36-1.3 IV 1): Yamm = "Beloved of El" (mdd 'il), River = "the Great God" ('il rbm), Arsh = "Beloved of El" (mdd ilm), Rebel ('tk) = "Calf of El" ('gl 'il), Fire ('išt) = "Dog of El" (klbt 'ilm), Flame (dbb) = "Daughter of El" (bt 'il).
- Mot also bears ydd 'il/mdd 'il elsewhere. H.Z. Szubin's legal analysis: ydd functions like Solomon's "Jedidiah" — a PUBLIC legal designation of succession. "This status invested the chosen 'beloved' designee with power, authority and title." 
- 'gl and klbt as terms of subservience: Amarna letters use kalbu ("dog") to express vassalage — "What is Abdi-Ashirta, servant and dog...?" (EA 71:16-19). Same usage in Lachish letters (KAI 192:3-4) and 2 Kings 8:13. Akkadian names: A-ga-al-dMarduk ("calf of Marduk"). Ugaritic/Hebrew/Phoenician names with *klb mean "servant."
- Yamm and Mot hold a claim to divine kingship from the perspective of divine patrimony. This preference comes at Baal's expense, "whose paternity marks him as an outsider to the divine family."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for "beloved" as legal term (Szubin's analysis is persuasive and backed by biblical parallel). MEDIUM for "calf" and "dog" as subservience terms — the cross-cultural data is good but the inference from Amarna usage to Ugaritic mythological usage requires a step.

**What's at stake:** This claim reveals a deep STRUCTURAL tension in the Ugaritic pantheon: the head god (El) favors the cosmic monsters over the warrior god (Baal). Baal is an outsider fighting El's favorites. This tension is a key piece of Smith's larger argument about Ugaritic polytheism's "flexibility" — the pantheon is not static harmony but dynamic conflict. And it provides the background for understanding why Israelite religion eventually collapsed El and Baal into Yahweh: the tensions between them had to be resolved.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read ydd/mdd as purely emotional ("beloved, darling") rather than legal. The legal reading depends heavily on Szubin's interpretation of Jedidiah, which is itself debated. Also, those who see Baal's outsider status as a literary device rather than a theological claim about divine family structure.

**Alternative reading:** The "endearment" language may be ironic in context — Anat is BRAGGING about killing El's favorites. The terms may mock El's poor judgment in selecting these monsters rather than neutrally reporting a legal relationship. The subservience reading of 'gl and klbt may over-read Amarna diplomatic conventions into mythological narrative.

**My assessment:** The legal reading of ydd/mdd is the strongest part. The Jedidiah parallel is tight. The "calf/dog" as subservience is plausible but less certain — these could simply be animal epithets without the legal-vassalage valence Smith reads into them. But even without the full legal reading, the basic point stands: El HAS a special relationship with the monsters, and this creates a structural tension with Baal. That's well-established by the primary text.

## Claim 5: Israelite texts progressively tame and deny divine status to cosmic enemies — this is "monotheistic poetics"

**Author's claim:** "Genesis 1... shows some displacement from the traditional picture of both the chief god and the monstrous forces... The narrative encloses the order of the divine creation around these monstrous enemies and, by omission, transforms them into another part of creation. This transformation... involves an alteration of theme as well as literary order... The text manifests a 'monotheistic poetics,' which alters the perception of reality with its created order."

**Evidence presented:**
- Psalm 74:12-17: cosmic enemies (Sea, Tannin, Leviathan) are defeated as prelude to creation. Still divine conflict, but Yahweh's mastery is total — no stalemate like Baal vs. Mot.
- Psalm 104:26: Leviathan as "whom you formed to sport with" — a plaything, not an enemy. Waters are raw material of creation, not opponent. Verse 7: divine rebuke of waters as PART of creation process, not pre-creation battle.
- Job 7:12, 3:8, 26:13: cosmic enemies still as hostile powers. But Job 38:8-11: Sea treated at creation as a newborn babe, not an enemy. Job 40:25-29: Leviathan caught by God's "fishhook," drawn by rope and nose-ring — a tamed pet.
- Genesis 1: tanninim (v. 21) placed WITHIN created order, not before it as primordial opponents. Sun and moon called "greater light" and "lesser light" (1:16) — traditional titles (Ugaritic nyr rbt for sun-goddess, nrt šmm for moon-god) but now referring to created objects, not deities. "Ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left in Genesis 1's 'monotheistic poetics.'"
- Isaiah 25:8: God swallows Death — reversal of Mot's demand to swallow Baal (1.5 I 6-8, II 2-6).
- Ugaritic texts DO treat cosmic enemies as divine: River called "Great God" ('il rbm, 1.3 III 39), Mot is bn 'ilm ("son of El/gods," 1.4 VIII 45-46), personal name ym'il ("Yamm is god," 4.75 V 14). All four named Baal enemies (Sea, Leviathan, Tannin, Death) appear as divine opponents.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The contrast between Ugaritic and biblical treatment of cosmic enemies is one of the best-documented shifts in the field. The specific texts Smith cites are unambiguous.

**What's at stake:** This is the first concrete demonstration of HOW monotheism changed the religious imagination — not by denying other gods existed (that comes later), but by reclassifying divine beings as non-divine. The "monotheistic poetics" concept is powerful: monotheism works at the level of literary structure and cosmology, not just theological assertion.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue Genesis 1 is NOT polemical — the "greater light/lesser light" language is just traditional astronomical terminology, not anti-polytheistic. The tanninim in Genesis 1:21 might simply reflect a different creation tradition, not a deliberate demotion. Conservative scholars who deny any dependency on or engagement with polytheistic mythology.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical texts' treatment of cosmic enemies could reflect Mesopotamian rather than Ugaritic influence (Marduk's definitive victory over Tiamat in Enuma Elish is more "monotheistic" in feel than Baal's stalemate with Mot). The trajectory might be Mesopotamian → Israelite rather than Ugaritic → Israelite.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading is persuasive. The cumulative case — Psalm 74 → Psalm 104 → Job → Genesis 1 — shows a clear trajectory of progressive depersonalization. The "monotheistic poetics" concept is one of the most useful coinages in the book. The one caution: Smith may overstate how DELIBERATE the Genesis 1 polemic is. "Greater light" and "lesser light" might just be the natural way to refer to sun and moon in a culture that had those terms, without conscious anti-polytheistic intent. The EFFECT is monotheistic poetics; the INTENT is harder to prove.

## Claim 6: Divine conflict mythology can encode political powerlessness as well as power — reversing Mendenhall

**Author's claim:** Reversing Mendenhall's claim that "dualistic mythology is always essentially political," Smith argues: "Divine power can encode human power as well as human powerlessness... Divine mastery stands in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)."

**Evidence presented:**
- Psalm 74:12-17 appeals to divine strength at a moment of perceived divine weakness/indifference — the temple has been destroyed. Yahweh's mastery is NOT fully realized; it's a debated matter.
- Isaiah 27:1 presents Leviathan's defeat as future, not accomplished. Isaiah 51:9-11 proclaims divine power as "hope and herald to human weakness at the time of exile."
- Genesis 1, if situated against foreign imperial domination of Judah, presents divine mastery in inverse relation to political reality.
- Baal Cycle shows Baal needing help from other deities and lacking definitive victory — also encoding weakness, not just power.
- "These mythologies, monotheistic and polytheistic, are ultimately not 'dualistic.' Instead, various lines subtly connect the deities and their blessing and the divine monsters... Hope then seems to be the key trope in these texts."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The reading of Psalm 74 as encoding powerlessness is standard. The extension to the Baal Cycle and Genesis 1 is Smith's own synthetic move. Mendenhall's dictum is indeed too narrow, but Smith's reversal may be too neat.

**What's at stake:** If divine conflict narratives encode hope rather than triumphalism, then monotheism's conflict language (Isaiah 27:1, 51:9-11) is not "violent monotheism" (à la Regina Schwartz) but expressions of an oppressed community's hope. This has ethical stakes for how monotheism is evaluated in contemporary discourse.

**Who disagrees:** Mendenhall's original claim has defenders. The "conflict encodes power" reading is standard in ancient Near Eastern studies (the king's victory over enemies mirrors the god's victory over chaos). Smith's reversal makes a valid point about exceptions but may not overturn the general pattern.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical texts that encode weakness (Psalm 74) are LAMENT, a different genre from the triumphalist conflict myths (Enuma Elish, Baal Cycle). Genre, not theological evolution, explains the difference.

**My assessment:** Smith's point about the flexibility of conflict mythology is well-taken and the examples are good. But he may be over-reading one text (Ps 74) as representative when it's actually genre-specific. The broader pattern in ANE royal ideology IS that divine victory mirrors royal/imperial power. Smith's reversal is a useful corrective to Mendenhall's absolutism, but not a replacement thesis.

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Center/periphery organizes Ugaritic divinity | HIGH | Imposing systematization where ancients had looser associations |
| 2: Deities get cult/mountains; cosmic enemies don't | HIGH | Argument from absence; apotropaic rites may be missing from corpus |
| 3: Domesticated animals for deities, monsters for enemies | HIGH | Literary convention vs. theological taxonomy |
| 4: El's "beloved" monsters reflect legal selection/vassalage | MEDIUM-HIGH | Legal reading may over-read Amarna diplomatic conventions into myth |
| 5: Israel tames/denies divine status to cosmic enemies | HIGH | Intent vs. effect; Genesis 1 may not be consciously polemical |
| 6: Conflict myths encode powerlessness as well as power | MEDIUM-HIGH | Genre (lament vs. myth) may explain the difference better |

**Strongest section:** Section 5 (Israelite Cosmic Enemies Tamed and Denied). The close reading of Genesis 1, Psalm 104, and Job is Smith at his best — precise textual analysis showing progressive depersonalization. The "monotheistic poetics" concept is genuinely illuminating.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 (The Head God and His Beloved Monsters). The legal reading of ydd/mdd and the subservience reading of 'gl/klbt are interesting but rely heavily on inference from other text corpora. The basic observation (El favors the monsters) doesn't need the full legal apparatus to stand.

**Relationship to book's argument:** This chapter establishes the STRUCTURAL BACKGROUND that Israelite monotheism would reorganize. The cosmic enemies are divine in Ugarit, depersonalized in Israel. The spatial logic that justified multiple deities collapses when one God claims all space. Chapter 1 is the "before" picture against which chapters 8-10 show the "after."

**Cross-reference:** The center/periphery argument connects to Smith's Introduction claim that the divine family provided polytheism's "oneness" — the center is where the family lives. The discussion of Baal as an outsider to El's family (Claim 4) sets up Chapter 3's divine family argument. The "monotheistic poetics" of Genesis 1 will return in Chapter 9. Heiser's *Unseen Realm* makes compatible arguments about the divine council's structure but is less interested in the center/periphery spatial dimension.
