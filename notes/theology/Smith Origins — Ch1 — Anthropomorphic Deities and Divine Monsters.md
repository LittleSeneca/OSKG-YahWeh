# Smith Origins — Ch1: Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters

Smith applies F.A.M. Wiggermann's center-periphery spatial model to Ugaritic divinity, demonstrating that the most fundamental structural division in the Ugaritic texts is between anthropomorphic, benevolent deities (associated with the "near" center, sacred mountains, domesticated animals, and blessing) and monstrous divine creatures (associated with the periphery/beyond, undomesticated animals, destruction, and no cult). He then traces how Israel inherited these cosmic enemy traditions and transformed them through taming, denial, and "monotheistic poetics" — most dramatically in Genesis 1, where cosmic monsters are depersonalized into mere creatures within the created order.

---

## Claim 1: The center-periphery spatial scheme (adapted from Wiggermann) provides the fundamental organizational structure for Ugaritic divinity

**Author's claim:** Smith applies Wiggermann's anthropological distinction between center and periphery to Ugaritic divine geography, proposing three zones: center (home + foreign), periphery (outback), and beyond the periphery (underworld, cosmic waters). "Deities inhabit 'near' places whereas 'monsters' or 'demonic forces' do not. The division of divinity between deities and demons, so to speak, corresponds to the mapping of divine space."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.23.65–69 expresses center vs. periphery in agrarian terms: the "sown" (*mdr'*) versus the "outback, steppe" (*mdbr*). The sown contains plenty of food and wine (1.23.70–76).
- The "sown" appears in administrative lists: royal workers (CAT 4.141 III 16; 4.618.6), wine records (4.149.16), and cultic devotion — "five (jars of) wine for the sacrifice of the queen in the sown" (*hms yn bdbh mlkt bmdr'*, 4.149.14–16).
- Schloen's description of center-periphery at Ugarit: "the social center was focused in the physical center of the kingdom at Ras Shamra... 'urban-rural' is more-or-less synonymous with 'center-periphery.'"
- The periphery: "rocks and brush" (*l'abnm wl'shm*, 1.23.66), site of grazing and hunting (1.12 I 34–35, 1.92.3), the locale where Baal meets Mot at the edge of the underworld (1.6 II 20; cf. 1.5 VI 6, 29).
- Beyond the periphery: the underworld (*'ars*), netherworld (Mot's realm), and the cosmic waters beyond (*thmtm* — e.g., Yamm).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The spatial mapping is well-supported by indigenous Ugaritic terminology (*mdr'*, *mdbr*, *thmtm*) and consistent across multiple text genres (mythological, administrative, ritual).

**What's at stake:** If the spatial scheme is valid, Ugaritic religion was not a chaotic collection of deities but a coherent conceptual system organized around the fundamental human experience of home vs. wilderness. This supports Smith's broader thesis that West Semitic polytheism had its own conceptual unity.

**Who disagrees:** The scheme is Smith's synthesis of Wiggermann (who applied it to Mesopotamia) with Ugaritic data. Scholars who see Ugaritic religion as primarily practical/cultic rather than conceptual might resist this level of systematization. The three-zone refinement (center, periphery, beyond-periphery) is Smith's own.

**Alternative reading:** The *mdr'*/ *mdbr* contrast in CAT 1.23 could be a narrative device specific to that text rather than a systematic cosmological principle. The three-zone model may over-structure data that the Ugaritians themselves experienced more fluidly.

**My assessment:** Smith is largely convincing. The multiple attestations of *mdr'* across genres and the consistent mapping of deities to mountains and monsters to wilderness make the spatial thesis strong. The refinement into three zones (home, foreign, far) does useful analytical work without overclaiming.

## Claim 2: Divine cult sites and sacred mountains are exclusive to benevolent deities, marking them as belonging to "near" space — while cosmic enemies lack such sites

**Author's claim:** "Within the center or area of human cultivation and civilization, deities are accorded sacred mountains or cult sites, but cosmic enemies are not." Baal's Mount Sapan (*spn*) functions as the cosmic center point. "The pantheon as a whole is marked by the topography of the sacred mountain, for it meets on Mount ll."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.100 snake-bite incantation lists deities with their mountains/cult sites: El on mount *ks* (1.100.9), Baal on *spn* (1.100.9), Anat and Athtart on *'inbb* (1.100.20), Dagan at *ttl* (Tuttul, 1.100.15), Resheph at *bbt* (1.100.31), Athtart at *mr* (Mari, 1.100.78), Mlk at *'ttrt* (Ashtarot, 1.100.41), Yarih at *lrgt* (1.100.26), Zz and KMT at *hryth* (1.100.36).
- Baal's mountain as center point: described as Baal's palace site in the Baal Cycle, heading of one deity list as "the gods of Sapan" (*'il spn* in 1.47.1), superscription of a ritual text as "the feast of Sapan" (*dbh spn* in 1.148.1; 1.91.3).
- Baal's mountain called "pleasant place" (*n'm*) — garden language that, in biblical texts, is a recurring motif for the center point of the cosmos (Genesis 2–3, Ezekiel 28).
- "Yamm, Mot, tnn, and the other cosmic enemies do not have holy mountains."
- Exceptions prove the rule: Mot is associated with mountain *knkny* (1.5 V 13), but it is the entrance to the underworld, not a dwelling on top. El's home at "the channels of the Double-Deeps" (*'apq thmtm*, 1.4 IV 21–22) and "the meeting-place of the Double-Deeps" (*b'dt thmtm*, 1.100.3) lies at the edge between near and far. Kothar wa-Hasis has no mountain — he dwells in Memphis and Caphtor (1.100.46), reflecting foreign trade centers.
- The pantheon meets on Mount *ll* (1.2 I 20).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The CAT 1.100 evidence is explicit and systematic. The exceptions are genuinely instructive rather than undermining.

**What's at stake:** This claim establishes that spatial location and cultic access are definitional for what counts as a "deity" vs. a "monster" in Ugaritic thought. It also sets up the later argument that Israel's denial of cult sites to cosmic enemies is an intensification of an existing Ugaritic pattern.

**Who disagrees:** Unlikely to attract direct disagreement — this is primarily descriptive. Scholars might quibble about whether Athirat has no mountain (Smith: she may share El's home part-time) or whether Kothar's foreign dwelling counts as a "mountain."

**Alternative reading:** The mountain listings in 1.100 are formulaic and may reflect ritual tradition more than cosmological theory. The absence of mountains for cosmic enemies could be a literary convention of the Baal Cycle's narrative world rather than a systematic theological claim.

**My assessment:** Strong claim, well-documented. The mountain/cult-site mapping is one of the clearest empirical findings in the chapter. The distinction between Mot's mountain-as-entrance vs. deities' mountains-as-dwellings is analytically sharp.

## Claim 3: Divine powers map onto spatial zones — blessing from home/foreign deities vs. destruction from periphery monsters

**Author's claim:** "The first and fundamental division marks deities who meet human need and functions versus divinities who pose a human threat or destruction." This operates across genres: myths, letters, personal names, and ritual texts. Foreign deities can also bless — the blessing/destruction divide is spatial, not ethnic.

**Evidence presented:**
- Letters: Opening greetings invoke the gods (*'ilm*) to provide well-being (*slm*) and protect (*ngr*) the addressee. CAT 2.4.4–6 asks gods to strengthen (*'zz*) the addressee. CAT 2.176.4–6 invokes "the gods of Ugarit" (*'ily 'ugrt*).
- Ritual: CAT 1.161 invokes blessing from deceased royal ancestors, also called "god" or "divine" (*'il*) in 1.113.14–26.
- Personal names: According to Caquot, Ugaritic personal names express desire for health, blessing, protection, and favor from deities.
- Narrative: In 1.15 II, Baal asks El to confer blessing upon King Kirta with other deities in attendance. Anat defeats human enemies in 1.3 II; El supports Kirta's campaign (1.14–1.15); Horon is invoked to smash a rebellious son's head (1.16 VI).
- Destructive powers: Yamm connected with Kirta's household demise (1.14 I 19–20). Mot's destruction in 1.127.30–32: "If the city is taken... if Mot should attack man, the house of the son(s) of mankind will take a goat and will look to the future (afar)."
- Monstrous forces generally do not receive cult. CAT 1.23's "beautiful gods" (*'ilm n'mm*) roam the steppe before entering the sown — possibly reflecting drought/famine.
- Foreign deities: Letters distinguish "the gods of Ugarit" (2.16.4–6), "the gods of Egypt" (*'il msrm*, 2.23.22), "the gods of Alashiya" (*'il alty*, 2.42.8). All are invoked for blessing. The divine family metaphor integrates foreign deities — Nikkal (Mesopotamian moon-goddess) wed to Yarih (West Semitic moon-god) in CAT 1.24.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The evidence is distributed across multiple genres and consistently supports the blessing/destruction dichotomy.

**What's at stake:** This claim establishes that Ugaritic religion was fundamentally practical — deities were evaluated by what they did for humans, not by abstract theological status. It also shows that "foreign" did not mean "hostile" — a point with implications for how Israel later reconfigures foreign gods as non-gods.

**Who disagrees:** Few would dispute the descriptive claim. The more debatable point is whether "foreign deities also bless" reflects genuine theological universalism or simply diplomatic courtesy in letters sent abroad.

**Alternative reading:** The blessing/destruction binary may be too neat. Anat murders Aqhat (1.18) — Smith calls this an "exception" (human disobedience, divine punishment), but it shows deities can be destructive within the center too. The "beautiful gods" of 1.23 are ambiguous — they enter the sown and receive plenty, complicating the spatial scheme.

**My assessment:** The pattern is real and well-documented, but Smith acknowledges the exceptions honestly. The foreign-deity point is important and underappreciated — it shows that Ugaritic theology had resources for recognizing the legitimacy of other peoples' gods, which makes Israel's later denial of foreign gods a more dramatic departure than often recognized.

## Claim 4: Animal associations follow the benevolent/monstrous divide — domesticated animals for deities, undomesticated snake-dragons for cosmic enemies

**Author's claim:** "Benevolent deities are often rendered anthropomorphically, whereas destructive divinities appear as monstrous in character. Moreover, theriomorphic representations reflect the dichotomy between deities and cosmic enemies." Domesticated species (bull, calf, bird, cow) are emblematic of deities; undomesticated species (snake, serpent) are emblematic of monsters.

**Evidence presented:**
- El bears the title "Bull" (*tr*) extensively across the Baal Cycle (CAT 1.1 III 26, IV 12, V 22; 1.2 I 16, 33, 36, III 16–21; 1.3 IV 54, V 10, 35; 1.4 I 4, II 10, III 31, IV 39, 47; 1.6 IV 10, VI 26). Personal name *'iltr* ("El is Bull," 4.607.32).
- Baal presented as a bull-calf (1.5 V 17–21; 1.10 II–III, esp. III 33–37). Amiet identifies the bull as the storm-god's "attribute animal" in Syrian glyptic.
- Anat as a bird: textual — title "flyer of flyers, she who soars" (*d'i d'it rhpt*, 1.108.8), hovering among birds over Aqhat (1.18 IV). Iconographic — a drinking mug from Ugarit depicts a bird (Anat) behind Athirat at El's abode, with a fish signaling the water. Tessier: "On Syrian seals the weather god is very often associated with a winged and armed goddess."
- The golden calf of Exodus 32 and Jeroboam's bulls at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12) may reflect El's bull iconography — Bethel ("house of El") was originally an old El cult-site (Genesis 28:10–22).
- Cosmic enemies as snake-dragons: CAT 1.3 III 40–42 — "I fought the Twisty Serpent, the Potentate with seven Heads." CAT 1.5 I 1–3 — Mot reminds Baal: "[Y]ou killed Litan, the Fleeing Serpent, annihilated the Twisty Serpent, the Potentate with seven heads."
- Glyptic from Ugarit attests to the anthropomorphic warrior-god with a snake in either hand.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The textual and iconographic evidence converges strongly. The specific terms and titles are well-attested.

**What's at stake:** This claim grounds the conceptual division between deity and monster in concrete, visual imagery that worshippers would have encountered in cult statues and seals. It also connects Ugaritic iconography to biblical traditions (golden calf), showing continuity of divine representation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars debate whether the golden calf reflects El or Baal iconography. Smith notes the divide: "Many scholars are inclined to see El's rather than Baal's iconography behind the famous 'golden calf'" but "this iconography has been traced back to Baal as well." The Bethel argument favors El, but the evidence is not conclusive.

**Alternative reading:** The bull iconography may have been shared by multiple West Semitic deities, making it difficult to assign specific biblical images to one god. The "calf" could be a generic symbol of divine strength rather than tied to a specific deity.

**My assessment:** Smith handles the golden calf debate judiciously — he presents both positions and leans toward El (via Bethel) without overclaiming. The Anat-as-bird evidence is particularly striking because it combines explicit texts, iconography, and one specific artifact (the drinking mug). The seven-headed serpent as shared Ugaritic-biblical imagery (Psalm 74:13–14, Revelation 12:3) is one of the most concrete links between the two corpora.

## Claim 5: El's special relationship with cosmic enemies uses legal designation language — "beloved" (mdd 'il) indicates heir/successor status, and animal terms ('gl, klbt) connote subservience

**Author's claim:** The cosmic enemies are not merely El's favored creatures — they are his legally designated successors and beloved servants. The term *mdd 'il* ("Beloved of El") carries legal force comparable to Solomon's name Jedidiah ("beloved of the Lord"), marking public designation as heir. The animal terms *'gl* ("calf") and *klbt* ("dog") connote subservience and service, not merely affection.

**Evidence presented:**
- The *locus classicus*: Anat's speech to Gpn w-Ugr in CAT 1.3 III 36–IV 1 lists cosmic enemies by their El-relationship terms: Yamm = *mdd 'il* ("Beloved of El"), River = *'il rbm* ("Great God"), Arsh = *mdd 'ilm* ("Beloved of El"), 'tk = *'gl 'il* ("Calf of El"), 'isht = *klbt 'ilm* ("Dog of El"), Flame = *bt 'il* ("Daughter of El").
- Mot also bears the title *mdd 'il* elsewhere. Yamm is called *ydd 'il*.
- Szubin on biblical *yadid/yedidyah*: "This status invested the chosen 'beloved' designee with power, authority and title and bestowed upon him special rights and privileges... in adoption, matrimony, inheritance and succession." The designation functioned to "silence also claims... which challenged the legitimacy of the lawfully chosen 'righteous' son."
- Amarna letters: *kalbu* ("dog") used to express vassalage to Pharaoh. "What is Abdi-Ashirta, servant and dog, that he takes the land of the king for himself?" (EA 71:16–19; cf. 60:1–9; 88:9–11). Same usage in Lachish letters (KAI 192:3–4) and 2 Kings 8:13.
- Akkadian personal names: *A-ga-al-dMarduk* ("calf of Marduk") and names using *buru* ("calf") plus divine name — indicating subservience.
- Ugaritic, Hebrew, and Phoenician names containing *klb* ("dog") understood as "servant, slave."
- Narrative context: In CAT 1.1 IV, El selects Yamm as champion of the gods — at Baal's expense. "These epithets mark not only El's preference for them over the other gods but also their status as his publicly designated successors."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Szubin legal analysis and Amarna parallels are persuasive, but the evidence is analogical — we lack an explicit Ugaritic legal text confirming the "designated successor" reading of *mdd*.

**What's at stake:** If correct, this transforms our understanding of the Baal Cycle's central conflict. Baal is not merely fighting monsters — he is fighting El's legally designated heirs, which makes his eventual kingship a usurpation of the divine succession. This also explains why El initially supports Yamm against Baal.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read *mdd* simply as "beloved" in an affective sense, without legal overtones. The legal reading depends on Szubin's biblical analysis and Amarna parallels — neither is Ugaritic. Some may argue the animal terms are purely descriptive (these enemies take animal form) rather than status designations.

**Alternative reading:** The "legal" and "affectionate" readings are not mutually exclusive — *mdd* could be the standard term for a favored subordinate without the specific inheritance implications Smith draws. The animal terms could reflect both form AND status simultaneously, which Smith himself allows.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most original and interesting arguments in the chapter. The Szubin parallel is genuinely illuminating — it explains why El's "love" for Yamm has political-narrative consequences rather than being mere sentiment. The Amarna/Lachish evidence for "dog" as vassal terminology is strong. The "calf" evidence is thinner (mostly onomastic) but consistent. I find the legal reading of *mdd* persuasive; the animal terms as subservience markers are probable but not certain.

## Claim 6: Israel inherited the names and basic mythic roles of cosmic enemies from West Semitic culture shared with Ugarit

**Author's claim:** "Ancient Israel inherited the names of some of the cosmic enemies from West Semitic culture (which it shares with Ugarit)." Four enemies have identical names: Sea, Leviathan, Tannin, and Death. Psalm 74:12–17 presents Yahweh defeating these enemies as prelude to creation — directly paralleling the Baal Cycle's conflict pattern.

**Evidence presented:**
- Sea: Hebrew *yam*, Ugaritic *ym*.
- Leviathan: Hebrew *liwyatan*, Ugaritic *ltn* (Litan).
- Tannin: Hebrew *tannin*, Ugaritic *tnn* (Tunnanu in the Ugaritic polyglot).
- Death: Hebrew *mawet*, Ugaritic *Mot* (both literally "Death").
- Psalm 74:12–17 explicitly names all three: "You smashed Sea with your Might, cracked the heads of the Tannin in the waters; you crushed the heads of Leviathan."
- Isaiah 27:1 presents Leviathan's defeat as end-times sign. Isaiah 25:8 proclaims reversal of Death's power — God swallowing Death reverses the Ugaritic image of Death demanding to swallow Baal (CAT 1.5 I 6–8, II 2–6).
- For Baal, Sea and Death are ongoing threats — a notion "entirely missing from most biblical or Mesopotamian conflict stories, though not from biblical apocalyptic."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The linguistic correspondences (ym/yam, ltn/liwyatan, tnn/tannin, Mot/mawet) are exact or near-exact. Psalm 74 is an unmistakable parallel to the Baal Cycle's conflict pattern.

**What's at stake:** This is the strongest single piece of evidence for Ugaritic-Israelite literary continuity. If these names and conflict patterns are shared, then Israel's religion developed within the same West Semitic mythic world as Ugarit's — it did not emerge in isolation. The entire comparative enterprise rests on links like these.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially no one disputes the linguistic correspondences. Minimalists might argue the names reflect common ANE stock rather than specific Ugaritic-Israelite continuity. But the specificity of the Psalm 74 constellation (Sea + Tannin + Leviathan together, defeated as prelude to creation) makes common stock explanations strained.

**Alternative reading:** The shared names could reflect a common West Semitic poetic tradition that diverged significantly in its Ugaritic and Israelite instantiations. The Israelite versions have been thoroughly Yahwized — Psalm 74 attributes creation to Yahweh, not to a Baal-like conflict followed by temple-building.

**My assessment:** This is rock-solid. Smith is building on decades of comparative scholarship here, not making a novel claim. The value is in his clear presentation of the data and his sensitivity to the differences (ongoing threat for Baal, mostly past tense for Yahweh — though with apocalyptic exceptions).

## Claim 7: Israelite texts transform cosmic enemies through "taming" — Leviathan becomes God's domesticated pet/plaything, echoing El's "beloved" relationship with monsters

**Author's claim:** "The biblical texts treating the monstrous figures instead as pets may echo their 'beloved' relationship with El. Just as the biblical material coalesced the differing imagery involving El and Baal with the national god of Yahweh, so too the differing roles of the cosmic forces as foes and beloved of the divine perhaps coalesced."

**Evidence presented:**
- Job 40:25–29 (NJPS): Leviathan is caught by God's fishhook, drawn by rope and nose-ring. "Will you play with him like a bird...?" (40:29).
- Psalm 104:26: "Leviathan whom you formed to sport with."
- Job 38:8–11: Sea treated at creation not as enemy but as newborn babe.
- Contrast with Ugaritic: In the Baal Cycle, these are serious opponents — Baal is initially slumped beneath Yamm's throne (1.2 IV 6–7), Baal and Mot reach stalemate (1.6 VI 16–22).
- In Ugaritic, cosmic enemies are explicitly divine: River/Yamm called *'il rbm* ("Great God," 1.3 III 39), Mot called *bn 'ilm* ("son of El/son of the gods," 1.4 VIII 45–46), personal name *ym'il* ("Yamm is god," 4.75 V 14).
- In Israel, their divine status is largely denied. But traces remain: personal name Yemuel (Genesis 46:10; Exodus 6:15) may = *ym'il*.
- Ugaritic enemies are divine in rank and power; biblical enemies are hardly comparable in power (Psalm 74). Contrast Baal needing Kothar's magical weapons (1.2 IV).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The taming pattern is clearly present in the biblical texts. The link to El's "beloved" relationship is more speculative — it's an attractive synthesis but depends on the coalescence model (El + Baal → Yahweh) which Smith develops more fully elsewhere.

**What's at stake:** This claim offers a unified explanation for why biblical texts oscillate between treating cosmic enemies as foes (Psalm 74) and as pets (Job 40–41, Psalm 104). The answer: both El's beloved-monsters tradition and Baal's warrior-vs-monsters tradition were absorbed into Yahweh's character. The tension in the biblical portrait reflects the tension in its sources.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the "pet" passages as simply late, domesticated wisdom traditions with no connection to El mythology. The Yemuel/*ym'il* parallel is slim evidence for early Israelite recognition of Yamm's divinity.

**Alternative reading:** The taming tradition could be an independent Israelite development — part of a wisdom theology that emphasizes God's sovereign control over all creation, including chaos monsters — rather than an inheritance from El traditions. Psalm 104's dependence on the Egyptian Aten Hymn suggests multiple influences.

**My assessment:** The coalescence argument is elegant and fits Smith's larger thesis, but it's underdetermined by the evidence in this chapter alone. The taming/domestication pattern is real regardless of its source — the biblical data clearly show both the conflict tradition (Psalm 74, Isaiah 27:1) and the domestication tradition (Job 40–41, Psalm 104:26, Genesis 1) coexisting. That's significant even without the El-connection.

## Claim 8: Genesis 1 achieves "monotheistic poetics" by eliminating conflict with cosmic enemies, depersonalizing them, and resolving Creator/creature ambiguity

**Author's claim:** "The literary order of Genesis 1 contributes to a monotheistic vision. The text manifests a 'monotheistic poetics,' which alters the perception of reality with its created order." Genesis 1 prepares the audience for cosmic conflict (verse 2's *tehom*, darkness, wind), then subverts that expectation — God speaks and it is done, without battle. The *tanninim* appear not as pre-creation enemies but as created beings within the order (v. 21). Sun and moon are depersonalized as "greater light" and "lesser light" (v. 16) — traditional divine titles used without divine status.

**Evidence presented:**
- Genesis 1:2 sets up expectations of conflict: *tehom* ("the deep" = Tiamat/Thmt), darkness, wind — standard chaos-battle opening. "The audience... is prepared for a cosmic conflict by the opening references to enemies in verse 2."
- But no conflict occurs: "In this passage the lack of any conflict, or even any personification of the cosmic oceans or waters, heightens the picture of a powerful God who but speaks and the divine will is accomplished."
- *Tanninim* appear in verse 21 (day 5) within the created order — not at the beginning as pre-creation foes. "By omission, [the narrative] transforms them into another part of creation."
- Sun and moon are "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (1:16). These are traditional titles: "great light" (*nyr rbt*) for Ugaritic sun-goddess (CAT 1.161.19), "light of the heavens" (*nrt shmm*) for Ugaritic moon-god (1.24.16, 31) — but in Genesis they are "no longer divinities."
- "Here, ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left in Genesis 1's 'monotheistic poetics.'"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The reading of Genesis 1 as subverting chaos-battle expectations is well-established in biblical scholarship (cf. Levenson, Fishbane). The "monotheistic poetics" formulation is Smith's, but the underlying observation is broadly accepted.

**What's at stake:** This is arguably the most important claim in the chapter for Smith's overall thesis. It shows that monotheism is not primarily a philosophical proposition ("only one god exists") but a literary-theological strategy that reorganizes traditional mythic materials to eliminate middle ground between Creator and creature. "Monotheistic poetics" is a more precise analytical tool than "monotheism" as a belief-stage.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Genesis 1 as dependent on P's monotheistic theology rather than as a creative literary transformation. Also those who see the lack of conflict as simply reflecting P's non-mythological Priestly style rather than a deliberate subversion. The sun/moon depersonalization could reflect anti-Babylonian polemic (against astral cults) rather than a general monotheistic strategy.

**Alternative reading:** Genesis 1's treatment of *tanninim* and celestial bodies may be less about "monotheistic poetics" and more about the Priestly author's concern with order, separation, and taxonomy. The *tanninim* are classified as creatures of day 5 because that's where sea creatures belong in the Priestly scheme — the theological implication may be secondary to the classificatory impulse.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading is powerful and persuasive. The phrase "monotheistic poetics" captures something real — Genesis 1 doesn't just assert monotheism, it performs it through narrative structure. The audience-knows-the-tradition point is crucial: the text's silences are meaningful precisely because the audience knows the chaos-conflict stories. My one reservation: Smith may overstate how much the Priestly author was consciously subverting specific Ugaritic traditions rather than drawing on generalized ANE chaoskampf motifs.

## Claim 9: Dualistic mythology encodes not only political power but also human powerlessness and communal hope — Mendenhall's reading is incomplete

**Author's claim:** Smith modifies Mendenhall's dictum that "dualistic mythology is always essentially political." He argues that divine conflict narratives can express political mastery (Enuma Elish = Babylon's power, Genesis 1 = inverse of Judah's weakness) but also political weakness, desperation, and hope. "Hope then seems to be the key trope in these texts."

**Evidence presented:**
- Mendenhall's claim: polytheistic mythologies of power reflect political realities.
- Smith's modifications: Psalm 74:12–17 "appeals to divine strength at a moment of perceived divine weakness or indifference." Yahweh's mastery is "not fully realized; for ancient Israelites, this divine dominion is in fact a debatable matter."
- Isaiah 27:1: prospect of divine victory held out for future — Yahweh "has not yet exercised complete mastery over the cosmos."
- Isaiah 51:9–11: power of Yahweh over cosmic enemy proclaimed "as an expression of hope and herald to human weakness at the time of exile."
- Genesis 1's divine mastery stands "in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)" — composed against backdrop of foreign empires imposing power on Judah.
- Baal's lack of definitive victory: needs help from other deities, no definitive victory over Sea and Death — also a form of encoded powerlessness.
- "Divine power can encode human power as well as human powerlessness. This, too, is a political statement that a community retains a sort of control over its identity despite its political powerlessness."
- These mythologies are ultimately not "dualistic" — "various lines subtly connect the deities and their blessing and the divine monsters. No hero is great without a great enemy to defeat."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The observation that conflict myths serve different political functions in different contexts is well-taken. The "hope" reading of exilic texts is standard. The claim about Genesis 1 being composed under imperial pressure is a plausible historical reconstruction but not provable from the text alone.

**What's at stake:** This claim pushes back against a reductive political reading of mythology, arguing that divine conflict narratives are more flexible — they can be tools of imperial propaganda, resistance literature, or expressions of communal hope. It also challenges the clean separation between "polytheistic" and "monotheistic" conflict narratives, noting that both can encode power or powerlessness.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that creation-by-conflict always serves political-legitimation functions (the king = the divine warrior). Also those who see Genesis 1 as a product of the Persian period's relatively stable conditions rather than imperial pressure — the "inverse relation" argument would need different historical anchoring.

**Alternative reading:** The "hope" reading may impose a modern existential category on ancient texts that were primarily liturgical and doxological. Psalm 74 is a lament — its recollection of past divine victory functions as accusation ("you did this before, why not now?") as much as hope. The emotional register may be protest rather than hopeful expectation.

**My assessment:** Smith's modification of Mendenhall is sensible and necessary — Mendenhall's claim was always too blunt. The best part is the observation about Genesis 1: "divine mastery stands in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)" — this is a genuinely illuminating principle that applies well beyond Genesis. The closing reflection that these mythologies are "ultimately not dualistic" because hero and monster are mutually constitutive is philosophically rich but somewhat tangential to the chapter's main argument.

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Center-periphery spatial scheme organizes Ugaritic divinity | HIGH | Over-systematizing diverse textual data |
| 2: Sacred mountains exclusive to benevolent deities | HIGH | Exceptions (Mot's knkny) may be more significant |
| 3: Blessing (center) vs. destruction (periphery) across genres | HIGH | Exceptions (Anat/Aqhat) complicate the binary |
| 4: Domesticated animals (deities) vs. snake-dragons (monsters) | HIGH | Bull iconography attribution (El vs. Baal) |
| 5: El's "beloved" = legal designation; animal terms = subservience | MEDIUM-HIGH | Analogical evidence (biblical, Amarna) not Ugaritic |
| 6: Israel inherited cosmic enemy names from West Semitic culture | VERY HIGH | Shared ANE stock vs. specific Ugaritic continuity |
| 7: Israelite "taming" of monsters echoes El's "beloved" relationship | MEDIUM-HIGH | Coalescence model needs more evidence |

| 9: Conflict myth encodes power AND powerlessness/hope | MEDIUM-HIGH | Historical context of Genesis 1 uncertain |

**Strongest section:** Section 5 ("Israelite Cosmic Enemies Tamed and Denied") — the analysis of Genesis 1's "monotheistic poetics" is the chapter's most original and consequential contribution. The reading of *tanninim* placement within the created order, the depersonalization of sun and moon using traditional titles stripped of divine status, and the argument that literary structure itself "contributes to a monotheistic vision" — this is vintage Smith, combining close reading with theological analysis. The Psalm 74/Jeremiah/Isaiah comparisons with Ugaritic are also precise and well-chosen.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 ("The Head God and His Beloved Monsters") — the Szubin legal analysis is intriguing but the argument depends heavily on biblical analogies (Jedidiah) and Amarna parallels that are not Ugaritic. The "calf" and "dog" as subservience terms are well-supported by comparative onomastics, but the leap from "these are terms of subservience" to "these enemies are El's designated successors/legal heirs" requires interpretive steps the chapter doesn't fully warrant. The section is rich and suggestive but less airtight than the rest of the chapter.

**Smith's intellectual stance in this chapter:** Smith operates primarily as a comparativist and systematizer, working with Wiggermann's anthropological categories to organize the Ugaritic data, then tracing the transformation of shared traditions in Israelite texts. The chapter's movement is from spatial mapping (sections 1–3) to relational dynamics (section 4) to historical transformation (section 5). This mirrors the book's larger arc from structure to change. Smith's method is inductive — he builds from specific Ugaritic texts (with CAT numbers throughout) to synthetic claims, then tests those claims against biblical data.

**Cross-reference note:** This chapter's spatial mapping (center/periphery/beyond) provides the conceptual infrastructure for Smith's later arguments about the divine council (Ch2) and divine family (Ch3). The claim that Israel inherited and transformed West Semitic cosmic enemy traditions directly supports the Introduction's thesis that Israel developed FROM West Semitic polytheism, not against it. Cross's treatment of the Divine Warrior (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) covers some of the same conflict-myth material but with different emphasis — Cross stresses the historical-political function of divine warfare, while Smith is more interested in the spatial and conceptual structures underlying it. Römer's *The Invention of God* would likely agree with Smith's reading of Genesis 1 but might place its composition later (Hellenistic rather than Persian period). Tigay's onomastic data doesn't directly engage this chapter's concerns, but the Yemuel/*ym'il* parallel suggests early Israelite naming practices preserved vestiges of Yamm's divine status that later theology suppressed.

**The chapter's most memorable insight:** "No hero is great without a great enemy to defeat, and few cosmic enemies exist without a hero to vanquish them." This closing aphorism captures the chapter's deepest claim: that the division between deity and monster is structural and relational, not merely taxonomic. Benevolent gods and destructive monsters define each other — and monotheism's genius was not to eliminate the monsters but to absorb them into a single God's sovereignty, either as defeated foes or as beloved pets.
