# Smith Origins — Ch1: Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters

Smith applies F.A.M. Wiggermann's center-periphery spatial model to Ugaritic divinity, demonstrating that the most fundamental structural division in the Ugaritic texts is between anthropomorphic, benevolent deities (associated with the "near" center, sacred mountains, domesticated animals, and blessing) and monstrous divine creatures (associated with the periphery/beyond, undomesticated animals, destruction, and no cult). He then traces how Israel inherited these cosmic enemy traditions and transformed them through taming, denial, and "monotheistic poetics" — most dramatically in Genesis 1, where cosmic monsters are depersonalized into mere creatures within the created order.

## Claim 1: The center-periphery spatial scheme organizes Ugaritic divinity
→ [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]] | **smith-obm-1.1** | Confidence: high
  Smith applies Wiggermann's center-periphery model to Ugaritic divine geography: benevolent deities inhabit the "near" center (home, foreign, sacred mountains, cultivated land), while monstrous creatures belong to the periphery (wilderness) and beyond-periphery (underworld, cosmic waters). The Ugaritic terms *mdr'* ("sown") and *mdbr* ("outback") express this indigenous spatial scheme.
  Supports: [[claim-conceptual-unity-west-semitic-polytheism-neglected]], [[claim-divine-family-polytheism-oneness]], [[claim-sacred-mountains-exclusive-benevolent-deities]], [[claim-divine-powers-map-onto-spatial-zones]]. Challenged by: the risk of over-systematizing diverse textual data.

## Claim 2: Sacred mountains and cult sites exclusive to benevolent deities
→ [[claim-sacred-mountains-exclusive-benevolent-deities]] | **smith-obm-1.2** | Confidence: high
  "Within the center or area of human cultivation and civilization, deities are accorded sacred mountains or cult sites, but cosmic enemies are not." CAT 1.100 lists nine deities with their mountains — Baal's Sapan is the cosmic center. Yamm, Mot, Tannin have no holy mountains. Exceptions are instructive: Mot's *knkny* is an entrance, not a dwelling.
  Depends on: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]]. Supports: [[claim-divine-powers-map-onto-spatial-zones]].

## Claim 3: Divine powers map onto spatial zones — blessing from center, destruction from periphery
→ [[claim-divine-powers-map-onto-spatial-zones]] | **smith-obm-1.3** | Confidence: high
  "The first and fundamental division marks deities who meet human need and functions versus divinities who pose a human threat or destruction." Evidence across letters, myths, personal names, and ritual. Foreign deities also bless — the blessing/destruction divide is spatial, not ethnic. Foreign gods recognized as legitimate: "the gods of Egypt" (*'il msrm*), "the gods of Alashiya" (*'il alty*).
  Depends on: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]], [[claim-sacred-mountains-exclusive-benevolent-deities]]. Supports: [[claim-animal-associations-benevolent-monstrous-divide]]. Challenged by: Anat/Aqhat exception (destruction within the center).

## Claim 4: Animal associations follow the benevolent/monstrous divide
→ [[claim-animal-associations-benevolent-monstrous-divide]] | **smith-obm-1.4** | Confidence: high
  Domesticated species (bull, calf, bird, cow) are emblematic of benevolent deities; undomesticated snake-dragons emblematic of cosmic enemies. El is "Bull" (*tr*), Baal as bull-calf, Anat as bird. Biblical golden calf may reflect El's bull iconography via Bethel ("house of El"). Seven-headed serpent in Ugaritic = Leviathan in Psalm 74 and Revelation.
  Depends on: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]], [[claim-divine-powers-map-onto-spatial-zones]]. Supports: [[claim-el-beloved-legal-designation-animal-subservience]]. Connects to: biblical golden calf traditions (El at Bethel).

## Claim 5: El's "beloved" (mdd 'il) for cosmic enemies = legal designation; animal terms = subservience
→ [[claim-el-beloved-legal-designation-animal-subservience]] | **smith-obm-1.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  Yamm = *mdd 'il* ("Beloved of El"), 'tk = *'gl 'il* ("Calf of El"), 'isht = *klbt 'ilm* ("Dog of El"). The term *mdd* carries legal force comparable to Solomon's Jedidiah ("beloved of the Lord") — publicly designated heir. Animal terms connote vassalage (Amarna *kalbu* = "dog" as vassal; Akkadian "calf of Marduk" names). Baal fights El's legally designated successors — his kingship is usurpation of divine succession.
  Depends on: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]], [[claim-sacred-mountains-exclusive-benevolent-deities]]. Supports: [[claim-israel-tamed-monsters-el-beloved-echo]]. Challenged by: analogical nature of evidence (biblical, Amarna, not Ugaritic).

## Claim 6: Israel inherited cosmic enemy names from West Semitic culture
→ [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]] | **smith-obm-1.6** | Confidence: very-high
  Four enemies have identical names: Sea (*yam*/ *ym*), Leviathan (*liwyatan*/ *ltn*), Tannin (*tannin*/ *tnn*), Death (*mawet*/ *Mot*). Psalm 74:12-17 names all three — Yahweh defeating them as prelude to creation, directly paralleling the Baal Cycle. The linguistic correspondences are exact or near-exact.
  Depends on: [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]], [[claim-biblical-myth-exists-baal-cycle-standard]]. Supports: [[claim-israel-tamed-monsters-el-beloved-echo]], [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]]. Contradicts: any model of Israelite religious isolation.

## Claim 7: Israelite texts "tame" cosmic enemies — echoing El's "beloved" relationship
→ [[claim-israel-tamed-monsters-el-beloved-echo]] | **smith-obm-1.7** | Confidence: medium-high
  Leviathan becomes God's domesticated pet (Job 40-41, Psalm 104:26: "whom you formed to sport with"). Sea treated as newborn babe (Job 38:8-11). Both El's beloved-monsters tradition and Baal's warrior-vs-monsters tradition coalesced into Yahweh's character. Explains why biblical texts oscillate between treating enemies as foes and as pets.
  Depends on: [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]], [[claim-el-beloved-legal-designation-animal-subservience]]. Supports: [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]], [[claim-yahweh-composite-deity-product-of-convergence]]. Challenged by: coalescence model underdetermined by Ch1 alone; wisdom theology as alternative source.

## Claim 8: Genesis 1 achieves "monotheistic poetics" by eliminating conflict with cosmic enemies
→ [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]] | **smith-obm-1.8** | Confidence: high
  Genesis 1:2 sets up expectations of cosmic conflict (*tehom*, darkness, wind = standard chaos-battle opening), then subverts them — God speaks and it is done, without battle. *Tanninim* appear in v.21 as created beings, not pre-creation enemies. Sun and moon are "greater light" and "lesser light" (v.16) — Ugaritic divine titles stripped of divine status. "No middle ground left between Creator and creatures."
  Depends on: [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]], [[claim-israel-tamed-monsters-el-beloved-echo]], [[claim-biblical-myth-exists-baal-cycle-standard]]. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]]. Challenged by: Priestly taxonomic concerns vs. conscious subversion of myth.

## Claim 9: Conflict mythology encodes power, powerlessness, and hope
→ [[claim-conflict-myth-encodes-power-and-powerlessness-hope]] | **smith-obm-1.9** | Confidence: medium-high
  Modifies Mendenhall's dictum that dualistic mythology is always political. Psalm 74 appeals to divine strength at moment of perceived weakness. Isaiah 27:1 holds divine victory out for future. Genesis 1's divine mastery stands "in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)" — composed under imperial pressure. "No hero is great without a great enemy to defeat."
  Depends on: [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]], [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]]. Challenged by: uncertainty of Genesis 1's historical context; hope vs. protest as emotional register in Psalm 74.

**Strongest section:** Section 5 ("Israelite Cosmic Enemies Tamed and Denied") — the analysis of Genesis 1's "monotheistic poetics" is the chapter's most original and consequential contribution. The reading of *tanninim* placement within the created order, the depersonalization of sun and moon using traditional titles stripped of divine status, and the argument that literary structure itself "contributes to a monotheistic vision" — this is vintage Smith, combining close reading with theological analysis. The Psalm 74/Jeremiah/Isaiah comparisons with Ugaritic are also precise and well-chosen.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 ("The Head God and His Beloved Monsters") — the Szubin legal analysis is intriguing but the argument depends heavily on biblical analogies (Jedidiah) and Amarna parallels that are not Ugaritic. The "calf" and "dog" as subservience terms are well-supported by comparative onomastics, but the leap from "these are terms of subservience" to "these enemies are El's designated successors/legal heirs" requires interpretive steps the chapter doesn't fully warrant. The section is rich and suggestive but less airtight than the rest of the chapter.

**Smith's intellectual stance in this chapter:** Smith operates primarily as a comparativist and systematizer, working with Wiggermann's anthropological categories to organize the Ugaritic data, then tracing the transformation of shared traditions in Israelite texts. The chapter's movement is from spatial mapping (sections 1–3) to relational dynamics (section 4) to historical transformation (section 5). This mirrors the book's larger arc from structure to change. Smith's method is inductive — he builds from specific Ugaritic texts (with CAT numbers throughout) to synthetic claims, then tests those claims against biblical data.

**Cross-reference note:** This chapter's spatial mapping (center/periphery/beyond) provides the conceptual infrastructure for Smith's later arguments about the divine council (Ch2) and divine family (Ch3). The claim that Israel inherited and transformed West Semitic cosmic enemy traditions directly supports the Introduction's thesis that Israel developed FROM West Semitic polytheism, not against it. Cross's treatment of the Divine Warrior (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) covers some of the same conflict-myth material but with different emphasis — Cross stresses the historical-political function of divine warfare, while Smith is more interested in the spatial and conceptual structures underlying it. Römer's *The Invention of God* would likely agree with Smith's reading of Genesis 1 but might place its composition later (Hellenistic rather than Persian period). Tigay's onomastic data doesn't directly engage this chapter's concerns, but the Yemuel/*ym'il* parallel suggests early Israelite naming practices preserved vestiges of Yamm's divine status that later theology suppressed.

**The chapter's most memorable insight:** "No hero is great without a great enemy to defeat, and few cosmic enemies exist without a hero to vanquish them." This closing aphorism captures the chapter's deepest claim: that the division between deity and monster is structural and relational, not merely taxonomic. Benevolent gods and destructive monsters define each other — and monotheism's genius was not to eliminate the monsters but to absorb them into a single God's sovereignty, either as defeated foes or as beloved pets.
