# Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy

Kaufmann surveys the United and Divided Monarchies, advancing several signature arguments: that Israelite kingship was fundamentally different from pagan sacral kingship; that the "sins of Jeroboam" (the golden calves) were not pagan idolatry but YHWH worship in an illicit form; that the Baal worship of the north was a foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel — not an indigenous syncretistic development; and that the reform movements culminating in Josiah's Deuteronomic centralization represent the logical unfolding of Israel's distinctive religious ideas. The chapter also covers the popular prophets, the emergence of a new eschatology, and the Book of Jonah. This is one of Kaufmann's most distinctive chapters — his interpretation of the golden calves as non-idolatrous is genuinely interesting and has partial support in scholarship, but his handling of Solomon's pagan chapels and the scope of Baal worship strains credibility.

---

## Claim 1: Israelite kingship was fundamentally different from pagan sacral kingship — grounded in prophetic election, not divine essence

**Author's claim:** "The Israelite conception rejected all the divine aspects of kingship and based itself exclusively on the idea of divine election." In pagan cultures, the king was endowed with divine qualities — Pharaoh is a god, Mesopotamian kings are sons of the gods, consorts of Ishtar. In Israel, "there are linguistic vestiges of the idea that the king is a 'son' of God (Pss. 2:7; 89:27 f.)," but "these are purely figurative." There is "no allusion to the divine origin of the reigning dynasty. No Israelite king is condemned for having vaunted himself a god or for instituting a cult celebrated in his honor." The Israelite king "does not incorporate any divine essence; he does not control the destiny of the cosmos through the cult; he is but the bearer of God's grace, appointed to office by his messenger-prophet."

**Evidence presented:**
- Pagan kings in Egypt and Mesopotamia were gods, sons of gods, or consorts of goddesses who maintained cosmic order through the cult.
- Israelite kings are never deified; even at the height of David's career he asks "Who am I Lord YHWH, and what is my house?" (II Sam 7:18).
- Israel's monarchy is grounded in "apostolic prophecy," not priesthood. The king succeeds the prophet-judge, not the priest.
- Israelite kings performed altar service but "never bore the official title 'priest'"; their priestly function "was but a by-role."
- The ideal future king has "no priestly features" (Isa 11:1 ff.) — he is a just judge, not a cultic mediator.
- Even in fierce antagonism toward Saul, Samuel "never again suggests that the monarchy itself is a sin."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The contrast Kaufmann draws between Israelite kingship and Egyptian/Mesopotamian sacral kingship is generally valid. Israelite kings were not deified, and the prophetic critique of individual kings never extended to the institution itself. However, Kaufmann understates the sacral dimensions that DID exist — the king as "son" of YHWH (Ps 2:7), the royal psalms' language of divine adoption, and the king's role in the temple cult.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right that Israelite kingship was categorically non-sacral, this supports his broader thesis of Israel's absolute distinctiveness. If the distinction is one of degree rather than kind, then Israelite kingship developed within a shared ancient Near Eastern paradigm.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars of the "royal psalms" and temple ideology argue that Israelite kingship had genuine sacral dimensions. The "Myth and Ritual School" (e.g., the Scandinavian school of Mowinckel, Engnell) argued that Israelite kingship was deeply sacral, with the king playing a central role in an annual enthronement festival. Even more moderate scholars acknowledge that the Davidic covenant (II Sam 7) and royal psalms invest the king with a quasi-sacral status — he is YHWH's "son" and sits at his right hand.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite kingship shared structural features with other ancient Near Eastern monarchies (divine sonship language, temple building as royal duty, the king as mediator of divine favor) while rejecting the most extreme claims (the king as literal god). The difference is real but not absolute — Israelite kingship represents a modification of Near Eastern royal ideology, not a complete rejection of it.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right that no Israelite king claimed to BE a god — this is a genuine distinction from Egypt. But his claim that Israelite kingship was entirely non-sacral overstates the case. The Davidic covenant's language of eternal dynasty, the royal psalms' elevation of the king, and the king's role as temple builder/patron all suggest significant sacral dimensions. Kaufmann's argument serves his larger thesis of absolute difference, but the data supports a more nuanced picture: Israelite kingship demythologized the ANE royal ideology without wholly abandoning its sacral character.

## Claim 2: The founding of the monarchy was not a sin — the "antimonarchic" passages are premonarchic in origin, not postexilic

**Author's claim:** "Classical criticism regards the antimonarchic version as a reflex of the 'theocratic ideal' of the postexilic age... The fact is, however, that later ages never were hostile toward the institution of kingship." The antimonarchic passages "preserve the mood of a specific occasion" — the crisis when the ancient prophetic kingdom of God gave way to a secular monarchy — and "remained forever linked with that occasion, without further ideological issue."

**Evidence presented:**
- Later biblical histories portray kings as sinning but "the monarchy itself is never regarded as a sin to be atoned for or repudiated."
- The prophets opposed individual kings but raised new dynasties — "their opposition takes the form of raising new dynasties to replace the old."
- The Chronicler's "extravagant idealization of the Davidic dynasty" was produced in Second Temple times — hardly antimonarchic.
- Hosea 13:10f., often cited as the source of antimonarchic ideology, shows a "telling divergence" — Hosea's Israel asks for "king and officials" (*sarim*), reflecting the bureaucracy of the later monarchy, whereas I Samuel 8 has *sarim* only as "servants" (*'abadim*), reflecting earlier conditions.
- "The origin of this hostility must be sought in the premonarchic period."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's argument that antimonarchic sentiment is premonarchic, not postexilic, is plausible and worth taking seriously. But the dating of I Samuel 7-8, 10:17-27, and 12 remains contested — many scholars see Deuteronomistic editing in these chapters.

**What's at stake:** The dating of the Deuteronomistic History's attitude toward kingship. If Kaufmann is right, the antimonarchic strain is genuinely ancient and not a retrojection of postexilic priestly ideology.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars date the "theocratic" version of Saul's election (I Sam 8; 10:17-27; 12) to the Deuteronomistic History (late monarchic/exilic) or later. The negative portrayal of monarchy as a rejection of YHWH's kingship is seen as reflecting the experience of monarchy's failure.

**Alternative reading:** Both the promonarchic and antimonarchic strands in I Samuel reflect genuine tensions present from the monarchy's beginning — the tension between charismatic leadership and institutional authority. The Deuteronomistic editors preserved both because both captured aspects of their experience with monarchy. Kaufmann's dating is not unreasonable, but his insistence on a neat premonarchic origin for the antimonarchic strand may be too tidy.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observation that later Israel never repudiated monarchy *as an institution* is correct and important. Even in the postexilic period, messianic hope centered on a Davidic king. The antimonarchic strand in I Samuel is anomalous within the broader biblical corpus and needs explanation. Kaufmann's explanation — that it preserves genuinely premonarchic sentiment — is reasonable, though the text as we have it has certainly been shaped by later editors.

## Claim 3: Solomon's foreign worship initiated the "second idolatrous period" — but it was a private court defection, not a popular paganization

**Author's claim:** According to the biblical narrator, I Kings 11:4 divides the history of the monarchy into two — from Saul to Solomon's last days there was "no national sin of idolatry," after which sin continued to the fall. "This view, however, is a dogmatic necessity for the biblical historians and cannot be taken at face value." The private chapels Solomon built for his pagan wives "ultimately brought about his own involvement in their cults." But "this was not a public, aggressive pagan influx, as later in the days of Jezebel." Kaufmann explicitly concedes: "There can be no doubt that the religious condition of the bulk of the people after Solomon's reign did not change substantially from what it was before. Vestigial idolatry existed among the vulgar, both before and after."

**Evidence presented:**
- I Kings 11:4 states Solomon's heart was turned after other gods in his old age — this was "private defection of the court."
- Ashtoreth, Milcom, and Chemosh had altars built "on the soil of Israel — and at the hands of an Israelite king" for the first time.
- But "the Bible does not know a popular cult of any foreign god" in this period.
- "The post-Solomonic age cannot, therefore, be regarded as a period of the paganization of the national religion."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann is doing something genuinely difficult here — he's simultaneously accepting the biblical verdict that Solomon sinned (and that this was a turning point) while denying that this represents a popular apostasy. The two-legged stool wobbles: if Solomon's worship of Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom was a "private" matter, why does the biblical historian treat it as inaugurating a new period of national sin? Kaufmann's answer — that the historians generalized the king's sin to the nation as a theological explanation for political decline — is plausible as a literary explanation but concedes that the biblical picture is unreliable as history.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's model of Israelite religion as fundamentally non-pagan throughout the monarchy. He needs to explain why the Bible says Solomon built high places for foreign gods without conceding that this reflected or produced popular polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars would point out that Solomon is credited with building temples for Chemosh (Moab), Milcom (Ammon), and Astarte (Sidon) — I Kings 11:5-7. If a king could do this openly without provoking a popular backlash, this suggests the religious atmosphere was more pluralistic than Kaufmann allows. The archaeological record — figurines, cult stands, inscriptions — suggests polytheistic practice was widespread at all levels.

**Alternative reading:** The Solomonic period was one of religious pluralism — Yahweh worship alongside the cults of other deities — that the Deuteronomistic historians later framed as "Solomon's sin" to explain the kingdom's division. The archaeological evidence of religious diversity during the monarchy period is hard to square with Kaufmann's picture of a fundamentally monotheistic populace with only "vestigial" idolatry.

**My assessment:** This is one of the weaker sections of the chapter. Kaufmann's admission that "vestigial idolatry existed among the vulgar, both before and after" is a significant concession that sits awkwardly with his broader thesis of Israel's categorical distinctiveness. What exactly distinguishes "vestigial idolatry" that has always existed from the "idolatry" that the Bible claims began with Solomon? The distinction seems driven by Kaufmann's need to protect his thesis rather than by the evidence. If Solomon could build chapels for Chemosh and Milcom on Israelite soil, and if "vestigial idolatry" existed throughout, the picture looks more like persistent polytheistic practice than a one-time royal defection.

## Claim 4: The "sins of Jeroboam" — the golden calves — were NOT pagan idolatry but YHWH worship in an illicit form

**Author's claim:** "It is perfectly plain that Jeroboam and the kings of Israel who perpetuated his 'sins' did not 'cast YHWH behind their backs' or regard the calves as 'other gods' (I Kings 14:9). Jehu, the prophetically appointed extirpator of Canaanite Baalism, does not remove the calves. Nor do Elijah and Elisha utter a word against them." The calves "must have belonged (like the image of Micah) to the cult of YHWH. They were NOT conceived of as representations of YHWH." Rather, "the calf or bull commonly served as the pedestal of Near Eastern gods... Jeroboam, however, adopted only the calf, without the figure of a standing or riding god. Corresponding to the cherubs of the ark, they represented only the vehicle, not the divine rider." "In essence, then, there is no difference between the calves and the cherubs."

**Evidence presented:**
- Jehu destroys Baalism but leaves the calves standing — clear evidence they weren't considered Baal worship.
- Elijah and Elisha, fierce opponents of Baal, never mention the calves.
- The biblical story blames Jeroboam for "inventing a festival and appointing non-Levite priests" — but "what additional sin can possibly consist in worshiping golden calves in this manner? What has Israel been commanded by YHWH concerning the festivals and priests of a calf-cult?"
- The calves were likely of the winged type, "no more than a variation on the theme of the cherub."
- "The rejection of the image of a divine rider upon the calf is the crucial evidence for the transformation worked by the Israelite idea on the calves of the north, no less than on the cherubs of the south."
- Jeroboam's calves "were designed by Aaronide priests" — "It is difficult to avoid the conclusion that... the calves belonged to the same priestly tradition from which the cherubs of the ark stemmed."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is one of Kaufmann's genuinely insightful and well-supported arguments. The observation that Jehu — who exterminated Baal worship — left the calves intact is a powerful piece of internal biblical evidence. The analogy between the calves and the ark's cherubs is illuminating. Many contemporary scholars have reached similar conclusions independently.

**What's at stake:** Whether the northern kingdom was fundamentally apostate (Judean polemic) or practiced a legitimate variant of YHWH worship (plausible northern self-understanding). If Kaufmann is right, the Judean condemnation of Jeroboam's calves is theological polemic, not historical fact.

**Who disagrees:** The biblical historians of Kings — Kaufmann is explicitly reading against the grain of the Deuteronomistic History. But in contemporary scholarship, Kaufmann's reading has considerable support. Römer (*The Invention of God*, Ch. 8-9) agrees that Jeroboam's calves were YHWH images, not foreign gods. The bull imagery associated with El and Baal in Canaanite religion was adapted to YHWH worship. Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) argued that the bull iconography reflected El imagery originally associated with YHWH.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the calves were YHWH worship (and Kaufmann is probably right about this), the biblical polemic against them reflects a genuine theological dispute, not mere Judean chauvinism. The Deuteronomistic historians viewed ANY cult image — even of YHWH — as idolatrous. The aniconic principle that developed in Judah saw the northern calves as inherently corrupting, even if northerners believed they were worshipping YHWH. The distinction between "YHWH worship with images" and "pagan idolatry" is real, but the Deuteronomists were making a principled theological argument, not just a political polemic.

**My assessment:** This is the strongest claim in the chapter and one of Kaufmann's most important contributions. The evidence that Jehu preserved the calves while destroying Baalism is almost impossible to explain if the calves represented foreign gods. The cherub analogy is persuasive and helps make sense of how the calves could be genuinely YHWHistic while also genuinely offensive to aniconic sensibilities. Kaufmann's reconstruction — that the calves were pedestals without a divine rider, parallel to the ark's cherubs — is elegant and has explanatory power. This claim has aged well and is broadly accepted in contemporary scholarship, even by scholars who reject Kaufmann's larger thesis.

## Claim 5: Baal worship in the north was a limited, foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel — not an indigenous Israelite development

**Author's claim:** "The Baal worship of the north was limited to court circles" and "no evidence for syncretism in the religion of YHWH during the period of the Omrides exists." Jezebel's Baalism was a Phoenician import that "had evaporated by the time of Joram." Jehu's revolution "cannot be interpreted as a life struggle of the religion of YHWH against the menace of Baalism." Rather, "No menace of 'syncretism,' but the strength and zealotry of the religion of YHWH are what demanded that the last vestiges of Baalism be eradicated, even after Baal had long since ceased being a living issue."

**Evidence presented:**
- After Elijah's Carmel victory, Baal worship "no longer appears... as a factor in the relations between Elijah and Ahab." Ahab goes to war surrounded by YHWH prophets (I Kings 22).
- The new conflict between Elijah and Ahab arises over Naboth's murder, not Baal worship.
- "The dynasty's doom is sealed, according to this, not by Baal worship, but by the murder of Naboth."
- Under Joram, the "worshipers in [the Baal temple] were a mere remnant of survivors from Ahab's time" (II Kings 10:18). Joram removes a "pillar of Baal" (II Kings 3:2).
- "The narratives concerning Elisha furnish glimpses of life throughout the north and involve persons of various condition; nowhere do we meet with Baal worship."
- "Jehu's revolution cannot be interpreted as a life struggle of the religion of YHWH against the menace of Baalism."
- Jehu's ruse (announcing he would "outdo Ahab in his zeal for Baal") "could have been successful only because in its first stages Jehu's revolt did not appear to have as its chief end the annihilation of Baalism."
- "What roused prophets and zealots into action was the national distress after the death of Ahab" — military defeats by Moab and Aram, interpreted as YHWH's punishment for the house of Ahab's bloodguilt.

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann is making a genuinely interesting argument that has some textual support, but it requires him to minimize the evidence for widespread Baal worship and to treat the biblical narrative as a transparent historical report when it suits him — while dismissing its theological framing when it doesn't.

**What's at stake:** Whether Baal worship in Israel was (a) an indigenous development from Canaanite religion that periodically threatened to syncretize with YHWH worship, or (b) a foreign import that was always alien, limited, and quickly eradicated. For Kaufmann's larger thesis, (b) is essential — it means the religion of YHWH was never genuinely threatened by indigenous polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological record complicates Kaufmann's picture. Smith (*Early History of God*, Ch. 2-3) documents extensive evidence for Baal worship in Israel through the monarchy period — not just under the Omrides. The Samaria ostraca contain Baal-ist theophoric names. Day (*Yahweh and the Gods of Canaan*, Ch. 3) traces the Baal-Yahweh conflict as a recurring, not one-time, phenomenon. Moreover, Hosea's polemic (which Kaufmann dates to Jeroboam II, generations after Jehu) still fights Baalism — suggesting it had deeper roots than a single Phoenician import.

**Alternative reading:** Baal worship in Israel was both foreign AND indigenous. The specific Tyrian Baal (Melqart) introduced by Jezebel was a Phoenician import, but Baal worship more broadly had Canaanite roots that predated the monarchy. The conflict between Yahweh and Baal was a recurring dynamic throughout Israel's history — not a one-time crisis under Ahab that was resolved by Jehu. Kaufmann conflates the specific Tyrian Baal cult with Baal worship generally, which allows him to characterize ALL Baal worship as a foreign import.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reading has some textual support — the biblical narrative really does suggest that Jehu's revolution was more about bloodguilt (Naboth, the murdered prophets) than about Baal worship per se, and Jezebel's Baal really was a foreign import. But Kaufmann overplays his hand. The persistence of Baal polemic in Hosea (mid-8th century), the archaeological evidence for Baal theophoric names, and the structural similarities between Baal and YHWH's imagery (both are storm gods who ride the clouds, both battle Sea/Leviathan) all suggest that the Baal-Yahweh relationship was more complex than a foreign import that was quickly eradicated. Kaufmann needs Baal worship to be foreign and ephemeral to protect his thesis of Israel's categorical distinctiveness; the evidence suggests it was more indigenous and persistent than he allows.

## Claim 6: The popular prophets — mantic, healing, and apostolic functions merged in a single institution

**Author's claim:** "The Israelite popular prophet is at once a seer, a wonder-healer, and a militant apostle of YHWH. The characteristic intertwining of functions is evident." The popular prophet operates on multiple levels — accepting payment for mantic services while also serving as a messenger of God. "The mantic and the messenger are two sides of the same prophet." Moreover, "the boundary between mantic and apostolic prophecy was never sharply drawn. And it was from among the 'professional' prophets that the first martyrs of the one God arose."

**Evidence presented:**
- Samuel: Saul comes to him as a seer to find lost asses but encounters a messenger of YHWH who anoints him king.
- Ahiah: Jeroboam's wife comes disguised with payment to inquire about her sick son; Ahiah foretells both the boy's death and the doom of the dynasty.
- Elisha: Naaman comes with payment for healing; the cure converts him to YHWH worship. Hazael and Ben-Hadad consult Elisha as a clairvoyant "man of God"; Elisha weeps and announces Hazael's destiny.
- Elijah (II Kings 1): Intercepts Ahaziah's embassy to Baal-zebub, asking "Is there no God in Israel that you send to inquire of Baal-zebub?" — defending the mantic function of YHWH prophecy.
- "These stories shed light on the conception that Israelite prophecy had of its mantic and healing functions. These were not viewed as powers inherent in persons, spirits, or materials, but as effects of and witnesses to the favor and might of Israel's God."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive claim. Kaufmann's observation that Israelite prophecy combined mantic, healing, and apostolic functions is well-grounded in the biblical narratives. The Elisha cycle in particular supports this picture.

**What's at stake:** The nature of pre-classical prophecy and its relationship to later literary prophecy. If the popular prophets were already functioning as apostolic messengers, the classical prophets (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah) represent continuity with, not a radical break from, earlier prophecy.

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars have drawn a sharper distinction between "professional" court prophets (who served the king for payment) and "free" prophets (who spoke independently). Kaufmann's portrait blurs this distinction. Additionally, critical scholars might question how much of the Elijah/Elisha cycle reflects 9th-century reality versus later legendary development.

**Alternative reading:** The Elijah/Elisha narratives reflect a convergence that wasn't always typical — many "professional" prophets were purely mantic and never functioned apostolically. The classical prophets represent a new phenomenon precisely because they broke with the professional/mantic model. Kaufmann may be generalizing from exceptional figures (Elijah, Elisha) to the institution as a whole.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observation is valuable and supported by the texts. The Elijah and Elisha narratives do depict prophets functioning across multiple registers — mantic, healing, and apostolic — without a sharp distinction. This challenges the common scholarly tendency to treat the classical prophets as an entirely new phenomenon. However, Kaufmann may be too quick to generalize from the Elisha cycle, which is legendary literature, to the historical reality of 9th-century prophecy.

## Claim 7: The distinction between "true" and "false" prophets first appears in Ahab's time — and reflects the emergence of "prophets of weal" vs. "prophets of woe"

**Author's claim:** "In Ahab's time, the dichotomy of 'true' and 'false' prophets makes its first historical appearance." The theoretical distinction is older, but "what is new is the division into what seem to be two parties of prophets, each marked by a distinctive outlook and ideology." "Crucial for the history of Israelite religion is the distinction that develops during this period between the prophet of weal and the prophet of woe."

**Evidence presented:**
- I Kings 22: Micaiah opposed by four hundred prophets headed by Zedekiah who "prophesy good things to the king."
- "National crises gave rise, on the one hand, to a mood of penitence; the expression of which was the prophecy of woe. But it also aroused the hope of deliverance; the prophet of weal was the organ of this mood."
- Both types are "rooted in Israelite faith" — one linked to "a religious-moral doctrine," the other to "a national-territorial promise."
- The prophets of weal "whom we meet in the writings of the literary prophets... represent a type that belongs to the stage of Israelite religion prior to the rise of classical prophecy."
- Kaufmann insists the classical prophets were NOT exclusively prophets of woe. "It is a mistaken notion that the classical prophets were exclusively prophets of woe." Even Elijah "runs before Ahab's chariot in the hour of favor." Isaiah "appears in II Kings 19 as a prophet of weal."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive framework. The "weal vs. woe" distinction is a useful analytical tool, and Kaufmann's insistence that the classical prophets also delivered oracles of salvation is correct and important.

**What's at stake:** The nature of prophetic conflict in Israel. If both "weal" and "woe" prophecy are rooted in authentic Israelite tradition, the "false prophets" weren't necessarily charlatans — they were giving voice to genuine aspects of Israelite faith (the national promise) while neglecting others (the moral demand).

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves characterize the conflict in starker terms — false prophets are liars, adulterers, and drunkards who prophesy for money (Mic 3:5; Jer 23:14; Ezek 13:10). Kaufmann is deliberately complicating the biblical picture, which is methodologically interesting but requires him to read against the grain of his own primary sources.

**Alternative reading:** The "weal vs. woe" framework captures a real dynamic but may be too schematic. Some conflicts between prophets (Jeremiah vs. Hananiah in Jer 28) involved genuine theological and political disagreement, not just temperamental differences. The "false prophets" may have been court functionaries whose "prophecy of weal" served royal interests — they weren't just giving voice to a legitimate strand of Israelite faith; they were propagandists.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's framework is genuinely helpful for understanding the prophetic conflicts. His observation that classical prophecy contained both judgment and salvation is important and often overlooked in popular characterizations of the prophets as purely doom-sayers. However, his attempt to salvage the "prophets of weal" as legitimate representatives of Israelite tradition may be too generous — the classical prophets' condemnation of their opponents is so fierce that it suggests more than a difference in emphasis.

## Claim 8: The Book of Jonah is an early (pre-exilic) northern composition — a masterpiece of moral speculation on repentance, not universalism

**Author's claim:** "The critical opinion that Jonah is a late work... is a tissue of errors." Jonah was composed "during the dynasty of Jehu" in the north, before Tiglath-pileser III and before Nineveh became a glorious imperial capital. "The Nineveh of the book of Jonah is not the capital of a great pagan empire; its king is not the ruler of Assyria, but 'king of Nineveh.'" "Of the universalism of literary prophecy there is not a trace in Jonah." Rather, Jonah "belongs, then, to the early literature of moral speculation" — like Job and the Sodom story — dealing with "sin, punishment, repentance, forgiveness — justice versus mercy."

**Evidence presented:**
- Nineveh in Jonah is a legendary "great city" with its own king, not the capital of a world-conquering empire. "This fabulous principality could have been conceived only before the reign of Sennacherib, who made Nineveh a glorious imperial capital."
- The book's universalism is not classical prophetic universalism (which involves the nations abandoning idolatry at the end of days) but "the early universalism of preclassical prophecy" — YHWH judges the nations for moral sins, not idolatry.
- "Jonah does not call on the Ninevites to abandon their idols."
- The Ninevites' faith is "the pagan 'fear of God' that figures in many early narratives (Gen. 20:11; 39:9; 42:18)."
- The book's hero is Jonah son of Amittai (II Kings 14:25), a northern prophet under Jeroboam II (784-744 BCE), before Tiglath-pileser.
- "The road to this idea was long" — tracing the development of repentance from early stories where it is absent (Flood, Sodom) through David's and Ahab's partial repentance to Jonah's full expression.
- "The book of Jonah shows what heights the religious-moral consciousness of Ephraim reached in the days of Jeroboam II."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann's arguments for an early date are creative and not without merit, but they run counter to strong linguistic and thematic evidence for a postexilic date. The universalism argument is clever — Kaufmann argues Jonah's universalism is NOT like Second Isaiah's, therefore it's early — but many scholars would say it represents a DIFFERENT kind of universalism (anti-particularist) that makes best sense in a postexilic context.

**What's at stake:** The dating of Jonah and the development of Israelite universalism. If Kaufmann is right that Jonah is early (8th century), it would mean that sophisticated moral reflection on repentance and divine mercy developed in the north centuries before the exile. This would support his thesis that Israel's religious genius was early and broadly distributed, not a late Judean development.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus dates Jonah to the postexilic period (5th-4th centuries BCE) based on linguistic features (Late Biblical Hebrew), the book's universalist/anti-particularist themes (which are seen as responding to postexilic exclusivism associated with Ezra-Nehemiah), and its narrative genre (a didactic novella, not prophetic oracle). Sasson's Anchor Bible commentary on Jonah thoroughly documents the linguistic and thematic arguments for a late date.

**Alternative reading:** Jonah may USE an 8th-century prophet as its protagonist while being composed much later. The book's fictitious, legendary character (the great fish, the instant repentance of an entire pagan city, the gourd that grows and withers in a day) suggests literary artifice rather than historical report. Its engagement with questions of divine mercy toward gentiles makes most sense in a context where Jewish-gentile relations were a live issue — the postexilic period. The anti-particularist message reads as a critique of Ezra-Nehemiah's exclusivism.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's argument is ingenious and worth engaging — the observation that Jonah's Nineveh is a legendary city with its own king rather than an imperial capital IS genuinely interesting, and his analysis of the repentance theme is excellent. But the late dating of Jonah rests on multiple converging lines of evidence (linguistics, genre, theme) that Kaufmann doesn't adequately address. His alternative early dating requires accepting that Jonah was an anomaly preserved for centuries before being included in the Book of the Twelve — not impossible, but less parsimonious than the standard view. The strongest part of Kaufmann's treatment is his analysis of the repentance theme, which stands regardless of dating. His weakest part is the insistence on an 8th-century date, which seems driven by his desire to locate Israel's greatest religious achievements in the early, northern period.

## Claim 9: The Josianic reform and Deuteronomic centralization were an organic development of Israelite ideas — not a priestly power grab or a reaction to paganism

**Author's claim:** "The Deuteronomic idea must perforce be regarded as one of the intuitive symbols through which Israelite religion sought expression. It is not a means to some didactic or material end, but an end in itself." Centralization "was no guarantor of a pure cult" — pagan influence radiated from the royal centers, not the rural altars. Nor did it serve priestly interests — rural priests lost their livelihoods, and Jerusalem priests had their monopoly challenged. The reform "aimed at concentrating all cultic activity in the one chosen temple" based on "the idea of divine election" — Israelite sanctity "is a creation of the will of God; it originates always in a historical election, a revelation of God's word."

**Evidence presented:**
- Earlier reforms (Asa, Jehoshaphat, Jehu, Jehoiada) targeted paganism and "idolatry" — Josiah's reform went further, centralizing even legitimate YHWH worship.
- "Nothing in the doctrines of literary prophecy implied centralization of the cult, nor is it a logical inference from the monotheistic idea."
- "Centralization was, therefore, no guarantor of a pure cult" — Manasseh "converted the Solomonic temple itself into a heathen pantheon."
- Rural priests "were, of course, hit hardest" by centralization. The Deuteronomic law "assured them the right to serve at the central sanctuary (Deut. 18:6 f.), but in fact it was impossible for so many priests to find employment at one temple."
- Jerusalem priests also lost — the law "encroached upon the monopoly of the Jerusalem priests."
- "The immediate and most far reaching result of centralization was to empty the daily religious life of the people at large of all priestly influence."
- The idea traces to the "tent" tradition of the Aaronic priesthood — a portable sanctuary unconnected with any sacred site — which was in tension with the popular idea of holy places.
- Deuteronomy 12 forbids worship at "every place" — "The ban is not grounded on the fact that these were pagan cult sites... but on the view that this very manner of worship was pagan: 'You shall not do so for YHWH your God' (Deut. 12:4)."
- "The Deuteronomic temple of the future is diametrically opposed to the pagan temple whose sanctity is 'prehistoric,' mythological. It is an eschatological temple."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's argument that centralization didn't serve priestly interests is genuinely important and often overlooked. But his alternative explanation — that it was an "intuitive symbol" of Israelite religion — is vague and under-argued.

**What's at stake:** Whether the Deuteronomic reform was (a) a pious reform based on religious principle, (b) a Jerusalem priestly power grab, or (c) an Assyrian-influenced political consolidation. Kaufmann argues for (a) while carefully distinguishing it from (b).

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholarship has offered several explanations Kaufmann rejects. The "priestly power grab" theory argues centralization concentrated cultic revenue in Jerusalem. The "Assyrian influence" theory (e.g., Weinfeld, though he nuanced it) sees Deuteronomic centralization as paralleling Assyrian imperial cult policies. Many scholars see the reform as a complex event with multiple motivations — religious, political, and economic — not reducible to any single factor.

**Alternative reading:** The Deuteronomic reform was driven by a coalition of Jerusalem priests, scribes, and court officials who had multiple motivations: genuine religious conviction, political consolidation of the Davidic state in the wake of the northern kingdom's fall, and material self-interest (even if centralization didn't benefit ALL priests, it benefited the Jerusalem temple establishment). Kaufmann's point that centralization HURT rural priests and limited Jerusalem priestly monopoly is well-taken and complicates simple "power grab" theories. But his alternative — that it was a purely religious "intuitive symbol" — is insufficiently grounded.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's critique of the "priestly power grab" theory is effective and valuable. His observation that centralization cost priests their livelihoods and emptied daily life of priestly influence is a genuine contribution that any adequate theory of the reform must account for. However, his positive explanation — that Deuteronomy's centralization was an "intuitive symbol" expressing Israelite ideas of historical election — is less persuasive. It's not wrong — Deuteronomy really DOES ground the chosen place in divine election rather than natural sanctity — but it doesn't explain WHY this particular "intuitive symbol" emerged in the late 8th-7th centuries specifically. Kaufmann's anti-materialist hermeneutic prevents him from considering the political and social factors that made centralization conceivable and attractive at that particular historical moment (the fall of the northern kingdom, the influx of northern refugees and traditions, Assyrian imperial pressure, Hezekiah's political ambitions).

## Claim 10: The new eschatology emerged during the Aramaean wars — Israel's first confrontation with the possibility of national destruction

**Author's claim:** "Before the Aramaean wars the possibility that YHWH would really break his covenant with Israel and expel it from its land could hardly have been seriously contemplated." But during the terrible century between Ahab and Jeroboam II, "a significant change took place in the mood of the people." The incessant wars "ravaged and impoverished the land," and to the pious "it seemed as if YHWH were withdrawing his favor." "The earlier curses and threats become an eschatological certainty." The hope for a "new act of divine grace" was born, and "the 'end of days' was transferred from the present to the future."

**Evidence presented:**
- Deuteronomy 4:25ff, 31:16ff — "Coming into the 'rest and the inheritance' are no longer the final promise. After a period of well-being, Israel will stray from YHWH to serve other gods; then a time of punishment and distress will come."
- The Song of Moses (Deut 32) — "enslavement to a 'no-people,' a 'foolish nation' (the Aramaeans), drought, famine, plague and wild animals, and death by the sword" — but no exile.
- Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 4:25-28 — exile appears as the final calamity, followed by repentance and restoration.
- "These monitions... reveal acquaintance neither with the conditions of the real exile nor with the historiosophy of classical prophecy" — they are pre-classical.
- Elijah's vision (I Kings 19:15ff.) — Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha charged with eradicating Baal — "Here the idea glimmers forth that religious faith, not national existence, is the ultimate value."
- The "remnant" concept appears as "the 'seven thousand' who will be saved by YHWH" in Elijah's vision.
- "In the south, such visions centered always about the house of David; this was the reason of their enduring importance."
- The "day of YHWH" mentioned by Amos (5:18ff.) took shape in this period — an expectation "when YHWH would wreak vengeance upon Israel's enemies."
- Elisha and Jonah's prophecies of salvation for the Jehu dynasty — "northern prophecy expected that through these kings YHWH would deliver his people."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's reconstruction of an emergent eschatology during the Aramaean crisis is plausible and textually grounded. His dating of key Deuteronomy passages to this period is creative but contested. His argument that the "exile" language in these texts predates the actual exile is important for his broader chronology (the Torah is pre-prophetic).

**What's at stake:** The development of Israelite eschatology and its relationship to historical crisis. If Kaufmann is right, the key elements of later prophetic eschatology — judgment, remnant, ideal king, day of YHWH — emerged in the 9th-8th centuries in response to Aramaean pressure, not in the 6th century in response to the Babylonian exile.

**Who disagrees:** Many scholars date the eschatological passages in Deuteronomy (especially those mentioning exile) to the exilic period or later, seeing them as *vaticinia ex eventu* — prophecies written after the events they predict. Kaufmann's argument that they "reveal acquaintance neither with the conditions of the real exile" is contestable and depends on his reading of what constitutes post-exilic perspective.

**Alternative reading:** The eschatology in Deuteronomy and Leviticus 26 developed over time, with earlier layers reflecting Aramaean and Assyrian crises and later layers incorporating the experience of the Babylonian exile. The texts as we have them are composite, and Kaufmann's attempt to date them entirely to the pre-exilic period overstates the case. However, the basic observation that eschatological thinking began before the exile — that the fall of Samaria in 722 generated theological reflection that laid groundwork for later developments — is widely accepted.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reconstruction of an emerging eschatology during the Aramaean crisis is one of the chapter's more valuable contributions. The observation that exile language appears in contexts that seem to predate the actual exile is worth taking seriously, and his argument that the "day of YHWH" concept originated in northern prophecy during this period is plausible. However, his insistence on dating specific texts to this period with precision overreaches. The Deuteronomy passages are composite texts that almost certainly include exilic and postexilic layers. Kaufmann's observation that they reflect a pre-exilic eschatological *mood* is valuable; his claim that they are pre-exilic in their *entirety* is less convincing.

## Chapter VIII Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Israelite kingship fundamentally different from pagan | MEDIUM-HIGH | Royal psalms and Davidic covenant language suggest sacral dimensions Kaufmann minimizes |
| 2: Antimonarchic passages are premonarchic, not postexilic | MEDIUM | Deuteronomistic editing may have shaped these texts significantly |
| 3: Solomon's worship was private defection, not popular paganization | LOW-MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence of persistent polytheistic practice; Solomon's open building of foreign chapels |
| 4: Golden calves were YHWH worship, not pagan idolatry | MEDIUM-HIGH | Well-supported by Jehu's behavior and cherub analogy; broadly accepted in current scholarship |
| 5: Baal worship was limited foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel | LOW-MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence of persistent Baal worship; Hosea's 8th-century Baal polemic |
| 6: Popular prophets merged mantic, healing, and apostolic functions | MEDIUM-HIGH | Supported by Elijah/Elisha cycles; may overgeneralize from exceptional figures |
| 7: True/false prophet distinction emerges as weal vs. woe | MEDIUM-HIGH | Useful framework; may understate genuine corruption of "false prophets" |
| 8: Jonah is early (8th c.) northern composition | LOW-MEDIUM | Linguistic evidence for late date; thematic fit with postexilic particularism debates |
| 9: Josianic reform was organic religious development, not power grab | MEDIUM | Effective critique of power-grab theory; positive alternative is vague |
| 10: New eschatology emerged during Aramaean wars | MEDIUM | Plausible reconstruction; dating of specific texts to this period is contestable |

**Strongest section:** Claim 4 — the interpretation of Jeroboam's calves as non-idolatrous YHWH worship. This is Kaufmann's most important and durable contribution in this chapter. The arguments are textually grounded (Jehu's behavior, the cherub analogy, the silence of Elijah and Elisha) and have been broadly accepted in subsequent scholarship.

**Weakest section:** Claim 5 — the characterization of Baal worship as a limited foreign import. Kaufmann needs Baal worship to be foreign and ephemeral to protect his thesis of Israel's distinctive, non-syncretistic religion. But the evidence — from Hosea's continued polemic against Baal generations after Jehu, to the archaeological record of Baal theophoric names and cult objects — suggests Baal worship was more deeply rooted and persistent than Kaufmann allows. His argument conflates the specific Tyrian Baal of Jezebel with Baal worship in general.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals both the strengths and weaknesses of Kaufmann's method in concentrated form. When he reads the biblical text closely and follows the evidence where it leads — as with the golden calves — he produces genuinely illuminating results that have stood the test of time. When he needs the evidence to support his larger thesis — as with Solomon's chapels or the scope of Baal worship — he minimizes, qualifies, or reinterprets data to fit his framework. The chapter is a masterclass in both the possibilities and the perils of Kaufmann's approach.

**Cross-reference evaluation:**
- **Smith Ch. 2-3 (Yahweh and Baal / Yahweh and Asherah):** Smith documents extensive evidence for Baal worship and Asherah veneration throughout the monarchy — not just as a one-time Phoenician import. This evidence directly challenges Kaufmann's Claim 5. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (YHWH and his Asherah) are impossible to square with Kaufmann's picture of a populace that practiced only "vestigial idolatry."
- **Day Ch. 3 (Yahweh Versus Baal):** Day traces the Baal-Yahweh conflict as a recurring structural feature of Israelite religion, not a single crisis under Ahab. This supports the view that Kaufmann's treatment of Baal worship is too narrow.
- **Römer Ch. 8-9 (Statue and Asherah):** Römer agrees with Kaufmann on Claim 4 (the calves were YHWH images) but disagrees fundamentally on the broader picture — Römer sees the monarchy period as one of genuine religious pluralism, not "vestigial idolatry."
- **Albertz Vol. 1 §3.8 (The Deuteronomic Reform Movement):** Albertz situates Josiah's reform within its political and social context in a way Kaufmann resists. The contrast highlights Kaufmann's anti-materialist hermeneutic.

**Final assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most successful chapter after Chapter I. The golden calves argument alone is worth the price of admission, and his treatment of popular prophecy, the new eschatology, and the repentance theme in Jonah contains genuine insights. But the chapter also exposes the systematic weakness of Kaufmann's approach: his need to minimize evidence of genuine polytheism in Israel forces him into strained readings of Solomon's reign, the scope of Baal worship, and the persistence of popular "idolatry." Kaufmann wants Israel to have been distinctively monotheistic from the beginning; the evidence suggests distinctiveness developed gradually through the monarchy period, and the "idolatry" the Bible condemns was often genuine polytheistic practice, not the contentless fetishism Kaufmann describes.
