# Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy

Kaufmann surveys the United and Divided Monarchies, advancing several signature arguments: that Israelite kingship was fundamentally different from pagan sacral kingship; that the "sins of Jeroboam" (the golden calves) were not pagan idolatry but YHWH worship in an illicit form; that the Baal worship of the north was a foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel — not an indigenous syncretistic development; and that the reform movements culminating in Josiah's Deuteronomic centralization represent the logical unfolding of Israel's distinctive religious ideas. The chapter also covers the popular prophets, the emergence of a new eschatology, and the Book of Jonah. This is one of Kaufmann's most distinctive chapters — his interpretation of the golden calves as non-idolatrous is genuinely interesting and has partial support in scholarship, but his handling of Solomon's pagan chapels and the scope of Baal worship strains credibility.

## Claim 1: Israelite kingship was fundamentally different from pagan sacral kingship — grounded in prophetic election, not divine essence
→ [[claim-israelite-kingship-non-sacral-prophetic-election-not-divine-essence]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Israelite kings were never deified and the institution was grounded in apostolic prophecy, not priesthood. The difference from Egyptian/Mesopotamian sacral kingship is real, but Kaufmann understates the sacral dimensions that DID exist (royal psalms, divine sonship language).

## Claim 2: The antimonarchic passages are premonarchic in origin, not postexilic
→ [[claim-antimonarchic-passages-premonarchic-not-postexilic]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.2** | Confidence: medium
  Later Israel never repudiated monarchy as an institution — even postexilic messianic hope centered on a Davidic king. The antimonarchic strand in I Samuel is anomalous and best explained as preserving genuinely premonarchic sentiment.

## Claim 4: The "sins of Jeroboam" — the golden calves — were NOT pagan idolatry but YHWH worship
→ [[claim-golden-calves-yhwh-worship-not-pagan-idolatry]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.4** | Confidence: medium-high
  Jehu destroyed Baalism but left the calves — clear evidence they were YHWH worship in an illicit form. The calves were pedestals without a divine rider, parallel to the ark's cherubs. Broadly accepted in contemporary scholarship.
  **Supports:** [[claim-golden-calves-legitimate-yhwh-symbols]], [[claim-golden-calf-story-depicts-fetishism-not-mythological-polytheism]]

## Claim 5: Baal worship in the north was a limited, foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel
→ [[claim-baal-worship-north-limited-foreign-import-ahab-jezebel]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.5** | Confidence: low-medium
  Jezebel's Tyrian Baal was a foreign import that evaporated after Jehu. But Kaufmann conflates the specific Tyrian Baal with Baal worship generally — Hosea's 8th-century polemic and the archaeological record suggest Baal worship was more indigenous and persistent.

→ [[claim-popular-prophets-mantic-healing-apostolic-functions-merged]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.6** | Confidence: medium-high

## Claim 7: The "true" vs. "false" prophet distinction emerges as weal vs. woe
→ [[claim-true-false-prophets-weal-woe-distinction-ahab-era]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.7** | Confidence: medium-high
  In Ahab's time, prophets divided into two camps: prophets of weal (rooted in national promise) and prophets of woe (rooted in moral demand). Both were rooted in authentic Israelite tradition — the "false prophets" weren't necessarily charlatans.

## Claim 8: The Book of Jonah is an early (pre-exilic) northern composition on repentance
→ [[claim-jonah-early-pre-exilic-northern-composition-repentance-moral-speculation]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.8** | Confidence: low-medium

## Claim 9: The Josianic reform was an organic religious development, not a priestly power grab
→ [[claim-josianic-reform-organic-israelite-development-not-priestly-power-grab]] | **kaufmann-ri-8.9** | Confidence: medium
  Centralization HURT rural priests (lost livelihoods) and limited Jerusalem's monopoly — it wasn't a power grab. It was an "intuitive symbol" of Israel's idea of divine election. But Kaufmann's anti-materialist hermeneutic ignores the political/social factors that made centralization possible in the 7th century.

## Chapter VIII Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Israelite kingship fundamentally different from pagan | MEDIUM-HIGH | Royal psalms and Davidic covenant language suggest sacral dimensions Kaufmann minimizes |
| 2: Antimonarchic passages are premonarchic, not postexilic | MEDIUM | Deuteronomistic editing may have shaped these texts significantly |
| 3: Solomon's worship was private defection, not popular paganization | LOW-MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence of persistent polytheistic practice; Solomon's open building of foreign chapels |
| 4: Golden calves were YHWH worship, not pagan idolatry | MEDIUM-HIGH | Well-supported by Jehu's behavior and cherub analogy; broadly accepted in current scholarship |
| 5: Baal worship was limited foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel | LOW-MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence of persistent Baal worship; Hosea's 8th-century Baal polemic |
| 6: Popular prophets merged mantic, healing, and apostolic functions | MEDIUM-HIGH | Supported by Elijah/Elisha cycles; may overgeneralize from exceptional figures |
| 7: True/false prophet distinction emerges as weal vs. woe | MEDIUM-HIGH | Useful framework; may understate genuine corruption of "false prophets" |
| 8: Jonah is early (8th c.) northern composition | LOW-MEDIUM | Linguistic evidence for late date; thematic fit with postexilic particularism debates |
| 9: Josianic reform was organic religious development, not power grab | MEDIUM | Effective critique of power-grab theory; positive alternative is vague |
| 10: New eschatology emerged during Aramaean wars | MEDIUM | Plausible reconstruction; dating of specific texts to this period is contestable |

**Strongest section:** Claim 4 — the interpretation of Jeroboam's calves as non-idolatrous YHWH worship. This is Kaufmann's most important and durable contribution in this chapter. The arguments are textually grounded (Jehu's behavior, the cherub analogy, the silence of Elijah and Elisha) and have been broadly accepted in subsequent scholarship.

**Weakest section:** Claim 5 — the characterization of Baal worship as a limited foreign import. Kaufmann needs Baal worship to be foreign and ephemeral to protect his thesis of Israel's distinctive, non-syncretistic religion. But the evidence — from Hosea's continued polemic against Baal generations after Jehu, to the archaeological record of Baal theophoric names and cult objects — suggests Baal worship was more deeply rooted and persistent than Kaufmann allows. His argument conflates the specific Tyrian Baal of Jezebel with Baal worship in general.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals both the strengths and weaknesses of Kaufmann's method in concentrated form. When he reads the biblical text closely and follows the evidence where it leads — as with the golden calves — he produces genuinely illuminating results that have stood the test of time. When he needs the evidence to support his larger thesis — as with Solomon's chapels or the scope of Baal worship — he minimizes, qualifies, or reinterprets data to fit his framework. The chapter is a masterclass in both the possibilities and the perils of Kaufmann's approach.

**Cross-reference evaluation:**
- **Smith Ch. 2-3 (Yahweh and Baal / Yahweh and Asherah):** Smith documents extensive evidence for Baal worship and Asherah veneration throughout the monarchy — not just as a one-time Phoenician import. This evidence directly challenges Kaufmann's Claim 5. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (YHWH and his Asherah) are impossible to square with Kaufmann's picture of a populace that practiced only "vestigial idolatry."
- **Day Ch. 3 (Yahweh Versus Baal):** Day traces the Baal-Yahweh conflict as a recurring structural feature of Israelite religion, not a single crisis under Ahab. This supports the view that Kaufmann's treatment of Baal worship is too narrow.
- **Römer Ch. 8-9 (Statue and Asherah):** Römer agrees with Kaufmann on Claim 4 (the calves were YHWH images) but disagrees fundamentally on the broader picture — Römer sees the monarchy period as one of genuine religious pluralism, not "vestigial idolatry."
- **Albertz Vol. 1 §3.8 (The Deuteronomic Reform Movement):** Albertz situates Josiah's reform within its political and social context in a way Kaufmann resists. The contrast highlights Kaufmann's anti-materialist hermeneutic.

**Final assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most successful chapter after Chapter I. The golden calves argument alone is worth the price of admission, and his treatment of popular prophecy, the new eschatology, and the repentance theme in Jonah contains genuine insights. But the chapter also exposes the systematic weakness of Kaufmann's approach: his need to minimize evidence of genuine polytheism in Israel forces him into strained readings of Solomon's reign, the scope of Baal worship, and the persistence of popular "idolatry." Kaufmann wants Israel to have been distinctively monotheistic from the beginning; the evidence suggests distinctiveness developed gradually through the monarchy period, and the "idolatry" the Bible condemns was often genuine polytheistic practice, not the contentless fetishism Kaufmann describes.
