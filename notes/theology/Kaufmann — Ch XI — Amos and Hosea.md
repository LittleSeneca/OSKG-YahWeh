# Kaufmann — Chapter XI: Amos and Hosea

Kaufmann's argument: the first classical prophets were NOT innovators of monotheism or ethical religion. They inherited both from the popular religion and the Torah, and their call was to covenant faithfulness — not to a new religious consciousness. Amos's innovation was making everyday social morality (not just idolatry) decisive for national destiny. Hosea is read as a prophet of divine love and repentance, not the inventor of the marriage metaphor. Kaufmann's most radical structural move: he splits Hosea into "First Hosea" (chs 1-3, pre-classical, ~850 BCE) and "Second Hosea" (chs 4-14, classical, ~730 BCE), arguing that the critical conflation of the two has produced a phantom "syncretistic Baal-YHWH cult" that never existed.

---

## Claim 1: Amos was not an innovator of monotheism or ethical religion — he inherited both from popular religion and Torah

**Author's claim:** Amos's monotheism and ethical demands are not argued for or introduced as novelties — they are the assumed starting point. "From the early religion he inherits the conception of YHWH as lord of history." The notion "that the destiny of all nations was decided by YHWH (9:7) was an old idea that Amos does not have to argue." The prophetic demand for social justice "echo[es], for the most part, the ancient covenant laws." Amos draws on the Exodus, the forty-year wandering, Sodom and Gomorrah, the plagues of Egypt, "YHWH's Torah and laws" (2:4) — all inherited tradition. He "utilized styles and material that were ready at hand" and "feels himself a link in the succession of Israelite prophecy."

**Evidence presented:**
- Amos uses standard prophetic formulas ("Thus said YHWH," "Hear," "In that day") that predate him
- Wisdom forms (ascending numbers "three...four," epigram in question form) show cultural inheritance, not innovation
- The elegy in *qina* meter (5:1f) uses an established genre
- Amos 9:7's assertion that YHWH brought Philistines from Caphtor and Aram from Kir is presented without argument — it's a given, not a thesis

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive claim (Amos uses inherited forms); LOW that this proves early popular monotheism. Drawing on tradition doesn't tell us whether that tradition was monotheistic or the product of later redaction.

**What's at stake:** If Amos is an innovator, then monotheism and ethical universalism emerge in the 8th century as prophetic creations. If he's a traditionalist, then monotheism predates classical prophecy by centuries — exactly the thesis Kaufmann needs to overturn Wellhausen.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus reads Amos as a genuine innovator. The very fact that Amos has to assert YHWH's rule over other nations (9:7) could indicate this was NOT an old, settled idea but a contested one. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheistic rhetoric; Amos would be an early stage, not a late traditionalist.

**Alternative reading:** Amos DID inherit traditions, but he transformed them radically. Inheriting the "day of YHWH" concept and INVERTING it (from Israel's vindication to Israel's judgment) IS innovation. Inheriting covenant laws and ELEVATING social morality over cult IS innovation. Kaufmann conflates "using inherited materials" with "not innovating," which is a category error.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann scores a real point: Amos demonstrably draws on prior traditions and doesn't argue for monotheism as if it were a new idea. But this doesn't require Kaufmann's conclusion that the traditions were monotheistic FROM THE BEGINNING. Amos could be drawing on traditions that had developed in a more monotheistic direction over the preceding centuries. The presence of inherited tradition is compatible with both Kaufmann's model and the critical model — it's the DATING and CONTENT of those traditions that's contested.

## Claim 2: Amos's actual innovation was making everyday social morality decisive for national destiny

**Author's claim:** Before Amos, "national doom and exile are, as a rule, threatened only for idolatry" — or for "particularly heinous sins which the whole society has committed" (Flood, Sodom, Canaanite dispossession). "Violations of everyday social morality — perversion of justice, bribe-taking, exploitation of the poor, and the like — are never mentioned. These are regarded as 'venial sins,' subject to the regular process of God's judgment and his individual providence." Amos is "the first to evaluate social morality as a factor in national destiny." He makes it plain that the ruling class in particular was involved, "and in the everyday moral sins. He never speaks of murder, but only of exploitation, oppression, and perversion of justice."

**Evidence presented:**
- Amos 1:3-2:3: the nations are judged for "particular enormities, in line with the early view"
- Amos 2:6ff: Israel and Judah judged "for selling the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" — a category shift
- Amos's "almost complete silence regarding idolatry, the chief offense which the early literature held crucial for the destiny of the people"
- Contrast with Nathan (rebukes David's adultery/murder but doesn't make it national), Elijah (rebukes Ahab's judicial murder but doesn't make it national)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is genuinely one of Kaufmann's sharpest observations in the chapter. The distinction between heinous sins (murder, sexual atrocity) and everyday social sins (exploitation, bribery, price-gouging) as triggers for NATIONAL judgment IS a real shift in Amos. Kaufmann is correct that prior literature treats these differently.

**What's at stake:** Whether Amos is merely applying inherited covenant law or transforming the very framework within which covenant violation is understood. If Kaufmann is right, Amos took an existing ethical tradition and made it politically existential.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the covenant lawsuit (*rîb*) form as already present in earlier traditions. If the Decalogue and Covenant Code are pre-Amos (which Kaufmann believes), then the moral demands aren't new — but Kaufmann's point is about their ELEVATION to national-decisive status, not their content.

**Alternative reading:** Amos may not be the first — he's simply the first whose oracles survive. The Elijah-Elisha cycles, even if later redacted, may reflect earlier prophetic concern with social morality. The absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.

**My assessment:** This is the strongest claim in the chapter and genuinely advances our understanding of Amos. Kaufmann correctly identifies a structural shift: prior literature treats social sins as individually adjudicated; Amos treats them as national-existential. Even if one rejects Kaufmann's larger thesis about early monotheism, this observation about Amos's framework stands on its own.

## Claim 3: Amos's rejection of the cult was not wholesale — it was a conditional rejection of cult divorced from morality

**Author's claim:** Amos 5:25 ("Did you offer me sacrifices and oblations forty years in the desert?") is NOT a blanket rejection of sacrifice. The opinion "that Amos unconditionally rejected the cult, especially the cult of sacrifice" is "quite out of the question. A wholesale repudiation of the cult would have meant a rejection of tradition which grounded it on an express command of YHWH." Amos is attacking "the legitimate, national cult of YHWH" — not because cult is intrinsically wrong, but because "it is rejected in favor of morality; God desires righteousness, not sacrifice!" The cult "has no intrinsic value" — its value is conditional on the moral state of the worshipper.

**Evidence presented:**
- Amos calls foreign lands "impure" and regards giving wine to Nazirites a sin (2:11) — he has cultic categories
- His condemnation extends "beyond sacrifice, to song and psalm as well" (5:23) — it's not specifically anti-sacrifice
- Isaiah, Jeremiah, Second Isaiah, and Zechariah take comparable positions — and "most of whom express veneration of the Jerusalem temple... did not conceive of a religion without prayer, festival, and Sabbath"
- Amos 5:25 is "hyperbole": the point is that the wilderness period, when Israel couldn't make daily offerings, "was the time of favor and grace" — the contrast is between present abundant empty cult and past needy faithful relationship
- Kaufmann notes that to take 5:25 literally, one must assume Amos was "ignorant of JE" (Exod 3:12, 18; 12:21-27; 24:1-11; Deut 12:8) — unlikely for a prophet steeped in tradition

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the interpretation of Amos 5:25 as hyperbole; MEDIUM on the claim that Amos didn't reject cult "in principle."

**What's at stake:** If Amos rejected the cult wholesale, he's a radical innovator breaking with the entire sacrificial system. If he rejected only cult-without-morality, he's continuous with Torah values that already prioritized obedience over sacrifice (1 Sam 15:22; Ps 51:16-17).

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Amos as an anti-cultic prophet. But Kaufmann's reading is actually close to the mainstream on this point — most scholars don't think Amos wanted to abolish the cult, only to subordinate it to ethics. The real disagreement is about whether the Torah's integration of cult and ethics predates Amos (Kaufmann) or develops after him (critical view).

**Alternative reading:** Amos may not have a systematic "theology of cult" at all. He's a poet of doom, not a systematician. His rhetoric is designed to shock, not to establish a nuanced position on sacrifice. Reading 5:25 as hyperbole is reasonable, but the extremity of the rhetoric IS significant — it shows that cult could be imagined as dispensable in a way that morality couldn't.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reading of Amos on cult is persuasive and relatively uncontroversial. The more interesting question is his framework for WHY the cult has only conditional value — his claim that Israelite religion, "essentially non-mythological and non-magical, could conceive of the relation of God and the world only in terms of free grace," so that "the absolute and autonomous value that paganism placed on the cult thereby vanished." This is Kaufmann's master thesis applied to Amos, and it's more theological than historical.

## Claim 4: Amos 9:7 and the apparent acknowledgment of other gods do not undermine Amos's monotheism

**Author's claim:** Amos 9:7 ("Are you not to me like the Ethiopians, O house of Israel?") is "a satirical hyperbole." The nations' destinies are decided by YHWH — but Israel's relationship is categorically different. "For to Amos, YHWH's bringing up Israel from Egypt differed radically from his bringing up Aram from Kir. In Israel only did YHWH act as a known God, as Amos himself tells us explicitly: 'Only you have I known out of all the families of the earth...'" (3:1ff). The gentiles "are not required to know YHWH, indeed their very land is impure (7:17)." Amos 5:26 (mention of Sakkuth and Kaiwan) is "almost incidental" — idolatry goes essentially unmentioned.

**Evidence presented:**
- Amos 3:1ff explicitly asserts Israel's unique status
- Amos 2:4: YHWH's Torah and laws given to Israel alone
- Amos 2:10ff: YHWH raised up prophets and Nazirites for Israel alone
- Amos 4:6ff: YHWH chastised Israel alone to make them repent
- Amos 2:7: only in Israel can YHWH's name be profaned

**Confidence:** LOW. This is where Kaufmann's reading is most strained. He needs Amos to be a monotheist who doesn't acknowledge real other gods, but Amos 9:7 and 5:26 require explanation. Calling 9:7 "satirical hyperbole" is a convenience label, not an argument.

**What's at stake:** If Amos genuinely acknowledges that YHWH brought other nations from their respective lands (Aram from Kir, Philistines from Caphtor) just as he brought Israel from Egypt, this suggests a henotheistic framework where each nation has its own patron deity — and YHWH is Israel's, supremely powerful but not uniquely existent. Kaufmann needs this NOT to be the case.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus reads Amos 9:7 as evidence that Amos was a henotheist, not a monotheist. The verse parallels Israel's exodus with other nations' migrations, which makes little sense in a monotheistic framework where YHWH is the only god. Römer and Smith both cite this as evidence of Amos's pre-monotheistic theology.

**Alternative reading:** Amos 9:7 IS henotheistic — but that doesn't make Amos a polytheist. Amos believes YHWH is the supreme god who controls all nations, while other nations may have their own (lesser, perhaps unreal) gods. This is consistent with the "YHWH alone" position that Smith identifies as the precursor to full monotheism. Kaufmann's insistence that Amos was already a full monotheist requires him to do violence to the text.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's weakest argument in the chapter. His treatment of Amos 9:7 as "satirical hyperbole" is hand-waving. The verse genuinely suggests that Amos conceived of YHWH's relationship with other nations as parallel to his relationship with Israel — a henotheistic or "YHWH-alone" framework, not monotheism. Kaufmann's reading of 3:1ff as the "real" Amos and 9:7 as rhetorical excess is special pleading. The two verses can be reconciled in a henotheistic framework (YHWH is supreme over all nations but has a unique covenant relationship with Israel); they CAN'T easily be reconciled in the full monotheistic framework Kaufmann needs.

## Claim 5: "First Hosea" (chs 1-3) is pre-classical prophecy from the time of Jehoram (~850 BCE), not the 8th century

**Author's claim:** Hosea 1-3 and 4-14 are "two distinct literary units, whose authors lived at different times." Chapters 1-3 belong to the period BEFORE Jehu's purge of Baal worship (i.e., before 842 BCE), during the reign of Jehoram son of Ahab (853-842). These chapters "were composed, then, before the destruction of Ahab's dynasty." The superscription in 1:1 (Uzziah to Hezekiah) "refer[s] only to chapters 4-14."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Style**: 1-3 has "almost mantic" style with an ordered series of events and objective presentation; 4-14 is "a formless aggregation of impassioned reproof, argument, threats, pleading, and hope"
- **Content**: 1-3 deals ONLY with idolatry as national sin — "moral corruption... never figures"; 4-14 focuses on *ḥesed*, knowledge of God, and moral decay
- **Baal worship**: In 1-3, Baal worship is a PRESENT sin; in 4-14, it's a sin of the PAST (9:10; 13:1)
- **Calves**: Samaria's calves are the present sin of 4-14 but "are never mentioned in 1-3"
- **Marriage metaphor**: The marriage/adultery metaphor for God's covenant is unique to 1-3 and "does not recur" in 4-14, where different images are used (grapes, trained calf, adopted son)
- **Historical context**: "We know of only one period of Israel's history when 'days of the Baals' were publicly celebrated: the reign of Jezebel." First Hosea speaks of distinct realms — YHWH's festivals vs. "days of the Baals" (2:13, 15)
- **Textual evidence on "blood of Jezreel"**: 1:4 refers to Naboth's blood, for which Ahab's dynasty was accountable. The MT reading "house of Jehu" is corrupt; LXX reads "Judah," graphically close to "Jehoram" — read "the house of Jehoram" (son of Ahab)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's structural observations are genuinely interesting and deserve engagement. But the argument that 1-3 is pre-classical rests heavily on the "blood of Jezreel" textual emendation and on establishing that Baal worship was exclusively pre-Jehu — both contestable points.

**What's at stake:** If Hosea 1-3 is pre-classical, the marriage metaphor is NOT an 8th-century prophetic innovation — it's inherited from earlier tradition. This undermines the critical narrative of progressive theological development. It also means the critical association of Hosea's "harlotry" language with syncretistic YHWH-Baal worship is doubly wrong: First Hosea speaks of distinct gods, and Second Hosea doesn't use the metaphor at all.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus dates all of Hosea to the 8th century. The stylistic differences Kaufmann observes could reflect different genres or redactional layers within a single author's work. The superscription's dating to Jeroboam II through Hezekiah is taken as controlling.

**Alternative reading:** The differences between 1-3 and 4-14 are real but reflect a SINGLE prophet working in different modes — biographical narrative (1-3) vs. collected oracles (4-14). The shift from present to past Baal worship reflects the prophet's own timeline during a long ministry that spanned Jehu's purge. The marriage metaphor's absence in 4-14 could simply mean the metaphor served its purpose in 1-3 and wasn't reused.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's structural case is compelling — the differences between 1-3 and 4-14 ARE striking. But he needs the two sections to be by DIFFERENT AUTHORS separated by a century, which is a much heavier claim than merely noting they differ. The textual emendation of "Jehu" to "Jehoram" is clever but not decisive. On balance, Kaufmann has identified real features that need explanation, but his specific historical reconstruction goes beyond what the evidence can bear.

## Claim 6: The marriage of Hosea was theatrical representation, not biography

**Author's claim:** Gomer was not an actual adulteress and the children were not illegitimate. The entire narrative of Hosea 1-3 is "a dramatic representation" — comparable to the prophet's wounds and bandage in 1 Kings 20:35ff, Isaiah's nakedness (20:2), and Jeremiah's bands and bars (27:2; 28:10ff). Gomer plays the ROLE of a harlot, wearing cosmetics and ornaments that signify harlotry. "The 'harlotries' seem to be something material, some cosmetic or face covering, and some whorish ornament on her breast." The children were "born to Hosea legitimately, for there is no hint that Gomer played the harlot after her marriage, or that the children were not Hosea's." They are "children of harlotry" only in their dramatic role. Chapter 3 is also theatrical: Hosea is commanded to "love" (not marry) a woman who represents the nation's forlorn interval without king or cult. "Here, as in all prophetic theatrical acts, the appearance is the essence, not the objective truth."

**Evidence presented:**
- Gomer is called "a wife of harlotries" (*ʾēšet zĕnûnîm*), not "an adulterous wife"
- 2:4 says "Let her remove her harlotries from her face and her adulteries from between her breasts" — these are removable items, not behaviors
- The children's history "is complete and unambiguous and does not require imaginative reconstruction" — they were born legitimately
- The names (Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah, Lo-ammi) are symbolic in function, like all prophetic sign-acts
- Chapter 3: Hosea is not commanded to "take" (marry) but to "love" a woman — he plays the lover. She doesn't bear him children. She sits solitary apart from all men — including Hosea

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's reading is textually defensible and avoids the biographical sensationalism that has plagued Hosea interpretation. But it requires assuming the text is a script for a performance rather than a report of events, which is itself an interpretive choice.

**What's at stake:** Whether Hosea's theology of divine love was born from personal trauma (God told him to marry a prostitute who betrayed him, giving him visceral knowledge of divine heartbreak) or from deliberate symbolic action. The biographical reading makes Hosea's theology more existentially powerful; the theatrical reading makes it more intellectually constructed.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish and Christian exegesis has generally read the marriage as biographical, though some medieval commentators (Ibn Ezra, Maimonides) read it as a prophetic vision, not an actual event. Modern critics tend toward biography.

**Alternative reading:** The narrative could be a prophetic allegory composed after the fact, neither biography nor theatrical performance. It may never have been "performed" at all — it may be a literary creation designed to convey theological truth through narrative.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's theatrical reading is genuinely illuminating and more textually careful than the standard biographical reading. The detail about "harlotries" being removable items (2:4) is an excellent observation that the biographical reading struggles with. This is one of Kaufmann's better exegetical moves, independent of his larger thesis.

## Claim 7: "Second Hosea" (chs 4-14) knows nothing of syncretistic YHWH-Baal worship — the critical reading is wrong

**Author's claim:** "Baal worship is mentioned explicitly only twice (9:10; 13:1), both times as a sin of the past. The current sin of Ephraim... is that of the calves (13:2). Of a continuous historical sin of YHWH-Baal syncretism Hosea knows nothing." The "harlotry" of Hosea 4-14 is literal sexual license, NOT a metaphor for Baal worship. "The harlotry spoken of in these passages is no more symbolic than the drunkenness with which it is linked; it has nothing to do with Baal worship (a misconception which has been furthered by the failure to separate the two Hoseas)." The mention of "the Baals" in 11:2 is "no more than a derogatory reference to idol-worship in general, not to actual Baal worship."

**Evidence presented:**
- 9:10 and 13:1 both frame Baal worship in the past tense
- 13:2 identifies the CURRENT sin as the calves
- 4-14 makes "no reference at all to worship of 'other gods' as a sin of the present"
- Hosea's depiction of Israel's history: "At first Israel was loyal to YHWH; apostasy set in only when Israel became fat and full (13:6; cf. Deut. 31:20; 32:15)"
- "Hosea knows of no punishment for past periods of sin; the past furnishes him only with sporadic examples of sin: Gibeah (9:9; 10:9) and Baal Peor (9:10)"
- The harlotry condemned in 4-14 is described alongside drunkenness, linked to "a spirit of harlotry" (5:4), and associated with threshing-floor festivities (9:1ff) — it is actual sexual behavior

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann is making a characteristically bold claim: that the entire critical reading of Hosea as a witness to syncretism is built on a category error (conflating First and Second Hosea). This would be a major contribution if true. But the claim that "harlotry" in 4-14 has "nothing to do with Baal worship" is hard to square with the prophetic use of sexual imagery for idolatry throughout the Hebrew Bible (Ezekiel 16, 23; Jeremiah 2-3).

**What's at stake:** Whether Hosea 4-14 depicts a syncretistic popular religion or a morally decayed but theologically monotheistic one. If Kaufmann is right, the critical narrative of Israelite syncretism loses one of its key textual witnesses.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus reads Hosea's harlotry language as a metaphor for Baal worship, rooted in the actual sexual rites associated with Canaanite fertility cults. Day (Chapter 3) documents the reality of Baal cult in Israel. Smith traces the Yahweh-Baal conflict as a central dynamic of Israelite religion.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is partly right that the "harlotry" language has been over-read as ALWAYS referring to cultic prostitution. Some passages in Hosea DO refer to literal sexual license. But the prophetic use of marriage/adultery as a root metaphor for covenant faithfulness/ idolatry is so pervasive that it's perverse to claim Hosea 4-14 uses it only literally. The "spirit of harlotry" (5:4) that prevents knowledge of God is theological language, not a commentary on sexual mores.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is making a real and valuable point about over-reading "harlotry = Baal cult" in every instance. But he overcorrects. The prophetic tradition consistently uses sexual unfaithfulness as a metaphor for religious unfaithfulness. To claim that Second Hosea somehow doesn't participate in this — while First Hosea DOES (the marriage metaphor) — requires Kaufmann's prior conclusion that the two are separate authors. The argument is circular: they're separate because Second Hosea doesn't use the marriage metaphor, and Second Hosea doesn't use the marriage metaphor because they're separate.

## Claim 8: Hosea was the first to condemn militarism and the power state as religious-moral sins

**Author's claim:** "The first biblical author, indeed the first man in history, to condemn militarism as a religious-moral sin was Hosea." He is "the father of all Utopians who believe in the power of love to bring salvation to man. He is the first to repudiate the power state."

**Evidence presented:**
- Hosea 8:14: "Israel has forgotten its maker, and built palaces, and Judah has multiplied walled cities"
- Hosea 10:13f: "For you have trusted in your chariotry, in the multitude of your mighty men. Therefore tumult will arise among your people, and all your fortresses will be ravaged"
- Hosea 14:4 (the future confession): "We will not ride on horses nor say, 'Our god' to the work of our hands" — trust in chariotry is as idolatrous as trust in an idol
- Hosea 7:7: "All their kings have fallen, not one of them calls upon me" — the monarchy has failed
- Hosea 8:4: "They made kings, without asking me; officers, without my knowing" — kings made without prophetic sanction
- Hosea 13:10f: echoes Samuel's warning about monarchy — it was "born in sin and rebellion"
- Hosea 5:13: reliance on Assyria and Egypt for healing is breach of faith with YHWH

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann accurately characterizes Hosea's critique of militarism and politics. Whether Hosea was literally the "first man in history" to make this critique is unverifiable and somewhat triumphalist, but the observation that Hosea's anti-militarism is a distinctive contribution is sound.

**What's at stake:** The origins of the prophetic critique of state power. If Kaufmann is right, Hosea stands at the beginning of a tradition that runs through Isaiah's anti-Egyptian alliance polemic to Jeremiah's surrender advocacy.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition generally sees Hosea's political critique as part of a broader 8th-century prophetic movement reacting to Assyrian pressure. Amos also critiques the ruling class, though not militarism per se. Isaiah's anti-alliance oracles are roughly contemporary.

**Alternative reading:** Hosea's anti-militarism is less a principled philosophical position than a theopolitical stance: reliance on military power is wrong BECAUSE it displaces reliance on YHWH, not because militarism is inherently evil. The distinction matters — Hosea isn't a pacifist; he's a theocrat.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right to highlight this as a distinctive Hosean contribution, but his characterization of Hosea as "the father of all Utopians" strains the text. Hosea's vision is not utopian in the sense of imagining a transformed political order — it's a call to return to covenant faithfulness within the existing (monarchic) order, guided by prophetic word. Kaufmann's language here reflects his own theological commitments more than Hosea's.

## Claim 9: Neither Amos nor Second Hosea had universal eschatology — it was born only after them

**Author's claim:** Amos knows nothing of "the new eschatology, and the new concept of universal history." The consolation prophecy in 9:8b-15, if authentic, is "typically antique" — the remnant consists of righteous survivors (like Elijah's remnant in 1 Kings 19:18), not those chastened into repentance. "Of this, the prophecies of Amos know nothing. There is no historical connection between the judgment of Israel and that of the nations." Similarly, Second Hosea "has not a word for the nations. YHWH is, of course, the ruler and judge of all men, but the realm of his grace is Israel. Even his eschatological visions, such as we have of them, do not embrace the nations. Universal prophetic eschatology was born only after him."

**Evidence presented:**
- Amos 9:12: restored Israel repossesses nations formerly part of the Davidic empire — nothing about nations turning from idolatry
- No historical connection between judgment of Israel and judgment of nations in Amos
- The conqueror of Israel has no role in Amos's vision of salvation
- Hosea's messianic vision: God will make the people "dwell in tents" (12:10; cf. 11:11 "their houses") — restored to simplicity in the land, not a transformed world order
- Hosea 11:8ff: divine soliloquy about grace for Ephraim — entirely national
- "Universal prophetic eschatology was born only after him"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is an accurate reading of the texts. Whatever universalist impulses exist in Amos (9:7) and Hosea, neither envisions the nations participating in eschatological salvation. That development belongs to Isaiah, Jeremiah, and especially Second Isaiah.

**What's at stake:** The developmental trajectory of Israelite religion. If Amos and Hosea are pre-universalist, they represent an earlier stage of theological development. This is actually consistent with the critical model (earlier = more particularistic, later = more universalistic) — which creates an interesting tension with Kaufmann's larger thesis that monotheism was fully present from the beginning.

**Who disagrees:** No serious disagreement. Even scholars who see universalist seeds in Amos 9:7 acknowledge that full universal eschatology emerges later.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of explicit universal eschatology doesn't mean Amos and Hosea REJECTED it — they may simply not have addressed the question. Their national focus was appropriate to their audience and mission.

**My assessment:** This claim is correct and largely uncontroversial. The interesting question is WHY Kaufmann emphasizes it: he wants to show that classical prophecy developed over time (contra the flattening tendency of some critical scholarship) while simultaneously arguing that monotheism itself was static (contra the developmental model). There's a tension here — if eschatology develops, why can't monotheism develop too?

## Claim 10: The prophets established a hierarchy of value — morality is essentially godlike, cult is only symbolic

**Author's claim:** Classical prophecy "established a hierarchy of value; both cult and morality are God's command and part of his covenant, and both are expressions of 'knowledge of God.' But while the cult is sacred only as a symbol, morality is essentially godlike, being a reflection of the qualities of God." This hierarchy was possible because Israelite religion, "essentially non-mythological and non-magical, could conceive of the relation of God and the world only in terms of free grace." God is not dependent on the cult — the cult is a manifestation of HIS grace to MAN. "Hence its value is conditional, not absolute."

**Evidence presented:**
- Torah literature "does not distinguish cultic from moral prescriptions, but indiscriminately juxtaposes them"
- The prophets, "taking their departure from the tradition of Israel's election, declare that its object was to publish the 'knowledge of God' and realize his moral will"
- "Morality... is an absolute value, for it is divine in essence. The God who demands righteousness, justice, kindness, and compassion is himself just, gracious, kind, and compassionate"
- "Moral goodness makes man share, as it were, in the divine nature"
- The cult becomes "an empty and broken vessel, an abomination of YHWH" through sin — its value is conditional

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is Kaufmann at his most theological and least historical. He's constructing a systematic theology of the prophets' view of cult vs. morality that the texts themselves don't articulate in these terms. It's elegant, but it's Kaufmann's construction, not the prophets'.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophetic critique of cult reflects a THEOLOGICAL PRINCIPLE (cult is inherently conditional) or a HISTORICAL SITUATION (the cult was corrupted, so the prophets attacked it). Kaufmann needs the former to support his claim that Israelite religion was essentially different from paganism.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the prophetic cult-critique as situational rather than principled. Isaiah's vision of nations streaming to Zion (Isa 2:2-4) envisions a future WITH cult. Ezekiel's temple vision (Ezek 40-48) is entirely cultic. The prophets didn't reject cult in principle — they rejected corrupted cult.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets' hierarchy of morality over cult is not a theological innovation — it's already present in texts like 1 Samuel 15:22 ("to obey is better than sacrifice") and Psalm 51:16-17 ("you do not delight in sacrifice... a broken spirit"). The prophets are drawing on existing wisdom, not creating a new theological framework.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's framework is theologically rich but historically thin. He's systematizing what the prophets do into a coherent "hierarchy of value" that may be more Kaufmann than Amos or Hosea. The claim that Israelite religion was "essentially non-magical" is particularly problematic — the prophets' very critique of cult-as-magic implies that many Israelites DID treat cult magically. Kaufmann attributes to "the Israelite idea" what may have been the prophetic minority position.

## Chapter XI Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Amos inherited monotheism, didn't create it | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptive); LOW (proves early monotheism) | Inheriting tradition ≠ tradition was monotheistic from start |
| 2: Social morality as national destiny = Amos's innovation | MEDIUM-HIGH | Absence of evidence in prior literature may be preservation bias |
| 3: Amos's cult-critique was conditional, not wholesale | MEDIUM-HIGH | Relatively uncontroversial |
| 4: Amos 9:7 doesn't undermine monotheism | LOW | Text most naturally reads as henotheistic; "hyperbole" is special pleading |
| 5: First Hosea is pre-classical (~850 BCE) | MEDIUM | Rests on textual emendation; stylistic differences may reflect genre, not authorship |
| 6: Hosea's marriage was theatrical, not biographical | MEDIUM | Textually defensible; assumes performance frame |
| 7: Second Hosea knows nothing of syncretism | LOW-MEDIUM | Prophetic harlotry = idolatry metaphor is pervasive; circular argument |
| 8: Hosea first to condemn militarism | MEDIUM | Claim of "first in history" is triumphalist and unverifiable |
| 9: No universal eschatology in Amos or Hosea | MEDIUM-HIGH | Largely uncontroversial |
| 10: Prophetic hierarchy: morality godlike, cult symbolic | LOW-MEDIUM | Kaufmann's systematic theology, not the prophets' own framework |

**Strongest section:** The analysis of Amos's innovation in making social morality nationally decisive (Claim 2). This is a genuinely sharp observation that holds up regardless of one's view on Kaufmann's larger thesis. The theatrical reading of Hosea's marriage (Claim 6) is also textually careful and illuminating.

**Weakest section:** The treatment of Amos 9:7 (Claim 4). Kaufmann's need to read this as "satirical hyperbole" betrays the strain his monotheism-from-the-beginning thesis places on the text. A more honest reading would acknowledge that Amos's theology is henotheistic — YHWH is supreme over all nations but not (yet) conceived as the ONLY god.

**Most radical move:** The division of Hosea into First (pre-classical, ~850) and Second (classical, ~730). If Kaufmann is right, this would require a complete re-dating of Hosea 1-3 and would remove a century of critical scholarship's key witness to 8th-century syncretism. The evidence he marshals is genuinely interesting, but the argument is not decisive.

**Methodological note:** This chapter exemplifies both the strengths and weaknesses of Kaufmann's method. His close reading often yields genuine insights (Amos's innovation, the theatrical marriage, the structure of Hosea). But his conclusions are always in service of his master thesis (monotheism is original, the Torah is pre-prophetic), and he never seriously entertains the possibility that the text might support a developmental model. His treatment of Amos 9:7 is the clearest case of thesis-driven distortion. Kaufmann reads the prophets as he wants them to be — traditionalists calling Israel back to a monotheism it always had — and when the text resists, he reaches for "hyperbole" or textual emendation.

**For the Truth project:** Kaufmann's reading of Amos and Hosea is essential for understanding the maximalist case for early monotheism. His claim that the prophets were NOT innovators but traditionalists is the linchpin of his entire reconstruction. If he's right about Amos and Hosea, the critical model of gradual monotheistic development loses its 8th-century starting point. If he's wrong — and Amos 9:7 strongly suggests he is — then the prophets remain the key agents of monotheistic development, even if they built on inherited traditions. The cross-reference to [[Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin and Moses]] is particularly important here: Römer traces monotheism's gradual emergence, and Amos 9:7 fits naturally into that trajectory in a way it doesn't fit Kaufmann's.
