# Lewis — Chapter 6: The Origin of Yahweh

This is the book's central chapter. Lewis examines the meaning, pronunciation, and extra-biblical attestation of the name Yahweh, then surveys the geographic origins debate — northern (Canaanite, via El) vs. southern (Midianite/Kenite). His conclusion is appropriately agnostic: we don't know the historical origin, but the cumulative weight of archaic poetry and the Shasu texts points south. Yahweh was then clothed in northern Canaanite cultural garments (storm/warrior imagery, El's epithets, Ugaritic literary conventions) to become Israel's national deity.

---

## Claim 1: The pronunciation "Yahweh" is well-established — not a scholarly guess, despite what textbooks say

**Lewis's claim:** Statements that the pronunciation is "a scholarly guess" (Thompson), "uncertain" (Baker), or "unknown" (Grabbe) are "overly circumspect and reflect a long-standing agnosticism born out of the post-rabbinic tradition that one ought not to pronounce the divine name at all. In contrast, historians of Israelite religion have confidently vocalized yhwh as Yahweh for years, and rightly so."

**Evidence:** (1) The abbreviated form Yah occurs independently (Exod 15:2; Ps 68:5) and in theophoric names (Nērî-yāh, Nĕtan-yāh) and hallelujah — establishing the first vowel as a-class. (2) The second vowel is i-class due to regular formation of third-he verbs in Hebrew. (3) Amorite personal names show yaqtil forms: yawi-ila, yahwi-Dagan. (4) Greek transcriptions give Iaoue and Iabe. (5) "Jehovah" is an artificial hybrid — the consonants of YHWH pointed with the vowels of ʾădōnay, never intended to be pronounced. The Masoretes were creating a reading indicator, not a new name.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is a settled philological conclusion that introductory textbooks have been slow to adopt.

**What's at stake:** Surprisingly little for religion — but a lot for scholarly credibility. The persistence of "Jehovah" in popular discourse and the agnosticism about "Yahweh" in textbooks reflects the gap between specialist knowledge and public understanding.

**Who disagrees:** Jehovah's Witnesses and other groups that use "Jehovah" as the divine name. Some scholars maintain agnosticism as a gesture of respect for Jewish tradition.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right, and this is one of those cases where scholarly consensus is much clearer than textbooks suggest. The evidence is multi-stranded and convergent.

## Claim 2: The meaning of Yahweh relates to "being" and "active presence" — the Exodus 3:14 wordplay is the best guide, and the Albright-Cross-Freedman causative theory ("he who causes to be" = creator) is elegant but ultimately unconvincing

**Lewis's claim:** The consensus that YHWH represents a verbal form of HYH "to be" is well-founded. The Exodus 3:14 wordplay (ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh) is "irrevocably" tied to the name. But rather than abstract existence ("He Who Is" in Greek metaphysical terms), the primary meaning is "active, helping presence" — God WILL BE with his people. The non-causative (Qal) stem is preferred because the causative never occurs in Hebrew for this root despite 3,561 attestations.

**Evidence against the causative theory:** (1) The verb HYH "to be" never occurs in Hebrew as a causative despite massive statistical sampling (Mettinger: 3,561 attestations, "not a single example in the causative stem"). (2) HYH is never explicitly used for creation — Hebrew uses BRʾ, ʿŚH, YṢR instead. (3) The grammatical argument (that a proper name can't be in construct state, so "Yahweh of Hosts" must be a sentence name) is weakened by Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions showing YHWH used in construct with place names (Yahweh of Samaria, Yahweh of Teman). (4) The theory requires rewriting Exodus 3:14 to a causative vocalization, ignoring the obvious wordplay. (5) The Arabic "blow/fell" etymology has the same problem — it can't be reconciled with Exodus 3:14 and the verb isn't productive in Hebrew.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the name relates to HYH "to be." MEDIUM-HIGH that the non-causative is correct vs. the causative. The Exodus 3:14 wordplay is the single best interpretive key.

**What's at stake:** If the causative theory were right, Yahweh's name means "creator" and the deity originated as an epithet of El — making Yahweh originally a northern/Canaanite figure. If the non-causative is right, Yahweh's name means "the one who is/will be present" — fitting a deity of active, personal presence, which could originate anywhere, including the south.

**Who disagrees:** Cross, Freedman, and their students (including many of Lewis's own teachers) maintain the causative theory. It has genuine elegance — explaining Yahweh Sebaoth as a sentence name and tying everything to El. Lewis's rejection of it is a notable departure from the Cross school.

**Alternative reading:** Both meanings may have coexisted in different communities. The biblical tradition (Exod 3:14) preserves the non-causative understanding, but a pre-biblical causative understanding may have existed among groups who first formulated the name.

**My assessment:** Lewis makes a strong case against the causative. The statistical argument (no causative of HYH in Hebrew) is devastating. The Kuntillet ʿAjrud counter-evidence to Cross's grammatical claim is also strong — proper names CAN be in construct. I think Lewis is right to prefer the non-causative, but I suspect the question will remain debated because the causative theory is so elegant.

## Claim 3: P's claim that the name Yahweh was revealed only at Moses' time (Exod 6:3) is more historically reliable than J's claim of antediluvian Yahwism (Gen 4:26)

**Lewis's claim:** J's "Reuel tradition" (Exod 2) has Moses' father-in-law bearing an El name (Reuel = "friend of El") and being a priest of Midian — suggesting he worshipped El, not Yahweh. P and E both agree that the name Yahweh was revealed at Moses' time. The preponderance of El names in patriarchal narratives (and absence of Yahweh names) supports this. The ninety occurrences of Yahweh in patriarchal narratives are "anachronistic" — the result of later editors retrojecting Yahweh worship backward.

**Evidence:** The Ishmael wordplay in Gen 16:11 is the clearest proof: "You shall call his name Ishmael [yišmāʿ-ʾēl, 'May El hear'] because Yahweh has heard your cry." The name presupposes El, yet the explanation uses Yahweh — a telltale sign of editorial updating. Similarly, Abram builds an altar to Yahweh at Bethel (bêt-ʾēl, "house of El"). P's careful structure (Elohim → El Shadday → Yahweh, with three eternal covenants) gives theological motivation but also preserves genuine historical memory.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the patriarchal narratives contain editorial retrojection. MEDIUM-HIGH that the name was first revealed at Moses' time — this depends on the historicity of Moses, which is itself debated.

**What's at stake:** The entire chronology of Israelite religion. If Yahweh was unknown to the patriarchs, then Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob worshipped El — making El the original god of Israel and Yahweh a later arrival. This aligns with Chapter 4's conclusions.

**Who disagrees:** Conservative scholars who accept J's chronology at face value. Heiser would argue that El and Yahweh were always the same deity, so the name change is merely progressive revelation, not the arrival of a new god.

**Alternative reading:** P's scheme could be entirely theological — a way to structure covenantal history — with no historical basis. But the cumulative evidence (El names, no Yahweh theophorics in patriarchal narratives, the Ishmael wordplay) suggests otherwise.

**My assessment:** This is standard critical scholarship, well-argued by Lewis. The Ishmael wordplay is the smoking gun — it's hard to explain as anything other than editorial updating of an El tradition.

## Claim 4: The Shasu texts (Egyptian, 14th-13th C BCE) provide the earliest possible attestation of Yahweh — but they're toponyms, not theonyms, and the connection to Seir is the crucial link

**Lewis's claim:** Egyptian geographical lists from Amenophis III and Ramses II refer to "the land of the Shasu of Yhw" (t3 š3sw yhw). "The Shasu are tantalizing for historians of Israelite religion due to their mention in conjunction with the term yh(w)/yhw3, which seems to be a toponym rather than a theonym." But "in the ancient Near East many anthroponyms could serve as toponyms" (Astour). The connection to Seir (š3sw sʿrr) ties these texts to archaic biblical poetry where Yahweh marches from Seir/Edom.

**Evidence:** Papyrus Harris I and Papyrus Anastasi VI both connect Shasu with Seir and Edom. Redford argues the skepticism about equating sʿrr with Seir is "wholly unwarranted." Schneider's Book of the Dead name ("My lord is the shepherd of Yah") provides a possible theophoric attestation from the same period. Ben-Yosef et al. connect Shasu to copper production at Faynan.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the Yhw toponym referring to the deity Yahweh. HIGH on the Seir connection. MEDIUM on Schneider's "shepherd of Yah" reading — it's intriguing but relies on a single name in a single manuscript.

**What's at stake:** If the Shasu texts reference Yahweh, the deity predates Israel by centuries and originated among nomadic peoples south/southeast of the Dead Sea. This would be the strongest evidence for southern origins.

**Who disagrees:** Weippert, Astour, Ahlström, and de Moor have argued sʿrr is not Seir, and Yhw should be located in Lebanon/Syria. These are minority positions.

**My assessment:** The Shasu texts are genuinely exciting and Lewis handles them well — not overclaiming, but recognizing their significance. The convergence with biblical poetry (Yahweh from Seir/Edom/Teman) is the strongest argument. If the toponym and the poetry both point south, that's unlikely to be coincidence.

## Claim 5: The Midianite/Kenite hypothesis — that Moses learned Yahweh from his Midianite father-in-law — has genuine strengths but the biblical texts don't actually support it in the way proponents claim

**Lewis's claim:** The hypothesis "asserts that Moses first learned of Yahweh through contact with his father-in-law Jethro, who was a priest in Midian." But "nowhere is there any explicit reference to Jethro being 'a priest of Yahweh' even in the three explicit references to his priestly office." A straightforward reading of Exodus 18:8 suggests "Moses, as the evangelist, helped bring about Jethro's conversion to Yahwism." J's "Reuel tradition" has Moses' father-in-law bearing an El name — suggesting he worshipped El, not Yahweh.

**Evidence:** Exodus 18:11's recognition formula (ʿattâ — "now, as a result") implies Jethro learned about Yahweh FROM Moses, not the reverse. The name Reuel means "friend of El." The E tradition (Exod 3:16) has Yahweh identifying himself as "the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob" — which would be strange if the real origin of Yahwism was Midianite. Moses' mother's name Yochebed contains a Yahwistic element. Yet the archaic poetry (Judg 5, Deut 33, Hab 3) consistently places Yahweh in southern locales, and Jethro does offer sacrifices to Yahweh in Exodus 18:12, which "can hardly be the action of a new initiate."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the hypothesis overall. HIGH that the biblical texts don't unambiguously support it. The strongest evidence is the geographic connection between Yahweh and southern locales, not the Jethro narrative.

**What's at stake:** Where Yahweh came from. If the Midianite hypothesis is right, Yahweh is a foreign import — the god of a neighboring people adopted by Israel. If wrong, we have no good explanation for the southern poetry.

**Who disagrees:** Cross (reformulated version via Reuben), Schloen (caravan hypothesis), van der Toorn (Kenites introduced Yahweh to Saul). Against: Meek (1950) and those who see Moses as the innovator.

**Alternative reading:** Both things could be true — Midianite clans worshipped a deity called Yahweh, but Moses (an Israelite by birth, per J) reformulated the cult around liberation from Egypt, creating something genuinely new. The tradition preserves both the foreign origin (southern poetry) and the Mosaic innovation (Exod 3, 6).

**My assessment:** Lewis's treatment is masterfully balanced. He presents the evidence for and against without forcing a conclusion. The most honest position is the one he takes: we don't know, but the southern connection is the strongest lead.

## Claim 6: Yahweh was a southern storm/warrior deity who was subsequently clothed in northern Canaanite cultural garments — the merger of southern origins and northern traditions produced the Yahweh of the Hebrew Bible

**Lewis's claim:** "Yahweh's origin seems to be southern, yet many of the garments put onto Yahweh are sewn from northern and inland Canaanite cloth." The complete absence of Yahweh from Ugaritic texts despite hundreds of deities is the strongest argument against northern origins. Yet Yahweh fights the same foes as Baal (Yam, Leviathan, Mot), rides the clouds like Baal, and inherits El's epithets and divine council. "We simply do not know the social settings and mechanisms by which the widespread pre-Israelite Canaanite lore... was woven on to the god Yahweh of southern, southeastern, and Transjordanian fame."

**Evidence:** Northern elements: Ugaritic combat myths (Baal vs. Yam/Leviathan → Yahweh vs. Sea/Leviathan), El's epithets (Elyon, Olam, Shadday, Creator), divine council, sacrificial vocabulary, poetic conventions, stock type-scenes. Southern elements: archaic poetry (Judg 5:4-5, Deut 33:2, Hab 3:3-7, Ps 68:8-9) consistently locating Yahweh in Seir/Edom/Teman/Paran/Sinai/Midian; Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions mentioning "Yahweh of Teman/the Southland"; Shasu texts; Cross's Reuben hypothesis.

**Confidence:** HIGH that both northern and southern elements are present. LOW on the specific mechanisms of merger. This is the Propp Principle's domain — we have perhaps 5% of the data.

**What's at stake:** The entire nature of Israelite religion. If Yahweh is essentially a southern storm god who absorbed Canaanite mythology, Israelite religion is a creative synthesis, not a unique revelation. The distinctiveness is in the CONFIGURATION (southern warrior + Canaanite high god + ethical demands + aniconism), not in any single element.

**Who disagrees:** Northern-origin-only scholars (Leuenberger: Yahweh "belongs to the North Levantine-Syriac type of the kingly weather-god Adad-Baal"). Kaufmann and confessional scholars who reject the comparative framework entirely.

**Alternative reading:** The southern poetry could be a late literary fiction — a "southernizing" of Yahweh to connect him to Sinai traditions. But Lewis argues this is unlikely given the persistence of Sinai in the face of Zion theology's tendency to absorb everything.

**My assessment:** This is where Lewis lands and it's the most defensible position. The evidence for southern origins (archaic poetry + Shasu + Kuntillet ʿAjrud) is strong. The evidence for northern cultural clothing (Ugaritic parallels) is overwhelming. The mystery is how they merged — and that may be permanently lost.

## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| "Yahweh" pronunciation is well-established | VERY HIGH | Only those deferring to Jewish tradition |
| Name meaning = HYH "to be"; non-causative preferred | HIGH | Causative theory has genuine elegance; debate continues |
| P's chronology (Mosaic revelation) more reliable than J's | HIGH | Depends on historicity of Moses |
| Shasu texts = earliest Yahweh attestation | MEDIUM | Toponym vs. theonym ambiguity; Seir equation debated |
| Midianite hypothesis: strengths but biblical texts don't support | MEDIUM | Could be both Mosaic AND Midianite input |
| Yahweh = southern god in northern clothes | HIGH | Mechanism of merger unknown; Propp Principle applies |

**Strongest sections:** The treatment of Kuntillet ʿAjrud (the most detailed and current synthesis I've seen); the critique of the causative etymology (statistically devastating); the balanced assessment of the Midianite hypothesis (neither credulous nor dismissive).

**Weakest section:** The Aramean religion discussion, while interesting, feels like a detour. It's more relevant for understanding northern influences on Israelite religion generally than for the specific question of Yahweh's origin.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Yahweh's name and meaning can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence
- The earliest extra-biblical attestation may be 14th C BCE (Shasu), but the earliest certain one is 9th C BCE (Mesha)
- Southern origins are the strongest lead, but the full picture requires both southern and northern elements
- The process of merger is unknown — this is the great unsolved problem
- Ethnic self-identification was crucial: Israel chose Yahweh just as Moab chose Chemosh, Edom chose Qos
- Lewis is epistemologically humble: he refuses to overclaim where the data is thin

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the natural companion to Römer's Chapters 2-3 (geographic origin, Moses and Midianites), Cross's Part I (which argues the opposite — that Yahweh emerged from El's cult), and Smith's *Origins* (which covers similar ground with more emphasis on the Ugaritic background). Lewis's agnosticism contrasts with both Cross's confidence and Römer's narrative coherence. Heiser is the absent interlocutor — his divine council reading of Deut 32:8-9 and his insistence that El = Yahweh from the start is never directly engaged, though Lewis's entire framework implicitly refutes it.
