# Lewis — Chapter 6: The Origin of Yahweh

This is the book's central chapter. Lewis examines the meaning, pronunciation, and extra-biblical attestation of the name Yahweh, then surveys the geographic origins debate — northern (Canaanite, via El) vs. southern (Midianite/Kenite). His conclusion is appropriately agnostic: we don't know the historical origin, but the cumulative weight of archaic poetry and the Shasu texts points south. Yahweh was then clothed in northern Canaanite cultural garments (storm/warrior imagery, El's epithets, Ugaritic literary conventions) to become Israel's national deity.

## Claim 4: Shasu texts (14th-13th C BCE) are the earliest possible Yahweh attestation — Seir link is crucial
→ [[claim-shasu-yhw-texts-earliest-yahweh-attestation-seir-toponym]] | **lewis-ocg-6.4** | Confidence: medium
  Egyptian "land of the Shasu of Yhw" (t3 š3sw yhw) is a toponym, not a theonym, but toponyms could derive from divine names. The Seir connection ties them to archaic biblical poetry. Convergence with Judg 5, Deut 33, Hab 3 makes coincidence unlikely.

## Claim 5: Midianite/Kenite hypothesis has strengths but biblical texts don't unambiguously support it
→ [[claim-midianite-kenite-hypothesis-strengths-biblical-texts-dont-support]] | **lewis-ocg-6.5** | Confidence: medium
  Nowhere is Jethro called "a priest of Yahweh" — Exodus 18:8 suggests Moses evangelized Jethro, not the reverse. Reuel means "friend of El." Yet archaic poetry consistently places Yahweh in southern locales, and Jethro's sacrifice (Exod 18:12) is not the action of a new initiate. The strongest evidence is geographic, not narrative.

## Claim 6: Yahweh was a southern storm/warrior deity clothed in northern Canaanite cultural garments
→ [[claim-yahweh-southern-warrior-clothed-northern-canaanite-garments]] | **lewis-ocg-6.6** | Confidence: high
  Complete absence of Yahweh from Ugaritic texts is the strongest argument against northern origins. Yet Yahweh fights Baal's foes, rides Baal's clouds, inherits El's epithets. ## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| "Yahweh" pronunciation is well-established | VERY HIGH | Only those deferring to Jewish tradition |
| Name meaning = HYH "to be"; non-causative preferred | HIGH | Causative theory has genuine elegance; debate continues |
| P's chronology (Mosaic revelation) more reliable than J's | HIGH | Depends on historicity of Moses |
| Shasu texts = earliest Yahweh attestation | MEDIUM | Toponym vs. theonym ambiguity; Seir equation debated |
| Midianite hypothesis: strengths but biblical texts don't support | MEDIUM | Could be both Mosaic AND Midianite input |
| Yahweh = southern god in northern clothes | HIGH | Mechanism of merger unknown; Propp Principle applies |

**Strongest sections:** The treatment of Kuntillet ʿAjrud (the most detailed and current synthesis I've seen); the critique of the causative etymology (statistically devastating); the balanced assessment of the Midianite hypothesis (neither credulous nor dismissive).

**Weakest section:** The Aramean religion discussion, while interesting, feels like a detour. It's more relevant for understanding northern influences on Israelite religion generally than for the specific question of Yahweh's origin.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Yahweh's name and meaning can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence
- The earliest extra-biblical attestation may be 14th C BCE (Shasu), but the earliest certain one is 9th C BCE (Mesha)
- Southern origins are the strongest lead, but the full picture requires both southern and northern elements
- The process of merger is unknown — this is the great unsolved problem
- Ethnic self-identification was crucial: Israel chose Yahweh just as Moab chose Chemosh, Edom chose Qos
- Lewis is epistemologically humble: he refuses to overclaim where the data is thin

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the natural companion to Römer's Chapters 2-3 (geographic origin, Moses and Midianites), Cross's Part I (which argues the opposite — that Yahweh emerged from El's cult), and Smith's *Origins* (which covers similar ground with more emphasis on the Ugaritic background). Lewis's agnosticism contrasts with both Cross's confidence and Römer's narrative coherence. Heiser is the absent interlocutor — his divine council reading of Deut 32:8-9 and his insistence that El = Yahweh from the start is never directly engaged, though Lewis's entire framework implicitly refutes it.
