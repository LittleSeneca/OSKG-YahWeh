# Tigay — Chapter I: The Onomastic Evidence

## THE BIBLICAL DATA

### Claim: Biblical personal names are overwhelmingly Yahwistic

**Tigay's data:** Of 466 individuals with theophoric names in the Bible (excluding ambiguous 'el/'eli names), 413 (89%) bear Yahwistic names, only 53 (11%) bear clearly or plausibly pagan names. The pagan names cluster in the early periods (Judges/United Monarchy: 14%); by the Divided Monarchy they drop to 3%.

| Period | Theo. Names | Yahwistic | Pagan |
|--------|------------|-----------|-------|
| Patriarchal | 3 | 0 (0%) | 3 (100%) |
| Exodus-Conquest | 6 | 3 (50%) | 3 (50%) |
| Judges-United Mon. | 163 | 140 (86%) | 23 (14%) |
| Divided Monarchy | 127 | 123 (97%) | 4 (3%) |
| Late Judah | 97 | 92 (95%) | 5 (5%) |
| **Total** | **466** | **413 (89%)** | **53 (11%)** |

**Tigay's honesty:** He counted ba'al names among the "plausibly pagan" to give the case for pagan names "every reasonable benefit of the doubt." The real number is probably lower.

### The Ba'al Names Problem

The 8 individuals with ba'al names (7 from Judges/United Monarchy, 1 later) underwent deliberate scribal revision: Eshbaal → Ishbosheth ("man of Baal" → "man of shame"); Beeliada → Eliada; Meribaal → Mephibosheth. These names prove the scribes were sensitive to pagan theophorics and changed them when found. But — crucially — the changes also prove "that the editors did not suppress all pagan names." Those that remain (like Eshbaal in Chronicles) suggest "the essentially non-polytheistic onomastic picture given by the Bible all the way back to the beginning of the Divided Monarchy, and perhaps earlier, is realistic."

### The Verbal Elements

Even non-theophoric elements show YHWH-devotion. The most common predicates in Israelite names are verbs meaning "he gave" (ntn), "he helped" ('zr), "he created" (br'), "he heard" (šm'), "he remembered" (zkr) — all directed toward YHWH.

## THE EPIGRAPHIC DATA

### Claim: Hebrew inscriptions show even stronger YHWH-dominance than the Bible

**Methodology:** Tigay collected ALL theophoric personal names from pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions (seals, ostraca, bullae, jar handles, letters). His Appendices A-E list them exhaustively.

**The numbers** (across ALL inscriptions, 8th-6th centuries):

- **Yahwistic names:** Overwhelming majority. Tigay's Appendix A runs for dozens of pages listing names ending in -yhw, -yh, beginning with yhw-.
- **Plausibly pagan names:** Tigay found only a handful. Appendix B lists them — and for each, he notes alternative (non-pagan) interpretations.

**The key finding:** The epigraphic onomasticon is MORE Yahwistic than the Bible's onomasticon. The Bible preserved a FEW pagan names through editorial oversight. The inscriptions preserve even fewer because there was no editorial process to filter. "The epigraphic evidence, with its lack of a comparable Tendenz, argues against attributing any statistical significance to those [biblical] indictments."

### The Counter-Arguments Tigay Anticipates

1. **"Names don't reflect belief"** — Tigay: In Northwest Semitic culture, names explicitly invoke deities. "The divine name within Hebrew personal names could not have gone unrecognized." Biblical characters comment on name meanings. Scribes changed pagan names precisely because the meanings mattered.

2. **"Names only reflect parents, not bearers"** — Tigay acknowledges this. But parents choose names that reflect THEIR religious loyalties. Multi-generational naming patterns reveal community norms.

3. **"Single names don't prove monotheism"** — Tigay agrees. "In dealing with the question of how many deities were worshipped within a particular group we must consider the total onomastic picture of that group, not the names of a few individuals." That's what he does.

4. **"Formal theophorics ≠ lived religion"** — Tigay: Names express "simple and elemental" beliefs — thanks, hope, submission. "Even if the names of a particular society should reflect the predominance of a single deity to the total exclusion of all others, this would tell us only that members of that society did not expect from other gods the kinds of actions that are mentioned in personal names, and perhaps that they did not worship other gods." But — crucially — "the absence of other gods from the onomasticon would not by itself tell us whether that society denied the existence or divinity of those gods."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the data (the name counts are publicly verifiable). HIGH that the epigraphic record is overwhelmingly Yahwistic. MEDIUM on what this PROVES about belief (Tigay himself is careful about this).

**What's at stake:** This is the direct empirical challenge to the consensus. "After the United Monarchy, and perhaps even earlier, the evidence currently available makes it very difficult to suppose that many Israelites worshipped gods other than YHWH." If true, Smith/Römer/Dever are wrong about the prevalence of popular polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** Smith's Introduction rebuts Tigay by arguing: (a) Ugarit had few Asherah-names despite Asherah worship (the "Ugarit parallel"); (b) Punic culture had few Tannit-names despite Tannit's popularity; (c) personal names may reflect elite fashion, not popular religion.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data could be right about ONOMASTICS without being right about RELIGION. The inscriptions come disproportionately from administrative/military contexts (seals, letters, ostraca from forts) — the very circles most likely to be Yahwistic. Women and rural peasants are underrepresented. The figurines (Dever's evidence) are a different dataset from the names (Tigay's evidence). Both could be right: the names show what people SAID (YHWH-devotion); the figurines show what people DID (goddess worship).

**My assessment:** Tigay's evidence is strong and his methodological caution is admirable. His conclusion — polytheism was limited, not rampant — is well-supported by HIS data. The question is whether his data (names and inscriptions) captures the FULL picture of Israelite religion, or only the official/elite/literate dimension. Dever's figurines tell a different story. They're not contradictory — they're complementary. One shows the public face. The other shows the private practice.
