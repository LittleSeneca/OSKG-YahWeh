# Tigay — Chapter I: The Onomastic Evidence

## THE BIBLICAL DATA

### Claim 1: Biblical personal names are overwhelmingly Yahwistic
→ [[claim-biblical-theophoric-names-89-percent-yahwistic]] | **tigay-nog-1.1** | Confidence: HIGH
  89% of biblical theophoric names are Yahwistic; pagan names drop from 14% (Judges/United Monarchy) to 3% (Divided Monarchy). Supports tigay-nog-1.3, challenged by Day ([[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]]) and Smith ([[claim-tigay-onomastics-does-not-disprove-polytheism]]).

Tigay's data: Of 466 individuals with theophoric names, 413 (89%) Yahwistic, 53 (11%) pagan. Ba'al names underwent scribal revision (Eshbaal→Ishbosheth) but not all were changed — the surviving pagan names suggest "the essentially non-polytheistic onomastic picture given by the Bible all the way back to the beginning of the Divided Monarchy, and perhaps earlier, is realistic."

## THE EPIGRAPHIC DATA

### Claim 2: Hebrew inscriptions show even stronger YHWH-dominance than the Bible
→ [[claim-epigraphic-onomasticon-more-yahwistic-than-biblical]] | **tigay-nog-1.2** | Confidence: HIGH
  The epigraphic onomasticon (seals, ostraca, bullae, jar handles, letters) is MORE Yahwistic than the Bible. The Bible preserved a few pagan names through editorial oversight; the inscriptions preserve even fewer. Depends on tigay-nog-intro.1 and tigay-nog-1.1. Challenged by Day, Smith, and Lewis/Albertz.

Tigay collected ALL theophoric personal names from pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions. Tigay's Appendices A-E list Yahwistic names exhaustively; Appendix B lists only a handful of plausibly pagan names with alternative interpretations.

### Claim 3: Post-United Monarchy polytheism was limited
→ [[claim-post-united-monarchy-polytheism-limited-not-rampant]] | **tigay-nog-1.3** | Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH
  "After the United Monarchy, and perhaps even earlier, the evidence currently available makes it very difficult to suppose that many Israelites worshipped gods other than YHWH." Depends on tigay-nog-1.1 and 1.2. Contradicts Dever ([[claim-dever-folk-polytheism-outlasted-monarchy-monotheism-book-religion]]).

### Claim 4: Onomastic evidence has inherent limits for proving belief
→ [[claim-onomastic-evidence-cannot-prove-exclusive-monotheism]] | **tigay-nog-1.4** | Confidence: HIGH
  "The absence of other gods from the onomasticon would not by itself tell us whether that society denied the existence or divinity of those gods." Tigay's own caveat — his data proves monolatry, not monotheism. Supports Day's critique and Albertz's methodology.

**Tigay's honesty:** He counted ba'al names among the "plausibly pagan" to give the case for pagan names "every reasonable benefit of the doubt." The real number is probably lower.

**The Counter-Arguments Tigay Anticipates:**
1. "Names don't reflect belief" — In Northwest Semitic culture, names explicitly invoke deities
2. "Names only reflect parents" — Parents choose names reflecting their religious loyalties
3. "Single names don't prove monotheism" — Tigay agrees; he considers the TOTAL picture
4. "Formal theophorics ≠ lived religion" — Tigay explicitly acknowledges the limits

**Confidence:** HIGH on the data (the name counts are publicly verifiable). HIGH that the epigraphic record is overwhelmingly Yahwistic. MEDIUM on what this PROVES about belief (Tigay himself is careful about this).

**What's at stake:** This is the direct empirical challenge to the consensus. If Tigay is right, Smith/Römer/Dever are wrong about the prevalence of popular polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** Smith ([[claim-tigay-onomastics-does-not-disprove-polytheism]]), Day ([[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]])

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data could be right about ONOMASTICS without being right about RELIGION. The inscriptions come disproportionately from administrative/military contexts. Dever's figurines are a different dataset. Both could be right: the names show what people SAID (YHWH-devotion); the figurines show what people DID (goddess worship).

**My assessment:** Tigay's evidence is strong and his methodological caution is admirable. His conclusion — polytheism was limited, not rampant — is well-supported by HIS data. The question is whether his data captures the FULL picture of Israelite religion, or only the official/elite/literate dimension. Dever's figurines tell a different story. They're not contradictory — they're complementary. One shows the public face. The other shows the private practice.
