# Albertz — Chapter 6: A Prospect on the History of Religion in the Hellenistic Period

Albertz treats the Hellenistic period (332-64 BCE) as a transitional era — continuing the Persian-period dialectic of splintering and integration, while introducing new developments: the formation of a true hierocracy (high priest as political leader), the completion of the canonization process (Prophets, writings), the rise of Torah piety as a distinctive Jewish personal theology, and the emergence of apocalyptic as a theology of resistance from marginalized groups. He explicitly limits himself to developments visible within the Old Testament writings, referring readers to histories of Judaism for fuller treatment.

---

## Claim 1: The Hellenistic period saw the formation of a genuine hierocracy — with the high priest becoming political leader, provoking aristocratic power struggles

**Albertz's claim:** "Only in the Hellenistic period did a hierocracy or theocracy form, which, if one ignores the sharp economic control exercised by Ptolemaean government, could give the Judaeans the feeling that they had regained something of the splendour of the former monarchy." This corrects his earlier claim (Ch 5) that post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy during the Persian period.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Ptolemies refrained from installing a governor in Judaea — the high priest became prostates (political representative to the king)
- The high priest extended his leadership from the priestly college to the council of elders (gerousia), assuming political leadership
- He controlled the temple treasury — giving fiscal autonomy (though supervised by Ptolemies)
- Sirach 50.1-4 praises the high priest Simon II for his building projects and political leadership
- However, this power concentration provoked resistance: the Tobiad Joseph challenged the prostasia of Onias II (mid-3rd century), and later (175 BCE) Jason and Menelaus bought the high priesthood from Antiochus IV
- "A hereditary office of rule, restricted to the Zadokide line, stood in the way of the economic and power interests of the aristocracy; if it could not be completely abolished, then they could degrade it into a venial state office"
- The social rift continued and deepened: Hecataeus of Abdera (c. 300 BCE) notes land purchases and exploitation; Koheleth and Sirach report oppression of the poor; debt slavery became so threatening the Ptolemies enacted protective laws
- The Ptolemaean tax-leasing system enriched aristocrats further — the Tobiad Joseph's 22-year tax lease is the prime example

**Confidence:** HIGH for the institutional shift (high priest as political leader is well-attested). MEDIUM for the specific causal mechanism (absence of governor → priestly power). The general social-economic analysis is solid.

**What's at stake:** Albertz is correcting his own earlier correction. In Chapter 5 he insisted post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy. Here he says it BECAME one in the Hellenistic period. This shows that "theocracy" isn't a uniform condition — it's a historical development driven by specific political conditions (Ptolemaic administrative policy). The subsequent Hasmonean state (combining high priesthood and kingship) has roots here.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see a continuous theocracy from the Persian period. Those who dispute the dating of the high priest's political powers.

**Alternative reading:** The high priest always exercised political leadership; the change under the Ptolemies was only in the absence of a separate governor — more continuity than Albertz allows.

**My assessment:** Albertz's distinction between Persian-period "not a theocracy" and Hellenistic "hierocracy" is nuanced and well-argued. The Ptolemaic decision not to appoint a governor is the key variable. The Tobiad romance (Josephus, Antt. XII, 160-236) provides vivid if tendentious evidence for aristocratic challenge to priestly power. The tax-leasing system as a mechanism for deepening social conflict is an important insight.

## Claim 2 [ADDED IN RETROSPECTIVE]: The Hellenistic period deepened the social crisis through Ptolemaic tax-leasing, adding a cultural split (Hellenizers vs. traditionalists) to the existing class conflict

**Albertz's claim:** "Social tensions were further intensified in comparison with the Persian period because a cultural split was now added to the social split." The Ptolemaean economic system "not only deepened the social rift in the community of Judah as compared with Persian times but also stoked up considerable aggression in the lower class against those aristocrats who... allowed themselves to be drawn right into the oppressive system of the foreign power."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Ptolemies imposed a centralistic economic administration — "their aim was to derive as high a profit as possible from the land"
- The tax-leasing system was the key innovation: aristocrats bid for the right to collect taxes; everything above the agreed sum was profit
- The Tobiad Joseph (c. 240-218) is the prime example: "he secured a royal tax lease for the whole province for twenty-two years by doubling the amount to be raised" and collected "heedlessly and sometimes by armed force"
- Hecataeus of Abdera (c. 300 BCE) already noted land concentration by the wealthy
- Koheleth and Sirach both report "constant destructive oppression of the poor by the rich"
- Debt slavery became so threatening the Ptolemies enacted protective laws; "Palestine became a land which exported slaves to meet the needs of the Graeco-Roman world"
- The cultural split: wealthy aristocrats adopted Greek language, names, clothing, festivals — while the lower class "hardly came into contact with Greek culture in Judaea"
- "As a result of this cultural reshaping, the chronic class contrast developed such a potential for conflict that it only needed an occasion to turn it into a civil war"
- The upper class was internally split: "pious" aristocrats (like Sirach) vs. "Hellenizers" (Tobiads), with a power struggle predating the Maccabean crisis
- The Hasidim emerge as the "hard core" of the conservative party — identified by Albertz (following Kampen) as an influential scribal group, not a lower-class pietistic conventicle

**Confidence:** MEDIUM for specific mechanisms (tax-leasing details rely on Josephus's Tobiad romance). HIGH for the general picture of deepening social conflict with a cultural dimension.

**What's at stake:** The Maccabean revolt wasn't just a religious persecution reaction — it was ALSO a class war. The cultural conflict (Hellenism vs. Torah Judaism) mapped onto an economic conflict (tax-leasing aristocrats vs. impoverished farmers). This dual nature explains why the revolt could be simultaneously a fight for religious freedom AND a civil war between Jewish factions.

**Who disagrees:** Bickerman emphasized the religious dimension of the Maccabean crisis over the economic. Tcherikover saw the Hasidim as scribal class rather than pietists (which Albertz follows). The reliability of Josephus's Tobiad romance is debated.

**Alternative reading:** The cultural-religious conflict was primary; the economic dimension was secondary. Hellenism was the real threat, and economic exploitation was a symptom of cultural apostasy, not a separate cause.

**My assessment:** Albertz's insistence on the social-economic dimension as co-equal with the cultural-religious dimension is a valuable corrective to narratives that treat the Maccabean crisis as purely about "freedom of religion." The identification of the Hasidim as scribes rather than pietists is significant but needs more evidence than Albertz provides here.

## Claim 3: The Chronistic history was a response to the Samaritan schism — an attempt to re-legitimate the Davidic monarchy and Jerusalem temple within the framework of the Torah

**Albertz's claim:** "The Samarian split provoked a new effort at integration within the community of Judah aimed at extending the consensus over the foundations of the official religion of Israel beyond the framework created by the Torah." Chronicles was "no less than a revision of the canon."

**Evidence presented:**
- By the early Hellenistic period, the Samaritans had built their own temple on Gerizim and could appeal to the Pentateuch (which says nothing about Jerusalem) to legitimate their cult
- The Pentateuch's deliberate pre-state focus — ending with Moses, excluding David and Zion — gave the Samaritans theological ammunition: "the Jerusalem temple and the Davidic kingdom on which Judah's claim to leadership was theologically based did not appear explicitly in the canonized foundation history of Israel"
- Chronicles responds by making David and Solomon the climax of Israel's foundation history — "a second Moses"
- David receives divine revelation of the temple model (like Moses on Sinai); Solomon builds it; both regulate the cult in detail
- The ark AND the wilderness sanctuary are brought into the Jerusalem temple — combining the pre-priestly and priestly traditions
- Chronicles "deliberately allowed the exodus to fade and distorted the pre-state period into a chaotic time without proper worship" (2 Chron 15.3-6)
- Chronicles offers a broad synthesis: Torah + DtrG, adapting DtrG to conform with BOTH Deuteronomic and Priestly regulations — harmonizing contradictory laws (e.g., the Josiah passover account harmonizes Deut 16 and Ex 12)
- However, Chronicles also sets limits: the king cannot enter the temple building (2 Chron 23.6; 26.18); the temple is financed by the people; priests are not royal officials but self-administer
- The monarchy is presented as "executive organ of the theocracy" — the Davidides administer Yahweh's rule but aren't semi-divine (the Nathan promise's "son of God" language is limited to Solomon alone)
- The kings are depicted as "human kingship, close to the people" — summoning assemblies, consulting leaders, avoiding dynastic quarrels — "all the negative experiences of oppression and misuse of power... were almost completely suppressed"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The connection to the Samaritan schism is plausible but inferential. The synthesis-of-traditions analysis is solid. The dating of Chronicles (early 3rd century) is widely accepted.

**What's at stake:** Chronicles isn't just boring repetition of Samuel-Kings — it's a strategic theological document responding to a crisis of legitimacy. It attempts a "second canonization" — integrating the excluded traditions of monarchy and state cult BACK into official religion, but on Torah's terms (limited monarchy, priestly autonomy, popular involvement). This is effectively what the later prophetic canon and the Writings accomplish.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Chronicles later or who see its primary purpose as internal community edification rather than anti-Samaritan polemic. Those who read Chronicles as straightforward history rather than tendentious revision.

**Alternative reading:** Chronicles is not primarily anti-Samaritan — it's an idealistic vision of Israel's history written for internal consumption, to inspire hope for restoration. The harmonization of legal traditions reflects scribal practice rather than strategic theology.

**My assessment:** Albertz's reading makes Chronicles interesting — which is an achievement given how often Chronicles is dismissed as "P riestly propaganda" or "boring." The anti-Samaritan context is plausible but not strictly provable. More persuasive is his analysis of Chronicles' compromise: re-legitimizing monarchy and temple while imposing Torah's limits. This is a genuine contribution.

## Claim 4: Torah piety (Psalms 1, 19, 119) transformed personal piety into a distinctively Jewish theology centered on scripture — creating the "Bible piety" that became characteristic of Judaism

**Albertz's claim:** "The concern of those who promoted this new personal theology becomes clearest where they transferred to the Torah expressions of prayer language which in current personal piety were applied to God." This created "a distinctive Jewish personal theology and practice of piety which could unite all those who took their faith seriously beyond social and territorial barriers."

**Evidence presented:**
- Psalms 1, 19, and 119 date to the first half of the 3rd century (Deissler's linguistic analysis)
- Linguistic links to Ezra 7.11 and Chronicles suggest origin in scribal circles — the verb daras ("seek, research") is applied to the law for the first time
- The "Torah of Yahweh" in these psalms IS the canonized Pentateuch — referred to in the singular, something to be "looked at" (nabat), "meditated on" (haga), and "reflected on" (siah)
- Torah piety transferred prayer language from God to Torah: trust (119.42), faith (v.66), hope (vv.43,74,81,114,137), longing (vv.82,123,131), love ('ahab, used 8 times in Ps 119), joy (vv.14,162)
- The Torah becomes "the medium through which the exchange between individuals and their God had to run"
- A new praxis pietatis emerged: "regular meditation exercises" — day and night study (119.97,147f.,164), meditative reading half-aloud (haga, Ps 1.2)
- The piety individualizes and internalizes ethics: "the normative exclusiveness of the ethical orientation" — follow the Torah's specific regulations, not just general wisdom ideals
- Torah piety also functioned as a social boundary marker: the "wicked" (rasa') are defined by failure to observe Torah, not just by social misconduct — creating "a black-and-white religious picture of society"
- Albertz offers a sharp critique: Torah piety "also contributed towards sublimating the social conflict in religious terms" — "the meditative immersion in the Torah which they propagated was also to some degree substitute religious satisfaction and a flight from reality"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the existence and content of Torah piety (the psalms are right there). MEDIUM for the dating and scribal context. The critique of sublimation is Albertz's own evaluation.

**What's at stake:** This is the birth of "Bible religion" — the transformation of scripture from a community's foundation document into the object of personal devotion and the medium of individual relationship with God. It's arguably the single most important development for the future of Judaism and Christianity. Albertz's critique — that this piety sublimated social conflict — is a significant challenge to any romanticized view of "love of Torah."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Torah psalms later (Maccabean period) or earlier (Persian). Those who argue the "Torah" in these psalms refers to wisdom instruction, not the Pentateuch. Those who see Torah piety as purely positive without the sublimation critique.

**Alternative reading:** Torah piety is a genuine spiritual achievement — the democratization of theological reflection — not a flight from reality. The "social conflict" reading reads too much into the generic "wicked" language.

**My assessment:** Albertz's analysis of Torah piety is rich and multi-sided — he recognizes its integrative power (creating a shared personal theology across social divides) while also criticizing its depoliticizing effect (turning social conflict into a question of personal piety vs. wickedness). Both judgments can be true simultaneously. His identification of Torah piety with scribal circles and the early 3rd century is well-argued, though not certain. The transfer of prayer language from God to Torah is a genuinely striking observation.

## Claim 5: Apocalyptic emerged from marginalized lower-class and scribal circles as a "theology of resistance" — combining eschatological prophecy with elements of wisdom and Torah piety

**Albertz's claim:** The late prophetic and apocalyptic literature represents "oppositional eschatological interpretation of history" that developed in marginalized groups. It progressed through three stages: (1) Zech 9-14's oppositional eschatology sparked by Alexander's conquests, (2) Isa 24-27's eschatological assurance from lower-class circles, and (3) Daniel's apocalyptic instructions for resistance under Antiochus IV.

**Evidence presented:**

**Zech 9-14 (Deutero-Zechariah, late 4th century):**
- Sparked by Alexander's conquest (332) — Zech 9.1-8 reflects the destruction of Phoenician and Philistine cities
- The group developed an "oppositional eschatological interpretation of history" — Yahweh himself would come as warrior-king to defeat the nations
- But critically, the "day of Yahweh" would first be judgment on Israel: Jerusalem conquered, half the population deported (14.2), two-thirds exterminated (13.7f.) — "the onslaught of the nations on Zion would certainly not be stopped by God at the beginning, as the traditional Zion theology wanted to make people believe"
- This prepared the way for "the apocalyptic notion of the 'terrors of the end-time'"

**Isa 24-27 ("Isaiah Apocalypse," early 3rd century):**
- Arises from lower-class circles — using the terminology of the social conflict (saddiq/rasa', 'ebyon/'aris)
- The unnamed destroyed "city" is not a specific historical city but "urban life generally" — universalized judgment
- The group's distress was threefold: political domination by foreign and native lords (26.13), theological distress that Yahweh's judgment tarried (26.8-10), and impotence — "they could not win any victories on earth or bring down their over-powerful opponents" (26.16-18)
- The parallel between "the wicked" and "the inhabitants of the earth" generalizes the class conflict to cosmic dimensions
- Hope centers on Yahweh's universal kingship, the resurrection of the dead (26.19 — one of the earliest clear statements), and the eschatological banquet for all peoples (25.6-8)

**Daniel (Maccabean period, 167-164 BCE):**
- Written during the persecution of Antiochus IV Epiphanes
- The Hasidim (hasidaioi) — identified by Albertz (following Kampen) as an influential scribal group, not a lower-class pietistic conventicle — were the tradents
- Daniel combines: wisdom tradition (court tales), prophetic eschatology (the four-kingdom scheme), Torah piety (Daniel's prayer practices), and apocalyptic vision (the Son of Man)
- The apocalyptic theology of history divides time into periods determined by God, culminating in the establishment of God's eternal kingdom
- It serves as "instructions for resistance" — calling for faithful endurance under persecution, promising resurrection and vindication

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM for the specific sociological location of each text. MEDIUM-HIGH for the general trajectory from prophetic eschatology to apocalyptic. The dating and sociological contexts are inherently uncertain.

**What's at stake:** Albertz traces apocalyptic not to foreign influence (Persian Zoroastrianism) or to the "failure of prophecy" but to the concrete social experience of marginalized groups within Judaean society. Apocalyptic is a theology FROM BELOW — it gives voice to those crushed by imperial and aristocratic power. This has political implications for how we read apocalyptic texts today.

**Who disagrees:** Hanson (The Dawn of Apocalyptic) locates apocalyptic origins earlier, in the early post-exilic period, and in conflicts between visionary and hierocratic groups. Ploger sees the Hasidim as a pietistic conventicle, not scribal elites. Scholars who emphasize Persian/Zoroastrian influence on Jewish apocalyptic. Those who read Daniel as wisdom literature rather than resistance literature.

**Cross-reference:** Smith (Origins, Part III) discusses apocalyptic as one trajectory of Israelite monotheism — where the denial of other gods becomes cosmic conflict between good and evil.

**My assessment:** Albertz's three-stage model (Deutero-Zechariah → Isaiah Apocalypse → Daniel) provides a coherent developmental narrative. His emphasis on the social dimension — apocalyptic as the voice of the marginalized — is important and often overlooked in favor of literary or comparative approaches. However, the sociological identifications are thin: we really don't know who wrote these texts. The scribal identification of the Hasidim (following Kampen) is suggestive but needs more evidence. The claim that Isa 24-27 comes from "lower-class circles" rests heavily on the presence of poor/righteous terminology that could be conventional.

## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Hellenistic hierocracy and aristocratic power struggles | MEDIUM-HIGH | Dating of high priest's political powers |
| 2. Tax-leasing, cultural split, and Maccabean preconditions | MEDIUM | Josephus's Tobiad romance reliability |
| 3. Chronicles as anti-Samaritan re-legitimation | MEDIUM | Samaritan connection inferential |
| 4. Torah piety as distinctive Jewish personal theology | MEDIUM-HIGH | Dating of Torah psalms |
| 5. Apocalyptic as theology of resistance from marginalized groups | LOW-MEDIUM | Sociological locations speculative |

**Strongest section:** 6.3 (Torah piety). Albertz's analysis of how Torah piety transformed personal religion is the best thing in the chapter — specific, textually grounded, and theologically significant.

**Weakest section:** 6.4.3 (Daniel). It's treated very briefly in the prospect, and the sociological identification of the Hasidim draws heavily on Kampen's disputed thesis.

**Overall assessment of the chapter:** This is explicitly a "prospect" not a full treatment, and it shows. The chapter is thinner than Chapters 4 and 5, with broader brushstrokes and less detailed claims. This is by design — Albertz is sketching trajectories that future scholarship would need to fill in. The most durable contributions are the Torah piety analysis and the institutional analysis of the emerging hierocracy.
