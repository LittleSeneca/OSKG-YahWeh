# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 2

## Yahweh and Baal

---

## Claim 1: Baal was an accepted Israelite god from the Judges period through the fall of Judah

**Smith's claim:** Baal worship was not a brief northern apostasy under Ahab and Jezebel. It was a persistent feature of Israelite religion for centuries. The ninth-century conflict was an innovation driven by political tensions with Phoenicia, not a return to original purity. "There is no evidence that prior to the ninth century Baal was considered a major threat to the cult of Yahweh."

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal-names in Saul's Yahwistic family (covered in Ch. 1)
- Samaria ostraca (9th-8th c.): 5 Baal names vs. 9 Yahweh names — a high ratio for the capital
- Ahab built a Baal temple but "was not quite the apostate... his sons bear Yahwistic names. He consults Yahwistic prophets. He repents before Elijah."
- Jehu's reform was incomplete — Kuntillet Ajrud texts mention Baal AND Yahweh together, with Hebrew vowel letters (not Phoenician script — these are Israelite texts)
- Hosea (8th c.) still polemicizes against Baal; his oracle in 2:23-24 directly parallels Baal's speech to Anat in the Ugaritic Baal cycle (KTU 1.3 III)
- Hezekiah tried to eliminate Baal; Manasseh restored him; Josiah purged him again
- Jeremiah condemns Baal worship in the final days of Judah
- "On the whole Baal was an accepted Israelite god... this god remained popular through the end of the southern kingdom."

**Smith partially rehabilitates Ahab.** The biblical portrait is "a considerable degree of negative typecasting." Ahab's program "perhaps did not initially include the eradication of the cult of Yahweh." The Elijah conflict was a clash of THREE visions: (1) Ahab/Jezebel elevating Baal alongside Yahweh, (2) Elijah's Yahweh-alone, (3) the traditionalist coexistence.

**Confidence:** HIGH that Baal worship persisted. HIGH that Ahab wasn't simply an apostate (his sons' Yahwistic names are hard data). MEDIUM that the pre-9th century period was truly conflict-free — we have so little data that "no evidence of conflict" could just be "no evidence."

**What's at stake for faith:** If Baal was always part of Israelite religion, the biblical narrative of a pure Yahwism corrupted by foreign queens is false. The Elijah stories are not about restoring lost purity — they're about INNOVATING a new exclusivity. Elijah wasn't a conservative; he was a radical.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue the biblical portrait of Ahab as apostate is historically reliable. The Baal-names could reflect the problem the Bible describes (syncretism), not evidence there was no problem. The historical books are ideologically shaped but not fabricated.

**Alternative reading:** Ahab really WAS an apostate who tried to replace Yahweh with Baal. The Yahwistic names of his sons reflect court convention, not personal theology. Jehu's reform really DID suppress Baal worship, and its later resurgence represents backsliding, not continuity. This reading requires trusting the biblical narrative more than Smith does.

**My assessment:** Smith's strongest evidence is the persistence of Baal AFTER multiple reform attempts. If Hezekiah purged Baal and Josiah purged him again, Baal kept coming back — which suggests popular demand, not just royal sponsorship. The cumulative timeline from Judges through Jeremiah is persuasive regardless of how you read Ahab. The question isn't whether Baal was worshipped (he was) but whether this represents "indigenous Israelite religion" (Smith) or "persistent syncretistic corruption" (traditional). That's a framing question, not an evidence question.

## Claim 2: Yahweh absorbed Baal's storm-warrior imagery through the political machinery of the monarchy

**Smith's claim:** The Davidic monarchy deliberately adopted Baal's language and imagery to describe Yahweh as the national god and dynastic patron. This wasn't passive "influence" — it was an active political project. The monarchy both used Baal's imagery AND fought Baal's cult, creating a complex dynamic where Yahweh became more Baal-like even as Baal-worship was condemned.

**Evidence presented — the shared motifs:**

| Baal (Ugarit) | Yahweh (Bible) |
|--------------|----------------|
| Cloud-rider (*rkb 'rpt*) | Rider on the clouds / steppes (Ps 68:5, Ps 18:11) |
| Storm theophany: march → nature convulses → returns to mountain → thunders | Same pattern in Ps 18, 29, 68, 86 |
| Seven thunders ("voice" of Baal) | Sevenfold "voice of Yahweh" (Ps 29) |
| Defeats Sea (*Yamm*) with weapons | Crushes/stills/dries up Sea (Pss 74:13, 89:10) |
| Defeats seven-headed Leviathan (*ltn*) | Leviathan in Isa 27:1, Ps 74:13-14 |
| Defeats Death (*Mot*) | Swallows Death (Isa 25:8), taunts Death (Hos 13:14) |
| Holy mountain Saphon (*ṣpn*) | Zion called *yarkĕtê ṣāpôn* (Ps 48:3) |
| Temple produces rains | Temple → rains (Mal 3:10, Hag 1:7-11) |
| Bull iconography | Golden calves, "Bull of Jacob," Samaria ostracon "Young bull is Yaw" |
| Title "Most High" (*'ly*) | Yahweh's title in Pss 18, 68; personal names *yhw'ly* |

**The Mari letter (18th c. BCE) — the earliest witness:** The storm-god Adad (equivalent to Baal) tells King Zimri-Lim: "When you sat on the throne of your father, I gave you the weapon(s) with which I fought against Sea." This explicitly shows the POLITICAL function: divine victory over chaos legitimates the king. The same logic operates in Israel: Psalm 89:26 extends Yahweh's cosmic power to the Davidic king — "I will set his hand on Sea and his right hand on River(s)."

**The enthronement festival:** Smith cautiously endorses the theory that the Feast of Tabernacles included an enthronement celebration for Yahweh modeled on Baal's kingship. Evidence: LXX Psalm 29 superscription, Zechariah 14:16-17 (Yahweh's kingship + Tabernacles + rain), and the Baal cycle's own seasonal structure tied to autumn rains.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the specific parallels — the motif catalogue is overwhelming. MEDIUM on the enthronement festival theory — plausible but underdetermined by the evidence. MEDIUM-HIGH on the political function — the Mari parallel is strong.

**What's at stake for faith:** The Psalms you sing in church describing God's majesty, power over storms, and kingship over creation are — historically — repurposed Baal hymns. Psalm 29, one of the most majestic descriptions of God's voice in the Bible, was almost certainly written for Baal. This doesn't make the theology false, but it does mean the LANGUAGE of biblical worship is borrowed from the worship of a god the Bible condemns. If God inspired the Psalms, he inspired them using Canaanite mythological language. If humans wrote them, they wrote them using the religious vocabulary they inherited from their polytheistic neighbors (or, on Smith's reading, from their own polytheistic past).

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue shared imagery reflects common ANE cultural idiom, not borrowing. The storm-god language is metaphorical — every culture in the region used similar metaphors. Hess: "imagery does not equal worship."

**Alternative reading:** The parallels are genuine but the direction of borrowing could go either way — or both could draw from a common older tradition. Smith assumes Israel borrowed from Canaan. It's possible that both Israelite and Ugaritic traditions descend from a shared West Semitic religious heritage without direct borrowing.

**My assessment:** The density and specificity of the parallels make "common idiom" strained. When Psalm 29 has the EXACT sevenfold thunder structure of Baal's theophany, when Yahweh defeats a seven-headed sea dragon with the same name as Baal's enemy (Leviathan/*ltn*), when Zion is called by Baal's mountain's name (*ṣpn*), "coincidence" requires more faith than "borrowing." This is one of Smith's strongest arguments. The political function claim (monarchy used Baal imagery to exalt Yahweh) is more speculative but plausible.

## Claim 3: Anat's martial imagery was absorbed into Yahweh even though her cult never existed in Israel

**Smith's claim:** The Canaanite warrior goddess Anat was never worshipped in Israel (no inscriptions, no cult sites, almost no personal names). But her distinctive battle imagery — gory, cannibalistic, ecstatic — appears in biblical descriptions of Yahweh. The monarchy probably mediated this transfer, using Anat's martial language (as Egyptian pharaohs did) to dramatize Yahweh's military power.

**Evidence presented — the Anat parallels:**
- KTU 1.3 II: Anat fights at a mountain, battles are universal ("peoples"), produces heaps of corpses and skulls, "gleans" (harvest imagery), holds a feast on flesh and blood, wades in gore, delights in carnage
- Biblical parallels: Divine battle at Zion (Ps 2, 48, Joel 4), universal scope ("the nations"), heaps of corpses/skulls (Ps 110:6, Isa 34:2), harvest imagery (Joel 3:13, Rev 14), drinking blood (Isa 49:26, Deut 32:42), wading in blood (Ps 58:11, 68:24), divine laughter (Ps 2:4), drunkenness with battle (Isa 63:3-6)
- Egyptian parallels: New Kingdom texts use Anat and Astarte to dramatize pharaonic prowess; Ramses III is described with Anat and Astarte as his shield
- Smith's caution: "Since Anat is not attested in the Bible except in a few personal names, the lack of contact between her cult and that of Yahweh forestalls any theory of direct dependence."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on direct dependence on Anat traditions specifically (the parallels could reflect general ANE battle conventions). HIGH that the biblical war imagery has Canaanite antecedents.

**What's at stake for faith:** Moderate. The goriest descriptions of God in the Bible (drenched in blood, drunk on slaughter, laughing at enemies) aren't unique revelations of divine wrath — they're borrowed from a Canaanite warrior goddess. This contextualizes the violence but doesn't directly challenge theological claims.

**My assessment:** Smith is appropriately cautious here. The Anat parallels are striking but harder to prove as direct borrowing than the Baal parallels (where the cultic contact is clear). The Egyptian mediation theory is interesting but speculative. Worth noting: some biblical passages COMBINE Anat's gore with Baal's storm (Deut 32:42-43, Ps 68:24) — which suggests convergence of multiple Canaanite divine profiles, not just borrowing from one.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 2's Overall Strength

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Baal worship persisted throughout monarchy | HIGH | None — the cumulative evidence is overwhelming |
| Yahweh absorbed Baal's storm-warrior imagery | VERY HIGH | "Common idiom" argument (but parallels too specific) |
| Monarchy drove the absorption politically | MEDIUM-HIGH | Insufficient pre-monarchic data for comparison |
| Anat's imagery transferred to Yahweh | MEDIUM | Direct dependence unprovable; could be general ANE conventions |

**Strongest section:** §2 (Imagery of Baal and Yahweh). The motif catalogue is the most empirically grounded part of the book so far. This is where Smith is hardest to challenge.

**Weakest section:** §4 (Anat excursus). Interesting but speculative. Smith acknowledges the limitations honestly.

**Most theologically disruptive:** The Psalms-as-repurposed-Baal-hymns argument. Psalm 29 in particular. If you've ever been moved by the "voice of the Lord" breaking cedars and shaking the wilderness — that was originally about Baal.
