# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 2

## Yahweh and Baal

This chapter traces how Baal and Yahweh competed, overlapped, and ultimately how Baal's characteristics were absorbed into Yahweh through the political machinery of the monarchy.

---

## Section 1: Baal Worship in Israel

### The Historical Evidence

Smith traces Baal worship from the Judges period through the fall of Judah:

**Early period (Judges/early monarchy):** Names with *ba'al* as theophoric element (Jerubbaal, Eshbaal, Meribbaal) suggest Baal was acceptable in Yahwistic circles. The Samaria ostraca (9th-8th c.) contain 5 Baal names vs. 9 Yahweh names — a surprisingly high ratio for the capital of the northern kingdom. No Baal names are attested from Judah.

**The ninth-century crisis:** Ahab and Jezebel elevated Phoenician Baal in Samaria. Smith argues this was NOT a radical innovation but built on existing compatibility:

> "Ahab's religious policies presumably would have appealed to those 'Canaanites' living in Israelite cities during the monarchy... The religious program of Ahab and Jezebel represented a theopolitical vision in continuity with the traditional compatibility of Yahweh and Baal."

**Smith rehabilitates Ahab somewhat.** Ahab's sons bear Yahwistic names (Ahaziah, Joram). He consults Yahwistic prophets. He repents before Elijah. "Ahab was not quite the apostate from Yahwism that the biblical polemics of 1 Kings 16:30-33 and 21:25-26 present." The negative portrayal is "a considerable degree of negative typecasting." Ahab's program "perhaps did not initially include the eradication of the cult of Yahweh."

> **The Elijah conflict was a clash of THREE theopolitical visions:** (1) Ahab/Jezebel: elevate Baal alongside Yahweh, (2) Elijah: Yahweh alone, (3) the traditionalists: coexistence. Elijah's vision eventually won.

### Which Baal?

Smith identifies the Baal of Jezebel as **Baal Shamem** (not Melqart), based on:
- The Tyrian royal onomasticon has no Melqart names, only *b'l* names
- Baal Shamem is explicitly a storm-god; Melqart is not
- The Baal of Carmel (1 Kings 18) is also a storm-god
- Aramaic Papyrus Amherst 63 preserves a version of Psalm 20 invoking Baal Shamem alongside Yahweh

### Jehu's Reform Wasn't Complete

Jehu slaughtered Baal's supporters and destroyed the Samaria temple (2 Kings 10), but Baal worship continued. Evidence:
- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions mention Baal AND Yahweh in the same texts
- Hosea (8th c.) still polemicizes against "the baal" and "the baals"
- Ahaz fostered Baal worship (2 Chron 28:2)
- Hezekiah tried to eliminate it; Manasseh brought it back
- Josiah purged the temple of Baal paraphernalia (2 Kings 23:4)
- Jeremiah still condemns Baal worship in the final days of Judah

> "On the whole Baal was an accepted Israelite god... criticism of his cult began in the ninth or eighth century, and despite prophetic and Deuteronomistic criticism, this god remained popular through the end of the southern kingdom. **There is no evidence that prior to the ninth century Baal was considered a major threat to the cult of Yahweh.**"

### The "Baals" Development

Smith traces how *ba'al* evolved linguistically: singular storm-god → local manifestations at cult sites → generic plural "the baals" (all foreign gods). This parallels the grouping of Asherah with Astarte and the generic use of *ilāni u ištarāti* in Akkadian.

### The Missing Martial Dimension

> "No biblical text expresses ideas about Baal's status as a warrior. Yahweh had perhaps exhibited and possibly usurped this role at such an early point for the tradents of Israel's religious literature."

Baal's martial function was absorbed by Yahweh so early and so completely that the Bible doesn't even remember Baal as a warrior — only as a fraudulent rain-god.

## Section 2: Imagery of Baal and Yahweh

This is the comparative evidence section. Smith catalogues the specific parallels.

### The Storm Theophany Pattern (Cross's Four Elements)

Frank Moore Cross identified a four-part pattern in Baal's storm theophany that appears in Yahweh descriptions:

1. **The march** of the divine warrior
2. **Nature convulses** as he manifests power
3. **Return to the holy mountain** to assume kingship
4. **The "voice"** (thunder) from the palace, bringing fertilizing rains

This exact pattern structures Psalm 18, Psalm 68, and Psalm 86. Psalm 29 is the most complete example — widely regarded as a Baal hymn with Yahweh's name substituted.

### Specific Shared Motifs

| Motif | Baal (Ugarit) | Yahweh (Bible) |
|-------|--------------|----------------|
| Cloud-rider | *rkb 'rpt* | *rōkēb bā'ărābôt* (Ps 68:5), rides on clouds (Ps 18:11) |
| Storm chariot | *mdl* in meteorological entourage | Wheels in storm (Ps 77:19), chariot of victory (Hab 3:8, 15) |
| Thundering voice | Seven thunders | "Voice of Yahweh" seven times (Ps 29) |
| Defeat of Sea (*Yamm*) | KTU 1.2 IV — Baal crushes Yamm | Pss 74:13, 89:10 — Yahweh crushes/stills/dries up Sea |
| Defeat of River (*Nahar*) | Paired with Yamm in Ugaritic | Isa 11:15 — seven channels |
| Seven-headed dragon (*ltn*) | Leviathan in KTU 1.5 I.1 | Leviathan in Isa 27:1, Ps 74:13-14, Rev 13:1 |
| Defeat of Death (*Mot*) | Baal vs. Mot (KTU 1.4-1.6) | Yahweh swallows Death (Isa 25:8), taunts Death (Hos 13:14) |
| Holy mountain (*Ṣpn*) | Baal's home on Mount Saphon | Zion called *yarkĕtê ṣāpôn* (Ps 48:3) |
| Temple produces rain | Baal's palace → rains (KTU 1.4 V-VII) | Temple → rains (Mal 3:10, Hag 1:7-11) |
| Bull iconography | Baal as bull | Yahweh as "Bull of Jacob," golden calves at Dan/Bethel, "Young bull is Yaw" (Samaria ostracon 41) |
| "Most High" (*'ly*) | Baal's epithet (KTU 1.16 III) | Yahweh's title in Pss 18, 68; personal names *yhw'ly*, *'lyhw* |

### The Mari Letter — Political Use of the Conflict Myth

Smith introduces a crucial piece of external evidence. A second-millennium letter from Mari quotes the storm-god Adad (equivalent to Baal) addressing King Zimri-Lim:

> "When you sat on the throne of your father, I gave you the weapon(s) with which I fought against Sea (*tâmtum*)."

This is the **earliest textual witness to the West Semitic conflict myth**, and it explicitly shows the POLITICAL function: the god's victory over cosmic chaos legitimates the king's rule. The king's power over his enemies mirrors the god's power over Sea. This pattern — divine victory → royal legitimation — continued in Israel.

## Section 3: The Role of the Monarchy

> **Core claim:** The Davidic monarchy deliberately adopted Baal's storm-warrior imagery to describe Yahweh as the national god and patron of the dynasty.

Psalm 18 (= 2 Samuel 22) is the parade example: verses 8-19 describe Yahweh in Baal-like storm theophany fighting FOR the king; verses 29-45 describe Yahweh enabling the king to conquer enemies. Psalm 89:26 explicitly extends Yahweh's cosmic power to the king: "I will set his hand on Sea and his right hand on River(s)" — quoting the very names of Baal's enemies.

**This explains why Baal remained popular.** The monarchy itself used Baal's language and imagery to describe Yahweh. If the royal court was singing psalms that described Yahweh in Baal's terms, the average Israelite could be forgiven for not seeing a sharp distinction.

### The Enthronement Festival Hypothesis

Smith discusses (cautiously) the theory that the Feast of Tabernacles included an enthronement celebration for Yahweh, modeled on Baal's kingship. Key evidence:
- Psalm 29 LXX superscription associates it with Tabernacles
- Zechariah 14:16-17 connects Yahweh's kingship, Tabernacles, and rain
- Psalm 65 celebrates autumn harvest through Yahweh's victory over cosmic waters

Smith also argues the BAAL CYCLE itself is structured around autumn rains — each of the three major sections (Baal vs. Yamm, palace building, Baal vs. Mot) builds toward the arrival of the life-giving rains. The Baal cycle was "performed" literature tied to seasonal festivals. Israel's enthronement psalms inherited this framework.

## Section 4: Excursus — Yahweh and Anat

Anat is the Canaanite warrior goddess par excellence. Her battle description in KTU 1.3 II (the goriest passage in ancient literature) shares specific motifs with biblical descriptions of Yahweh:

| Anat's Battle (KTU 1.3 II) | Yahweh's Battle (Bible) |
|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Fights at the mountain | Divine battle at Zion (Ps 2, 48, Joel 4, Zech 14) |
| Universal scope — "peoples" | Yahweh fights "the nations" |
| Heaps of corpses/skulls | Ps 110:6, Deut 32:43, Isa 34:2 |
| "Gleaning" (harvest imagery) | Joel 3:13, Rev 14:14-20 |
| Aftermath as feast — eating flesh, drinking blood | Isa 49:26, Deut 32:42, Ps 68:24 |
| Wading in blood | Ps 58:11, Ps 68:24 |
| Delight in carnage | Divine laughter (Ps 2:4), drunkenness with battle (Isa 63:3-6) |

**But Smith is cautious about direct dependence.** Anat had no cult in Israel. The parallels could reflect:
1. Common ANE battle language (Egyptian and Moabite texts also describe carnage similarly)
2. Mediation through the monarchy — Egyptian texts used Anat and Astarte to dramatize pharaonic prowess; Israel may have done the same for Yahweh

> Some biblical passages "combine bloody martial imagery with storm language" — conflation of Anat's gore with Baal's storm. Deuteronomy 32:42-43 and Psalm 68:24 are examples. Israel didn't just borrow from one Canaanite deity; it fused multiple divine profiles into Yahweh.

## Key Takeaways from Chapter 2

1. **Baal was a legitimate Israelite god for centuries.** The ninth-century conflict was an innovation, not a return to purity. Before Ahab/Jezebel, Baal and Yahweh coexisted.

2. **The monarchy absorbed Baal even as it fought him.** Davidic psalms describe Yahweh in Baal's exact language — storm theophany, defeat of Sea/River, holy mountain, cloud-rider. This made Baal BOTH a threat (he competed for devotion) AND a source (his imagery elevated Yahweh).

3. **The conflict myth had an explicitly political function.** From Mari (18th c. BCE) to Israel, the god's victory over cosmic chaos legitimated the king. Yahweh's defeat of Sea wasn't just theology — it was royal propaganda.

4. **The parallels are so extensive and specific** that coincidence is implausible. Psalm 29 as a Baal hymn, the seven-headed Leviathan, the defeat of Sea/River, the holy mountain language — these are not general ANE motifs. They are specific to the West Semitic storm-god tradition.

5. **Anat's martial imagery was absorbed even though her cult wasn't.** Israel took the goriest battle descriptions from the goddess they didn't worship and applied them to Yahweh. Convergence operated at the level of literary imagery, not just cultic practice.
