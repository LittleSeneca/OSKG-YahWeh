# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 8-9

---

## Chapter 8: The Statue of Yhwh in Judah

### Claim: Yhwh was represented by statues and images in Judah. The prohibition on images was late, not original.

**Römer's claim:** Contrary to the traditional view that Israelite religion was aniconic from the beginning (no images of God), Yhwh WAS represented by statues in pre-exilic Judah. Standing stones (*maṣṣēbôt*) functioned as representations of Yhwh, seals depict an enthroned god who is probably Yhwh, and a Persian-era coin shows Yhwh as a sky god on a winged wheel. The prohibition on images was a LATE innovation — probably 6th century — and was then projected backward as if it had always been the rule.

**Evidence presented:**

**Standing stones as Yhwh representations:**
- *Maṣṣēbôt* are frequently mentioned in the Bible and found archaeologically — at Mari (2nd millennium), at Arad, and at other Israelite sites
- Four functions: funerary markers, commemorative steles, contract witnesses, and **divine representations**
- Genesis 28: Jacob anoints a *masseba* at Bethel and calls it "a house of God" (*bêt 'ĕlōhîm*). "The masseba was a way of representing the god Yhwh."
- The Arad temple had two steles — "representing Yhwh and another deity"
- The "high places" (*bāmôt*) — which were Yahwistic sanctuaries — contained steles and sacred poles
- **"The presence of standing stones is no evidence at all in favor of assuming that there was an aniconic cult of Yhwh."**
- Deuteronomy 16:22 and Leviticus 26:1 prohibit BOTH statues AND standing stones — proving they were considered equivalent. If steles were just memorials, why ban them alongside idols?
- At Mari, betyles and statues COEXIST. "It would be a mistake to oppose aniconism and iconism sharply."
- Some steles at Mari were sculpted with human features and painted. The Arad steles had traces of paint — possibly covered with painted divine images.

**Seals and coins depicting Yhwh:**
- A 7th-century Hebrew seal belonging to "Elishama, son of Gedalyahu" shows a god seated on a throne flanked by two trees of life
- Two 7th-century seals with lunar attributes may represent Yhwh — the Yahwistic names of their owners support this identification
- A Persian-era coin (c. 380 BCE) from Judah shows a deity seated on a winged wheel — almost certainly Yhwh as sky god
- "Yhwh was represented by images."
- The representation may have drawn on Mesopotamian models (the winged wheel resembles Assyrian depictions of Assur)

**The prohibition on images was LATE:**
- The Decalogue's second commandment (no graven images) dates to the 6th century at the earliest
- Older texts show no concern about divine images. The ark, the cherubim throne, and the temple itself were visual representations of Yhwh's presence
- The prohibition was formulated during the Exile, when contact with Babylonian idolatry made image-worship seem like a distinguishing mark of "paganism"
- It was then PROJECTED BACKWARD — the golden calf story (Exodus 32) was written to make image-worship seem like the primordial sin

> **Römer's key point:** "The biblical texts make no direct link between Asherah and Yhwh... the Deuteronomistic school strongly suggests that this association was invented to break the link between Yhwh and Asherah."

**Confidence:** HIGH that steles represented Yhwh (the Arad evidence and the prohibition texts are clear). MEDIUM-HIGH that the seals depict Yhwh (plausible but can't be definitively proven without inscriptions). HIGH that the image prohibition was late (well-established in critical scholarship).

**What's at stake for faith:** The second commandment — "you shall not make for yourself a graven image" — was not given to Moses on Sinai. It was invented by 6th-century priests who wanted to distinguish Yahwism from Babylonian religion. Yhwh was originally a god you could SEE — on seals, on coins, as a standing stone, as a bull. The invisible, transcendent God of Jewish and Christian theology is a LATE theological construction.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives who date the Decalogue to Moses. Scholars who argue Israelite aniconism was genuinely ancient (Mettinger). Those who say the seals depict other deities, not Yhwh.

**Alternative reading:** The aniconic tradition may be older than Römer allows — the ark and cherubim throne represent "empty space" where the deity dwells, which IS a form of aniconism. The standing stones could be memorials, not divine images. Römer may overstate the case.

**My assessment:** Römer is persuasive on the standing stones (the prohibition texts lumping *massebot* with idols is the strongest evidence). The seals and coins are more debatable. But the overall trajectory — from represented to unrepresentable God — fits the larger pattern of convergence and differentiation. The invisible God is the product of making God different from other gods.

## Chapter 9: Yhwh and His Asherah

### Claim: Yhwh had a consort. Asherah was a goddess in monarchic Israel and was paired with Yhwh, not Baal.

**Römer's claim:** This is where Römer directly contradicts Smith. Where Smith argued the asherah was a YAHWISTIC SYMBOL that had lost its goddess connection, Römer argues the asherah WAS the goddess Asherah and she was Yhwh's consort. The biblical association of Asherah with Baal is a Deuteronomistic invention designed to break the Yhwh-Asherah link.

**Evidence presented:**

**Asherah in the ancient Levant:**
- Origins in western Semitic culture; earliest references in Mesopotamia (Hammurabi era, 18th century BCE)
- At Ugarit (14th-13th centuries): Asherah (*Aṯiratu*) is El's consort, "mother of the gods," called "the seventy sons of Aṯirat"
- She is associated with fertility and kingship — the heir to Keret's throne "shall suck the milk of Aṯirat"
- In southern Arabian inscriptions (1st millennium), *aṯrt* means either the goddess or "goddess" generically

**Asherah in the Hebrew Bible:**
- 40 occurrences, mostly with the article ("the asherah")
- Four categories: (a) plural in stereotypical destruction formulas, (b) associated with Baal, (c) with standing stones, (d) near the altar/house of Yhwh
- "The biblical texts make no direct link between Asherah and Yhwh" — BUT they also don't link her with Baal except in Deuteronomistic passages. The Baal-Asherah link is a "Deuteronomistic invention."

**The inscriptions — the smoking gun:**
- **Kuntillet Ajrud** (c. 800 BCE): "Yhwh of Samaria/Teman and his Asherah"
- **Khirbet el-Qom** (c. 700 BCE): a Judean burial inscription invoking "Yhwh and his Asherah"
- These inscriptions directly link Yhwh and Asherah — something the Bible never does. The Bible's silence is censorship, not absence.
- Kuntillet Ajrud was a caravanserai/way-station, not a fringe site — the religious practices there reflect mainstream Yahwism

**Römer on the grammar question:**
- The possessive suffix on *'šrth* ("his Asherah/asherah") is debated
- Römer is more willing than Smith to read it as "his Asherah" (the goddess) rather than "his asherah" (the symbol)
- "In sharp contrast to the biblical texts, a close link between Yhwh and Asherah is attested in the inscriptions"

**What happened to Asherah?**
- When Yhwh absorbed El's identity, he inherited El's consort
- The Deuteronomistic reformers, pursuing Yhwh-alone theology, had to eliminate Asherah
- Their strategy: associate her with BAAL (the enemy god) rather than Yhwh. This made her seem foreign and idolatrous.
- Josiah's reform removed the asherah from the Jerusalem temple (2 Kings 23:6-7)
- The goddess was suppressed; her symbol was destroyed; the memory was rewritten

**Confidence:** HIGH that Asherah was a goddess in the ANE (Ugaritic evidence is unambiguous). HIGH that Kuntillet Ajrud links her with Yhwh. MEDIUM that she was Yhwh's consort specifically (versus a subordinate deity or hypostasis). The grammar remains ambiguous.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Römer is right, the biblical God had a wife. The goddess was worshipped alongside Yhwh in the Jerusalem temple itself. She was removed by reformers, not because she was foreign, but because she was TOO indigenous — too deeply woven into Israelite religion. The God of the Bible was part of a divine couple. This is the single most explosive claim in Römer's book.

**Who disagrees:** Smith, obviously — he reads the evidence as referring to the symbol, not the goddess. Frevel and Korpel agree with Smith. See [[Smith Chapter 3 — Yahweh and Asherah]] for the detailed debate and my assessment that Smith is probably wrong here.

**Alternative reading:** Smith's: the asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol that had lost its goddess connection by the monarchic period. The Deuteronomists suppressed it because of its divinatory/healing functions, not because it represented a goddess.

**My assessment:** This is the sharpest disagreement between Smith and Römer. Smith says symbol; Römer says goddess. I previously assessed Smith as "probably wrong" on this point. Römer strengthens that assessment. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptional evidence is more naturally read as a divine couple than as a deity and his cult object. The Deuteronomistic invention of a Baal-Asherah link to suppress the Yhwh-Asherah link is a compelling argument. The Ugaritic background (Asherah as El's consort → Yhwh inherits El's consort) provides a coherent developmental narrative. I'm now at: Römer is probably right, Smith is probably wrong. Yhwh probably had a consort.
