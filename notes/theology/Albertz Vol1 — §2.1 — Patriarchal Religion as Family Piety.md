# Albertz — §2.1: Religious Elements of Early Small Family Groups ('Patriarchal Religion')

This is the first substantive historical section. Albertz argues that what Genesis depicts as "patriarchal religion" is not a chronological predecessor to Yahweh worship but a *sociological substratum* — family-level personal piety that existed alongside, beneath, and before official Yahweh religion. His key move: the patriarchal narratives in Gen 12-50 are tradents retrojecting what they knew of family piety onto their ancestors.

---

## Claim 1: "Patriarchal religion" is not a pre-Yahwistic stage but a substratum of family piety, retrojected onto the ancestors by later tradents

**Albertz's claim:** "Patriarchal religion is to be defined not as a preliminary stage but as a substratum of Yahweh religion. This stratum of family religion is a preliminary stage only to the degree that it shows an amazing similarity to other Near Eastern religions, going as far back as Sumero-Babylonian religion." In other words: the patriarchal narratives preserve authentic material about family-level religious practice — but this practice was contemporaneous with (and older than) Yahweh religion, not chronologically prior to it. The tradents described what they knew of family piety in their own time and projected it onto the ancestors.

**Evidence presented:**
- Alt's "God of the Fathers" hypothesis (1929) tried to reconstruct a pre-Yahwistic nomadic patriarchal religion from the Genesis designations "God of my father," "Fear of Isaac," etc. — but this has collapsed under criticism (Diebner 1975, Köckert 1988)
- Alt's key claims can't be sustained: "God of the fathers" is not specifically nomadic (attested in sedentary cultures too); the cultural milieu of Gen 12-50 doesn't predate Iron Age I (1200 BCE); the genealogical framework presupposes tribal conditions of the late pre-state period
- **Yet** the religious world depicted in Genesis is "clearly distinct from the world of the symbols of Yahweh religion" — this difference demands explanation
- Albertz's sociological explanation: the difference is between *levels* (family vs. people), not *periods* (before vs. after Yahweh). Genesis depicts the small group of the family; Yahweh religion belongs to the wider group of tribe/tribal alliance/people
- The patriarchal stories relate "the beginnings of the people of Israel from the aspect of family history" — so they accumulate the religious patterns native to the family

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The negative case (Alt's hypothesis is broken) is well-established and widely accepted. The positive case (family piety as substratum) is elegant but depends on Albertz's "internal religious pluralism" framework being correct. If the family/state distinction is artificial, this collapses into circular reasoning.

**What's at stake:** This is foundational for Albertz's entire method. If patriarchal religion is family piety rather than a pre-Yahwistic stage, then (a) Yahweh religion didn't evolve FROM patriarchal religion — it was always distinct and operated at a different social level, (b) the tensions in Israelite religion (tolerant vs. exclusive, unwarlike vs. warlike) are tensions between social strata that coexisted, and (c) the history of Israelite religion is not a straight line but a constant negotiation between family piety and official religion.

**Who disagrees:** Any scholar who still treats patriarchal religion as a chronological predecessor to Mosaic Yahwism. The Alt school (and its successors) treated the "God of the fathers" as a pre-Yahwistic nomadic type. Evangelical scholars who accept the historicity of the patriarchal narratives would reject the claim that the religious material is retrojected.

**Alternative reading:** One could accept Alt's collapse while rejecting Albertz's alternative. Van Seters (1980) argued the patriarchal narratives are late literary creations with no historical value whatsoever — not family piety, not anything pre-exilic. The "difference" from Yahweh religion could be explained by literary artistry rather than authentic family tradition.

**My assessment:** This is Albertz's most creative move and the one that most clearly distinguishes his project from competitors. It's productive and makes sense of otherwise puzzling features (why patriarchal religion has no polemic, no exclusiveness, no warlike characteristics). But it also carries risk: Albertz is simultaneously claiming the patriarchal narratives are NOT historically reliable (the chronology is fictitious, Alt's reconstruction is wrong) AND that they DO preserve authentic material about family piety (because the tradents knew what family religion looked like and projected it). This is a subtle hedge — he wants to eat his critical cake and have his traditional material too. Whether this works depends on the strength of the triangulation with other evidence about family religion.

## Claim 2: Early Israelite families worshipped various regional forms of the god El (El-Shaddai, El-'Olam, El-Bethel, etc.) as their family god, and this was a practical monolatry without exclusiveness

**Albertz's claim:** Before Yahweh worship was established at the tribal level, early Israelite families worshipped various regional manifestations of the high god El. The Priestly writing correctly preserved the memory that the patriarchs worshipped God under the name El-Shaddai. The family god was worshipped monolatrously (one god per family) but without exclusiveness or intolerance toward other families' gods — because "what is expected and experienced from various gods is in any case more or less identical" at the family level.

**Evidence presented:**
- The patriarchal narratives connect the ancestors with a series of El deities: El-'Elyon (Jerusalem), El-Bethel (Bethel), El the God of Israel (Shechem), El-'Olam (Beersheba), El-Ro'e (Negeb)
- The Priestly writing says the patriarchs knew God as El-Shaddai (Gen 17:1; Ex 6:3)
- No proper names containing Yahweh appear in the patriarchal narratives — all theophoric names are El-compounds (Yishma'el, etc.)
- The "God of my father" designation is the most original type of family god reference — a personal god passed down through family tradition
- Family onomastics: theophoric elements in names can change (adapting to the religious environment), but the predicates (what is experienced from the god) remain constant — showing that *function* matters more than *name* at the family level
- Striking absence of religious polemic in the patriarchal narratives — "only in the late Deuteronomistic text Gen 35:2ff. does Jacob call on his family to put away the strange gods"
- Even in the monarchical period, it was "in no way customary to give one's children names containing Yahweh; this only changes in the late period"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The El-name connections are real textual data. But the argument that these represent *early family practice* (rather than later aetiological constructions by sanctuary priesthoods) is hard to verify. The Priestly writing's El-Shaddai tradition could be theological archaizing rather than authentic memory.

**What's at stake:** If early Israelite families worshipped El (not Yahweh), then Yahweh worship was *introduced* at the tribal level — Yahweh was not originally the god of Israelite families. This supports the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis (Yahweh came from outside) and complicates any claim that Israel was "always Yahwistic." It also means the identification of Yahweh with El was a secondary theological move.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue that El and Yahweh were always identified in Israelite religion, or that "El" in the patriarchal narratives is just a generic term for deity, not a distinct god. Richard Hess argued that El and Yahweh are too intertwined in biblical Hebrew to separate neatly.

**Alternative reading:** The El deities associated with patriarchal sites could be retrojections from later sanctuary traditions. El-Bethel might reflect Bethel's sanctuary theology, not family practice. The entire El → Yahweh progression could be a theological construct of the Priestly writer rather than historical memory.

**My assessment:** Albertz is on solid comparative ground — the pattern of family-level monolatry with interchangeable divine names is well-attested in ANE family religion. His reading of the patriarchal El-names as authentic memories of pre-Yahwistic family religion is plausible but not proven. The strongest single piece of evidence is the absence of Yahweh theophoric names in the patriarchal narratives — this is hard to explain if the tradents were simply projecting their own Yahwism backward. They would have used Yahweh-names if they didn't know better.

## Claim 3: Family piety was characterized by unconditional divine care, short time-horizons, female religious experience, and a striking absence of warlike, ethical, or cultic-elaborated features — it was "pre-cultic, pre-political and pre-moral"

**Albertz's claim:** "The piety which was alive in the early Israelite families was... a pre-cultic, pre-political and pre-moral religion." Its content was "determined by the central problems of survival experienced by the farming and nomadic family." Key features: the promise of a son as paradigmatic divine intervention (short time-horizon — one pregnancy cycle, unconditional, female-centered); the god accompanying people on journeys; divine protection from other groups through non-violent means; no specialized cult personnel (the father was priest); no fixed holy places/times.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Promise of a son:** Attested multiple times in Abraham stories (Gen 16:11; 18:9f.), also in Judges 13 and II Kings 4. The wife is the original recipient — a "typical female religious experience." The time-span is short (one pregnancy). The promise is unconditional — "the moral quality of the woman is a matter of complete indifference" (cf. Gen 16:7ff on Hagar)
- **Theophany outside the cult:** The divine messenger (mal'ak) appears in everyday human encounters, not in cultic settings — "one can meet God in everyday human encounters"
- **God accompanying on journeys:** The motif of the god who is "with you" on the way (Gen 28:20; 26:3, 28; 31:5, 42) is especially important for itinerant groups — it is "a protective covering which wards off all danger"
- **Unwarlike divine action:** In contrast to tribal Yahweh who fights wars, the family god is "strikingly unwarlike." In the dispute with Gerar's shepherds over wells (Gen 26:19ff.), the god doesn't fight — he helps the family find new wells. In the Jacob-Laban conflict, the family gods only protect the treaty between them
- **Passover:** Probably originated as a family-level nomadic ritual accompanying transhumance (the spring change of pasture) — a warding off of demonic powers endangering man and beast during movement. It remained a family festival even after later integration into the Exodus tradition and centralization

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The individual observations are well-supported from the texts. But the cumulative claim — that all these features cohere into a single "type" of religion — depends on Albertz's construction being right about which features are genuinely early and which are later projections.

**What's at stake:** This characterization of family piety as "pre-moral" and "unconditional" sets up a sharp contrast with Yahweh religion (which Albertz will present as ethical, conditional, warlike). The history of Israelite religion is then the story of how these two religious strata — family piety and official Yahweh religion — interacted, conflicted, and partially merged. If Albertz has overstated the contrast, the drama of his narrative collapses.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more ethical content in family religion (e.g., the wisdom tradition's moral instruction within the family) or who see more continuity between El worship and Yahweh worship. Van der Toorn's work on family religion emphasizes moral dimensions more than Albertz does.

**Alternative reading:** The contrast between "pre-moral" family piety and "ethical" Yahweh religion may reflect Albertz's Lutheran theological commitments (law/gospel, faith/works) more than the ancient evidence. The unconditional nature of family piety sounds suspiciously like Protestant soteriology projected onto ancient families.

**My assessment:** There's a genuine tension here. Albertz is both trying to reconstruct early family religion FROM Genesis (which is a late, theologically-shaped text) and arguing that the same late tradents who shaped Genesis "corrected" or suppressed elements of family religion (ancestor cult, teraphim). If the tradents could suppress ancestor cult, why couldn't they have suppressed moral dimensions of family piety? Or added unconditional elements to make the patriarchs look better? The source-critical tension is unresolved. That said, Albertz's portrait of the promise-of-a-son complex as female-centered and unconditional is textually well-grounded and striking.

## Claim 4: There was no fundamental nomadic/sedentary religious opposition in early Israel; the Passover likely originated as a nomadic transhumance ritual later historicized into the Exodus narrative

**Albertz's claim:** The old idea (Alt, et al.) that Israelite religion emerged from a conflict between nomadic religion and sedentary Canaanite religion is wrong. Ethnologically, herders and farmers lived in close symbiosis, and the same clans practiced both. The real opposition was between the hill-country population (farmers and herders) and the city-states of the plains. The Passover festival, despite its later anchoring in the Exodus liberation tradition, probably originated as a family ritual accompanying the spring transhumance.

**Evidence presented:**
- Mari texts (c. 1800 BCE) show the same clan engaged in both agriculture and nomadic herding
- The patriarchal tradents depict the patriarchs doing both: Isaac engages in agriculture (Gen 26:12f.) and pastoralism; cattle (baqar) appear alongside sheep and goats
- Passover features suggesting nomadic origin: spring date, eating in haste, prohibition on leaving leftovers, blood rite to ward off the destroyer — all fit the dangers of transhumance
- Despite later connection with maṣṣot (agricultural festival) and centralization under Josiah, Passover "remained a family festival"
- Texts like Gen 32:23-33 and Ex 4:24-26 indicate people felt exposed to demonic attack especially when traveling

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the nomad/sedentary symbiosis (ethnologically well-established). LOW-MEDIUM on Passover's transhumance origin (the evidence is suggestive but circumstantial; the Exodus connection is very old and deeply embedded).

**What's at stake:** If Passover originated as a family-level nomadic ritual before being historicized into the Exodus liberation narrative, it's a perfect example of Albertz's model: family piety (pre-political, concerned with survival) gets absorbed and reinterpreted by official religion (political liberation theology). The two strata don't just coexist — they merge, with official religion reinterpreting family practice.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the Exodus connection as original to Passover. The biblical tradition is unanimous and ancient that Passover commemorates the Exodus. Separating a hypothetical pre-Yahwistic nomadic Passover from the Exodus Passover requires speculative reconstruction.

**Alternative reading:** Passover's features (blood on doorposts, haste, nocturnal celebration) could equally be explained by its function as an apotropaic ritual within an already-Yahwistic framework, without requiring a pre-Yahwistic nomadic origin. Not everything that looks "primitive" is pre-Yahwistic.

**My assessment:** Albertz is probably right about the direction of travel (family practice → official reinterpretation, not the reverse), even if the specific Passover reconstruction is speculative. The broader point — that religious practices moved between the family and official levels, getting reinterpreted in the process — is well-taken and doesn't depend on this one festival.

## Claim 5: Ancestor cult (teraphim, necromancy) was present in early Israelite family religion but was progressively suppressed by official Yahweh religion

**Albertz's claim:** Teraphim were likely figurines of deified ancestors, part of the basic equipment of early Israelite household cults. Ancestor veneration was a genuine element of early family piety, but it was already being "reduced to remnants" by the personal relationship to the God of the fathers, and later came under increasingly severe attack from official Yahweh religion. The largely negative evidence for ancestor cult in the OT is "at least in part the result of later dogmatic correction."

**Evidence presented:**
- Rachel's teraphim (Gen 31:19, 34f.) — small figurines called "my/your gods" ('ĕlōhaykā), compared to the ilāni (household gods) in Nuzi family documents
- Nuzi parallels: ilāni were part of ancestral heritage, and disinherited persons were denied access to household gods — suggesting the teraphim functioned to "secure the continuity of the family and solidarity between generations"
- In Nuzi, eṭemmu (spirits of the dead) are mentioned in partial parallel to ilāni — suggesting household gods = deified ancestors
- Teraphim appear alongside 'ōbôt and yiddĕ'ōnîm in II Kings 23:24 (Deuteronomistic polemic) — the latter are clearly spirits of the dead
- Necromancy (consulting the dead) was "probably widespread" down to the early post-exilic period (I Sam 28)
- Yet the patriarchal narratives show emotional solidarity with ancestors (being "gathered to his fathers," significance of ancestral tombs) without regular cult of the dead (no kispu ritual like Mesopotamia)

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The teraphim-as-ancestor-figurines theory is plausible but contested. The Nuzi parallels are suggestive but from a different culture and earlier period. The "dogmatic correction" argument is a classic appeal to silence.

**What's at stake:** If Albertz is right, Yahweh religion was not just adding to family piety but actively *suppressing* elements of it — ancestor cult, divination, image-use. The history of Israelite religion includes a repressive dimension: official religion fought to eliminate forms of family piety it considered incompatible.

**Who disagrees:** Loretz argued for a MUCH stronger ancestor cult (full Canaanite-style cult of the dead), which Albertz rejects as overreaching. Other scholars (Bloch-Smith, Lewis) have documented Judahite burial practices suggesting more robust ancestor veneration than Albertz allows. The minimalist position would be that teraphim were simply household idols without specific ancestor reference.

**Alternative reading:** The evidence for ancestor cult might be *stronger* than Albertz says. Bloch-Smith's *Judahite Burial Practices and Beliefs about the Dead* (1992) — cited in Albertz's bibliography — documents extensive provisions for the dead in Judahite tombs (food, vessels, lamps) suggesting active ongoing care for the dead, not mere emotional solidarity. Albertz might be underplaying the evidence that contradicts his "already reduced to remnants" claim.

**My assessment:** This section feels like Albertz hedging. He wants to acknowledge the ancestor cult evidence (because it's there) but minimize its significance (because it complicates his picture of family piety as primarily oriented toward the personal father-god). The most honest move would be to say: we don't have enough evidence to know how extensive ancestor cult was in early Israelite families, and the biblical texts have been shaped by later polemic against it. The Nuzi parallels are interesting but don't prove the teraphim were ancestors specifically.

## §2.1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Patriarchal religion = family piety substratum, not pre-Yahwistic stage | MEDIUM-HIGH | If the family/official distinction is artificial or if patriarchal narratives are pure literary invention |
| 2. Early families worshipped El deities; practical monolatry without exclusiveness | MEDIUM | If the El-names are later aetiological constructions by sanctuary priesthoods |
| 3. Family piety was pre-cultic, pre-political, pre-moral | MEDIUM | If Albertz has suppressed moral/ethical dimensions of family religion; if the "contrast" is a Lutheran projection |
| 4. No nomad/sedentary opposition; Passover from transhumance ritual | MEDIUM-HIGH on symbiosis; LOW-MEDIUM on Passover | The Exodus-Passover connection is ancient and deeply embedded |
| 5. Ancestor cult present but already suppressed | LOW-MEDIUM | Bloch-Smith's burial evidence suggests more robust ongoing cult of the dead |

**Strongest section:** The rejection of Alt's "God of the Fathers" and the sociological reinterpretation of patriarchal religion as family piety (Claim 1). This is Albertz at his best — using social theory to resolve a genuine puzzle in the evidence.

**Weakest section:** The ancestor cult discussion (Claim 5). Albertz seems to want it both ways — acknowledging the evidence for ancestor veneration while downplaying its scope to preserve his picture of family piety as primarily oriented toward the personal father-god.

**Key cross-reference:** Dever's *Did God Have a Wife?* documents extensive archaeological evidence for domestic cult (female figurines, household shrines) that complement Albertz's textual reconstruction of family piety. Dever would agree that family religion was a distinct stratum, but would emphasize the goddess dimension more than Albertz does here (Albertz barely mentions goddesses in this section).

**What to watch:** As Albertz moves to §2.2 (the Exodus group), the contrast between family piety (unwarlike, unconditional, pre-moral) and Yahweh religion (warlike, conditional, ethical) will become the engine of his narrative. If that contrast is overblown, the narrative loses its dramatic tension.
