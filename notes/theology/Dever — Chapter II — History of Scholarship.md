# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Chapter II

## The History of the History

---

## Structure: Dever evaluates six approaches

Dever surveys how scholars have studied Israelite religion, judging each by whether it takes folk religion and archaeological evidence seriously. His assessments are frank — sometimes brutally so.

---

## 1. The "History of Religions" School (late 19th-early 20th c.)

**Representatives:** Smend, Gressmann, Sellin
**Dever's verdict:** Pioneering but primitive. "What resulted from early studies based largely on the biblical texts themselves was what I would call 'histories of the history of the texts about religions,' rather than of religion itself." Predated archaeology.

---

## 2. The "Myth and Ritual" School

**Representatives:** Hooke, Frazer, S.H. Hooke, the Scandinavian "patternists"
**Dever's verdict:** Fascinated by "fertility cults" and "sacred marriage" — mostly fantasy. "There never was much actual evidence for the more titillating aspects of such cults." Dever quotes his own teacher G. Ernest Wright: "The sexual emphasis of Canaanite religion was certainly extreme and at its worst could only have appealed to the baser aspects of man." Dever's response: "Of course, the key phrase here is 'judged by our standards.' But standards change and improve with better knowledge."

---

## 3. Old Testament Theology

**Representatives:** Brueggemann, Gerstenberger, Childs
**Dever's verdict:** Comprehensive — but useless for understanding what ordinary Israelites actually practiced. "I have concluded, albeit somewhat reluctantly, that it is useless in the attempt to reconstruct a reliable portrait of ancient Israelite religion. Indeed, it is a barrier to understanding."

Dever's ten-point critique of biblical theology:
> "It has been: Clerically dominated. Androcentric. Establishment-oriented. Focused on unrealistic concepts of 'the unity of the Bible' and 'normative religion.' Static, denying evolutionary developments. Didactic and governed by Christian apologetics. Lacking sound historical foundations. Obsessed with verbal formulations, and largely insensitive to symbolic representations. Oblivious to revolutionary archaeological data. Increasingly irrelevant in the modern 'secular' world."

**On Brueggemann (1997):** Acknowledges his erudition and pastoral concern, but "despite his immense and sincere effort, I come away from reading Brueggemann's book with sadness. I cannot escape the conclusion that it entirely misses the point."

**On Gerstenberger (2002):** Notes his focus on family, clan, ordinary people — "All this is the context of our Israelite folk religion." But "under 'sources' for the monarchy, for instance, Gerstenberger cites me and another archaeologist or two only in a footnote, then simply moves on."

**Dever's key methodological distinction** (following Krister Stendahl):
- **What happened in history?** → The historian's job
- **What did these events mean then?** → The historian may speculate
- **What do these supposed events mean now?** → The theologian's job

---

## 4. Sociological Approaches

**Representatives:** Gottwald (1979), Weber, Mendenhall
**Dever's verdict:** "Perhaps the single most significant influence in contemporary biblical studies." Gottwald's *The Tribes of Yahweh* was "one of the most seminal works of 20th-century American biblical scholarship." Gottwald's stress on ideology as a "prime mover" anticipated current trends. His indigenous origins model for early Israel has been confirmed by subsequent archaeology.

---

## 5. The Revival of Interest (1990s-2000s) — Dever's Detailed Evaluations

| Scholar | Work | Dever's Verdict |
|---------|------|----------------|
| **Albertz** (1994) | *A History of Israelite Religion* | "One of the first to make considerable, and generally competent, use of the pertinent archaeological data." Recognizes folk religion ("family religion"). Good on Canaanite background. |
| **Niditch** (1997) | *Ancient Israelite Religion* | "A disappointment. It is brief (123 pages); reads mostly like an undergraduate syllabus... seems oblivious to the potentially revolutionary nature of the current archaeological data." |
| **Day** (2000) | *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* | "Excellent discussion of the 'theology of the pantheon'" but "scarcely seems concerned with the actual realities of religious life and experience for most folk." Ignores archaeology. |
| **Hadley** (2000) | *The Cult of Asherah* | Well-documented but unoriginal. "Another 'disembodied' portrait of Israelite religion." |
| **Miller** (2000) | *The Religion of Ancient Israel* | "Perhaps the most expert, sophisticated, and balanced discussion yet of the biblical texts." BUT: "It is little more than a study of these texts." Reluctant to acknowledge Asherah as a goddess despite archaeological evidence. "It would be good if it were the last work of its kind, because it seems to me that it says all that can be said as far as the biblical texts are concerned." |
| **Mark Smith** (2002a) | *The Early History of God* (2nd ed.) | "A masterly, thoroughly documented study... literally all you ever wanted to know." BUT: "I regret to say that I find Smith's work is not informed by any archaeological data that would change his almost exclusively text-based approach in any essential way." Specifically on Asherah: Smith "is still not inclined to accept the likelihood that it names a goddess." → **"It is precisely my intent here to demonstrate that a cult of Asherah did flourish in ancient Israel."** |
| **Nakhai** (2001) | *Archaeology and the Religions of Canaan and Israel* | Dever's student. Accords archaeology primacy. "One hopes that Nakhai's book will draw attention to the rich but overlooked data." |
| **Zevit** (2001) | *The Religions of Ancient Israel* | "The most ambitious, the most sophisticated, the most important study of ancient Israelite religion ever undertaken." 821 pages. "No other current biblicist can match Zevit's command of a broad range of archaeological data." |

---

## 6. Works on Folk Religion

**Ackerman** (1992): *Under Every Green Tree.* Praised for recognizing that "popular religion is... about losers. But ironically, perhaps these losers probably held the majority and represented the mainstream." Ackerman defines popular religion as "everything that those who wrote the Bible condemned."

**Other scholars Dever cites:** van der Toorn ("Book religion"), Berlinerblau (terminological precision), Keel/Uehlinger (iconography).

---

## Chapter II Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| Biblical theology is useless for understanding folk religion | Dever's opinion, strongly held — debatable whether theology has NO value |
| Archaeology is systematically ignored by text scholars | HIGH — Dever documents this extensively |
| Zevit is the most important recent work | Dever's opinion — shared by many |
| Smith's work lacks archaeological substance | Debatable — Smith would argue he engages archaeology, just differently |

**Dever's most important contribution in this chapter:** His direct critique of Mark Smith. This is the central scholarly dispute. Dever says Smith ignores the archaeological evidence. Smith says the archaeological evidence is ambiguous and doesn't settle the Asherah question. The reader has to adjudicate based on the evidence Dever presents in Chapters V-VI.

**Dever's rhetorical strategy:** By surveying and critiquing everyone else first, Dever positions himself as the corrective. He's saying: "All these scholars have part of the picture. I have the part they're all missing — the ground truth."
