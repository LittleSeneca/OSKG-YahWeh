# Kaufmann — Ch V — The Sources

This is the technical heart of Kaufmann's project and his most radical historical argument. He accepts the documentary analysis (JE, D, P are distinct sources) but **reverses the chronological sequence**: P is the EARLIEST stratum, not the latest. The Torah literature is pre-prophetic, pre-exilic, and ancient — even to its language. If this argument fails, everything Kaufmann builds in Chapters VI-XIII rests on sand. If it succeeds, the entire Wellhausen consensus collapses.

## Claim 1: Torah-Group Literature Independent of and Prior to Literary Prophecy
→ [[claim-torah-independent-prior-to-prophecy]] | **kaufmann-ri-5.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Torah-group and prophetic literature represent distinct domains with genuinely different theological outlooks — the Torah's silence on prophetic eschatology, moral primacy, and cult critique is a real datum. Contradicted by: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]] (Smith dates monotheistic rhetoric to exilic period). Supports: [[claim-torah-sealed-canonized-before-prophecy]].

## Chapter V Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Torah-group independent of/prior to prophecy | MEDIUM-HIGH (independence); LOW (priority) | Parallel development, not sequence |
| 2: Three codes are independent crystallizations | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptively); LOW (chronologically) | Independence ≠ contemporaneity |
| 3: P lacks centralization, therefore is earliest | MEDIUM (descriptive); LOW (inference) | Silence could be programmatic, not ignorant |
| 4: P's high priesthood is pre-exilic | MEDIUM | Archaizing could produce same features |
| 5: P's tithe/priesthood laws are ancient | MEDIUM | Ancient traditions ≠ ancient document |
| 6: Torah's historical horizon proves early crystallization | MEDIUM (content); LOW (date) | Literary setting, not compositional date |
| 7: Torah compiled without revision = texts were fixed early | MEDIUM-HIGH (compilation); LOW (dating) | Fixity at compilation ≠ fixity centuries before |
| 8: Torah sealed before prophetic literature | VERY LOW | Linguistic evidence for late P; convergent evidence for Wellhausen sequence |

**Strongest section:** Claim 5 (tithes and priesthood). Kaufmann's detailed engagement with the tithe laws is the most textually sophisticated argument in the chapter, and he identifies genuine problems with the simple "post-exilic priests composed P for their own benefit" model. The implausibility of priests endowing Levites with the richest clerical due is a genuinely sharp point.

Also strong: Claim 1's observation that the Torah-group has a different theological outlook from prophecy. This is real and important, even if the chronological inference doesn't follow.

**Weakest section:** Claim 8 (the final synthesis). This is where Kaufmann's argument reaches its most radical conclusion — and where the evidence is thinnest. The leap from "the Torah contains ancient traditions and was canonized before the prophets" to "the Torah literature was composed before the prophets" is a non sequitur.

Also weak: Kaufmann's **complete silence on linguistic evidence**. This is the elephant in the room. P contains distinctive vocabulary, syntax, and phraseology characteristic of Late Biblical Hebrew — features shared with Ezekiel, Chronicles, Ezra-Nehemiah, and other demonstrably late texts. This is one of the most robust results of biblical scholarship, and it directly contradicts Kaufmann's thesis that P is the earliest source. Kaufmann doesn't address it in this chapter. He owes his readers an explanation for why P's language looks late if P is actually the earliest document — and he doesn't provide one.

**The chapter's logical structure:** Kaufmann's argument has a "pincer" shape. One arm argues that the Torah-group is independent of prophecy (therefore early). The other arm argues that P lacks centralization (therefore pre-D). The two arms converge on the conclusion that P is the earliest source. But both arms rely on the same logical move: treating *silence* (about prophecy, about centralization) as evidence of *ignorance* rather than *choice*. And both arms ignore the possibility of PARALLEL DEVELOPMENT — that priestly and prophetic traditions could coexist, each producing literature reflecting their own concerns, over the same centuries.

**Cross-references:**
- [[Tigay — Introduction]] — Tigay's onomastic study uses an independent dataset to argue for early monolatry WITHOUT accepting Kaufmann's dating of P. Tigay's strength is precisely that he breaks the circularity Kaufmann identifies.
- [[Tigay — Chapter II + Conclusions]] — Tigay's Conclusions (especially claims 1 and 4) partially vindicate Kaufmann's picture of popular religion as predominantly Yahwistic and largely fetishistic. But Tigay explicitly limits his conclusions to the 8th-6th centuries and doesn't date P early.
- [[Romer — Introduction and Chapter 1]] — Römer explicitly traces monotheism's gradual emergence from polytheism, directly contradicting Kaufmann's "monotheism from the beginning" thesis. Römer's model of "sedimentary strata" is essentially the critical consensus Kaufmann is fighting.
- [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.8 — The Deuteronomic Reform Movement]] — Albertz's account of Deuteronomy as a Josianic "mediating theology" that synthesized pre-state and state traditions fits the critical framework Kaufmann rejects. Albertz dates Deuteronomy to ~622 BCE, which Kaufmann would accept for D's *promulgation* but not for its *relationship* to P (which Kaufmann places earlier).
- [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Smith's claim that Israel was "broadly West Semitic in its religion" and that monotheism was "an inner-Israelite development over hundreds of years" is the polar opposite of Kaufmann's position. Smith's model of gradual differentiation from polytheism is what Kaufmann's entire project is designed to refute.

**Methodological note — the "silence = ignorance" fallacy:** Kaufmann's core argumentative move throughout the chapter is to treat textual silence as proof of chronological priority. The Torah-group doesn't mention literary prophets → therefore it's earlier. P doesn't mention centralized worship → therefore it's pre-D. The Torah doesn't mention the Assyrian empire → therefore it's pre-monarchic. This move is fallacious in every instance. Silence can equally reflect: (a) different genre/institutional setting, (b) deliberate programmatic choice, (c) the constraints of the narrative setting, or (d) genuine chronological priority. Kaufmann always chooses (d) without adequately excluding (a)-(c).

**What Kaufmann's best arguments actually support:** Not his radical reversal of the Wellhausen sequence, but a MORE NUANCED version of the critical model in which:
- The law corpora are more independent than simple linear derivation allows
- P preserves genuinely ancient priestly traditions, even if its final form is late
- The Torah-group and prophetic literature represent parallel streams, not a single evolutionary line
- The post-exilic compilers were more collectors than composers
- Some "late" features of P may actually be archaic survivals

This nuanced model preserves what's valuable in Kaufmann's observations without requiring the chronological reversal his evidence can't support. It's essentially the position held by many contemporary scholars — and it's a position Kaufmann's work helped make possible, even though he himself would have rejected it as insufficiently radical.
