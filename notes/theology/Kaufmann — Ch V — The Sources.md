# Kaufmann — Ch V — The Sources

This is the technical heart of Kaufmann's project and his most radical historical argument. He accepts the documentary analysis (JE, D, P are distinct sources) but **reverses the chronological sequence**: P is the EARLIEST stratum, not the latest. The Torah literature is pre-prophetic, pre-exilic, and ancient — even to its language. If this argument fails, everything Kaufmann builds in Chapters VI-XIII rests on sand. If it succeeds, the entire Wellhausen consensus collapses.

---

## Claim 1: The Torah-group literature is independent of, and prior to, literary prophecy

**Author's claim:** The Torah-group (Pentateuch + Former Prophets) and the literary prophets represent "distinct domains." The Torah cannot be understood as "a later outgrowth of prophetic faith." Literary prophecy was not "the fountainhead or ideal source of Israelite monotheism." Instead, the Torah literature "belongs not after, but before literary prophecy. It is the literary product of the earliest stage of Israelite religion."

**Evidence presented — five arguments:**
1. **Historical books never mention literary prophets.** Kings mentions only Isaiah (as foreteller/healer), not Amos, Hosea, or Jeremiah. "Had the prophetic books not been preserved, we should have known nothing of the existence of literary prophecy, let alone its distinctive ideas."
2. **Moral sin as historical factor.** The prophets denounce social corruption as grounds for national destruction. The historical books never mention social decay; for them, Israel's sin is exclusively "the worship of foreign gods and the adoption of heathen customs." Torah admonitions (Lev 26; Deut 28) specify exile for idolatry, not social injustice.
3. **Primacy of morality over cult.** The prophets' great doctrine — "I desire steadfast love, not sacrifice" (Hos 6:6) — "is never found" in the Torah-group. The Torah juxtaposes moral and cultic equally, silent on the issue. "The absence of this idea is a sign, not of a stage later than prophecy, but of an earlier stage."
4. **Centralization of worship has no roots in prophecy.** No pre-Deuteronomic prophet (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah) reproaches the north for not sacrificing at Jerusalem. The prophets attacked idolatrous rites at high places and Jerusalem alike. "The sole conclusion that could be drawn from prophetic rebukes was that the worship...must be purged of its heathen elements, but not that the high places must be destroyed."
5. **Josiah's reform was purely cultic, not social.** The reform purged the cult of YHWH of its dross but had "no trace of social reform." Josiah was motivated by the Torah book's warnings (which concern religious sins, not social corruption), not prophetic preaching. "Priests are its standard bearers," not prophets.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The observation that the Torah-group and prophetic literature have genuinely different outlooks is solid and important. The inference that this difference proves *chronological* priority rather than *institutional/theological* distinction (priestly circles vs. prophetic circles, running in parallel) is less secure.

**What's at stake:** If the Torah literature predates and is independent of prophecy, monotheistic law was not a prophetic creation. The prophets become traditionalists calling Israel back to a monotheism it always had — not innovators creating it. This inverts the critical narrative.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition treats the differences as evidence that priestly writers ("later") selected from and modified prophetic traditions to serve institutional ends. On this view, the Torah-group's "silence" about prophetic ideas reflects deliberate selectivity, not chronological priority. Albertz (Vol1 §3.8) argues the Deuteronomic reform WAS influenced by prophetic circles (Hosea-influenced groups among the 'am hā'āreṣ), not an independent priestly creation.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's observations about DIFFERENCE are largely correct. But difference doesn't prove sequence. Two parallel streams — priestly Torah tradition and prophetic oracular tradition — could coexist, each with its own concerns, without either being "later" than the other. The Torah-group's focus on idolatry as THE national sin may reflect the priestly institutional concern with proper cult, not chronological earliness. The prophetic emphasis on social justice may reflect a *competing* tradition, not a *later* one.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's strongest argument in the chapter. The independence of Torah literature from prophetic ideas is a genuine datum that the critical tradition has underexamined. The Torah really doesn't know prophetic eschatology, prophetic moral emphasis, or prophetic critique of the cult. The question is whether this independence proves *antiquity* or *institutional separateness*. Kaufmann assumes the former, but the latter is equally plausible. Priestly circles and prophetic circles may have operated in parallel for centuries, each producing literature reflecting their own concerns. The Torah's "silence" about prophecy would then reflect deliberate boundary-maintenance, not ignorance.

## Claim 2: The three law corpora are independent crystallizations — not evolutionary developments from one another

**Author's claim:** "The evolutionary sequence and literary dependence assumed by Wellhausen has no foundation." Each of the three codes is "an independent crystallization of Israel's ancient juristic-moral literature." They are not revisions of each other but parallel developments from a common ancient Near Eastern legal tradition.

**Evidence presented:**
- **D doesn't derive from the Covenant Code (JE).** D omits many JE laws inexplicably (laws about striking parents, bailees, goring ox, slave manumission for injury) while including others unchanged. "Such haphazard divergences can be accounted for only on the assumption that D and the Covenant Code are independent crystallizations."
- **P doesn't derive from JE.** P omits JE laws about indemnity for lost time, physician's costs, injury to pregnant women, goring ox, the three annual appearances, the ban on boiling a kid. These omissions "cannot be explained on the basis of P's peculiar character."
- **P doesn't derive from D.** P's priestly gifts "do not supplement, but differ completely from, those of D." P omits D's priestly portions (Deut 18:3), first shearings (18:4), the ban on working/shearing firstlings. P lacks D's social-moral laws about bribes, boundary removal, kidnapping, restoration of lost property.
- **No cross-contamination.** "Not a single peculiarity of one legal corpus has insinuated itself into either of the others." D's style nowhere appears in P; P's style nowhere appears in D. "The monumental fact is that not a single peculiarity of one legal corpus has insinuated itself into either of the others."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the DESCRIPTIVE observation (the codes are more independent than simple linear models allow). LOW on Kaufmann's INFERENCE that this disproves all genetic relationship.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right, the Wellhausenian sequence (JE → D → P, each modifying the previous) collapses. The codes become parallel witnesses to a common tradition rather than successive stages. P could be as old as JE.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition acknowledges the codes are more complex than simple linear derivation but maintains that thematic development (especially around centralization and the priesthood) reveals a chronological sequence. Even if D didn't "copy" JE, D's treatment of the same topics shows later development. Kaufmann's "inexplicable omissions" are explained by critics as selective use — later codes didn't need to repeat everything in earlier ones; they supplemented and modified where they disagreed.

**Alternative reading:** The independence Kaufmann documents is real and important. But it doesn't require his conclusion. Codes can develop independently of each other while still being chronologically sequential. The question is not whether D "derives from" JE in a direct literary sense, but whether D's *ideas* (centralization, name theology, cultic exclusivity) presuppose and respond to earlier conceptions. Kaufmann's list of "inexplicable omissions" is genuinely puzzling for simple linear models — but it's a problem for the *simplicity* of the model, not necessarily for the *direction* of development.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann makes a strong case that the law corpora are more independent than Wellhausen's model allowed. The omission patterns he documents are real and rhetorically effective. But he overreads his evidence. Independence of formulation does not equal independence of development. D could be later than JE in its *ideas* (centralization) while independently formulating its *laws*. P could be later than D in its *priestly hierarchy* while independently formulating its *priestly dues*. Kaufmann conflates "these codes weren't directly copied" with "these codes are all from the same period" — a leap the evidence doesn't justify.

## Claim 3: P is the EARLIEST source, not the latest — the complete silence on centralized worship proves it

**Author's claim:** P must be pre-Deuteronomic because "there is no trace whatever of D's centralization idea in P." This is "the monumental fact" that overshadows all alleged tokens of P's lateness.

**Evidence presented — multiple dimensions of P's non-centralized worldview:**
1. **No chosen city.** D's centralization concept involves BOTH a chosen temple AND a chosen city — the latter "figuring in the laws no less than the former." Sacred meals must be eaten in the chosen city; the paschal night must be spent there; the court of highest appeal is there. "Nowhere in P is this basic and peculiarly Deuteronomic idea reflected."
2. **P's spatial theology is different.** P distinguishes three realms: holy place (tent + court), camp, and outside the camp. The camp corresponds to the city but "none of [P's laws] endows the camp with the sanctity of the Deuteronomic chosen city." Sacred meals may be eaten in any "pure place" — inside OR outside the camp. "P thus fails to take notice of a basic consequence of the centralization law."
3. **The camp = any Israelite city with a high place.** P's laws about lepers expelled "outside the camp" parallel the practice of expelling lepers outside cities. The camp represents "at once a temple site as well as any city; D's separation of the two concepts is unknown to it."
4. **No pilgrimage requirement.** P doesn't prescribe the three annual appearances before YHWH at a chosen site — "is such an oversight credible after the Deuteronomic reform had made the pilgrimage an indispensable part of proper celebration?"
5. **Passover is a home sacrifice in P (contra D).** P's Passover (Exod 12:2-20) is "performed in the home with special rites; it belongs to the type of ancient home sacrifice." D forbids celebrating "in your gates." "The conception of the house as a sanctuary and the ancient popular custom of smearing blood leave no doubt that P's law antedates D."
6. **Festival laws are agricultural and local in P.** P preserves the celebration of first sheaf and dwelling in booths — rural practices that "naturally arose before centralization." D preserves only the name "Tabernacles," replacing local customs with spending seven days at the chosen site.
7. **Leviticus 17 assumes multiple legitimate sanctuaries.** The law requiring slaughter at "the door of the tent of meeting" — if post-D, would ban all meat-eating for people far from Jerusalem. Kaufmann: "The only plausible interpretation...is that it presupposes the existence of many legitimate local sanctuaries, each of which is represented here by the tent." P bans quasi-sacrifice on field altars (Saul-type), not profane slaughter (which D invents). "P bans it to the advantage of the high places and temples."
8. **P recognizes multiple YHWH temples.** Lev 26:31: "I shall make your temples desolate" — the Holiness Code assumes YHWH has many temples. "Significantly enough, the post-Deuteronomic parallel to this passage in Ezekiel 6 omits 'temples.'"
9. **P's tent is an archetype, not a law.** The desert tent was the only legitimate sanctuary "as a historical necessity." With settlement, "each 'camp' has its own 'tent,' [and] the 'centralized worship' of the desert period will no longer exist." D's centralization is different — it's linked to an exclusive FUTURE site in the land.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the descriptive observation (P genuinely lacks D's centralization concepts). LOW on the inference to P's chronological priority.

**What's at stake:** This is the linchpin of Kaufmann's entire reversal. If P is pre-centralization, the Wellhausen sequence (D → P) must be thrown out, and with it the entire evolutionary picture of Israelite religion.

**Who disagrees:** The Wellhausen school's counterargument: P doesn't lack centralization; it *presupposes* it. The portable tent of meeting IS the central sanctuary, retrojected into the Mosaic age. P doesn't need to ban high places because — in P's idealized vision — there never WERE any. The single tent in the desert IS the law applied retroactively. P's silence about the sin of high places is not ignorance but programmatic: P presents the world as it SHOULD have been, not as it was.

**Alternative reading:** Both Kaufmann and Wellhausen are doing the same thing — interpreting silence. Wellhausen says: P is silent about high places because they're assumed abolished (post-D). Kaufmann says: P is silent about high places because the ban didn't exist yet (pre-D). The silence proves neither position. What's needed is external evidence — and the linguistic data (Late Biblical Hebrew in P) points strongly to lateness, as do the institutional developments (the elaborate hierarchy, the developed sacrificial system). Kaufmann doesn't address the linguistic argument in this chapter.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's argument about P's non-centralized worldview is genuinely significant and deserves more attention than most critics give it. He's right that P's spatial theology (camp vs. outside camp; "pure places" anywhere) doesn't fit the Deuteronomic paradigm. P is not just "D minus the ban on high places" — it has a DIFFERENT spatial theology entirely.

But Kaufmann's inference from "P doesn't know centralization" to "P is EARLIER" is not the only possible inference. An alternative: P is a *competing* vision — a priestly ideal of the desert period that deliberately ignores (or wishes away) the centralization achieved by D. P might be post-D without being *dependent on* D. P represents what priestly circles WISHED Israelite religion had always been — portable, hierarchical, under Aaronic authority — in contrast to D's vision of a chosen city under popular Torah study.

The real problem for Kaufmann is that he needs P to be not just independent of D but *earlier than* D — and his evidence for "earlier" is entirely the silence argument plus a few details (home Passover, tithe types) that could as easily be archaizing as archaic.

## Claim 4: P's high priesthood and tent reflect pre-exilic conditions, not the Second Temple

**Author's claim:** The critical view that P's high priest and tent mirror the post-exilic theocracy "will not bear examination." P's portrayal reflects pre-exilic realities.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **P's attitude toward monarchy is favorable.** Genesis 17:6, 16; 35:11 promise kings. "P's representation of the monarchy as a divine blessing...is enough to show that its desert camp is not intended to depict the ideal form of Israelite society" (i.e., it's not polemicizing against monarchy as a theocracy would).
2. **P's camp is an armed camp, not a church.** Rules of war (Num 10:9), division of booty (31:25ff), conquest of Canaan — "its purpose is not cultic exercise under the protection of foreign rulers."
3. **The prophet Moses — not the priest Aaron — leads the camp.** "The importance of Aaron is restricted to the realm of the cult...In the camp itself, the priest has no authority." This contrasts with the post-exilic situation where priesthood held political authority.
4. **High priestly anointing was pre-exilic.** "Precisely in postexilic times high priests were no longer anointed. That P makes unction a mark of high priesthood is thus a sign of its antiquity."
5. **The tent is an oracular/prophetic vehicle, not a Second Temple reflex.** The Urim and Thummim, divine revelation, lawgiving functions — "by postexilic times the ancient channels of divine revelation were all but stopped up." The Second Temple "was never a priestly, let alone a prophetic, oracle." P's detailed descriptions of tent vessels, porters, guardians, and wood/metal altar "were entirely without practical application in Second Temple times."
6. **The roles of post-exilic temple personnel are absent from P.** Porters and singers of the Second Temple "are not even hinted at in P (contrast the anachronism of Chronicles which predates them to David's time)."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann identifies genuine discontinuities between P's tent and the Second Temple, but overstates their probative value for dating.

**What's at stake:** If P's institutions are pre-exilic, the Wellhausen picture of an evolving priesthood culminating in post-exilic hierocracy is backwards.

**Who disagrees:** Critics note that idealized portraits don't need to match present reality point-for-point. P's tent is an IDEAL — not a photograph of any actual institution. The mismatch between P's tent and the Second Temple doesn't prove P is early; it proves P is UTOPIAN. P presents what the priesthood SHOULD be (prophetic, oracular, led by an anointed high priest) — not what it actually was in the compromised reality of Persian Yehud.

**Alternative reading:** P's idealized picture functions as a charter for priestly authority precisely IN the post-exilic period. The mismatch between ideal and reality is the POINT — P provides the blueprint that the Second Temple FAILED to live up to, which is why it was preserved as sacred scripture rather than discarded as irrelevant. The archaic features (anointing, Urim) are nostalgic, not contemporary.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observations about discontinuities between P and the Second Temple are genuinely interesting and expose a real weakness in simplistic "P = Second Temple program" readings. But the alternative is not necessarily "P is pre-exilic." P may be an exilic/post-exilic UT opian vision that deliberately looks back to an idealized Mosaic past. The presence of archaic features doesn't prove antiquity — it could equally prove deliberate archaizing. Kaufmann needs to show why archaizing is IMPOSSIBLE, and he doesn't.

## Claim 5: P's tithe and priesthood laws are ancient, not products of post-exilic hierocracy

**Author's claim:** P's tithe laws "are ancient" and the Levitical tithe of Numbers 18 "was, by the beginning of Second Temple times, an obsolete law, a relic of another age." The priests/Levites distinction is pre-exilic, traceable to tribal origins — not a post-exilic invention.

**Evidence presented — Tithes:**
- **Three independent tithe laws** exist (Lev 27:30-33; Num 18:21-32; Deut 14:22-27), not two as harmonizers claim. "There are no grounds for combining and reducing the three tithes to two."
- **The animal tithe of Lev 27 is ancient.** "Tithing animals, far from being a sign of lateness, is a genuine mark of antiquity, being rooted in the early pastoral age." The Second Temple "knew of no priestly tithe of animals."
- **The Levitical tithe (Num 18) was votive, not annual.** Nothing in Num 18:21ff specifies an annual levy. It was "a votive offering which is to be given to the Levites." The annual interpretation is a later midrashic development.
- **Post-exilic reality contradicts P.** If priests composed P in the post-exilic period, "is it credible that the priestly authorities of later times bestowed this most lucrative of all sacred gifts upon lowly temple servants [Levites]?" Thousands of priests were impoverished while the tithe went to a handful of Levites. "The priests of those times should have created a tithe law which condemned them to penury, only to annul it in effect shortly afterward, is, of course, an absurd assumption."
- **No post-exilic tithe law was ever added.** Despite the need, "no explicit law providing for an obligatory priestly tithe made its way into the Torah." This proves the Torah was fixed and unalterable by post-exilic times.

**Evidence presented — Priests and Levites:**
- **The degradation theory is implausible.** Neither D (Deut 18:6-8) nor II Kings 23 indicates rural priests were demoted. "II Kings 23:9 speaks of a personal penalty that applied only to those who actually served at the high places" — they retained priestly status.
- **Levites existed as a class in the Exile (Ezra 2:40; 8:15ff).** "They cannot, then, have come into being as a result of the centralization of worship, or in accordance with Ezekiel's demand." Their existence "requires not merely a pre-exilic, but a pre-Josianic origin for the class."
- **The vast majority of rural priests were NOT demoted.** The 20,000+ returning priests "could not have stemmed from the family of Zadok; the vast majority of them must have been descendants of rural priests."
- **P presents Levite status as an honor, not a degradation.** Contrast Ezekiel, for whom Levitical status is "a punishment and a disgrace." P portrays Levi's consecration as a reward for loyalty at the golden calf (Exod 32:26-29).
- **Ezekiel was ignored.** His plan to replace Nethinim with Levites "was also disregarded." The post-exilic Levites were a revival of an ancient class from P's scrolls, not Ezekiel's creation.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is Kaufmann's most detailed and textually sophisticated argument. The tithe argument is genuinely clever. But the larger chronological inference remains fragile.

**What's at stake:** The tithe/priesthood argument is Wellhausen's strongest evidence for P's lateness. If Kaufmann can overturn it, Wellhausen's castle loses its foundation stone.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition reads the same data oppositely. The three tithe laws are evidence of DEVELOPMENT: from votive to annual, from local consumption to centralized consumption, from priestly portion to Levitical portion. The contradictions show successive stages, not parallel origins. P's complex sacrificial system (sin offerings, guilt offerings, graded purification) shows a systematization absent from earlier sources. The Levitical hierarchy (high priest → priests → Levites) mirrors the stratified society of the Persian period.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann has a point about the implausibility of priests composing laws that disadvantage them. But the counter-explanation is that P wasn't composed as a practical legal manual — it was composed as an idealized charter. The Levitical tithe in Num 18 may preserve the memory of an earlier practice (votive tithes to temple guardians) even if the text was composed late. Similarly, the priest/Levite distinction may have pre-exilic roots (as Kaufmann argues) while still receiving its definitive textual formulation in P during the exile — preserving old distinctions in a newly systematized form.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most impressive argument in the chapter. The tithe evidence is genuinely hard to explain on the assumption that P was composed by post-exilic priests for their own benefit. The priest/Levite argument is also more nuanced than most critics acknowledge — the simple "rural priests → Levites" narrative doesn't account for all the data Kaufmann marshals.

But Kaufmann's conclusion ("therefore P is the oldest source") goes beyond his evidence. What his data supports is: (1) P preserves genuinely old priestly traditions; (2) the simple post-exilic-composition model has problems. It does NOT support "P was composed before D." The tithe laws could be ancient TRADITIONS preserved in a late DOCUMENT. The priest/Levite distinction could have pre-exilic ROOTS while receiving its full literary EXPRESSION in the exile. Kaufmann consistently conflates "this tradition is old" with "this document is old."

## Claim 6: The Torah literature's historical horizon ends at the early monarchy — proving its antiquity

**Author's claim:** "There is, then, no doubt that the Torah literature was crystallized at the latest by the beginning of the monarchy." The Torah "alludes briefly to the new political institution of kingship, but did not flourish on its soil."

**Evidence presented — multiple lines:**
1. **The promised land boundaries pre-date the conquest.** The boundaries include Philistia, Lebanon, Tyre, Sidon, Byblos — places Israel never conquered or aspired to conquer later. Crucially, Transjordan is EXCLUDED — "why should territory conquered by Moses, settled by Israelite tribes...have been excluded by patriots from their dreams?" The boundaries "are a legacy from before the conquest...an ancient oracle promising the land of Canaan to Israel."
2. **The Torah's national enemy is Amalek, not later foes.** Amalek was "the first of the nations" (Num 24:20) — defeated by David and never a major threat afterward. Aram, the bitter enemy from David's time, is still "part of the family" (Jacob's covenant with Laban). "Edom's later enmity is nowhere foreshadowed." No reference to Assyria, Egypt, or Babylon as enemies.
3. **The patriarchal narratives contain genuinely ancient details.** The connection of Simeon and Levi, the primogeniture of Ishmael/Esau/Reuben/Manasseh "corresponds to nothing in later times." The notion of Edom's fraternity "has no ground in later events."
4. **Only one law deals with monarchy (Deut 17:14-20).** The Torah legislation "reflects not the monarchy, but the early 'kingdom of God' based on the primitive democracy that existed in premonarchic Israel." Property laws "know only the tribal and familial basis."
5. **Torah's exile descriptions are pre-exilic fantasies.** The Torah imagines exiles as "dispersed bands, wandering in the lands of their enemies, harried and persecuted. Their lives hang daily in the balance." This bears no resemblance to the Babylonian exile, where exiles "built houses, planted vineyards" (Jer 29:4ff). "The image of exile in the Torah must, therefore, be an ancient one, antedating the historical experience."
6. **Genesis is the most ancient stratum.** Genesis preserves unique anthropomorphisms (God walking in the garden "at the cool of the day"), folkloristic naïveté ("sons of God" consorting with women), customs attested in Hammurabi and Hittite law, and "no trace of the influence of literary prophecy." Genesis lacks Israel's unique religious status — Abraham "is a God-fearing, just man; but neither in the promises made to him nor in those made to his descendants is the distinction of possessing YHWH's Torah...mentioned."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the observation that Torah contains genuinely ancient traditions. LOW on the inference that the FINAL FORM of Torah literature was "crystallized at the latest by the beginning of the monarchy."

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right about the early crystallization, the critical model (in which Torah literature was still being composed in the exile) is impossible. The Torah becomes a direct witness to pre-monarchic Israelite religion.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition acknowledges that the Torah contains ancient traditions (Genesis narratives, early poetry, legal precedents). But the FINAL LITERARY FORM is a different question. The Promised Land boundaries, for example, could reflect early oracles preserved and reinterpreted by later writers. The absence of later historical references could reflect the literary SETTING (the Mosaic/Conquest period) rather than the date of COMPOSITION. Late authors writing about the Mosaic period wouldn't mention the Assyrian empire — they'd maintain the fiction of the narrative setting.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's data about the Torah's "historical horizon" is compatible with an alternative model: the Torah literature was composed OVER CENTURIES but deliberately maintained its Mosaic-period setting. The absence of references to the monarchy (except one law) and later enemies is a function of literary genre, not date of composition. Just as a modern historical novelist writing about ancient Rome doesn't mention airplanes, a post-exilic priestly writer composing Mosaic-period legislation wouldn't mention the Babylonian exile — especially if the text was meant to BE Mosaic law.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observations about the Torah's limited historical horizon are genuinely valuable. The Promised Land boundaries argument is particularly striking — the exclusion of Transjordan IS hard to explain on a late-composition model. The exile descriptions ALSO present a real problem: if the Torah's exile passages were composed after the Exile, why do they describe something completely different from what actually happened?

But Kaufmann's inference is too sweeping. "Contains ancient traditions" ≠ "was crystallized before the monarchy." The Torah could preserve pre-monarchic oracles about land boundaries, pre-monarchic enmity toward Amalek, and pre-exilic fantasies about exile WHILE STILL being compiled and edited centuries later. Kaufmann's argument shows that the Torah's CONTENT is ancient, not that its COMPOSITION was early — a crucial distinction he systematically elides.

## Claim 7: The Torah book was compiled without revision — proving the sources were already fixed and "canonical"

**Author's claim:** "The age of the Restoration was an age of compilation, not of edition and revision, let alone of innovation." The compilers transmitted the ancient scrolls intact, unrevised, without harmonizing contradictions — because the texts were already regarded as sacred and unalterable.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Narrative sources were blended, but legal corpora were preserved intact.** The redactors blended parallel narratives into one account, but "the laws, however, have not been so blended. Duplications and contradictions have neither been eliminated nor harmonized." This is not just a different approach — it's evidence that the legal texts were ALREADY FIXED and too sacred to touch.
2. **Embarrassing material was preserved unchanged.** The Torah retains stories "certainly offensive to later religious tastes" — the "sons of God" consorting with women, God walking in the garden at the cool of the day, Reuben with Bilhah, Judah with the "harlot," patriarchs erecting pillars and marrying forbidden relatives. "If the literature was still in flux...how could this embarrassing material have survived unaltered?"
3. **Obsolete laws were retained.** The tithe laws, the Levitical provisions, the home Passover — all "obsolete" by post-exilic times, yet preserved unrevised. The community accepted these laws "as 'found written' in the Torah" and tried to harmonize them midrashically rather than altering them.
4. **No new laws were added.** Despite the pressing need, "no explicit law providing for an obligatory priestly tithe made its way into the Torah." Even when economic realities made the existing law unworkable, the solution was midrashic reinterpretation (Ezra's "penalty"), not textual addition.
5. **Deuteronomy was the first stage, then JE+P were added.** "The nucleus of the Torah book was Deuteronomy, born out of the conviction that Israel could redeem itself only by steadfast adherence to the Torah of God." Later, "the ancient priestly materials transmitted from early times were added to the corpus of Deuteronomy."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the observation that the compilers treated the legal corpora differently from the narratives (preserving them intact). LOW on the inference that this proves the texts were fixed in pre-monarchic times.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right, the development of Torah literature STOPPED long before the exile. The post-exilic community were collectors, not composers. The Torah is a genuine witness to pre-exilic religion.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition acknowledges that post-exilic compilers preserved earlier materials — nobody claims they FABRICATED everything. But the selection, arrangement, and framing of materials IS composition. The fact that embarrassing stories were preserved doesn't prove they were already "canonical" — it could reflect the compilers' commitment to preserving tradition even when it was awkward. The legal contradictions were retained because the compilers believed ALL the laws were divinely given — a theological commitment, not proof of pre-existing fixity.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the post-exilic community treated the Torah texts as authoritative. But this doesn't require those texts to have been CENTURIES old. A text can be treated as authoritative shortly after composition — especially if it's attributed to Moses. The preservation of embarrassing material and obsolete laws proves the compilers' REVERENCE for the texts they had, not the texts' absolute antiquity. And the lack of new additions to the Torah could simply mean the community considered the Torah CLOSED — a decision that could have been made at any point, early or late.

**My assessment:** This is a genuinely valuable observation about the character of the Torah's compilation. Kaufmann is right that the preservation of contradictions, embarrassing stories, and obsolete laws is hard to explain if composition and compilation were happening simultaneously. The most natural explanation IS that the texts were already considered authoritative when they were gathered.

But this only proves the texts were authoritative AT THE TIME OF COMPILATION (which Kaufmann dates to Ezra-Nehemiah). It doesn't prove they were composed CENTURIES earlier. A text can be composed, achieve authoritative status, and be preserved without revision — all within a single generation. The dynamic Kaufmann observes (texts treated as fixed and sacred) is compatible with composition in the exile or even early post-exilic period, followed by rapid canonization.

---

## Claim 8: The final synthesis — the Torah was sealed and canonized before prophetic literature was even ordered

**Author's claim:** "Thus, the Torah book was formed out of the desire of a penitent community to know and keep the law of God." The Torah literature "became the first stratum of the Bible, sealed and canonized at a time when prophetic literature was still unordered and still without decisive influence on the life of the people. The entire book is a testimony to the first age of Israelite religion, the age prior to literary prophecy."

**Kaufmann's proposed strata (from earliest to latest):**
1. Genesis 1-11 (Near Eastern materials; folkloristic naïveté)
2. Genesis 12-49 (formative acts/words; no prophecy)
3. JEP — Genesis-Numbers (no trace of Deuteronomic ideas)
4. Deuteronomy-II Kings (centralization influence apparent)
5-7. The three law corpora (JE laws, P laws, D laws)
8. The Torah-group as a whole (Genesis-II Kings; no prophetic influence)
9. Literary prophets (the climax of biblical religion)
10. Hagiographa (post-exilic synthesis)

**Confidence:** VERY LOW. This is the conclusion toward which the entire chapter has been driving — and it's the least supported by the evidence presented.

**What's at stake:** Everything. If the Torah was "sealed and canonized" before prophecy, then monotheistic law existed before the prophets. The prophets become traditionalists, not innovators. Israelite religion was monotheistic from the beginning. The entire critical reconstruction collapses.

**Who disagrees:** Effectively everyone. Even scholars sympathetic to Kaufmann's overall project (Tigay, Sommer) do not follow him on P's dating. Tigay's onomastic evidence supports EARLY MONOLATRY but he doesn't date P early. Sommer accepts Kaufmann's literary insights about the distinction between Torah and prophetic worldviews but doesn't endorse his chronological reversal. The linguistic evidence for Late Biblical Hebrew in P — which Kaufmann does not address in this chapter — is one of the strongest results of biblical scholarship and directly contradicts his thesis.

**Alternative reading:** The Torah-group's independence from literary prophecy doesn't require chronological priority. It could equally reflect PARALLEL DEVELOPMENT: priestly Torah circles and prophetic circles produced different kinds of literature, with different concerns and different theological vocabularies, over the SAME centuries. The Torah was canonized first (which Kaufmann is right about) because it was considered more foundational, not because it was composed first.

**My assessment:** This is where Kaufmann's entire project stands or falls — and it's where he's weakest. The claim requires:
1. Dismissing the linguistic evidence for P's lateness (which he doesn't even engage)
2. Reinterpreting ALL evidence of institutional development (priestly hierarchy, sacrificial system) as archaic rather than evolved
3. Treating P's silence about centralization as proof of ignorance rather than programmatic idealism
4. Ignoring the convergence of multiple independent lines of evidence (language, institutions, historical references, literary relationships) that point to the Wellhausen sequence

Kaufmann has identified genuine weaknesses in the Wellhausen model: the codes are more independent than simple linear derivation allows; the Torah-group has a genuinely different outlook from prophecy; some traditions in P are demonstrably ancient; the silence of the Torah on later historical events needs explanation. But these weaknesses don't require his radical reversal. They require a MORE NUANCED version of the critical model — not its abandonment.

## Chapter V Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Torah-group independent of/prior to prophecy | MEDIUM-HIGH (independence); LOW (priority) | Parallel development, not sequence |
| 2: Three codes are independent crystallizations | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptively); LOW (chronologically) | Independence ≠ contemporaneity |
| 3: P lacks centralization, therefore is earliest | MEDIUM (descriptive); LOW (inference) | Silence could be programmatic, not ignorant |
| 4: P's high priesthood is pre-exilic | MEDIUM | Archaizing could produce same features |
| 5: P's tithe/priesthood laws are ancient | MEDIUM | Ancient traditions ≠ ancient document |
| 6: Torah's historical horizon proves early crystallization | MEDIUM (content); LOW (date) | Literary setting, not compositional date |
| 7: Torah compiled without revision = texts were fixed early | MEDIUM-HIGH (compilation); LOW (dating) | Fixity at compilation ≠ fixity centuries before |
| 8: Torah sealed before prophetic literature | VERY LOW | Linguistic evidence for late P; convergent evidence for Wellhausen sequence |

**Strongest section:** Claim 5 (tithes and priesthood). Kaufmann's detailed engagement with the tithe laws is the most textually sophisticated argument in the chapter, and he identifies genuine problems with the simple "post-exilic priests composed P for their own benefit" model. The implausibility of priests endowing Levites with the richest clerical due is a genuinely sharp point.

Also strong: Claim 1's observation that the Torah-group has a different theological outlook from prophecy. This is real and important, even if the chronological inference doesn't follow.

**Weakest section:** Claim 8 (the final synthesis). This is where Kaufmann's argument reaches its most radical conclusion — and where the evidence is thinnest. The leap from "the Torah contains ancient traditions and was canonized before the prophets" to "the Torah literature was composed before the prophets" is a non sequitur.

Also weak: Kaufmann's **complete silence on linguistic evidence**. This is the elephant in the room. P contains distinctive vocabulary, syntax, and phraseology characteristic of Late Biblical Hebrew — features shared with Ezekiel, Chronicles, Ezra-Nehemiah, and other demonstrably late texts. This is one of the most robust results of biblical scholarship, and it directly contradicts Kaufmann's thesis that P is the earliest source. Kaufmann doesn't address it in this chapter. He owes his readers an explanation for why P's language looks late if P is actually the earliest document — and he doesn't provide one.

**The chapter's logical structure:** Kaufmann's argument has a "pincer" shape. One arm argues that the Torah-group is independent of prophecy (therefore early). The other arm argues that P lacks centralization (therefore pre-D). The two arms converge on the conclusion that P is the earliest source. But both arms rely on the same logical move: treating *silence* (about prophecy, about centralization) as evidence of *ignorance* rather than *choice*. And both arms ignore the possibility of PARALLEL DEVELOPMENT — that priestly and prophetic traditions could coexist, each producing literature reflecting their own concerns, over the same centuries.

**Cross-references:**
- [[Tigay — Introduction]] — Tigay's onomastic study uses an independent dataset to argue for early monolatry WITHOUT accepting Kaufmann's dating of P. Tigay's strength is precisely that he breaks the circularity Kaufmann identifies.
- [[Tigay — Chapter II + Conclusions]] — Tigay's Conclusions (especially claims 1 and 4) partially vindicate Kaufmann's picture of popular religion as predominantly Yahwistic and largely fetishistic. But Tigay explicitly limits his conclusions to the 8th-6th centuries and doesn't date P early.
- [[Romer — Introduction and Chapter 1]] — Römer explicitly traces monotheism's gradual emergence from polytheism, directly contradicting Kaufmann's "monotheism from the beginning" thesis. Römer's model of "sedimentary strata" is essentially the critical consensus Kaufmann is fighting.
- [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.8 — The Deuteronomic Reform Movement]] — Albertz's account of Deuteronomy as a Josianic "mediating theology" that synthesized pre-state and state traditions fits the critical framework Kaufmann rejects. Albertz dates Deuteronomy to ~622 BCE, which Kaufmann would accept for D's *promulgation* but not for its *relationship* to P (which Kaufmann places earlier).
- [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Smith's claim that Israel was "broadly West Semitic in its religion" and that monotheism was "an inner-Israelite development over hundreds of years" is the polar opposite of Kaufmann's position. Smith's model of gradual differentiation from polytheism is what Kaufmann's entire project is designed to refute.

**Methodological note — the "silence = ignorance" fallacy:** Kaufmann's core argumentative move throughout the chapter is to treat textual silence as proof of chronological priority. The Torah-group doesn't mention literary prophets → therefore it's earlier. P doesn't mention centralized worship → therefore it's pre-D. The Torah doesn't mention the Assyrian empire → therefore it's pre-monarchic. This move is fallacious in every instance. Silence can equally reflect: (a) different genre/institutional setting, (b) deliberate programmatic choice, (c) the constraints of the narrative setting, or (d) genuine chronological priority. Kaufmann always chooses (d) without adequately excluding (a)-(c).

**What Kaufmann's best arguments actually support:** Not his radical reversal of the Wellhausen sequence, but a MORE NUANCED version of the critical model in which:
- The law corpora are more independent than simple linear derivation allows
- P preserves genuinely ancient priestly traditions, even if its final form is late
- The Torah-group and prophetic literature represent parallel streams, not a single evolutionary line
- The post-exilic compilers were more collectors than composers
- Some "late" features of P may actually be archaic survivals

This nuanced model preserves what's valuable in Kaufmann's observations without requiring the chronological reversal his evidence can't support. It's essentially the position held by many contemporary scholars — and it's a position Kaufmann's work helped make possible, even though he himself would have rejected it as insufficiently radical.
