# Schmid — §5: The Romantic Devaluation of the Concept of Theology

Schmid traces how the late 18th through early 20th centuries produced a downward revaluation of "theology" in favor of "religion." Schleiermacher's distinction (religion = living experience, theology = dead reflection) was absorbed by Hebrew Bible scholars who increasingly saw their task as excavating "living religion" from beneath theological redaction. The result: OT theology virtually disappeared, replaced by "history of Israelite religion."

---

## Claim 1: Schleiermacher's religion/theology distinction fundamentally restructured how biblical scholars understood their task

**Schmid's claim:** The late 18th and early 19th centuries introduced "the distinction between religion and theology that would become definitive for subsequent periods." Schleiermacher discovered religion as "the individual province of the mind" — a unique phenomenon that "cannot be evaluated sufficiently with the traditional tools of theology and philosophy. Religion is always more than what theology and philosophy are able to comprehend."

**Evidence presented:**

- Johann Salomo Semler and the Neology movement: theology as reflection ON religion
- Johann Gottfried Herder and Schleiermacher: religion is experience, feeling, sensibility — theology corrupts it
- De Wette explicitly injected Schleiermacher's concept of religion into exegetical disciplines
- The result: "Hebrew Bible studies developed an increasingly negative view of theology"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The intellectual history is well-documented. Schleiermacher's influence on 19th-century theology is standard.

**What's at stake:** This distinction is the ROOT of the modern disciplinary separation between "theology of the HB" and "history of Israelite religion." Schmid's project aims to heal this split — but to do so, he has to show that the split was a historical contingency, not a necessary development. The religion/theology distinction made sense in Schleiermacher's context but has outlived its usefulness.

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the historical facts. The question is whether Schmid is right that the distinction has outlived its usefulness, or whether it still captures something real about the difference between living practice and elite reflection. The HB IS elite scribal literature — so maybe Schleiermacher's instinct was right that it's "theology" (reflection) not "religion" (experience).

**My assessment:** There's an irony here that Schmid doesn't fully address. He insists the HB is elite literature (theology, not folk religion), yet he wants to undo the religion/theology split that was partially motivated by the same recognition. The HB IS theology in Schleiermacher's sense — it's reflective, systematizing, elite. Schmid wants to value that without denigrating it relative to "living religion." Fair enough — but the split had real intellectual content, not just Romantic prejudice.

## Claim 2: Duhm and the early historical critics practiced criticism so passionately because they believed it was the tool to excavate "true religion" from beneath "theology"

**Schmid's claim:** Bernhard Duhm "views his primary task in his work on the Hebrew Bible as 'examining the stage of religion prior to theology, or at least prior to being fully dominated by theology.'" Duhm's commentary on Jeremiah portrays the Deuteronomistic theologians as systematizers who killed the living prophetic impulse: "It is actually a tragedy of religion that the dead prophet kills the living one."

**Evidence presented:**

- Duhm, *Das Geheimnis in der Religion* (1896): the task is to find religion "prior to theology"
- Duhm, *Das Buch Jeremia* (1901): Deuteronomistic theology "kills" Jeremiah's living religion
- Schmid's interpretation: "One must unearth the living religion of the Hebrew Bible that lies beneath the theologically shaped and revised form. In the end, this conviction was one of the most important motivations for the success of historical-critical biblical scholarship"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Duhm quotations are direct. Schmid's interpretation (that the religion/theology distinction motivated historical criticism) is plausible and well-argued.

**What's at stake:** If Schmid is right that historical criticism was originally motivated by the religion/theology distinction, then the discipline's founding impulse was to reach PAST the texts to the "living religion" beneath. This is exactly the opposite of Schmid's own approach, which takes the texts' theological shaping as the primary object of study. Schmid is inverting the founding impulse of his own discipline — and he knows it.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see historical criticism as motivated primarily by Enlightenment rationalism rather than Romantic religion-seeking would narrate this differently. Duhm may be an outlier rather than representative.

**Alternative reading:** The religion/theology distinction was never the PRIMARY motivation for historical criticism. It was a post-hoc justification that appealed to Romantic sensibilities. The real driver was the Enlightenment commitment to treating biblical texts like any other ancient texts — which is methodologically the same move Schmid makes.

**My assessment:** Schmid's reading of Duhm is fascinating and important. It shows that the "theology is bad, religion is good" framework is baked into the discipline's origins. Schmid's project — taking "theology" seriously as the texts' own mode of reflection — is a genuine departure from this founding impulse. Whether that departure is correction or regression depends on whether Duhm was right that the theological shaping obscures something valuable, or whether Schmid is right that the theological shaping IS the valuable thing.

## Claim 3: The history-of-religions school absorbed OT theology — the subdiscipline "basically disappeared"

**Schmid's claim:** "As a result, theology of the Old Testament as a project and as a nominally identifiable discipline basically disappeared from the stage." Key figures simply treated "theology of the OT" and "history of Israelite religion" as synonymous — or replaced the former with the latter.

**Evidence presented:**

- Hermann Schultz (1896): "The task of biblical theology is a purely historical presentation" — theology = historical description of "faith perspectives and customary conceptions"
- Bernhard Stade (1905): "By biblical theology of the OT one understands the history of the religion under the old covenant"
- Karl Marti (1897): Simply retitled Kayser's *Theologie des Alten Testaments* to *Geschichte der Israelitischen Religion* — "By History of Israelite Religion, as well as by Theology of the Old Testament is meant that discipline concerned with the presentation of the religious and ethical content of the Old Testament"
- Rudolf Smend (1893): *Lehrbuch der alttestamentlichen Religionsgeschichte*
- Even conservative theologies (Oehler 1891) adopted historical-genetic framing, though within salvation-history framework
- New Testament parallel: William Wrede's *Über Aufgabe und Methode der sogenannten Neutestamentlichen Theologie* (1897) — "strictly historical character of New Testament theology"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The titles and quotations are direct evidence. The merger of theology into history of religion is an empirical fact of 19th-century scholarship.

**What's at stake:** This is the crucial historical precedent for Schmid's own project. The 19th century tried to solve the theology/history problem by ABSORBING theology into history. Schmid's project is the opposite: he wants to restore theology as a distinct (but still historical) enterprise. He needs to show why the 19th-century absorption was inadequate — which sets up the neo-orthodox reaction in §§7-8.

**My assessment:** Schmid's genealogy is compelling. The pendulum swung from theology (pre-critical) → history of religion (19th century) → neo-orthodox theology (early 20th) → crisis (now). Schmid proposes a synthesis: historical theology. Whether this is a genuine synthesis or just a relabeling of history of religion with "theology" is the question the rest of the book must answer.

## Claim 4: Hermann Gunkel articulated the most radical version — "the spirit of historical scholarship" had replaced the doctrine of inspiration, and "theology" should yield to "religion"

**Schmid's claim:** Gunkel argued that both "theology" AND "biblical" were problematic terms. "Theology" because Schleiermacher showed religion is more than reflection. "Biblical" because the canon boundary is artificial — "the maturing and blossoming religion from the soil of the Israelite nation is an extraordinarily diverse picture, in which all its forms and orientations, higher and baser, should be recognized."

**Evidence presented:**

- Gunkel, RGG (2nd ed.): "the living religion of the heart was quite prominent, while reflection on religion plays a comparatively minor role. Neither the nature of the Israelite people nor the period tended toward reflection"
- "The fence implied in the word 'biblical' continues to fall"
- "If one looks deeper...one notices that [biblical theology] is dominated by the early church doctrine of inspiration"
- Gunkel's Genesis commentary foreword: "Whoever calls himself a theologian must study religion"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Gunkel quotations are direct and unambiguous.

**What's at stake:** Gunkel represents the extreme version of the position Schmid is arguing AGAINST. If Gunkel was right — that Israelite religion was primarily "living religion of the heart" with minimal theological reflection — then Schmid's project (a theology of the HB as reflective literature) is fundamentally misguided. Schmid needs to show that Gunkel was wrong about the texts' character, not just about the discipline's method.

**My assessment:** Schmid quoted Gunkel at length, and I appreciate his intellectual honesty in presenting the strongest version of the opposing view. But Schmid hasn't yet answered Gunkel's substantive claim: that the HB doesn't contain much "theology" in the reflective sense. Schmid will need Parts E-H to demonstrate that the texts DO contain the kind of reflective, synthesizing theology he claims. Gunkel's challenge hangs over the entire project.

## §5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Schleiermacher's religion/theology distinction restructured biblical studies | HIGH | Overstates Schleiermacher's influence relative to other factors |
| Duhm used historical criticism to excavate "religion" from "theology" | HIGH | Duhm may be atypical |
| OT theology disappeared into history of religion | VERY HIGH | The titles don't lie |
| Gunkel's radical rejection of "theology" as inspiration-doctrine residue | HIGH | But Gunkel's claim about the texts' character is unevaluated here |

**Strongest section:** The documentation of OT theology's absorption into history of religion (Schultz, Stade, Smend, Marti). The title changes are empirical and devastating — you can't argue with a discipline that literally renamed itself.

**Weakest section:** Schmid hasn't yet answered Gunkel's substantive claim that the texts contain minimal reflective theology. He's presented Gunkel's position clearly but hasn't refuted it. That refutation must come in the constructive parts of the book.

**Overall for the Truth project:** This chapter is essential background but not directly engaged with the dating/Israel/Judah questions. The key takeaway: Schmid's historical-theology project is a deliberate reversal of the 19th-century religion-over-theology consensus. He's rehabilitating "theology" as a legitimate category for describing what the HB actually IS — not just what later tradition made of it.
