# Keel & Uehlinger — Part III: The Hidden God, Victorious Gods, and the Blessing of Fertility (Iron Age I)

Part III surveys the iconographic repertoire of Iron Age I Palestine (ca. 1150–1000 BCE) — the period of Philistine settlement, the emergence of early Israel in the hill country, and the final withdrawal of Egyptian imperial control. K&U argue that this period's seal amulets, figurines, and cult stands reveal two dominant and sometimes overlapping concerns: (1) aggressive, triumphant male deities who dominate enemies, and (2) a depersonalized concern for animal and plant fertility, now almost entirely stripped of anthropomorphic goddess imagery. The Egyptian god Amun — "the hidden one" — emerges as a surprisingly widespread figure whose cryptographic lion-name propagates via mass-produced seals from Philistine-controlled Gaza into the Shephelah and hill country. Meanwhile, the Canaanite warrior gods Seth-Baal and Reshef are shown standing *on* their former opponents (lion, gazelle) rather than fighting them, a shift from combat to command. The chapter's most consequential argument is that the disappearance of the anthropomorphic goddess during Iron I was a necessary precondition for Yahweh's later absorption of all fertility-blessing functions in Iron II.

---

## Claim 1: The repertoire of deities contracts dramatically from the Late Bronze Age — and what survives is overwhelmingly male, aggressive, and Egyptian-mediated

**Authors' claim:** "By comparison with the repertoire of deities in the Late Bronze Age, that of Iron Age I is reduced markedly. Ptah, whose presence is noted at many different sites during the Late Bronze Age, disappears completely." (§62)

The authors argue that the iconographic "cast of characters" shrinks radically. Egyptian goddesses like Hathor and the Egyptian-Canaanite hybrid "Lady Anat" vanish. What remains is a short list of male deities — Amun, Seth-Baal, Reshef — transmitted largely through Egyptian mass-produced glyptic art, plus a few local bronzes (a bull figurine near Dothan, an enthroned male from Hazor). The reduction is not just quantitative but qualitative: the deities who survive are those associated with hiddenness, triumph, and domination.

**Evidence presented:**
- Ptah, widely attested in LBA across multiple sites, leaves zero Iron I attestations
- The goddess repertoire collapses: "As the Egyptians departed, their goddesses, such as Hathor, and also the Egyptian versions of goddesses, such as the lady Anat, disappeared from Palestinian iconography. Images of female deities made using costly materials (metals) are entirely absent in Iron Age I." (§79)
- Even Egyptian cults that survive (Beth-Shean Str. V B stelae) are located in former garrison cities, not new foundations
- The only new anthropomorphic female figure introduced — the Philistine "Ashdoda" type, a seated goddess fused with her throne — "disappears totally at the end of Iron Age I" (§73)
- The anthropomorphic figures that remain "show deities that are exclusively masculine, domineering, and triumphant" (§79)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The empirical basis is strong: K&U work from the full corpus of ~8,500 stamp seals, and the pattern of goddess disappearance is consistent across all media (glyptic, metal, terracotta plaques). The negative evidence is genuinely significant here because Ptah, Hathor, and qudshu-type figures were *common* in LBA — their absence is not a sampling artifact.

**What's at stake:** If the iconographic record genuinely shows goddesses vanishing *before* the rise of Israelite monolatry, then the standard biblical-studies narrative — that Yahwistic reformers *suppressed* a thriving goddess cult — may have the chronology backwards. The goddess was already receding from elite/urban iconography before Yahweh became dominant.

**Who disagrees:** William Dever (*Did God Have a Wife?*, 2005) argues that the ubiquitous Judean pillar figurines of Iron II demonstrate persistent goddess devotion. K&U would respond that these terracottas are cheap, non-elite, and represent "personal piety" — precisely the register where goddess imagery survives, but without attributes, without names, and without temple backing. The disagreement is partly about *which* evidence counts most.

**Alternative reading:** The disappearance of goddesses from *seals* could reflect changes in seal *use* (fewer women owning seals? shift from personal to administrative sealing?) rather than changes in *belief*. K&U acknowledge that terracotta plaques show "some continuity" (§79) but dismiss them as marginal to the "symbol system."

**My assessment:** The pattern is real and striking. But K&U may overstate the case when they say female imagery was "no longer directly connected with a personal power who was acting consciously." The suckling mother animal, the tree, and the scorpion are all traditional *attributes* of a goddess — so the question is whether Iron I users understood these as depersonalized "numinous powers" or as the goddess herself, now represented aniconically through her symbols. K&U insist on the former; I suspect the answer varies by region and social class.

## Claim 2: The Amun temple at Gaza propagated the "Hidden God" throughout Philistine-controlled territory via mass-produced seals with cryptographic lion-names

**Authors' claim:** "The cult of this Egyptian deity figure could have influenced the cult of the Canaanite El, who was thought of in a similar way and then consequently might have had an impact on the way Yahweh was conceived of as well." (§64)

K&U reconstruct an extraordinary picture: Ramesses III established (or supported) a temple of Amun at Gaza — a "house that no one can enter" (Pap. Harris I 9.1-3) — to which "foreigners from the Levant come to him and present him with their gifts." This temple's workshops mass-produced steatite scarabs and faience seals whose base designs cryptographically write the name *Ymn* ("Amun") by combining the reed-stalk (*y*), the lion (*m3y* = *m*), and the sun-disk or *nb*-lord sign (*n*). The lion-image simultaneously evokes the god's hiddenness and his aggressive power.

**Evidence presented:**
- Pap. Harris I explicitly names a temple of Amun in *p3 Kn'n* (Canaan/Gaza) that receives tribute from the Levant (§62)
- Three scarabs from Beth-Shemesh and Tell el-Far'ah South indicate temple treasuries in the Shephelah helped fund it (§62)
- Hieratic ostraca from Tel Sera and Tel Haror are probably connected to the tribute system for this temple (§62)
- A unique scarab from Tell el-Far'ah South appears to show a Philistine prince before a ram-headed Amun figure (illus. 129; §62)
- Truncated pyramid seals — a new Iron I type found "only in areas where the Philistines exercised control" — repeatedly feature cryptographic *Ymn* and *Ymn-r'* writing (§63)
- The Megiddo hoard (Str. VI A, 11th c.): of 25 identifiable seal amulets, 9 (36%) bear the name Amun cryptographically — "by far" the dominant motif (§64)
- The name is written semi-cryptographically on the Tel Gerisa seal using lion + sun-disk + reed-stalk combinations (illus. 131; §63)
- Oval faience seals with bundled handles, typical of Iron I, also "dominate" with Amun names (§63)

**Confidence:** HIGH regarding the *presence* of Amun cryptography on seals. MEDIUM regarding the *cultic influence* on El and Yahweh. The seal evidence is solid and quantified. But the chain from "Amun seals are widespread" to "Amun's nature influenced El/Yahweh" is inferential and rests on a similarity argument (both are "hidden" high gods).

**What's at stake:** If Amun-the-hidden-one influenced El, and El was later merged with Yahweh, then one root of Yahweh's transcendence and hiddenness may trace to an *Egyptian* theological import mediated through Philistine Gaza — a far more complex genealogy than either the "Yahweh came from Midian" or "Yahweh was always a Canaanite storm god" narratives allow.

**Who disagrees:** Mark S. Smith (*The Early History of God*, 2002) emphasizes El's indigenous Canaanite character and sees Yahweh-El convergence happening internally within Israelite religion, without Egyptian mediation. De Moor (1990, cited approvingly by K&U) is one of the few who argues for early El-Yahweh connection, but he traces it to northern Syria/Ugarit, not Egyptian Amun.

**Alternative reading:** The Amun seals could reflect Egyptian cultural prestige and apotropaic magic rather than theological influence. A Philistine wearing an Amun seal may have cared about the *power* it represented, not the Amun-El conceptual parallel. The Egyptian *Wenamun* tale (ca. 1075, cited by K&U at §64) shows Amun's name used as a diplomatic and commercial claim ("The sea is his, and Lebanon is his"), not necessarily a theological statement absorbed by locals.

**My assessment:** The Amun-at-Gaza reconstruction is one of the most fascinating and under-appreciated arguments in the book. The concentration of Amun seals in Philistine territory is hard to explain otherwise. But the El-Yahweh influence claim is the weakest link — it's a "could have" that K&U never quite demonstrate. More persuasive is their observation that the *mode* of Amun's presence (hidden, known through cryptographic name, lion-empowered) created a template for how a high god could be simultaneously absent from anthropomorphic depiction and overwhelmingly present in symbolic discourse.

## Claim 3: Seth-Baal and Reshef shift from combat to command — standing on their former opponents rather than fighting them — reflecting a new emphasis on domination over process

**Authors' claim:** "Images of Reshef wielding a weapon and Baal-Seth in battle are not typical of the Iron Age I. Images showing them in command and standing on animals become the norm." (§66)

In the LBA, Seth-Baal fights the serpent and conquers lions; Reshef brandishes weapons. In Iron I, both are shown standing triumphantly *on* their animal opponents — Seth-Baal on a lion (probably representing Mot, the god of summer drought and death), Reshef on a gazelle. The shift is from narrative combat to static domination, from *how they win* to *that they rule*.

**Evidence presented:**
- The truncated pyramid seals from Tel Qasile and Tel Gerisa show a winged, bearded god with horned cap standing *on* a lion — identified as Seth-Baal (§65)
- LBA scarabs showed Seth-Baal defeating the serpent and conquering lions (illus. 88a–90a); Iron I pieces show him standing *on* the lion as a pedestal, not as an active opponent (§65)
- K&U suggest the posture may have been borrowed from the qudshu-figure type, since the goddess standing on a lion virtually disappears in Iron I glyptic — "Baal-Seth appears to have taken over the role of the goddess" (§65)
- Reshef on a gazelle appears both on carefully executed scarabs (illus. 137a–b) and on truncated pyramid seals alongside Seth-Baal (illus. 138a–b)
- Local, possibly Philistine-produced seals show a generic deity on an unidentifiable animal — suggesting the "god-on-animal" motif became a widely copied template for lordship (§66)
- A small bronze from Megiddo (Area BB, Str. V B, ca. 1050–975) likely depicts Reshef with weapon and shield — but K&U wonder whether such pieces are LBA heirlooms rather than Iron I productions (§66)
- The relationship between Baal-Seth and the sun god (LBA) is reconfigured: "the sun god is replaced frequently by Amun" (§65)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The iconographic shift is visually unambiguous and can be tracked across the published seal corpus.

**What's at stake:** If the primary imagery for warrior gods shifts from *fighting* to *standing-on*, this suggests a theological change: deities are no longer defined by what they do (storm, battle, defeat chaos) but by what they rule over. This is precisely the direction that Yahweh theology takes — Yahweh is less often depicted *fighting* Leviathan than *ruling over* the nations. The "standing on" posture is inherently royal and static; it prepares the ground for a god who is primarily *king* rather than *warrior*.

**Who disagrees:** John Day (*Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, 2000) would argue that Yahweh's combat mythology (Chaoskampf) remained central throughout, pointing to Psalm 74, Isaiah 27, and Job. The iconographic shift K&U document may reflect Egyptian glyptic conventions rather than Canaanite-Israelite theology.

**Alternative reading:** The shift from combat to command could be an artifact of the *medium* — seal amulets are small and favor static, emblematic compositions over narrative scenes. The "combat" may have migrated to other media (textiles, wall paintings, oral poetry) that haven't survived. The seal evidence shows a change in *what gets put on seals*, not necessarily in how people thought about their gods.

**My assessment:** K&U's observation is genuine and important, but I think they underplay one crucial implication they themselves hint at: Seth-Baal adopting the *qudshu* posture (standing on a lion) represents a *masculinization of a formerly feminine symbolic position*. The goddess who stood on a lion in LBA (as "Mistress of Animals" or as a militarized qudshu figure) disappears, and the male warrior-god steps into her exact visual slot. This is not just "the goddess recedes" — it's "the male god appropriates her symbolic position." This has implications for how we understand Yahweh's later absorption of Asherah's attributes.

## Claim 4: The "Conqueror" imagery — pharaoh striking enemies, the lion-attack, and the archer — is stripped of Egyptian specificity and generalized into symbols of aggressive triumph available to anyone

**Authors' claim:** "It is probable that knowledge about the relationship with Egyptian royal iconography was lost gradually, so that this depiction became a popular motif associated with triumph in general." (§71)

K&U trace a fascinating evolution: LBA scarabs show the pharaoh in his war chariot, with full regalia (uraeus, blue crown), striking enemies or hunting. In Iron I, the pharaonic insignia disappear. The chariot-archer loses his crown and uraeus; the chariot itself vanishes from some scenes, with the archer continuing on foot and the chariot-horse morphing into a lion. The triumphant *lion* (originally a metaphor for pharaoh) becomes an independent, generalized symbol of aggressive power — one that can even appear on seals bearing the name of Amun.

**Evidence presented:**
- The classic "striking down of the enemies" scene survives on only a few pieces (Megiddo hoard, Beth-Shean, Tel Masos, Tell el-Far'ah South) — sometimes with a worshiper in the background, showing the pharaoh still dominates (§70)
- The pharaoh-as-lion motif (striding over an enemy, attacking a caprid from behind) appears on pieces from Megiddo and Tel Masos; the Tel Masos scarab is carved from bone, showing local artisans adopted it (§71)
- Chariot-archer scenes lose pharaonic insignia — the archer "is now shown without any of the typical insignia of the pharaoh (uraeus, blue crown)" and targets both caprids and human beings (§72)
- In the next stage, the chariot disappears entirely and the horse becomes a lion — "the drawn bow is now the dominant power over caprids, human beings, and lions" (illuss. 147a–b; §72)
- A composite scene shows a lion attacking a caprid while itself being attacked by an archer — K&U interpret this as "a high level of aggressiveness that the person owning the image wanted to possess" (§71)
- A sherd from early Iron Age Shiloh shows a panther(?) assaulting a stag — "Aggressiveness, as an attribute to be valued" (§71)
- "Baal-Seth and Reshef... are represented in a parallel manner in the socio-political symbol system by the archer who takes aim at caprids and lions (and human beings)" (§72)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The iconographic genealogy is meticulously traced and the progressive stripping of Egyptian markers is documented across multiple find-sites and seal types.

**What's at stake:** This is a "democratization" of royal imagery — the pharaoh's exclusive claim to aggressive triumph becomes a generalized attribute that any seal-owner can appropriate. This parallels what happens in Israelite theology: Yahweh absorbs royal prerogatives (kingship over the gods, divine warrior, judge of nations) that in surrounding cultures belonged to the king or to a pantheon of specialist deities. The Iron I seals show, in miniature, the cultural process by which "triumph" becomes a transferable quality rather than a royal prerogative. It also raises the uncomfortable question of whether Yahweh-as-conqueror is simply the Israelite version of this generalized, democratized Egyptian royal aggression.

**Who disagrees:** Ziony Zevit (*The Religions of Ancient Israel*, 2001) might argue that the biblical conquest traditions reflect genuine historical memory rather than borrowed royal iconography. K&U are not making a direct claim about biblical texts here, but the implication is clear: the "conqueror" motif in Israel's symbolic world has an Egyptian pedigree mediated through Philistine glyptic art.

**Alternative reading:** The stripping of Egyptian markers could reflect not "generalization" but *de-skilling* — Iron I seal-cutters were less proficient and simply couldn't execute detailed pharaonic regalia. The "local adaptation" argument works against this, however: the bone scarab from Tel Masos shows deliberate choices, not incompetence.

**My assessment:** The evolution from pharaoh-in-chariot → archer-on-foot → archer-as-lion-dominance is one of the book's most elegant demonstrations of how iconographic motifs transform across cultural boundaries. For the Truth Project, the key takeaway is that "Yahweh the divine warrior" did not emerge in a vacuum — the very *vocabulary* for depicting divine triumph over enemies was being forged in Iron I through the Philistine-mediated adaptation of Egyptian royal imagery. When early Israelite poets described Yahweh as a lion (Hos 5:14, 13:7-8) or as an archer (Hab 3:9, Ps 7:12-13), they were drawing on a visual-cultural repertoire that was already widespread.

## Claim 5: The goddess disappears from anthropomorphic iconography and is replaced by depersonalized fertility symbols — the suckling mother animal, the scorpion, and the stylized tree

**Authors' claim:** "As the goddess disappeared, being represented only by the use of her attribute symbols, the personality of this power faded into the background as well. Blessing and fertility were no longer directly connected with a personal power who was acting consciously but was thought of increasingly as a numinous power and force." (§80)

This is arguably K&U's most consequential argument for the entire book. They claim that during Iron I, the anthropomorphic "Naked Goddess," the "Branch Goddess," and the qudshu-type goddess all vanish from the seal repertoire. Fertility is no longer personified as a female deity but is instead evoked through her former *attributes* — the suckling cow, the scorpion (connected to the love goddess through mating-dance symbolism), and the stylized tree flanked by caprids. These become "icons that depict blessing" (§79) — depersonalized, transferable, and eventually absorbable by a male deity.

**Evidence presented:**
- The "Branch Goddess" and qudshu-type goddess "disappeared from the iconography of Palestine" in Iron I (§77)
- The suckling mother animal (cow with calf, head turned back) appears on quartz conoids — a new motif, probably imported from northern Syria, that "remained in widespread use from Iron Age I through Iron Age IIB — ca. 1150-750" (§76)
- On a conoid from Megiddo Str. VI, the suckling cow is a quartz import with the backward-turned head characteristic of Syrian origin (§76)
- Locally produced black calcite conoids simplify the motif (head forward), sometimes adding a scorpion above the animal's back (Beth-Shemesh, Tell el-Far'ah South, Tel Eitun; §76)
- The stylized tree appears on "crude, light-colored limestone seals that are typical of Iron Age I" as a stand-alone blessing icon — without the goddess who normally accompanies it in LBA (§77)
- The tree flanked by caprids — the classic "Mistress of the Caprids" composition — can still be seen on some Iron I conoids from Tell el-Far'ah South and Taanach, but it's "not entirely clear" whether the central figure is a tree or an anthropomorphic tree-goddess (§77)
- "We now find images of the female deity only on inexpensive terracottas... only the simplest forms of the 'naked woman,' without attributes... used almost only in settings where 'personal piety' was practiced and were used primarily for religious rites performed by women" (§79)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the iconographic shift; MEDIUM on the theological interpretation. The visual evidence that *anthropomorphic* goddesses disappear from seals is solid. But whether seal-users understood the suckling cow and tree as "depersonalized numinous powers" or as the goddess herself, now represented aniconically, is inherently uncertain.

**What's at stake:** This is the lynchpin of K&U's entire argument about how Israelite religion became possible. If the goddess was already being "de-personified" in Iron I — reduced to her attributes — then Yahweh's later absorption of fertility functions in Iron II wasn't a violent suppression of a living goddess cult. It was the *assignment* of already-depersonalized blessing icons to a male deity. The goddess didn't have to be killed; she had already faded into her own symbols, which Yahweh could then claim. This substantially revises the standard feminist-historiographical narrative (e.g., Raphael Patai, *The Hebrew Goddess*) that imagines an active, persecuted goddess driven underground by patriarchal Yahwism.

**Who disagrees:** William Dever, as noted above, argues the Judean pillar figurines prove ongoing goddess devotion. Francesca Stavrakopoulou (*God: An Anatomy*, 2021) might push back on the "depersonalization" thesis by arguing that the biblical Yahweh was himself understood in highly embodied, gendered terms — so the goddess didn't disappear; she was absorbed into Yahweh's own body/character. Susan Ackerman (*Warrior, Dancer, Seductress, Queen*, 1998) would note that women's religious practice (where terracotta goddesses survive) is marginalized in K&U's seal-focused methodology.

**Alternative reading:** The shift from anthropomorphic goddess to her symbols could reflect an *aniconic* turn in goddess worship, not its decline. In many religious traditions, aniconism signals *elevation*, not demotion — the deity becomes too sacred/hidden/powerful to depict. K&U consider this possibility for Amun ("the hidden one") but not for the goddess. The suckling cow and tree may have been *more* potent as symbols precisely because they evoked the goddess without limiting her to a human form.

**My assessment:** K&U's argument is elegant and powerfully evidenced, but I think it's overstated. They describe the shift as the goddess's "personality fading" and fertility becoming "numinous power." But the suckling cow, scorpion, and tree had *always* been associated with a goddess — and the fact that Iron I seal-users still chose these specific symbols (rather than, say, geometric patterns or entirely new motifs) suggests continuity of reference. The question is whether the reference was understood as "the goddess is present in this symbol" or "this symbol has autonomous power now that the goddess is gone." K&U assert the latter; I find the evidence compatible with both readings. Furthermore, their own evidence about the Ben-Anat warrior class (§78) shows that goddess traditions persisted in specific social groups — the name *'Abdlabi'at* means "servant of the Lioness," an epithet of a goddess. So the goddess didn't vanish; she became socially stratified.

## Claim 6: The Philistines introduced Mycenaean goddess imagery and male cultic music, but both were largely assimilated or abandoned by the end of Iron I

**Authors' claim:** "The melding together of the figure and the chair (throne) as a symbol of rulership is reminiscent of the preference for depicting the 'Mistress' in Late Bronze Age Beth-Shean. ... After this time period the 'Ashdoda' type disappears. It apparently did not survive the move to accommodate Philistine symbolism to the indigenous system." (§73)

K&U document a specifically Philistine contribution to Iron I iconography that proves to be a dead end. The "Ashdoda" — a seated goddess fused with her throne, derived from Mycenaean female idols — flourishes in the 12th century at Ashdod, Ekron, and Aphek, but vanishes by the 11th. Meanwhile, a new element of *male* cultic music (lyre players, double-pipe players, tambourine) appears on seals and cult stands, replacing the female court musicians of the LBA. Female imagery survives only in the specialized domain of mourning rites — small figurines of women striking their hands above their heads, decorating the rims of burial vessels.

**Evidence presented:**
- Mycenaean female idol fragments found in LBA levels at Beth-Shemesh and Hazor — pre-Philistine imports (§73)
- The "Ashdoda" (illus. 148) and many fragments of similar figures found at Ashdod, Ekron, and Aphek — "quite popular during the twelfth century" (§73)
- A fragment from Tel Qasile Str. X (late 11th c.) shows one such figure holding a child at her breast — the last gasp of the type (§73)
- "After this time period the 'Ashdoda' type disappears. It apparently did not survive the move to accommodate Philistine symbolism to the indigenous system" (§73, citing Bunimovitz 1990)
- Male lyre players: an anchor-shaped seal from Tel Batash (Timnah, 12th/11th c.) shows a stylized lyre player, confirmed by a more realistic half-seal from Ashdod (Iron II level but Iron I style) showing a seated lyre player, perhaps performing before a deity (illus. 149a; §74)
- The Ashdod cult stand (late Iron I): five figures in window-like cutouts — four musicians (lyre, double pipe, tambourine) and a fifth female figure (possibly a singer with exposed breasts) (§74)
- The "Orpheus jug" from Megiddo Str. VI A: a male lyre player emerging from a "fairy-tale world" of animals and a giant lotus blossom — K&U compare it to a Babylonian boundary stone showing a similar "terrestrial world" between heaven and underworld (§74)
- "In Iron Age I — something new by contrast with the Late Bronze Age — cultic and/or palace music was performed exclusively by men" (§74; cf. 1 Sam 16:14-23, 18:10)
- Mourning women: Mycenaean-derived practice of decorating vessel rims with small figurines of women striking hands above heads; found in Philistine regions "but not only there" (§75)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the Ashdoda evidence; MEDIUM on the "exclusively male musicians" claim. The cult stand and seal evidence is compelling but sparse.

**What's at stake:** The Philistine case study demonstrates that even a militarily dominant culture (the Philistines defeated the Israelites repeatedly in 1 Samuel) could not sustain its own goddess iconography against the indigenous Canaanite pattern of goddess-erasure. The Ashdoda disappears not because Israelites suppressed it but because Philistines themselves "accommodated" to local symbolic norms. This has implications for how we understand cultural transmission: military power does not equal symbolic power. It also suggests that the shift away from anthropomorphic goddesses was a *regional* phenomenon, not specifically an Israelite one — the Philistines, with a robust goddess tradition (Mycenaean idols), abandoned it within two centuries of settlement.

**Who disagrees:** Trude Dothan (*The Philistines and Their Material Culture*, 1982), the primary excavator of Ashdod, might emphasize the continuing Philistine distinctiveness rather than their assimilation. The Ashdoda *type* disappears, but Philistine bichrome pottery and other cultural markers persist.

**Alternative reading:** The Ashdoda may have been replaced by *other* forms of goddess worship that are archaeologically invisible — perishable wooden statues, textile imagery, or oral traditions. Its disappearance from the archaeological record doesn't prove the goddess cult ended; it may have become aniconic in the Philistine sphere, just as K&U argue happened in the Canaanite-Israelite sphere.

**My assessment:** The most striking detail here is the gendered shift in music: LBA court music included female lyre players (illus. 65); Iron I cultic music is exclusively male. This parallels the broader masculinization of public religion that K&U document across multiple media. For the Truth Project, the takeaway is that the Israelite Temple's all-male Levitical musicians (1 Chronicles 25) were not a uniquely Yahwistic innovation but part of a broader Iron I pattern of excluding women from public cultic performance. Whether this represents "Israelite" influence on Philistines or a shared regional development is unclear — but either way, it predates the monarchy.

## Claim 7: The two dominant factors of Iron I religion — aggressive domination and depersonalized fertility — map precisely onto the two main literary complexes of the Hebrew Bible: the conquest narratives and the patriarchal promises

**Authors' claim:** "The iconographic symbol system used during Iron Age I highlights two factors... One factor is aggressiveness, oriented toward superiority and domination; it appears in the Hebrew Bible, roughly speaking, in the exodus narratives, in the book of Judges, and in the narratives describing the battles against the Philistines in 1 Samuel. ... The second factor is evident in a desire for human, animal, and agricultural fertility, as can be detected most clearly in the imagery of the suckling mother animal. This is shown most obviously in the Hebrew Bible in the patriarchal histories in Genesis." (§80)

K&U offer a concluding synthesis that ties their iconographic findings to the biblical text — not as proof of historicity, but as evidence that the Iron I *symbol system* (the available vocabulary for expressing religious concerns) matches the thematic preoccupations of the earliest biblical narrative complexes. They note that these two factors "are not mutually exclusive" and point to the Gideon cycle (Judges 6–8) and the Ben-Anat warrior class as evidence of their combination.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Baal-Seth/Baal-X tradition for newly founded hill-country settlements: "The significance of the Baal-(Seth) tradition for the newly settled groups, who were colonizing the hill country, appears also in the names of the numerous newly established sites in the region that made up their names by using the pattern Baal-X" (§80, citing Isserlin 1957 and Rosen 1988)
- The Gideon traditions combine concerns with fertility (Gideon's threshing floor, the fleece) and military aggression (the Midianite campaign) in a single narrative complex (§80)
- The Ben-Anat warrior class: about two dozen arrowheads from Lebanon and el-Hadr (near Bethlehem), dated 11th century, inscribed with owner names. Arrowhead no. 5 reads *'bdlb't / bn 'nt* — "servant of the Lioness, son of Anat" — linking warriors directly to a goddess (§78)
- The name *'Abdlabi'at* (also found at Ugarit) means "servant of the Lioness," where *Lb't* is an epithet of the goddess — not "Lion Lady" (Dever's translation) but "Lioness," emphasizing her aggressive character (§78, n. 8)
- A recently published dagger blade from the early Iron Age watchtower at Giloh is stamped with a stylized tree — linking the tree-symbol (fertility/blessing) to warrior identity markers (§78)
- "Whoever wants to use this evidence to show the continuing significance of one or more goddesses in early Iron Age Palestine must immediately narrow the focus and recognize that this evidence can be linked to traditions that are at home only within specific social classes" (§78)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The correlation between iconographic themes and biblical narrative complexes is suggestive but inherently loose — the biblical texts were written/edited centuries later. The Ben-Anat evidence is genuinely compelling for goddess survival in warrior circles.

**What's at stake:** This synthesis is K&U's answer to the question: "What does Iron I iconography tell us about early Israel?" Their answer is that early Israel's symbolic world was organized around these two poles — domination and fertility — and that the biblical texts preserve this dual structure, even if the specific narratives are later literary products. It means the patriarchal promises of land, progeny, and blessing (Genesis) and the conquest/warrior traditions (Exodus–Judges) are not arbitrary theological constructs but reflect the actual religious concerns of Iron I populations as documented in their seals and amulets.

**Who disagrees:** Minimalist historians (e.g., Philip Davies, Niels Peter Lemche) would reject any correlation between Iron I material culture and biblical narratives on the grounds that the texts are Persian or Hellenistic period constructs with no connection to Iron I reality. K&U's approach is more nuanced than direct correlation — they're talking about *symbol systems*, not events — but minimalists would still find the linkage methodologically suspect.

**Alternative reading:** The two-factor model could be an artifact of the *medium* (seals tend to depict power/domination and fertility/prosperity because those are what seal-owners want) rather than a reflection of the full religious landscape. Oral traditions, household rituals, and non-elite practices (which don't produce seals) may have been organized around entirely different themes. K&U acknowledge this implicitly when they note that terracotta figurines (personal piety, women's rites) tell a different story.

**My assessment:** The two-factor model is elegant but risks reductionism. K&U themselves note that the Ben-Anat evidence shows goddess traditions persisting in warrior circles — which means the "aggression" factor and the "fertility" factor were not cleanly separated into male and female domains but could be combined in the figure of a warrior goddess (Anat as both suckling cow and battle-frenzied destroyer). The combination of archer and suckling mother animal on the Gezer and Tell el-Far'ah South conoids (§78) makes exactly this point. So the two factors are real but their relationship is more complex than K&U's summary suggests: aggression and fertility could be fused in a single figure (Anat), separated into complementary male/female deities, or depersonalized into blessing-icons. The biblical text preserves all three configurations.

## Methodological Reflection

K&U's methodology in Part III is at its strongest and also reveals its characteristic limitation. The strength: by restricting themselves to securely stratified, dateable seal amulets and figurines, they produce a chronologically controlled picture that text-based scholars simply cannot match. The limitation: the very rigor of their method means they are looking at a *residue* — the durable, depositional record of seal use and discard — and extrapolating a "symbol system" from it. Seal amulets were worn on the body, used for sealing documents, and deposited in graves and hoards. They represent a specific *register* of religious expression: portable, personal-but-not-private, associated with administrative and commercial activity. The religion of the seal-wearer may not be the religion of the farmer, the mother, the child, or the priest. K&U are aware of this and acknowledge it in their discussion of terracottas (women's piety) and the Ben-Anat class (warrior piety). But the structure of their argument — moving from seals to "the iconographic symbol system used during Iron Age I" in the singular — tends to flatten these social distinctions. The truth is probably that Iron I had *multiple* symbol systems, some overlapping and some in tension, and that the seal-based system K&U reconstruct so brilliantly was the one that eventually "won" — becoming the substrate for Israelite state theology — but was not the only game in town.
