# Schmid — §§2-4: The Concept of Theology from Plato to Gabler

These three short subsections trace the evolution of "theology" as a term: from Plato, where it meant myth, through the Reformation's existential reinterpretation, to Gabler's 1787 lecture that split biblical theology from dogmatics — the birth of OT theology as a discipline. Schmid's purpose is to show that the current confused state of the term is the product of contingent historical developments, not a stable definition.

---

## §2: The Premodern Conception — From Mythology to Systematic Doctrine

### Claim 1: "Theology" originates in Greek philosophy as derogatory — it meant myth, not systematic doctrine

**Schmid's claim:** "The concept of theology originates from philosophical discourse." In Plato, θεολογία means "legend of the gods" — myths requiring "critical evaluation" for educational purposes. Aristotle lists it among the "theoretical philosophies" but "primarily uses it to denote mythology." Josephus and Philo use it as "almost an alternate term for religion" — not a reflective or synthesizing approach.

**Evidence presented:**

- Plato, *Republic* 379a: θεολογία as myth needing critical assessment
- Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 1026a.18-19, 1064b.1-3: theology as one of three theoretical philosophies alongside mathematics and physics, but still primarily mythology
- Josephus, *Contra Apionem* 1.225: "our theology" = Jewish religion
- Philo, *De Vita Mosis* 2.115: Moses as "theologian"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is philological fact. The Greek sources are unambiguous.

**What's at stake:** If theology ORIGINALLY meant myth, then the later development into systematic doctrine is a historical contingency, not the term's "natural" meaning. This supports Schmid's argument that "theology" can legitimately mean something other than normative systematics — including his own historical-descriptive approach.

**Who disagrees:** No serious disagreement about the philology. Some might argue that the term's ORIGIN is less important than its developed meaning, but Schmid is making a genealogical point, not an etymological one.

**My assessment:** This is standard lexicography, not controversial. Schmid's use of it is effective: if the term started as "mythology," the claim that "real" theology must be normative/systematic is historically false. The term's meaning has shifted repeatedly.

---

### Claim 2: Scholasticism transformed theology from "doctrine of God" to "entirety of Christian doctrine" and made it an academic discipline

**Schmid's claim:** "Observable contours of the modern conceptualization of 'theology' first arise in the High Middle Ages. These dimensions especially concern the expansion of the concept of theology from the doctrine of God to the entirety of Christian doctrine." The emergence of university *facultas theologica* in the 11th-12th centuries "provided the foundation for the further predominance of the concept of theology."

**Evidence presented:**

- Aristotelian reception enabled systematic exposition of Christian doctrine as an academic task
- University theology schools gave theology its academic connotation: "a process involving reason and systematic reflection"
- This scholastic understanding "have largely persisted into the modern period" in English-speaking scholarship — theology = intellectualizing and systematizing
- Schmid notes that criticisms of theology as reductionist or inappropriate for historical inquiry "generally present caricatures of what Scholasticism intended"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The historical development is well-documented. The connection between Aristotle's reception and the rise of scholastic theology is standard medieval intellectual history.

**What's at stake:** This scholastic equation of theology with systematic doctrine is what later reformers and biblical theologians reacted AGAINST. Schmid is setting up the genealogy: Plato (myth) → Scholasticism (system) → Reformation (existential relationship) → Gabler (historical discipline). Each shift was a response to the previous definition's inadequacies.

**My assessment:** Schmid's genealogy is efficient and accurate, though compressed. The key insight: the "theology = systematics" equation that dominates English-speaking scholarship is a medieval development, not inherent to the term.

## §3: The Reformation's Reconstrual — From Doctrine to Relationship

### Claim 3: Luther redefined theology from speculative doctrine of God to existential relationship with God — creating confusion between "theology" and "religion"

**Schmid's claim:** "The Reformation introduced an influential modification to the concept...This semantic shift involved a change from defining 'theology' in terms of the doctrine of God to defining it in terms of the relationship of the person with God." Luther: "The proper subject of theology is man, sinful, guilty, and abandoned, and God the justifier and savior of sinful man." And: "true theology is practical, therefore speculative theology belongs in hell with the devil."

**Evidence presented:**

- Luther, *Lectures on Psalm 2, 45, and 51* (1532): theology's subject is sinful humanity and justifying God
- Luther, *Table Talks*: "true theology is practical"
- Luther: "God and faith belong in one heap"
- Luther "continued to hold firmly to the notion of theology despite his rejection of Aristotelian-influenced scholasticism"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Luther quotations are directly cited and well-known. The Reformation's existential turn is standard intellectual history.

**What's at stake:** This Luther move is the ROOT of the later confusion between "theology" and "religion." By defining theology as the relationship with God rather than doctrine about God, Luther made theology overlap with what later thinkers would call "religion." Schmid traces the current terminological muddle to this Reformation moment.

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the Reformation's existential turn. The question is whether Schmid's genealogy (connecting Luther's move to the later theology/religion confusion) is correct or overdetermined.

**My assessment:** This is a genuinely illuminating historical observation. The theology/religion distinction that plagues modern scholarship (is theology normative? historical? descriptive?) traces back to Luther's decision to keep the term "theology" while redefining its content to be practically indistinguishable from what would later be called "religion." Schmid's genealogy explains why the confusion exists — it's not just sloppy thinking, it's built into the term's history.

### Claim 4: This book is "Protestant in terms of the history of scholarship" — not in content

**Schmid's claim:** "The present theology of the Hebrew Bible also participates in the effects of this fundamental decision. Is it then a Protestant theology of the Hebrew Bible? This is true in one sense. It also shares this fate with other subdisciplines of Hebrew Bible studies. Historically speaking, Protestant biblical scholarship bears the primary responsibility for forming and developing the disciplines and methods of Hebrew Bible studies since the eighteenth century."

**Evidence presented:**

- "The present volume on the theology of the Hebrew Bible does not set a particularly Protestant accent in terms of its outline or content"
- But "it is Protestant in terms of the history of scholarship"
- The Reformation's definition of theology as interpretation of experiences with God "has broadly permeated Hebrew Bible studies"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The historical claim (Protestant scholarship shaped the discipline) is undeniable. Whether Schmid's work is genuinely non-Protestant in content is more debatable — see my assessment in the Preface and §1 notes.

**What's at stake:** This is Schmid attempting to acknowledge his confessional location while maintaining that his method is not confessionally determined. It's more honest than the "neither Christian nor Jewish" framing but still somewhat evasive.

**My assessment:** This is a better version of the confessional-location claim than the Preface offered. Schmid admits Protestant influence "in terms of the history of scholarship" — that's honest. Whether he can actually do non-confessional theology from within a Protestant faculty is still an open question, but at least he's not claiming neutrality anymore.

---

### Claim 5: Protestant orthodoxy returned to scholastic systematizing — and the dicta probantia method treated the Bible as prooftext quarry

**Schmid's claim:** "Protestant orthodoxy of the later sixteenth and seventeenth centuries returned to the systematizing forms of theology that were common prior to the Reformation." The *dicta probantia* (prooftexts) method treated the Bible as a source of quotations to support doctrinal positions.

**Evidence presented:**

- Melanchthon preferred *doctrina christiana* to "theology"
- Bartholomäus Keckermann's *Systema S.S. Theologiae* (1602) listed theology alongside other academic disciplines
- Johann Gerhard's *Loci theologici* (1610-1622) as comprehensive academic presentation of doctrine
- Sebastian Schmidt's *Collegium biblicum* (1671) — the paradigmatic prooftext work, listing OT and NT quotations by theological topic

**Confidence:** HIGH. Standard history of Protestant scholasticism.

**What's at stake:** This sets up the crisis: Protestant orthodoxy's prooftext method collapsed when historical-critical scholarship showed the Bible didn't say what the prooftexts needed it to say. The "slackening of the unity between text and systemization" is what created the need for biblical theology as a mediating discipline. Without understanding the prooftext crisis, Gabler's project is unintelligible.

**My assessment:** Schmid's genealogy is clear: Luther existentialized theology → Protestant orthodoxy re-systematized it → historical criticism exposed the gap → Gabler tried to bridge it with "biblical theology." This is compressed but accurate.

## §4: From Biblical Theology to the Theology of the Old Testament

### Claim 6: Gabler's 1787 lecture created "biblical theology" as a HISTORICAL discipline mediating between Bible and systematics — but his solution failed

**Schmid's claim:** Johann Philipp Gabler's 1787 inaugural lecture *Oratio de justo discrimine theologiae biblicae et dogmaticae* is the conventional starting point for biblical theology. Gabler distinguished biblical theology (historical, reconstructing what biblical authors thought) from dogmatic theology (contemporary, formulating theology for the present). He further distinguished "true biblical theology" (historically bounded) from "pure biblical theology" (universal, timeless perspectives) — the latter being the bridge to systematics.

**Evidence presented:**

- Gabler's lecture formalized earlier efforts: Hofmann (1770), Turretini (1776), Büsching (1756, 1758), Cocceius, Calixt
- "Gabler in no way lamented the breakup of the Bible and systematics; he instead attempted to conceive of an appropriate determination (*discrimen*) of the process"
- Gabler's four-part model: Bible → true biblical theology → pure biblical theology → systematics
- But "Gabler's edifice was not stable. Specifically, 'pure biblical theology' was shown to be a Trojan horse"
- Gotthilf Traugott Zachariä's alternative (1780): biblical theology as polemical critique of systematics — but he "was in danger of replacing traditional dogmatism with the biblicism that also consumed him"

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is standard disciplinary history. Gabler is universally recognized as the founder of biblical theology as a distinct discipline.

**What's at stake:** Gabler's move is the beginning of modern biblical theology — AND the beginning of its problems. By trying to save both history (what the Bible MEANT) and normativity (what it MEANS), Gabler created a tension that has never been resolved. Schmid's own project (descriptive historical theology) is essentially choosing the "true biblical theology" side of Gabler's dichotomy and abandoning the "pure biblical theology" bridge to normativity.

**My assessment:** This is Schmid setting up his own project in the lineage of Gabler's historical side while rejecting Gabler's normative ambitions. Schmid is more honest than Gabler: he admits the bridge to normativity can't be built, rather than pretending "pure biblical theology" can extract timeless truths from historically contingent texts.

---

### Claim 7: The split of biblical theology into separate OT and NT theologies (Bauer, 1796) introduced theological PLURALITY into the concept of "theology"

**Schmid's claim:** Georg Lorenz Bauer's *Theologie des A.T.* (1796) is "usually seen as the first standalone representative of the new subdiscipline." This sundering "results in the first conception of theology that contains a notion of plurality. The Bible does not have a single theology, but...at least two theologies." After this, "no fundamental obstacles stood in the way of the further pluralization of theology that unfolded over the course of the twentieth and the beginning of the twenty-first centuries."

**Evidence presented:**

- Bauer 1796: first independent OT theology
- Subsequent separate treatments: Gramberg, Bruno Bauer, Hävernick, Hitzig, Kayser, Dillmann
- De Wette's *Biblische Dogmatik* (1813) and Vatke's *Die biblische Theologie* (1835) still attempted whole-Bible approaches but through historical-developmental frameworks
- The Old and New Testaments "do not even fit together in terms of a progressive linear sequence"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The historical facts are undisputed. The interpretive claim (that this opened the door to theological pluralism) is a logical inference: if the Bible has TWO theologies, why not more?

**What's at stake:** This is the crucial moment in Schmid's genealogy. Once "theology" can be plural, the door opens to "theologies of the Hebrew Bible" — the position Schmid partially endorses but partially resists. His book is a theology (singular) of the HB, but one that acknowledges internal plurality. The tension between singular and plural is baked into the discipline's history.

**My assessment:** Schmid's genealogy of theological pluralism is compelling. The move from one Bible → two Testaments → two theologies → many theologies is a logical unfolding of implications already present in Gabler and Bauer. Schmid's own position (theology in the singular, but with acknowledged internal plurality) tries to hold the tension rather than resolve it. Whether that's coherent or evasive depends on the execution.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Theology originated as "myth" in Plato | VERY HIGH | None — philological fact |
| Scholasticism made theology academic/systematic | HIGH | None — standard intellectual history |
| Luther redefined theology as existential relationship | HIGH | None — direct quotations |
| This book is Protestant only in scholarly lineage | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Outline or content" vs. "history of scholarship" is a blurry distinction |
| Protestant orthodoxy returned to prooftext method | HIGH | None — standard history |
| Gabler created biblical theology as historical discipline | HIGH | None — foundational to the discipline |
| Bauer's OT/NT split introduced theological plurality | VERY HIGH | None — the fact of the split is documented; the interpretation follows logically |

**Strongest section:** The genealogy from Plato through Gabler is clean, well-documented, and genuinely illuminating. Schmid shows that every major definition of "theology" was a response to a prior definition's failures — there is no stable meaning, only a series of reactions.

**Weakest section:** The "Protestant only in terms of scholarly history" claim is more honest than the Preface but still somewhat evasive. Schmid wants to have it both ways: Protestant enough to claim continuity with the tradition, not Protestant enough to be bound by its normative commitments.

**Overall:** These background sections are historiographical rather than argumentative, so there's less to evaluate critically. They succeed in their purpose: showing that the term "theology" has no stable meaning and that Schmid's descriptive-historical approach is as legitimate as any other in the term's long and contested history. The genealogy is efficient and well-sourced. The real test is whether Schmid's constructive proposal (Parts E-H) delivers on the promise set up here.
