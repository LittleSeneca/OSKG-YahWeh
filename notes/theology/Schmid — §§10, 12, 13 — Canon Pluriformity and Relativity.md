# Schmid — §§10, 12, 13: Canon Comparison, Christian Reception, and the Relativity of Canon

These three sections bracket the crucial §11 (Tanakh formation). §10 maps the pluriformity of canons across traditions. §12 traces the Septuagint's development and the Christian double-canon. §13 argues for the relativity of the canon — the boundary between canonical and non-canonical is historically contingent, not theologically absolute.

---

## §10: The Pluriformity of the Tradition

### Claim 1: "Neither the Old Testament nor the Hebrew Bible exists" — there are multiple canonical forms

**Schmid's claim:** The Jewish and Christian traditions preserve different arrangements AND different inventories of books. The Hebrew Bible has 24 books in three parts; the Septuagint expands this with additional books; the Vulgate adds still more; Protestant Bibles return to the Hebrew inventory but keep the Septuagint order; Eastern churches have their own canons.

**Evidence presented:**

- Detailed comparison tables showing four canonical orders (Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, Vulgate, Protestant) across 3 pages
- The Torah is the only section with fixed order across all traditions
- The Nevi'im has at least 9 variations in the Latter Prophets; the Ketuvim at least 70 different orders
- The Babylonian Talmud (b. B. Bat. 14b-15a) orders the Major Prophets as Jeremiah-Ezekiel-Isaiah-Twelve — with a THEOLOGICAL justification ("entirely judgment...half judgment, half consolation...entirely consolation") that Schmid shows is a rationalization of an order based on book LENGTH (Jeremiah 21,835 words > Ezekiel 18,730 > Isaiah 16,392 > Twelve 14,355)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. Empirically verifiable from any Bible comparison.

**What's at stake:** If the canon's shape is historically contingent (not divinely ordained), then theology cannot appeal to canonical order as carrying theological authority. The order-and-arrangement arguments that some canonical theologies rely on are undercut.

**My assessment:** Schmid's deconstruction of the Babylonian Talmud's "theological" ordering is a perfect example of his method: what looks like a theological decision turns out to be a material one (scroll length), retroactively theologized.

---

### Claim 2: Canonical differences do NOT serve denominational theological interests — they're accidents of philological history

**Schmid's claim:** "It is theologically noteworthy that the different Old Testaments of the various denominations do not serve the special theological interests or accentuations of the various churches. The books of Sirach and Tobit contribute just as little to the support of Roman Catholic doctrine as does the book of Jubilees to the Ethiopian church. The divergent canonical boundaries are instead based in different philological and historical developments and decisions."

**Evidence presented:**

- Roman Catholic canon includes Sirach, Tobit, Wisdom, Maccabees — none of which directly support distinctive Catholic doctrines
- Ethiopian canon includes Enoch and Jubilees — not distinctively Ethiopian theological concerns
- The Council of Trent (1545) was the "one and only decree regarding the canon" — a Counter-Reformation political decision, not a theological one
- Protestant churches "stipulated that only books of the Hebrew Bible should remain in the canon" — a humanistic/philological decision, not a theological one

**Confidence:** HIGH. Schmid's observation that canonical differences don't map onto theological distinctives is counterintuitive but persuasive.

**What's at stake:** This undercuts any argument that "my canon is theologically superior." The differences are accidental, not driven by theological content.

**My assessment:** This is an elegant observation. It's also a subtle argument FOR Schmid's own project: if canon boundaries are historically contingent, then a historical theology of the HB shouldn't be bound by any particular canon's boundaries.

## §12: Reception and Transformation into the Old Testament

### Claim 3: Early Christianity had no "Old Testament" — just "the Law and the Prophets" — and established a double-canon, not a supersession

**Schmid's claim:** "Earliest Christianity...were familiar neither with an Old Testament nor a New Testament. There was not even a stable collection of literature." NT references to "the Law and the Prophets" reflect the still-fluid two-part canon. Critically: "the writings of the New Testament were neither joined to the Hebrew Bible — in something like a fourth part of the canon... — nor made superior to the Hebrew Bible as the normative interpretive authority. Christianity instead came up with a double canon in which neither part is superior or inferior to the other."

**Evidence presented:**

- Luke 16:16, 29, 31; 24:27, 44; Acts 26:22; 28:23 — all use "Moses and the prophets" or "law of Moses, prophets, and psalms"
- NT writings were occasional writings, not a planned canon; Gospels established liturgically around 100 CE
- Marcion (2nd century) was the first to propose dismissing the OT — early Christianity REJECTED this
- "The New Testament canon that was developed preserved the theological polyphony of early Christianity"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The NT references and Marcion controversy are well-documented.

**What's at stake:** Schmid is arguing that Christianity's double-canon is a deliberate theological decision to maintain polyphony, not to establish supersession. This has implications for how Christian theology should relate to the HB — not as "old" (superseded) but as part of an ongoing conversation.

**My assessment:** Schmid's reading of the double-canon as deliberately preserving polyphony is an important theological argument. It means the HB's theological voices are not "fulfilled" by the NT — they continue to speak alongside it.

---

### Claim 4: The Septuagint likely drove the CLOSURE of the Hebrew Torah — a translated Torah can't be extended

**Schmid's claim:** "The translation was likely one of the decisive factors for the literary closure of the Hebrew Torah; a Torah that has already been translated can no longer be successfully extended."

**Evidence presented:**

- Septuagint translation of Torah began ~250 BCE in Alexandria
- The Letter of Aristeas provides the legendary account
- Translation of other books continued into the 2nd century CE
- The Septuagint is a conglomeration of individual translations — varying from "extremely literal to relatively free"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The causal claim (translation → closure) is plausible but speculative. The correlation (translation by 250 BCE, apparent closure by then) is stronger than the causation.

**What's at stake:** This provides an additional argument for Torah closure by ~250 BCE — consistent with Schmid's Persian-period dating. If the Torah was translated by 250 BCE, it must have been substantially complete.

**My assessment:** This is a clever argument that I haven't seen elsewhere. The logic is sound: once a translation exists, any additions to the Hebrew would create discrepancies with the Greek. The translation functioned as a kind of textual "freeze." Whether this is the CAUSE of closure or merely a CONSEQUENCE of an already-closed text is debatable.

---

### Claim 5: The Septuagint's placement of prophets at the END likely serves Christian theological interests — prophecy → fulfillment

**Schmid's claim:** "Placing the prophets in the final position appears most likely to serve Christian theological interests: then the Old Testament and the New Testament relate to one another as the prophecy of the Christ and the fulfillment of this prophecy in the emergence of Jesus of Nazareth."

**Evidence presented:**

- The Sirach prologue (originating in Alexandria) is oriented toward the Torah-Prophets-Writings order — not the Septuagint's order
- The Christian codices vary in details but consistently place prophets last
- The theological rationale (prophecy → fulfillment) is a Christian interpretive move

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. It's debated whether the Septuagint's order originated in Hellenistic Judaism or was a Christian innovation. Schmid favors the Christian-origin theory.

**What's at stake:** If the Septuagint order is a Christian innovation, then the Jewish canon's order (Torah-Nevi'im-Ketuvim) is the original one. The Christian rearrangement is theologically motivated — it makes the OT point toward the NT.

**My assessment:** Schmid is probably right that the Septuagint order is Christian, but the evidence isn't definitive. The Sirach prologue's orientation toward the Jewish order in Alexandria is suggestive but not conclusive.

## §13: The Meaning and Relativity of the Canon for a Theology of the Hebrew Bible

### Claim 6: The canon's boundaries cannot be absolute for a historically oriented theology — a "center and periphery" model is better

**Schmid's claim:** "The boundaries of the canon cannot serve fundamentally as absolute for a historically oriented approach to the theology of the Hebrew Bible." Instead, adopt a "center of recognized texts that is surrounded by different concentric circles of further writings that established themselves to successively lesser degrees in Judaism and Christianity."

**Evidence presented:**

- Other literature from the Second Temple period (Jubilees, Enoch, Temple Scroll, pesharim) was "dedicated to the theological pursuit of treating and resolving difficulties in the preexisting tradition"
- These texts "are highly important for the reconstruction of the historically complex thought world of the Bible, particularly because they are reckoned as part of the Bible in some canons"
- Jubilees: "little Genesis" — resolves the problem of how ancestors could follow the law before Moses gave it (through "heavenly tablets")
- Enoch literature: elaborates Gen 6:1-4, reveals heavenly secrets, preserved in Ethiopian canon
- Temple Scroll (11Q19-21): systematizes Torah law as direct divine speech, possibly intended to supersede Torah
- "What belongs to the center and to the periphery depends on multiple factors determined more by historical coincidence than by the importance of the content of each text"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The historical observation (that non-canonical texts contain theology comparable to canonical ones) is correct. The normative claim (that canon shouldn't be absolute) is a methodological judgment.

**What's at stake:** This is Schmid's final word on canon: it's historically contingent, theologically non-absolute, and should not constrain historical-theological inquiry. This frees him to use extra-canonical literature (Jubilees, Enoch, Qumran) as theological data alongside canonical texts.

**Who disagrees:** Canonical approach scholars (Childs, Seitz) who argue the canon IS the theologically significant boundary. For them, excluding non-canonical texts is not arbitrary — it reflects the community's theological discernment.

**Alternative reading:** The canon's boundaries, while historically contingent, represent genuine theological judgments by the communities that preserved these texts. Schmid's "historical coincidence" framing undervalues the theological intentionality of canonical decisions.

**My assessment:** Schmid's "center and periphery" model is historically accurate and methodologically liberating. But he may overstate the "historical coincidence" factor. The exclusion of Enoch from rabbinic Judaism was not arbitrary — it reflected theological judgments about pre-Mosaic revelation and apocalyptic speculation. Canonical decisions may be historically contingent AND theologically motivated. Schmid's reduction of canon to "accident" is his most reductive move.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| No single "Bible" exists — multiple canonical forms | VERY HIGH | Empirically verifiable |
| Canonical differences aren't theologically driven | HIGH | Counterintuitive but persuasive |
| Early Christianity had double-canon, not supersession | HIGH | Historical evidence is clear |
| Septuagint translation drove Torah closure | MEDIUM | Correlation ≠ causation |
| Septuagint order is Christian, not Jewish | MEDIUM | Evidence is suggestive, not conclusive |
| Canon boundaries are historically contingent, not absolute | MEDIUM-HIGH | Normative judgment, not empirical claim |

**Overall:** These sections provide essential framing for §11's dating arguments. Schmid's canon relativism — the claim that canon boundaries are accidental rather than theologically significant — is both his most freeing move and his most reductive one. It lets him do historical theology without canonical constraint, but it may undervalue the theological reasons communities had for their canonical decisions.
