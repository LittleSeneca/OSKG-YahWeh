# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapter 1

## The God of Israel and His Name

---

## Claim 1: The original pronunciation of Yhwh was "Yahô" or "Yahû," not "Yahweh"

**Römer's claim:** "The ancient pronunciation of the name of the god of Israel was 'Yahô,' which amounts to saying that the tetragrammaton was originally a trigrammaton. The w in 'Yhwh' was not a consonant, but a mater lectionis indicating the sound 'o.'" The pronunciation "Yahweh" is probably a later theological development derived from Exodus 3's wordplay on the root "to be."

**Evidence presented:**

- **The short forms:** Theophoric names consistently use Yhw (Yahu/Yaho) — Jeremiah (*Yirmĕyāhû*), Isaiah (*Yĕša῾yāhû*), Jonathan (*Yĕhônātān*). The short form Yāh appears in liturgical exclamations (*hallĕlû-yāh*) and poetry. "This suggests that the short form of the divine name was pronounced 'Yahu/Yaho.'"
- **Babylonian transliterations (6th century BCE):** Judeans in Babylon wrote their names with *ia-a-ḫu-ú* (Yahu) or *ia-a-wa* (Yahwa). The majority evidence points to Yahu/Yaho.
- **Elephantine papyri (5th century BCE):** The Judean community in Egypt called their god Yhw, vocalized as "Yahô" in personal names.
- **Qumran Greek manuscript (4QpapLXXLevb):** Renders the tetragrammaton as Iaṓ — two syllables, pronounced *ia-o*, corresponding to Hebrew/Aramaic Yahô.
- **Diodorus of Sicily (1st century BCE):** Writes that Moses received laws "from the god named Iaṓ."
- **The Masoretic substitution:** The vowels applied to Yhwh were those of *'ădōnāy* (Lord), creating the artificial form YĕHoWaH — which a 13th-century Dominican mistook as the actual pronunciation, producing "Jehovah."
- **The Church Fathers:** Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-220 CE) gives Iaoue; Epiphanius (4th century) gives Iabé; Theodoret (5th century) says Samaritans say Iabé, Jews say Aiá. These suggest "Yahweh" was known in some circles.
- **Römer's resolution:** The "Yahweh" pronunciation may have developed from theological speculation on Exodus 3:14 (*'ehyeh 'ašer 'ehyeh* — "I am who I am"), connecting the divine name to the root *h-y-h* ("to be"). The causative form "he who causes to be" (= creator) would yield "Yahweh." But this is a later learned construction. The ORIGINAL pronunciation was Yahô/Yahû.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on Yahô/Yahû being the more ancient form (the Elephantine and Babylonian evidence is strong). MEDIUM that "Yahweh" is a later theological development rather than a parallel ancient pronunciation (the Church Father evidence suggests "Yahweh" was known earlier than Römer allows).

**What's at stake for faith:** Moderate. If the divine name originally meant something like "he blows" (storm god) rather than "I am who I am" (philosophical being-itself), the most famous self-revelation of God in the Bible is a later theological reinterpretation, not the original meaning. The God who said "I AM" to Moses was originally a storm god whose name meant "he blows."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain "Yahweh" is the original pronunciation (based on the Church Father testimonia and the Exodus 3 wordplay being ancient rather than late).

**Alternative reading:** Both pronunciations could be ancient — Yahô in the south (Judah) and Yahweh in the north (Israel), reflecting dialect differences. The northern/southern difference in theophoric name endings (-yw in the north, -yh in the south) supports this.

**My assessment:** Römer makes a strong case that Yahô/Yahû was the dominant pronunciation in the biblical period. His argument that "Yahweh" is a later theological development is plausible but not proven — the Church Father evidence is earlier than he acknowledges. The truth may be that both pronunciations coexisted, with Yahô being more common and "Yahweh" being a specialized priestly/scholarly form.

## Claim 2: The prohibition on pronouncing the divine name was a monotheistic development

**Römer's claim:** The gradual prohibition on speaking Yhwh's name was driven by monotheistic theology: "Within the framework of a monotheistic view it is inappropriate for the one god to have a proper name, because if he had one, this might suggest that there was some need to distinguish him from other gods."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Septuagint (3rd-2nd century BCE) already substitutes *kúrios* (Lord) or *theós* (God) for Yhwh
- The Mishnah (1st-2nd century CE) permits the high priest to pronounce the name only on Yom Kippur in the Holy of Holies
- The Masoretes (3rd-10th century CE) systematically applied the vowels of *'ădōnāy* to Yhwh as a reading instruction, producing the hybrid forms YĕHWaH / YĕHoWaH
- The substitution of "the Name" (*haš-šem*) in Judaism

**Römer's theological interpretation:** A god with a proper name is a god among gods — you need a name to specify WHICH god. The one and only God doesn't need a name because there's no one to distinguish him from. The prohibition on pronouncing the name was thus a consequence of monotheism, not its cause.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the fact of the prohibition and its gradual development. MEDIUM on Römer's claim that it was specifically monotheistic theology that drove it (magical concerns were also a factor, as Römer acknowledges).

**What's at stake for faith:** The name "Yahweh" — the personal name of God, the name revealed to Moses at the burning bush — was deliberately suppressed by Jewish tradition. The God you pray to as "Lord" was originally a god with a specific name, and that name was systematically hidden. The "Lord" of Jewish and Christian liturgy is a replacement word.

## Claim 3: The name Yhwh probably means "he blows" — a storm god, not a creator god

**Römer's claim:** "Yhwh... would be 'he who blows,' he who brings the wind, a god of storms, who might also have certain characteristics of a warrior and a desert god. This would accord rather well... with the primitive functions of Yhwh."

**Evidence presented — the etymological investigation:**

Römer evaluates five proposed etymologies:

| Etymology | Meaning | Römer's Verdict |
|-----------|---------|-----------------|
| From *h-y-h* ("to be") | "I am who I am" — philosophical being-itself | **Theological, not historical.** Exodus 3 is a later wordplay, not the original meaning. |
| From Amorite *Yaḫwi-* names ("El manifests himself") | "He manifests himself" | **Implausible.** Requires missing divine name element. "Depends heavily on theological considerations." |
| From causative of *h-y-h* ("to cause to be") | "He who creates" — El as creator | **Two problems:** No causative form of *h-y-h* is attested in Hebrew; implausible that Yhwh was originally a creator god. |
| From *ḥ-w-y* ("destroy") | "He destroys" | Possible but less likely. |
| From south Semitic *h-w-y* ("to blow") | **"He blows" — a storm god** | **Römer's preferred reading.** "Especially appropriate for a storm god." |

**The southern Semitic root argument:** The root *h-w-y* in southern Semitic (Arabic, Edomite) has three meanings: "to desire," "to fall," and "to blow." The meaning "blow" is NOT attested in biblical Hebrew — possibly because it was deliberately avoided to suppress the storm-god connotations of the divine name. Wellhausen noted as early as the 19th century: "Er fährt durch die Lüfte, er weht" (He flies through the air; he blows).

**Confidence:** LOW. All etymologies of Yhwh are speculative. Römer's preferred reading is plausible but unprovable. The very fact that FIVE serious etymologies exist, none commanding consensus, tells you how uncertain this is.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Römer is right, the name of God originally meant "he blows" — a description of a storm deity. The profound theological interpretation in Exodus 3 ("I AM WHO I AM") is a later reinterpretation of a name whose original meaning was meteorological, not metaphysical. But Römer himself offers an important qualification: "Is it really so important, if one wishes to name or invoke a god, to know the etymology of his name? The etymology might have been forgotten... The name does not necessarily define the 'nature' of a divinity."

**My assessment:** Römer's caution about the limits of etymology is well-placed. We simply don't know what Yhwh originally meant. The "he blows" reading is consistent with the southern storm-god hypothesis but depends on a reconstruction of a root not attested in Hebrew. This is one of the weaker claims in the chapter — and Römer knows it.

## Claim 4: The biblical narrative itself acknowledges that Yhwh was not originally Israel's god

**Römer's claim:** "Both of these narratives [Exodus 3 and Exodus 6] assert... that the relation between Yhwh and Israel had not existed from the beginning, but was instead the result of a certain encounter." Yhwh reveals himself to Moses as a previously unknown god. The Bible itself preserves the memory of Yhwh's foreign origin.

**Evidence presented:**
- Exodus 3: Yhwh appears to Moses while Moses is pasturing his Midianite father-in-law's flocks. The encounter happens at a "mountain of god" outside Israel — between Egypt and Judea.
- Exodus 6:2-3: God explicitly tells Moses: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yhwh I did not make myself known to them."
- "The idea that the god Yhwh has a non-Israelite origin has become the established consensus in scholarly circles."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Exodus 6:2-3 acknowledges Yhwh was not known by that name to the patriarchs. MEDIUM that this reflects genuine historical memory rather than theological retrojection.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the same claim Smith and Römer both make: the Bible's own narrative admits Yhwh was a latecomer. The patriarchs worshipped El, not Yhwh. The "God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" was, historically, El — and only later identified with Yhwh.

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Original pronunciation was Yahô/Yahû | MEDIUM-HIGH | Church Father evidence suggests "Yahweh" was also ancient |
| Prohibition on pronunciation was monotheistic | HIGH (fact), MEDIUM (motive) | Magical concerns were also a factor |
| Name means "he blows" (storm god) | LOW | All etymologies are speculative |
| Bible acknowledges Yhwh's foreign origin | HIGH (Exodus 6:2-3 is explicit) | Could be theological retrojection, not memory |

**Strongest section:** The explanation of the Masoretic vocalization and the origin of "Jehovah" — clear, accessible, and well-evidenced.

**Weakest section:** The etymology of Yhwh — multiple competing theories, none provable. Römer's preferred reading ("he blows") is consistent with his larger thesis but underdetermined by the evidence.

**Römer's honesty:** He explicitly warns that etymology may not matter much for understanding a deity's function. This is a rare moment of a scholar admitting his own investigation may not be that important. Worth noting.
