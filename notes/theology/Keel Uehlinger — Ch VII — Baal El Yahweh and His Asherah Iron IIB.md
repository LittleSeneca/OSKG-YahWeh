# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter VII: Baal, El, Yahweh, and "His Asherah" in the Context of Egyptian Solar and Royal Imagery (Iron Age IIB, c. 925-700 BCE)

This is THE chapter. It covers the divided monarchy period — Israel and Judah as separate states — and brings the full weight of iconographic evidence to bear on the most explosive questions in the field: Did Yahweh have a consort? What was the asherah — goddess or cult object? How did solar imagery get absorbed into Yahwism? The answer, delivered across 165 sections and 4,617 lines of dense argument, is nuanced in ways that neither Smith nor Dever quite capture.

---

## Claim 1: Anthropomorphic divine imagery continues its long decline in Iron IIB — goddess motifs specifically are "very infrequently" found

**Author's claim:** During Iron IIB, "the shift toward avoiding anthropomorphic depictions of deities iconographically, on any one of a wide variety of media during Iron Age IIA, held true by and large for Iron Age IIB" (lines 327-330). Specifically regarding the goddess: "Motifs that were connected in a special way to the female deity, or could perhaps even be used as a way to portray this deity, are found very infrequently in the repertoire used by the individuals who made seals in Israel and Judah during the ninth and eighth centuries" (lines 383-386).

**Evidence presented:**
- Nursing mother animals — prominent in Bronze Age and on Syrian/Phoenician ivories — are absent from the Samaria ivories and from local Israelite/Judahite glyptic art
- The scorpion that had regularly accompanied caprids in earlier periods appears less often
- Human worshipers are rarely shown with caprids (unlike Iron IIA)
- Caprids now appear alone, without numinous elements between them — even the tree disappears from caprid pairs
- The "Lord of the Ostriches" sometimes gets replaced by his attribute animal — a key indicator of the substitution process
- The tree flanked by caprids motif "disappears soon after" the beginning of Iron IIB, at least in local glyptic art

**Confidence:** HIGH. They've documented this across the entire seal corpus. The pattern is consistent across Israel and Judah. This is an empirical finding, not an interpretation.

**What's at stake:** If goddess-connected imagery was already in steep decline during Iron IIB (925-700), then the deuteronomic reform of Josiah (c. 622) cannot be the cause of the goddess's disappearance. The reform was responding to something, but it wasn't the first push against goddess imagery — that had been happening organically for two centuries. This fundamentally challenges Dever's narrative of a vibrant folk goddess religion suppressed by Jerusalem orthodoxy.

**Who disagrees:** Dever explicitly — he reads the 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines (which appear mostly in Iron IIC, the NEXT period) as evidence of widespread Asherah worship. Keel & Uehlinger note that these figurines "came into Palestine in great numbers only toward the end of the eighth century" (line 1008-1009) and are a phenomenon of the NEXT chapter (Iron IIC), not Iron IIB.

**Alternative reading:** The iconographic evidence might reflect elite seal-owner preferences, not popular religion. The persistence of the goddess in personal names and folk practice could be invisible in the glyptic record.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most important empirical findings in the book. It forces a re-periodization of the Asherah question. The debate isn't "did Josiah's reform kill goddess worship?" — it's "was the goddess already moribund in the 8th century, and if so, why did pillar figurines explode in the 7th?" That paradox is exactly what they address in Chapter VIII.

## Claim 2: The bull calf images of Jeroboam were NOT a new Baal cult — they were a revival of an older El tradition at Bethel

**Author's claim:** "When all the evidence is considered, the conclusion is clear: images of bulls play no dominant role in the specialty crafts of Iron Age IIB Israel, which is certainly not what one would expect in light of the literary traditions concerning the significance attached to the cultic images of Jeroboam" (lines 667-671). Jeroboam's bull images were "probably a leftover from the Late Bronze Age or from Iron Age I... which was probably connected originally with El rather than Baal (cf. the place name Bethel, 'house/temple of El')" (lines 678-683).

**Evidence presented:**
- Bull imagery is RARE in Iron IIB Israelite glyptic — far rarer than expected if Jeroboam had introduced a major new bull cult requiring propaganda
- Images of a deity on a bull (the traditional "weather god on his attribute animal" motif) "are extremely rare in Iron Age IIB" — the type went out of fashion at the end of Iron I
- Charging/striding bulls appear only sporadically in Israelite glyptic, in contrast to Ammonite seals where the bull (Milcom) is prominent
- The 143 terra-cotta bovines from Iron II mostly date to Iron IIC (7th century), not IIB — and most are from Judah, not Israel
- The personal name 'glyw on a Samaria ostracon should be translated "Bull calf of YW" (the person's name), not "YW is a bull calf" (a theological statement)
- Amos and Hosea polemicize against the Bethel bull, but don't mention other bull cult sites

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The iconographic evidence is clear: bull imagery is not prominent. But the conclusion that Jeroboam's bulls were an El tradition rather than a Baal innovation depends partly on the Bethel etymology and the assumption that Jeroboam wouldn't have introduced new imagery without propagandistic small-finds — both plausible but not ironclad.

**What's at stake:** If the Bethel bull was El, not Baal, then the northern cult was not a "Canaanization" of Yahwism but a continuation of the old El identification. Yahweh's absorption of El's iconography (bull) and sanctuary (Bethel) happened before the monarchy, not as a royal innovation. This supports Smith's convergence thesis and Cross's El-Yahweh identification.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read 1 Kings 12 as describing a Baal cult (the traditional "sin of Jeroboam" interpretation). Knauf's triad theory (Yahweh + Anat + Ishim at Bethel) is explicitly rejected: "the image of the bull calf cannot in any way, shape, or form depict Anat as a (hypothetical) partner for Yahweh" (lines 712-713).

**Alternative reading:** The lack of bull imagery in small finds might simply mean bull worship was restricted to state temples and didn't generate popular devotional objects. State cults don't always produce portable propaganda.

**My assessment:** Their reading is more plausible than the alternatives. The Bethel-as-El-sanctuary argument is strong, and the negative evidence (no bull seals) is genuinely puzzling if Jeroboam launched a major new bull cult. The "old El shrine revived" thesis makes the best sense of the data.

## Claim 3: Anthropomorphic goddess imagery is virtually ABSENT from Iron IIB Israel and Judah — only 2-3 possible examples exist, none unambiguous

**Author's claim:** "The iconography, which has appeared to date in Palestine/Israel on the specialty craft items that are typical of Iron Age IIB in Phoenicia and Israel, has produced only two or three depictions of female deities depicted in a style typical of the Near East. We dare not ignore this fact as we investigate the possible position and significance of a (hypothetical) partner of Yahweh" (lines 874-877). "In the glyptic art of Iron Age IIB from Israel and Judah, there is not a single seal known to us on which a goddess might have been depicted anthropomorphically" (lines 1019-1021).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Hazor ointment spoon (illus. 214): a female face with doves in her hair on the underside, palmetto tree on top — a "transparent" depiction, not overt. Identified as probably Asherah, a late manifestation of the Tree Goddess. But the goddess is on the HIDDEN side of the object.
- The Samaria "woman at the window" ivory (illus. 215): debate about whether goddess or priestess/temple slave. Keel thinks priestess due to missing forehead jewelry. Either way, Phoenician-inspired, not indigenous.
- Naked Goddess plaques: present in Philistine territory (Ashdod, Tel Batash) but "absent from the material known to us from Israel and Judah" during Iron IIB (lines 973-987). The terra-cottas are "relatively rare by comparison with the so-called pillar figurines that appear in the following periods" (lines 990-991).
- Hebrew name seals: "not a single seal" shows a goddess. The ONE exception is a late 8th-century imported piece with inscription added later.
- Hosea's polemic: attacks Baal constantly, but "a comparable, explicit polemic against a female deity is never delivered by Hosea" (lines 858-870).

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH for the glyptic evidence. The absence is striking and systematic. HIGH for the broader claim — the few ambiguous examples prove the rule.

**What's at stake:** This is the single most damaging empirical finding for the "Yahweh had a consort" thesis in its strong form. If goddess imagery is THIS rare in the 9th-8th centuries, then either Asherah worship didn't involve anthropomorphic images, or it was extremely marginal in Israel/Judah. Either way, the popular picture of thriving goddess worship alongside Yahwism is wrong for THIS period. (Iron IIC, the next chapter, is a different story.)

**Who disagrees:** Dever, who reads the same evidence as showing ongoing goddess worship. M. Weippert, who asserts Yahweh had a goddess partner. Ahlström, who sees the biblical censorship as having covered up widespread polytheism.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of goddess imagery on seals might reflect a deliberate aniconic tendency specifically for seals (which were administrative/official), while popular terra-cotta figurines in domestic contexts continued goddess worship outside the "official" record.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger aren't saying there was NO goddess worship — they're saying the iconographic evidence we DO have doesn't support the "thriving consort" model. The few goddess images that exist are transparent, hidden, or Phoenician-inspired. This is a more careful reading than either Dever's maximalism or Smith's minimalism. They're not denying the goddess — they're asking what FORM her presence took.

## Claim 4: Yahweh was worshiped AS Baal in Iron IIB Israel — the theophoric elements yw- and b'l refer to the SAME deity

**Author's claim:** "It is probable that both elements, yw- and b'l, refer to the same deity, namely Yahweh, who was worshiped also as Baal in Iron Age IIB Israel (cf., in the Old Testament's Yahwistic transmission of tradition, the names *'išba'al, y‘rubba'al, m*ri(b)ba'al, and b'alyah)" (lines 1113-1116).

**Evidence presented:**
- Samaria ostraca (early 8th c.): 50 different personal names. 11 with yw element, ~6 with b'l element. They "stand alongside each other" with the same verbal elements and same piety expressions.
- The name types are NOT ethnically differentiated (Israelite = yw, Canaanite = b'l) — people with both name types appear in the same administrative contexts.
- The biblical tradition itself preserves names like *'išba'al ("man of Baal") and b'alyah ("Yah is Baal"), showing the identity was accepted before later scribes changed b'l to bōšet ("shame").
- Toward the end of the period (just before 722), b'l names disappear from the ostraca — the differentiation was beginning.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The onomastic evidence is what it is. But "Yahweh was worshiped AS Baal" could mean different things: (a) Yahweh absorbed Baal's functions and epithets ("Baal" as title, not separate god), or (b) people literally didn't distinguish between Yahweh and Baal. The evidence supports (a) more than (b).

**What's at stake:** This is the onomastic confirmation of Smith's convergence thesis. If "Baal" in Israelite names means "Yahweh-in-his-Baal-aspect," then the Elijah vs. Baal prophets conflict (1 Kings 18) was an INTRA-Yahwistic dispute, not Israelites vs. foreigners. Elijah wasn't fighting Canaanite religion — he was fighting a version of Yahwism that looked too Baal-like.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that b'l names refer to the Canaanite Baal as a separate deity alongside Yahweh. The deuteronomistic editors certainly read them this way.

**Alternative reading:** The b'l names might reflect a transitional period when some Israelites genuinely worshiped Baal AS Baal, not as Yahweh. The shift to exclusively yw- names at the end of Iron IIB would then reflect Yahwistic purists gaining influence.

**My assessment:** Their reading is the most economical. It explains why Hosea can attack "Baal" while using Baal-language for Yahweh. It also explains why there's no separate Baal iconography in Israel (the Baal imagery they DO find — the four-winged youthful god — is "Baal" as a type of Yahweh, not a competitor).

## Claim 5: The Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi do NOT depict Yahweh and his consort — the Bes figures are apotropaic daemons, the inscriptions were added by different hands at a different time

**Author's claim:** The two figures on Pithos A (often identified as "Yahweh and his Asherah") are Bes-type daemons — "no doubt" about this identification (lines 1645-1651). The inscription runs OVER the head ornamentation, meaning it was added later. The figures are "not to be treated as a heterosexual pair, in the sense of Bes and Beset, but it is rather more likely that they are two Bes variants, one masculine and one bisexual-feminized" (lines 1705-1708).

**Evidence presented:**
- Iconographic features of both figures match Bes exactly: grotesque lion-like face, protruding ears, beard/collar, arms akimbo, crooked legs, tail, frontal pose, blossom/feather crown
- The two figures are "placed together paratactically" not as a coherent group — they were "perhaps even drawn by different individuals" (lines 1713-1715)
- The smaller figure (N) has round female breasts but no Beset genitalia or shoulder-lock hairstyle — it's an androgynous Bes variant, not a goddess
- Bes was a protector daemon for pregnancy, birth, children, and general disaster prevention — not a major deity
- The inscriptions were written by different hands at different times than the paintings
- The lyre player is "more likely a human figure rather than a divine one" — Dever's identification as an enthroned goddess on a cherub throne is rejected based on comparative furniture analysis (lines 1961-1962)
- The five striding figures are worshipers, not deities — and they're all MALE

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH for the Bes identification — the iconographic markers are unambiguous. HIGH for the rest. The "different hands, different times" argument is supported by Beck's original publication and is the scholarly consensus among iconographers.

**What's at stake:** The popular reading of Kuntillet Ajrud — "here is a picture of Yahweh and his wife!" — is wrong. This doesn't mean the inscriptions aren't important (they are — see Claim 6), but they can't be used as visual evidence for a divine couple. Every popular book and documentary that shows the Kuntillet Ajrud pithos and says "these are Yahweh and Asherah" is misrepresenting the evidence.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984), Margalit (1990), and popularizers who read the Bes figures as Yahweh and consort. Dever explicitly argued the lyre player was an enthroned goddess — Keel & Uehlinger dismantle this in detail. Gilula (1979) read the Bes figures as bovines — also rejected.

**Alternative reading:** Even if they're Bes figures, they could still be associated with Yahweh if Bes was absorbed into Yahwistic apotropaic practice. But that's a much weaker claim than "this IS Yahweh."

**My assessment:** This section is a methodological masterclass. They insist on treating images and inscriptions as independent evidence first, then asking about their relationship. The iconographic analysis is definitive: these are Bes figures. The inscription was added later by someone who may not have even been the artist. The pithos is evidence for blessing-formula practice in the Yahweh cult, not for divine couples in Israelite iconography.

## Claim 6: "'šrth" in the Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions refers to a CULTIC OBJECT (a stylized tree/pole), NOT the goddess Asherah personally — but the object was "transparently" connected to the goddess tradition

**Author's claim:** After reviewing all four interpretations (goddess, generic consort, shrine, cultic object), they conclude: "The controversial reference to 'šrth in the inscription from Kuntillet Ajrud thus most likely refers to such a cultic object" — a wooden pole/stylized tree (lines 2435-2436). BUT: the tree functions "transparently" — "it is possible for the tree to seem to be associated with the goddess in both images, without the goddess having to make an actual appearance as a personal power" (lines 2497-2500).

**Evidence presented:**
- Grammatical: Hebrew proper names don't take possessive suffixes. "'šrth" = "his asherah" cannot be a goddess name with a suffix.
- Biblical: Where the Hebrew Bible mentions the asherah, it uses ha'ăšerah (with article), never the bare divine name with suffix. The deuteronomistic redactors are polemicizing against a cult OBJECT, not naming a goddess.
- Epigraphic: The blessing formulas at Kuntillet Ajrud invoke "Yahweh... and his asherah" together, but the concluding blessing wish is SINGULAR — "may HE bless you and keep you." Yahweh alone is the agent of blessing; the asherah is the medium.
- The Khirbet el-Qom inscription (late 8th c.): "Blessed is Uriyahu by Yahweh... by his asherah, he has saved him from his oppressors." Yahweh saves THROUGH his asherah, not alongside a goddess partner.
- Iconographic: The stylized tree appears flanked by caprids, guarded by cherubs, or venerated by worshipers — functioning as a numinous power symbol that can be assigned to a male deity, not just a goddess.
- The tree becomes "a sexually undifferentiated cultic symbol that can be assigned also to a male deity" (lines 2567-2568) — the tree guarded by cherubs represents the deity's power to bring blessing, completely disconnected from female deity concepts.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the grammatical/epigraphic argument. MEDIUM-HIGH for the "transparency" thesis — this is their most original contribution, and it depends on reading subtle visual cues that other scholars might interpret differently.

**What's at stake:** This is the resolution of the Smith vs. Dever debate. Smith is right that the asherah is a symbol, not a personal goddess (the grammatical argument is strong). But Dever is right that the symbol WAS originally and still "transparently" connected to a goddess tradition — it's not just a meaningless stick. Keel & Uehlinger chart a middle path: the asherah IS a cult object, AND it carries residual goddess associations that were still perceptible to Iron Age viewers, AND it's increasingly becoming a gender-neutral symbol of Yahweh's blessing power.

**Who disagrees:** Coogan, M. Weippert, and others who read 'šrth as the goddess Asherah directly. Olyan and Day, who accept the cult-object reading but insist the object REPRESENTS the goddess as Yahweh's partner.

**Alternative reading:** If the grammatical argument against a divine name with suffix is correct (and it's widely accepted), the strongest alternative is Olyan's: the cult object represents the goddess and therefore the inscriptions DO attest to Asherah as Yahweh's partner, just indirectly. Keel & Uehlinger accept this is possible in some contexts but argue it's not compelled by the evidence.

**My assessment:** This is the single most important contribution of the book to the Asherah debate. The "transparency" concept is genuinely illuminating — it explains how the same object could be a mere stick to a Yahwistic purist and a goddess-presence to a traditionalist. The question "goddess or symbol?" is the wrong question. The right question is: "At what point in the Iron Age did the symbol detach from the goddess, and for whom?"

## Claim 7: Solar and celestial imagery was absorbed into Yahwism as part of a PAN-LEVANTINE religious shift — Yahweh became a solar-connoted "Lord of Heaven" like the Phoenician Baalshamem

**Author's claim:** "The correlation of the iconographic symbols for the sun and the heavens with epigraphic documentation that gives evidence for a contemporary context, in which Yahwism was dominant, leads one inevitably to formulate a hypothesis that the movement in which the Phoenician Baal took on celestial and solar attributes and became Baalshamem is to be seen as part of a larger shift in religion concerning the entire Levant, a shift that affected Israel as well" (lines 3738-3742).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Phoenician/Israelite glyptic of the 9th-8th centuries is dominated by Egyptian solar motifs: sun-child in lotus blossom, winged solar disk, two- and four-winged scarab pushing the sun-ball, eye of Horus, falcon-headed winged sphinxes with solar disks
- These motifs form a "coherent, celestial, and solar-oriented symbol system" — NOT random decoration
- Winged protective powers (falcons, uraei, cherubs, sphinxes) are "the most important iconographic symbols during the ninth and eighth centuries"
- They convey "a mysterious connection between unapproachable distance and effective protection" — exactly the theological profile of a "Lord of Heaven"
- The selectivity of the Israelite repertoire is telling: Israel imports solar-celestial motifs but EXCLUDES anthropomorphic goddess imagery from the Phoenician repertoire
- "Arguments from silence prove nothing," but the fact that Israel SELECTIVELY adopted solar motifs while rejecting goddess imagery suggests intentional theological choices
- In Judah, solar imagery arrives differently — through bone seals with royal Egyptian cartouches and falcons, later evolving toward direct solar worship symbols (winged solar disk, crowned sun)
- "If Yahweh was represented in eighth-century Israel more often as the 'Lord of Heaven' (Baalshamem), was he seen in Judah more in the role of a solar El?" (lines 4531-4532) — they leave this as a question

**Confidence:** HIGH for the descriptive claim about iconographic prevalence. MEDIUM-HIGH for the causal claim about pan-Levantine religious shift — the correlation is clear, but proving causation from images alone is difficult.

**What's at stake:** This is the ICONOGRAPHIC confirmation of Smith's convergence thesis. Smith argued from texts that Yahweh absorbed Baal's traits and El's identity. Keel & Uehlinger show the VISUAL mechanism: Egyptian solar imagery, mediated through Phoenician craft production, provided the iconographic vocabulary for representing a "Most High God." Yahweh didn't need his own iconography — he borrowed the sun god's. The solarization of Yahweh happened EARLIER than most text scholars think: "one sees the trend toward accentuating solar and celestial depictions of the deity much earlier and much more unequivocally in the iconography than in the very sparse number of relevant texts" (lines 4523-4524).

**Who disagrees:** Niehr, who dates the solarization to the Assyrian period (7th c.) at the earliest. Keel & Uehlinger explicitly reject this: the process began in the 9th-8th centuries. Text scholars who treat solar language in the Psalms as "late" may be misdating it.

**Alternative reading:** The solar motifs could be conventional — seal-cutters reproducing popular Egyptian patterns without theological investment. Keel & Uehlinger anticipate this objection and reject it forcefully: "The purely aesthetic use of figurative elements is recent, and the notion that such a use existed in the ninth and eighth centuries is anachronistic" (lines 3634-3635).

**My assessment:** This is the strongest vindication of the solar-Yahweh thesis in print. The iconographic evidence is abundant and consistent. The selective adoption pattern (solar yes, goddess no) is the clincher — if these were just pretty decorations, we'd see all Phoenician motifs equally. The selectivity IS the theological signal.

## Claim 8: The asherah was the most important MEDIATING ENTITY subordinate to Yahweh, not an equal partner — the iconography shows a hierarchy, not a pair

**Author's claim:** "Neither the iconography nor the texts force us to interpret the relationship between 'Yahweh... and his asherah' in Iron Age IIB in the sense of a (sexually-determined) relationship of two forces that are paired and thus compel us to assume that the asherah has the status of a partner. 'Yahweh's asherah' does not have equal rank with Yahweh but is rather a mediating entity that brings his blessing and is conceived in the mind in the shape of a stylized tree that was thus subordinate to Yahweh" (lines 2683-2689).

**Evidence presented:**
- In the Kuntillet Ajrud blessing formulas, when a concluding blessing wish is added (Inscriptions B2 and B3), the verbs are SINGULAR — "may HE bless you and keep you," "may Yahweh give him according to his intention." Yahweh alone is the agent.
- The Khirbet el-Qom inscription: "by his asherah, he has saved him" — Yahweh saves, the asherah is the instrument.
- The iconography of the sacred tree guarded by cherubs: the flanking creatures "indicate, by their position, the hierarchical priority of the power that they flank and guard" (lines 4581-4582). The asherah-tree is flanked; Yahweh is never depicted — he's the implied power BEHIND the tree.
- The broader pattern: "these powers and authorities, asherahs, Bes figures, Shaddayim, cherubs, uraei, etc., were not of the same order as Yahweh, but were rather subordinate to him, mediating the protection and blessing of Yahweh" (lines 4574-4576).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The textual argument (singular blessing verbs) is strong. The iconographic argument about hierarchy is plausible but depends on reading spatial arrangement as theological statement — which their constellation method justifies.

**What's at stake:** If the asherah was a mediating entity rather than a partner, then the entire "Yahweh had a wife" framing may be misleading. It wasn't a marriage of equals; it was a hierarchical relationship where the asherah channeled Yahweh's blessing. The model isn't "divine couple" but "god and his hypostasis/attribute." This connects to Sommer's fluidity model — the asherah could be understood as a localized manifestation of Yahweh's blessing power rather than a separate personal deity.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone in the "Asherah was Yahweh's consort" camp (Dever, M. Weippert, Olyan in his stronger moments). They would argue that "mediating entity" is semantic evasion — if it looks like a goddess and functions like a consort in blessing formulas that elsewhere invoke paired deities, it IS a goddess.

**Alternative reading:** The singular blessing verbs could simply reflect standard epistolary convention rather than theological statement. And the mediating-entity reading might be anachronistically monotheistic — projecting later Jewish angelology back onto Iron Age practice.

**My assessment:** Their reading is more faithful to the evidence than the "consort" reading. It's not that they're denying the asherah's importance — they're arguing it was the MOST important subordinate entity in the Yahwistic system, which is a significant claim in its own right. The asherah isn't nothing; it's the primary channel of blessing. That's religiously significant without requiring a divine marriage.

## Chapter VII Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Goddess imagery in steep decline in Iron IIB | HIGH | New finds of goddess seals from this period |
| 2: Jeroboam's bulls = El tradition, not Baal innovation | MEDIUM-HIGH | Better evidence for bull as Baal symbol in 10th c. north |
| 3: Only 2-3 possible goddess images in Israel/Judah Iron IIB | VERY HIGH | None — the absence is systematic |
| 4: yw and b'l refer to same deity in Israelite names | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence that b'l names were non-Yahwistic |
| 5: Kuntillet Ajrud Bes figures ≠ divine couple | VERY HIGH | Already the consensus among iconographers |
| 6: 'šrth = cult object with transparent goddess connection | HIGH | New epigraphic finds showing 'šrh as divine name |
| 7: Solar imagery = pan-Levantine shift, Yahweh solarized | HIGH | Proving intentionality vs. convention |
| 8: Asherah = mediating entity subordinate to Yahweh | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence of paired deities in blessing formulas |

**Strongest section:** §§134-140 on Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom, and the "transparency" thesis. This is the most original and carefully argued part of the chapter — it resolves the goddess-vs-symbol debate with methodological sophistication that neither Smith nor Dever achieved.

**Weakest section:** §§120-121 on the four-winged Baal and Knauf's triad. This gets tangled in specialist debates about obscure deities (Ishim, Shagar) that most readers can't evaluate.

**Most consequential finding for the Truth project:** The convergence of three independent lines of evidence — iconographic (solar motifs dominate), onomastic (Yahweh IS Baal in names), and epigraphic (asherah is subordinate medium, not partner) — all pointing to the same conclusion: Iron IIB Yahwism was a solarized, celestialized "Lord of Heaven" religion with a subordinate blessing-mediating entity (the asherah), not a polytheistic pantheon with a divine couple.

**What changes in the big picture:** The deuteronomic reform of Josiah (late 7th c.) cannot be the starting point for Israelite aniconism or goddess-suppression — those trends were already 200 years old by Josiah's time. The reform was accelerating an existing trajectory, not creating a new one. And the solarization of Yahweh — what Smith traces textually in Second Isaiah — was already well underway ICONOGRAPHICALLY in the 9th-8th centuries, mediated through Phoenician craft production and Egyptian solar symbolism.
