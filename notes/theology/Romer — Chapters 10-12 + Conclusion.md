# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 10-12 + Conclusion

---

## Chapter 10: The Fall of Samaria and the Rise of Judah

### Claim: The fall of Israel in 722 BCE was the catalyst for Judah's theological transformation — the moment when regional Yhwh worship began its journey toward monotheism

**Römer's claim:** When Assyria destroyed Samaria in 722, refugees from the north flooded into Jerusalem. They brought their traditions, their scribes, and their texts. This transformed Judah from a minor vassal state into the center of Yhwh worship. The "invention" of a distinctively Yahwistic identity — Yhwh as the god who controls history, who punishes apostasy, who demands exclusive loyalty — began in this moment.

**Evidence presented — the historical sequence:**

- **The Syro-Ephraimite War (c. 734):** Israel and Aram attacked Judah to force it into an anti-Assyrian coalition. King Ahaz of Judah refused, paid tribute to Assyria, and became a vassal — saving Judah from destruction. Isaiah 7 contains the original oracle: "If you do not have trust, you shall not survive."
- **733:** Assyria destroyed Damascus. Israel's territory was reduced to the hill country around Samaria. King Peqah was assassinated; Hosea was installed as an Assyrian puppet.
- **724-722:** Siege of Samaria. The city fell after three years. Sargon II deported the elite and turned Israel into Assyrian provinces.
- **The refugee crisis:** Northern priests, scribes, and elites fled south to Judah. They brought their texts (the "E" source? early prophetic collections?) and their traditions. Jerusalem's population swelled.
- **Judah's transformation:** What had been a minor highland chiefdom became a real kingdom with a real bureaucracy. The northern refugees brought literacy, administrative expertise, and religious texts. **"The fall of Samaria was the condition of possibility for the birth of Judaism."**

**The theological response:**
- Northern prophets had already begun moving toward Yhwh-alone theology (Hosea, Amos). Their texts were preserved and edited in Judah after 722.
- The disaster was interpreted theologically: Israel fell because it worshipped other gods. Yhwh himself had destroyed his own people as punishment.
- This created a new template: Yhwh controls history. Defeat is not evidence of Yhwh's weakness — it's evidence of his justice.
- This template would later be applied to Judah's own fall in 587.

**Hezekiah's reform (late 8th century):**
- Possibly the first attempt at centralization — eliminating high places, removing the Nehushtan (bronze serpent) from the temple
- Politically motivated: consolidate control over the expanded Jerusalem, eliminate northern cult sites
- Römer is more skeptical than the Bible: the reform was probably limited and partly a later retrojection

**Confidence:** HIGH that the fall of Samaria transformed Judah (archaeological evidence of Jerusalem's expansion in the late 8th century is clear). MEDIUM on the refugee-scribe thesis (plausible but the northern texts were heavily edited later, making it hard to isolate what was genuinely northern).

**What's at stake for faith:** The theological revolution — Yhwh as the god who controls ALL history, who uses foreign empires to punish his own people — was born from political catastrophe. It wasn't revelation. It was making theological sense of military defeat.

## Chapter 11: The Reform of Josiah

### Claim: Josiah's reform (c. 622 BCE) was the pivotal moment — the invention of Deuteronomy and the creation of exclusive Yahwism as state ideology

**Römer's claim:** The "book of the law" discovered in the temple during Josiah's reign was Deuteronomy — specifically, an early version of it. The "discovery" narrative (2 Kings 22-23) is a foundation myth, not an eyewitness report. Deuteronomy was WRITTEN to justify Josiah's centralizing reforms: one God, one temple, one people. The reform was as much political as religious — an assertion of Judean independence as Assyrian power waned.

**Evidence presented:**

**The "discovered book" — a pious fraud?**
- The narrative of finding a scroll during temple renovations is "a foundation myth produced by the biblical editors." It cannot be used "naively as if it were the report of an eyewitness."
- The motif of discovering a hidden book is well-known in ancient literature: Hittite priest Murshili "found" tablets explaining a plague; Philo of Byblos claimed to translate rediscovered Phoenician tablets; the Egyptian Book of the Dead was "found" in a temple.
- The narrative already contains references to the destruction of Jerusalem (587) — it was redacted AFTER the exile. The oracles of Huldah (2 Kings 22:16-17) presuppose the catastrophe.
- **"The first edition of Deuteronomy was written to promote the reforms of Josiah, and the scroll was then disguised as a testament of Moses."**

**What Josiah's reform actually did:**
- Eliminated the "heavenly army" (astral worship) from the temple
- Discharged the priests of Baal and Asherah
- Destroyed the "high places" (*bāmôt*) — Yahwistic sanctuaries outside Jerusalem
- Desacralized the *tōphet* (site of child sacrifice)
- Demolished the altar at Bethel — the ancient Yahwistic sanctuary of the north
- Centralized sacrifice in Jerusalem
- Celebrated a Passover (23:21-23) — possibly the first Passover as a centralized national festival

**The double standard:** Before Josiah, kings who worshipped Yhwh at multiple sanctuaries with steles and asherah poles were the NORM. After Josiah's reform, this became "idolatry." The reform didn't restore an original purity — it INVENTED a new standard and projected it backward.

**The political context:** Assyria was collapsing. Josiah saw an opportunity to assert Judean independence and expand northward into former Israelite territory. Centralizing worship at Jerusalem was state-building: one capital, one temple, one god.

**Confidence:** HIGH that Deuteronomy was connected to Josiah's reform (widely accepted since de Wette in 1805). HIGH that the "discovery" narrative is a literary motif, not an eyewitness report. MEDIUM on the exact scope of Josiah's reform (the biblical account may exaggerate).

**What's at stake for faith:** The book of Deuteronomy — the theological heart of the Old Testament, the source of the Shema ("Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One") — was written as political propaganda for a 7th-century king. The most monotheistic book in the Torah was composed to justify tearing down Yahwistic sanctuaries and concentrating power in Jerusalem. The "word of God" was, historically, royal policy.

## Chapter 12: From One God to the Only God

### Claim: True monotheism — "Yhwh is the ONLY god, all others are non-existent" — emerged in the Babylonian Exile as a response to national catastrophe

**Römer's claim:** The exile (587-538 BCE) was the crucible. With the temple destroyed, the monarchy ended, and the people deported, Judean theologians faced a choice: abandon Yhwh (he had lost), or radically redefine him. They chose the second option. Yhwh became the only god — not just Israel's god, but the god of the whole world, whose power extended to Babylon and beyond. Second Isaiah (Isaiah 40-55) is the first unambiguous articulation of this radical monotheism.

**The historical context:**
- 597: First deportation (Jehoiachin, court elite, priests, artisans)
- 587: Jerusalem destroyed. Temple burned. Second deportation.
- 582: Third deportation after Gedaliah's assassination
- Judah wasn't empty — life continued, especially in Benjamin. But the elite were in Babylon.
- The population dropped from ~100,000 to ~40,000

**The theological innovation — three moves:**

1. **Yhwh is no longer tied to land or temple.** If Yhwh lived in the Jerusalem temple, the temple's destruction meant Yhwh was homeless — or dead. The solution: Yhwh transcends place. He is the god of the whole earth. He can be worshipped anywhere — even in Babylon.

2. **Other gods don't exist.** Pre-exilic texts warn against worshipping other gods without denying their reality. Second Isaiah (c. 540 BCE) goes further: "I am Yhwh, and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (Isa 45:5). "Before me no god was formed, nor shall there be any after me" (Isa 43:10). Satires on idol-making (Isa 44:9-20) mock the very IDEA of other gods.

3. **Yhwh uses foreign empires as his instruments.** Cyrus the Persian — a pagan king — is called Yhwh's "anointed one" (Isa 45:1). Yhwh controls ALL history, not just Israel's. The God who destroyed Jerusalem is the same God who will restore it through a Persian king.

**The Persian context:**
- Cyrus' edict (538 BCE) allowed deported peoples to return and rebuild temples
- Persian imperial theology was not monotheistic, but Ahura Mazda was supreme. This may have influenced Jewish monotheism (Römer is cautious — "it remains difficult to prove direct borrowing")
- The Persians encouraged local cults as a tool of imperial stability. Returning exiles rebuilding the Jerusalem temple was consistent with Persian policy.

**The writing of the Torah:**
- The Persian period (538-333 BCE) was when the Pentateuch took its final form
- Different traditions (Deuteronomistic, Priestly) were combined into a single authoritative text
- This was possibly encouraged or even mandated by the Persian administration
- "The Torah became the 'portable fatherland' of Judaism" — replacing the lost temple and monarchy

**Confidence:** HIGH that radical monotheism emerged in the exile (Second Isaiah is unambiguous). MEDIUM on Persian influence (parallels exist but direct borrowing is hard to prove). MEDIUM on the Persian role in Torah compilation (plausible but contested).

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the culmination of Römer's entire narrative. Monotheism — the belief that there is only one God — was not revealed to Moses on Sinai. It was INVENTED in Babylon by displaced priests and scribes trying to make sense of catastrophe. The most fundamental claim of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam is a human response to historical trauma. Römer doesn't say this disproves God. He says this is HOW the idea of the one God came into existence — through history, not despite it.

## Conclusion: The Invention of God

Römer ends by returning to his title. "Invention" doesn't mean fabrication. It means a gradual construction — "a progressive construction arising out of a particular tradition."

**The arc in summary:**
1. A desert war god from the south (Chs. 1-3)
2. Adopted by a group called "Israel" who originally worshipped El (Ch. 4)
3. Installed in Jerusalem by David as a political-theological masterstroke (Ch. 5)
4. Worshipped in diverse forms with images, steles, and a consort goddess (Chs. 6-9)
5. Centralized and made exclusive by Josiah's reform (Chs. 10-11)
6. Transformed into the ONLY god during the Babylonian exile (Ch. 12)

**Römer's final word:** The god of the Bible is real — as a historical phenomenon, as a tradition, as an idea that has shaped civilization. Whether he is real in any other sense is a question Römer, as a historian, does not answer.

## Final Cross-Reference: Römer vs. Smith on Monotheism's Emergence

| Question | Smith | Römer |
|----------|-------|-------|
| When did exclusive Yhwh worship emerge? | 9th-8th c. (prophetic movement) | 8th-7th c. (fall of Samaria, Hezekiah/Josiah) |
| When did true monotheism (no other gods exist) emerge? | Exile (6th c.) — same | Exile (6th c.) — same |
| What drove it? | Multiple factors: writing, covenant, monarchy, international crisis | Primarily political: imperial pressure, state centralization, exile trauma |
| Josiah's reform | Important but one factor among many | THE pivotal moment; Deuteronomy as political manifesto |
| Rolle of Persia | Mentioned briefly | More emphasis on Persian context for Torah compilation |

Both agree on the broad arc: polytheism → monolatry → monotheism, with the exile as the crucible. They differ in emphasis and causal explanation. Römer's narrative is more political; Smith's is more text-critical and comparative. Reading both gives you the strongest picture.
