# Kaufmann — Chapter X: The Literature and the Age

This chapter serves as the framing introduction to Part Three on Classical Prophecy. Kaufmann surveys what classical prophecy inherited from the early religion, demarcates its chronological limits against apocalyptic, argues for the compositional integrity of the prophetic books (against the prevailing view of generations-long accretion), explains prophecies of consolation as native to covenant theology rather than mythological imports, traces the emergence of a new "historiosophy" (universal history), and examines the relationship between oral and written prophecy. The chapter is less argumentatively dense than Kaufmann's major thesis chapters (IV, VI), but it establishes the framework within which his reading of individual prophets (XIII on Jeremiah and Ezekiel) will operate. Its central contention: classical prophecy is a coherent, bounded literary phenomenon — not a fluid tradition gradually blending into apocalyptic — and its literary form is the product of the prophets themselves, not anonymous circles of disciples writing generations later.

---

## Claim 1: Classical prophecy inherited its monotheism and worldview from the popular religion — it did not create them

**Author's claim:** "Classical prophecy sprang from the soil of Israel's popular monotheism." It "does not so much repudiate the popular religion as rise above it." The classical prophets "do not announce a new God or teach a new concept of the nature of deity." Their God is "the God of the people," and their reproof rests on "the popular legends telling of his wonders in Israel." The rupture between prophets and people is about practice, not theology.

**Evidence presented:**
- From the popular religion, classical prophecy "received its idea of the one God, sole creator and judge of the world; a non-mythological, non-magical deity, a supreme will, unfettered by fate or compulsion"
- "Classical prophecy does not recognize or combat mythological or magical beliefs. It arose among a people out of which genuine paganism had been uprooted centuries before"
- The prophets "bewail the lack of 'knowledge of God' in the land, but the God they speak of is the God of the people"
- The historical and moral presuppositions are shared: Israel alone knows YHWH; pagan lands are "impure"; the nations are judged only for gross moral sins, not idolatry; the covenant with YHWH is foundational
- Classical prophets are steeped in past literature: narrative traditions of the Torah, legal literature, psalmody, wisdom forms — even ancient Canaanite poetry
- Jeremiah is "strongly influenced by Deuteronomy"; Ezekiel "by the Priestly Code"
- Like their predecessors, the classical prophets have a mantic element, work wonders, and are moved by an individual call
- Yet unlike popular prophets, they are "exclusively apostles of God" who "come of their own accord to a people who have not sought them" — they do not prophesy for pay

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's observation that the prophets never argue *for* monotheism — only *from* it — is genuinely important and matches the textual evidence. The prophets presuppose YHWH's uniqueness; they don't argue for it. But Kaufmann's inference that this proves *popular* monotheism depends on the case made in Chapter IV, which is contested. The shared theological vocabulary could equally reflect a common literary tradition rather than popular belief.

**What's at stake:** The entire direction of causation in Israelite religion. If the prophets inherited monotheism from the people, then monotheism predates classical prophecy — reversing the critical sequence (prophets create monotheism → Torah codifies it). If Kaufmann is wrong, the prophets' monotheistic language was new and revolutionary, and their audience was polytheistic.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus. Smith (Origins Ch 8, 10) traces monotheistic *rhetoric* as emerging within the prophetic corpus itself, reaching full expression only in Second Isaiah. Römer sees monotheism developing through the prophetic movement, not predating it. The standard reading of texts like Amos and Hosea is that they are *arguing against* polytheistic practice, not assuming monotheistic belief.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets may share theological vocabulary with the popular religion without that religion being monotheistic. Popular religion could have been henotheistic (YHWH supreme among gods) while the prophets pushed toward exclusive monotheism. The "rift" Kaufmann acknowledges between prophets and people (they suffer violence, are expelled) suggests the people didn't share the prophets' full vision — even if both used the name YHWH.

**My assessment:** This claim is the load-bearing pillar for Kaufmann's entire prophetic section. He is right that the classical prophets never argue *that* YHWH is the only God — they argue *what YHWH demands*. This is a real textual datum that evolutionary models must explain. But the inference from "the prophets don't argue for monotheism" to "the people were already monotheists" is a leap. The prophets might not argue for monotheism because (a) their audience already accepted it (Kaufmann), or (b) their rhetorical strategy was to assert it as premise rather than argue it, or (c) the monotheistic framing was supplied by later editors who collected and shaped the prophetic books. Kaufmann's reading is one plausible explanation, but not the only one.

## Claim 2: Classical prophecy ends before apocalyptic — the traditional chronological limits are correct; no "perspective visions" exist in the prophetic corpus

**Author's claim:** "The distinctive feature of apocalypse is its anxious inquiry and research into the secrets of the cosmos. The literary prophet, on the other hand, is first of all a messenger whose task is not to reveal hidden things, but to command or reprove in the name of God." The crucial diagnostic: "Nowhere in the entire corpus of classical prophecy... can there be found a vision with historical perspective. No succession of ages and events ever unfolds before our eyes." Therefore, "classical prophecy does not gradually blend into apocalypse" and the traditional claim that prophecy ended by the mid-5th century (Malachi) is correct. Late datings of Joel, Habakkuk, Zechariah 9-14, and Isaiah 24-27 are "groundless."

**Evidence presented:**
- In apocalypse, the visionary's chief task is "to reveal hidden things" — disclosure of secrets is "the very essence of apocalypse as is shown by its name, the meaning of which is 'revelation'"
- Apocalyptic visions are of two types: cosmic (tours of unearthly realms) and historical (succession of generations/ages). "This perspective vision... is the very heart of apocalyptic"
- Classical prophets see visions of God "but never as ends in themselves; such visions are always either an introduction or an adjunct to a message"
- "There is never a succession of scenes; the vision stands alone, and is followed by the word which the prophet is charged to convey"
- Ezekiel's tours in space make him "the father of the apocalyptic tour," but even Ezekiel "is not taken into extra-mundane realms"
- The classical prophet knows "only of two ages: the kingdom of his time and the visionary kingdom of God at the end of days, which follows directly upon... the prophet's vision concerning his own time"
- "All the detail that a prophet may amass adds up to one picture of what will happen 'on that day'"
- The complete absence of perspective visions "testifies that the two are essentially different; no literary commingling of the two realms has taken place"
- Passages assigned to late dates (Hellenistic period) are identified only through "ingenious modern midrash" — when Daniel speaks of Greek kingdoms, "it is in express terms which require no ingenuity to interpret"
- The idea that "Assyria" in certain passages means Seleucid Syria "has no basis in Hebrew... usage"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Kaufmann's distinction between prophetic visions (static, single-scene, adjunct to message) and apocalyptic visions (panoramic, succession of scenes, ends in themselves) is genuinely clarifying and well-supported by the texts. The absence of historical-perspective visions in the prophetic corpus is a real observation. But the dating question is separate — Joel or Zechariah 9-14 could be late without containing perspective visions. Kaufmann's diagnostic may exclude apocalyptic but not necessarily prove early dating.

**What's at stake:** Whether classical prophecy is a bounded historical phenomenon (8th-5th centuries) or a fluid tradition that gradually evolved into apocalyptic (extending into the Hellenistic period). If Kaufmann is right, the sharp break between prophecy and apocalyptic supports his larger claim that Israelite religion has distinct ideological stages rather than continuous evolution.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date portions of the prophetic corpus late. The critical consensus places Isaiah 24-27 ("the Isaiah Apocalypse"), Zechariah 9-14, and Joel in the Persian or Hellenistic periods. These texts share features with apocalyptic (cosmic judgment, resurrection language, vague historical referents). Plöger, Hanson, and others have argued for a gradual transition from prophecy to apocalyptic.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's distinction between "two ages" (prophecy) and "succession of ages" (apocalyptic) is real, but it's a spectrum, not a cliff. Some late prophetic texts (Isaiah 24-27, Zechariah 14) exhibit features that look transitional — cosmic scope, diminished historical specificity, universal judgment — even if they lack full-blown periodization. The absence of perspective visions in the prophetic corpus doesn't prove these texts are early; it proves they're pre-apocalyptic in form, which is compatible with a 4th-century date. Kaufmann may be right that there's no *literary* commingling, but wrong that this proves the *chronological* boundaries tradition asserts.

**My assessment:** This is one of Kaufmann's strongest literary-critical arguments. He identifies a formal feature (perspective vision) that genuinely distinguishes prophecy from apocalypse and then observes its complete absence from the prophetic corpus. This is a better argument than the standard apologetic one (tradition says so). However, his inference that "therefore the texts must be early" doesn't fully follow. A late text could still be formally prophetic rather than apocalyptic. The real test is whether the texts he defends as early (Joel, Zechariah 9-14) show other signs of lateness — linguistic features, historical allusions, theological concepts. On that question, Kaufmann's formal argument is necessary but not sufficient.

## Claim 3: The prophetic books were composed by the prophets themselves, not by circles of disciples over generations — the fixity of the text proves it

**Author's claim:** "The fixity of the text implies that prophecies were written down by the prophets themselves." The common view — that prophetic literature developed through (a) the prophet, (b) disciples who transmitted and colored the words, and (c) generations of compilers and annotators — is wrong. "A purposeful, continuous, large-scale revising of the ancient prophecies themselves is nowhere in evidence." Later editors did not penetrate the text of the prophecies; their work was limited to arrangement and narrative framework.

**Evidence presented:**
- The historical horizon of each prophet is unified and natural. Amos doesn't mention Assyria (he prophesied before Tiglath-pileser III); his words were preserved "in their original vagueness" — "Assyria was nowhere explicitly inserted." Hosea's prophecies were similarly untouched despite later generations knowing Assyria was the agent of destruction
- Isaiah son of Amoz knows only Assyria as the final enemy; "that it in turn was to fall before and be succeeded by Babylonia is nowhere indicated." Even though tradition *did* annex Isaiah 40-66 (Babylonian-Persian) to his book, "no suggestion of a succession of empires was interpolated into the early visions"
- Micah has one genuine revision: "and you shall come to Babylon" (4:10) — "one of the few instances in the whole corpus of prophecy of a genuine revision in light of later events. It is characteristically obvious, no exegetical subtlety being required to detect it"
- Jeremiah 1-19 never mentions Babylonia; only after 605 does the Babylonian theme enter (chap. 21ff.). Even though Jeremiah himself later identified the "nation from the north" as Babylon, his earlier prophecies "have not been penetrated by the Babylonian motif"
- Ezekiel "nowhere hints that Persia would have a hand in" Babylon's fall
- "The rift between the plain sense of prophecies and the actual course of events passes through the whole of the literature." If prophecies were fluid, how did this gap survive? "How is the intact transmission of prophecies that failed... to be explained except by the assumption that already during the time of the prophets themselves their words had become sacred?"
- Jeremiah 36 is paradigmatic: Jeremiah dictates to Baruch, who compiles faithfully — "despite the fact that henceforth Jeremiah's entire prophecy was dominated by the theme of Babylonia, in setting down all the earlier prophecies the scribe does not insinuate into them a single line of the new theme"
- Each prophetic book "bears the stamp of one creative personality." Isaiah 1-33 is "unique in the literature for its disregard of the motif of national revenge." Jeremiah is "altogether rooted in the ideas, symbols, and language of Deuteronomy." "Such homogeneity cannot be naturally explained on the assumption of a generations-long accretion of glosses, revisions, and supplements"
- No classical prophet is depicted as head of a prophetic guild; none works through "sons of prophets." Amos is expelled from Bethel alone; Hosea and Isaiah use children as symbols but have no disciples; Ezekiel's audiences are the exiles and elders
- The disciples "were not themselves prophets, nor did they have a hand in the creation of the prophecies"
- Against Nyberg's oral transmission theory: Israel was "not at its inception a 'primitive' people, but one that had lived for centuries among the high civilizations of the ancient Near East." Egypt and Babylonia were scribal cultures; pre-Israelite Canaan had written literature

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the *observation* that the historical horizons have not been artificially extended; MEDIUM on the *inference* that this proves authorial composition. Kaufmann's demonstration that the prophetic books faithfully preserve the limited historical horizons of their authors is genuinely impressive and underappreciated. The argument about the "rift" between prophecy and reality is particularly clever: if editors were freely revising, they'd have closed that rift.

**What's at stake:** The entire model of how prophetic literature was produced. If Kaufmann is right, the prophets were *writers* who composed their own books — not oral performers whose words were later collected and reshaped by tradition. This supports his larger claim that classical prophecy is a literary phenomenon with fixed texts, not a fluid oral tradition.

**Who disagrees:** Form criticism and tradition history. The standard critical model (Gunkel, Mowinckel, von Rad) holds that prophetic oracles circulated orally among disciples before being written down, and that the books underwent extensive redaction. The presence of third-person narrative, superscriptions, and editorial frameworks is taken as evidence of later hands. Scholars like Blenkinsopp and Petersen argue for significant redactional activity.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the historical horizons weren't artificially extended — but wrong that this means no editing occurred. Redactors could have worked *within* the existing historical framework rather than extending it. They might have rearranged, expanded, or reinterpreted oracles without adding explicit references to later empires. The unity of each book could reflect the work of a single *editor* rather than a single *author*. And the case of Micah 4:10 ("you shall come to Babylon") actually demonstrates the opposite of what Kaufmann claims — it shows that glossators *did* alter the text. The fact that this revision is "obvious" to us doesn't mean other, subtler revisions aren't present.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann makes a genuinely powerful case for the relative fixity of the prophetic text. The limited historical horizons ARE striking — if later editors were freely revising, why didn't they make Amos name Assyria? Why didn't they give Isaiah son of Amoz a vision of Babylon? The "rift" argument is Kaufmann at his most ingenious: the very fact that prophecies don't match history is evidence they weren't tampered with. However, Kaufmann may overstate. Redaction can take forms other than updating historical referents — theological glossing, rearrangement, expansion of existing themes. A redactor who adds "on that day" expansions or universalizing statements isn't changing the historical horizon but IS changing the text. Kaufmann's argument proves (convincingly) that historical updating was minimal, but doesn't prove the absence of other forms of editing. Still, this is one of his best chapters methodologically — he's identified a genuinely testable claim about the text and supported it with systematic evidence across multiple prophetic books.

## Claim 4: The chronological limit of classical prophecy is validated book-by-book by historical horizon analysis

**Author's claim:** "When the historical purview of each of the prophets is examined, it proves to belong to a definite setting and to reflect the events of one period only. The historical horizon of each prophet is always a natural one." This supports "the tradition that classical prophecy came to an end at the beginning of Second Temple times."

**Evidence presented (book-by-book survey):**
- **Amos:** Dates from Jeroboam II, before Tiglath-pileser III (before 745). Foretells exile but never names Assyria. Assyria "was nowhere explicitly inserted." "Amos' prophecies are a faithful document of the Aramaean-early Assyrian period."
- **Hosea:** Couples Egypt with Assyria, foresees destruction, but gives "no indication that Assyria would be Ephraim's scourge." Not one verse was reworked to make the Assyrian interpretation explicit.
- **Isaiah (1-39):** Bulk reflects 8th century — Aram, Egypt, Assyria locked in battle. Assyria is "the rod of YHWH's wrath," the *final* enemy. "That it in turn was to fall before and be succeeded by Babylonia is nowhere indicated." Chapters 40-66 reflect the decline of Babylonia and rise of Persia — but were kept "entirely separate from the Assyrian prophecies."
- **Micah:** Assyrian menace — fall of Samaria, deliverance of Judah. Only one later adaptation: "and you shall come to Babylon" (4:10). "Where Assyria is named... there is not a word about the empire which is to succeed it."
- **Zephaniah:** Reign of Josiah — "clearly Assyrian; there is no allusion to Babylonia or the Chaldeans."
- **Nahum:** Prophesies at the collapse of Assyria. "For him the Assyrian domination is the last; there is no hint of a new heathen empire."
- **Habakkuk:** Rise of the Chaldeans. "The prophet comforts himself that their empire will speedily collapse; he knows nothing of Persia which was to follow."
- **Jeremiah:** Chapters 1-19 never mention Babylonia (before 605). After 605, Babylonia identified as conqueror. Even after Jeremiah interprets the "nation of the north" as Chaldeans, the text of 1-19 "has not been penetrated by the Babylonian motif." Chapters 50-51 foresee Babylon's collapse — but "not a word concerning Persia, its successor."
- **Ezekiel:** Reign of Nebuchadnezzar. Chapter 21:33-37 concerns Babylon's fall — "but nowhere is there a hint that Persia would have a hand in this."
- **Isaiah 40-66:** Rise of the Persian Empire. Also the background of Haggai, Zechariah 1-8, Malachi. "No allusion to its destruction by yet another heathen empire is to be found in these books."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the observation that historical horizons are limited and consistent. This is Kaufmann's most empirically grounded argument in the chapter — he surveys the entire prophetic corpus and the pattern holds. The silence about successor empires is genuinely remarkable.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophetic books are reliable historical documents from the periods they claim, or retrospective compositions from later periods. If Kaufmann's horizon analysis is correct, the books contain authentic 8th-5th century material that wasn't substantially rewritten.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date portions of the prophetic corpus (especially Isaiah 24-27, Zechariah 9-14, Joel) to the Persian or Hellenistic periods. These texts have "undefined" historical backgrounds, which Kaufmann acknowledges but treats as neutral rather than evidence of lateness.

**Alternative reading:** The limited historical horizons prove that the *original* oracles are early — but not that the *final form* of the books is early. Redactors could have preserved the original horizons while adding material that didn't contradict them (e.g., universalizing expansions, liturgical frameworks, "on that day" additions). Moreover, the argument from silence about successor empires cuts both ways: the fact that Habakkuk doesn't mention Persia could mean Habakkuk is genuinely pre-Persian (Kaufmann) OR that a Persian-period author deliberately restricted his work's horizon to the Chaldean period for literary verisimilitude.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann at his most persuasive. He does what good historical criticism should do: he tests the texts against a clear prediction (if the books were heavily revised, historical horizons would be extended) and finds the prediction falsified. The pattern is consistent across twelve prophetic corpora — that's not coincidence. The silence about Persia in books that would naturally mention it if they were late (Habakkuk, Nahum, Jeremiah 50-51) is genuinely hard to explain on the redaction model. However, Kaufmann's conclusion that ALL the material in these books is early goes beyond what the horizon analysis proves. The horizons prove the *oracles* are early; they don't prove that every editorial framework, superscription, and arrangement is equally early. Kaufmann is right about the core prophetic material; he may overclaim about the editorial superstructure.

## Claim 5: Prophecies of consolation are not late mythological imports — they are the necessary complement to prophecies of doom within covenant theology

**Author's claim:** "Both the reproof and the consolation of the prophets spring from the same ground — the perpetuity of the kingdom of God." There is "no need... to look for a special source of Israel's prophecies of consolation (e.g., the Gunkel-Gressmann view that it stems from popular mythological-eschatological beliefs)." The prophets denounce Israel for profaning the covenant; "the denunciation cannot... be an end in itself; it aims at arousing the people to repentance so that the glory of the divine kingdom may be restored."

**Evidence presented:**
- Early Israelite eschatology is rooted in "faith in the perpetuity of Israel as the kingdom of God, of the covenant and its concretizations: the land, the people, national independence, and the monarchy"
- YHWH's kingdom "is not something to be achieved in the future as a result of YHWH's triumph over forces of evil in the end of days, but has already come into being through his covenant with Israel"
- "Through chastisement and suffering, Israel's sin will be expiated, and then it will return to its former state, or be even more glorious than in time past"
- The prophets share this faith: "They, too, regard the land, the people, the temple, Jerusalem, and the monarchy as the visible symbols — in the future, no less than in the past — of God's kingdom"
- Destruction "does not come from demonic sources whose defeat is a condition of YHWH's kingship. YHWH himself brings it on to expunge sin, and thereby to reconstitute his kingdom"
- "He remains king during the period of chastisement"
- "To separate the religious-moral from the national-messianic elements of prophecy reflects a modern prejudice which cannot serve as a criterion for the ideological analysis of Israelite prophetic literature"
- Prophecies of ingathering "do not necessarily imply a date after 586. From the times of Tiglath-pileser an ever growing Israelite dispersion existed"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the theological coherence argument; MEDIUM on the historical claim. Kaufmann's demonstration that consolation follows naturally from covenant logic is convincing — if YHWH's kingdom is eternal, punishment must be temporary. But whether this eliminates the need for *any* external influence on the *form* of consolation prophecies is less certain.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophetic books are theologically unified (doom + consolation = single message) or theologically composite (doom = original prophet, consolation = later additions). The critical view often treats consolation oracles as secondary precisely because they seem to undermine the urgency of repentance — if restoration is guaranteed, why repent? Kaufmann's answer: because restoration *through* repentance is the whole point.

**Who disagrees:** The Gunkel-Gressmann school, which traced Israelite eschatology to borrowed mythological themes (the combat myth, the dying and rising god, the New Year festival). More generally, scholars who see consolation oracles as exilic or post-exilic additions to pre-exilic doom oracles. The historical-critical distinction between "authentic" doom and "secondary" consolation remains influential.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that consolation *theologically* follows from covenant logic, but wrong that this means all consolation oracles are *historically* from the named prophets. A post-exilic writer could generate consolation oracles from the same theological logic — they wouldn't need to borrow from mythology. The question is not whether consolation is theologically coherent with doom (it is), but whether particular consolation passages come from the 8th-century prophet or from later tradents working within the same theological framework. Kaufmann's theological argument doesn't answer the historical question.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's theological argument is elegant and genuinely insightful. He correctly identifies that in a covenant framework, judgment and restoration aren't opposites — they're two movements of the same divine action. Punishment is purgative, not destructive; the kingdom persists through judgment. This is a better reading of the prophetic logic than the fragmentation approach that assigns doom and consolation to different hands based on theological tension. However, Kaufmann's rejection of *any* mythological influence is too sweeping. The imagery of the "day of YHWH" as cosmic battle, the language of creation's transformation, the vision of universal peace — these have genuine parallels in ancient Near Eastern mythology even if their *theological function* in Israel is different. Kaufmann is right about the theological logic but overly dismissive of the cultural repertoire the prophets drew upon. You can have covenant logic AND mythological imagery — they're not mutually exclusive.

## Claim 6: Classical prophecy created the idea of universal history — a new "historiosophy" between the early religion's dualism and apocalyptic's periodization

**Author's claim:** The early religion knew only two periods (primeval monotheism → Babel → nations with idolatry; then YHWH reveals himself to Israel alone). "Israel alone was henceforth the subject of history." What happens to the nations "has no divine meaning, and is therefore not history in the biblical sense." This division was conceived as "perpetual." Classical prophecy broke this framework: "A reconstituted, universal history first appears with Isaiah's vision of the ultimate return of the nations to YHWH in the future (Isa. 2). At the culmination of history mankind is reunited, all sharing the divine grace which heretofore had been the portion of Israel alone."

**Evidence presented:**
- The early religion's view: "prior to the tower of Babel, a primeval monotheism reigned among men; subsequently the nations came into existence with idolatry as their 'portion'"
- "The nations, deprived of the supreme human value — knowledge of God — stood outside"
- The new eschatology of the Aramaean wars "did conceive of a universal 'day of YHWH,' a judgment of all nations, but it was left for classical prophecy to give full expression to this new idea"
- Isaiah 2's vision of nations streaming to Zion is the breakthrough
- "With Habakkuk and Jeremiah, the idea that Israel's historical mission is to combat the idolatry of men is born. Idolatry has no right to existence; the ideal is one humanity under God"
- This is "the background of the prophecies of the Second Isaiah"
- Apocalyptic takes the final step: Daniel's four monarchies succeeded by the kingdom of God; idolatry becomes the sin for which nations are punished; the heathen realm becomes "the kingdom of Belial, of Satan"
- "This view is at once the climax of universalism and particularistic intolerance"
- "Again we see that there is no promiscuous mixing of realms, as current opinion would have it"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Kaufmann's three-stage schema (early religion = dualistic particularism → prophecy = universal history → apocalyptic = universalism + intolerance) is elegant and captures real ideological differences. The identification of Isaiah 2 as the pivot point is plausible. But the schema is perhaps too neat — it may compress a messier developmental process.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophetic vision of universal history was a genuine innovation (Kaufmann's view, though he locates the innovation within an already-monotheistic framework) or the natural outgrowth of an evolving monotheism (the critical view). Kaufmann wants to show that the *idea* of universal history appears at a specific stage — classical prophecy — and doesn't belong to either the earlier or later stages.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see universalism developing more gradually. Smith (Origins Ch 10) traces universalistic language emerging in Second Isaiah as a response to the exile, not present in the 8th-century prophets. Römer sees Israel's universal mission as a late development. The critical view would place Isaiah 2's Zion pilgrimage vision later than the 8th century — possibly exilic or post-exilic.

**Alternative reading:** The "new historiosophy" may not be as unified or as early as Kaufmann presents it. Isaiah 2:2-4 (paralleled in Micah 4:1-3) is widely regarded as a late insertion — its universalism is anomalous in 8th-century prophecy. If that's correct, the breakthrough Kaufmann attributes to Isaiah son of Amoz may actually belong to the exilic or post-exilic period. The stage of "classical prophecy" would then be less universalist than Kaufmann claims, and the distance between prophecy and apocalyptic would be smaller.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's three-stage schema is illuminating as an *ideological* typology, even if the *chronological* assignment of particular texts is contested. He's identified real differences in how the three bodies of literature conceive of history: (1) Torah/early literature = Israel alone is history's subject, nations are background; (2) classical prophecy = nations will eventually join Israel in worshipping YHWH; (3) apocalyptic = history is periodized, nations are judged for idolatry, and the kingdom of God replaces human empires. These are genuine ideological shifts. But Kaufmann's insistence on sharp boundaries ("no promiscuous mixing") may reflect his systematic mind more than the messier reality of literary development. The Zion pilgrimage vision (Isa 2/Mic 4) doesn't fit neatly — it's universalist in a way that looks more like Second Isaiah than 8th-century prophecy. Kaufmann's dating of it to Isaiah son of Amoz is crucial to his schema but debatable.

## Claim 7: Writing was a customary mode of prophetic communication — not a late development or emergency measure

**Author's claim:** "Writing was a means of communication hardly less customary than speech." The prophets "were not only speakers, they also created a written literature. There is no internal conflict between speech and writing, nor is there any basis for the view that only in Second Temple times were prophecies set down in writing." The notion that prophets resorted to writing only in emergencies "is naive."

**Evidence presented:**
- The prophets could "fulfill their primary task only through speech or action," and they "confronted their audience directly in the first instance"
- But "it is naive to assume that the prophets could really convey their message 'to the people' through speech alone. In fact, they addressed only a limited audience — even when in the temple court or the market"
- "The limited circle of listeners was supplemented by the broader circle of readers"
- The prophets were poets; "there is no ground for asserting that such poetry was not collected and indited"
- Explicit references to prophetic writing: Isaiah writes "on a large tablet" (8:1) and "in a book" (30:8); Habakkuk writes his vision "on tablets" (2:2); Jeremiah writes prophecies of consolation "in a book" (30:1ff.) and prophecy of woe "in a book" (36:1ff.); Ezekiel receives a "scroll" (2:8-3:4); Isaiah 34:16 mentions a "book of prophecies"
- Ancient Israel had written poetic collections: "the book of the Wars of YHWH" (Num 21:14), "the book of Jashar" (Josh 10:13; II Sam 1:18)
- Against Nyberg's oral transmission theory: "Israel was not at its inception a 'primitive' people" — it had lived among high civilizations; pre-Israelite Canaan had written literature; Egypt and Babylonia were scribal cultures
- The rule: "speech is followed by writing" — God speaks the Decalogue, then writes it; Moses rehearses laws, then writes them; Samuel tells the rule of monarchy, then writes it
- Jeremiah 36 shows the pattern: Jeremiah dictates to Baruch, who compiles. Old prophecies were "not recited by heart, but were written down and read from writing"
- The reason prophetic sermons were preserved in extenso beginning with Amos: "the new level of thought" — "Unlike their predecessors, the classical prophets were important for what they said more than for what they did"
- This literature clusters around the two falls: "it arises just before the fall of Ephraim and ends not long after the fall of Judah"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Kaufmann's case that prophetic writing was early and customary is well-supported by the internal biblical evidence (explicit references to writing) and the comparative ancient Near Eastern context (scribal cultures). His argument against Nyberg's oral-transmission theory is decisive.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophetic books are the *original literary compositions* of the prophets (Kaufmann) or the *written deposits of oral tradition* (form criticism). If Kaufmann is right, the literary quality of the prophetic books is authorial, not the product of scribal schools.

**Who disagrees:** The form-critical school (Gunkel, Mowinckel) and Scandinavian oral-tradition theorists (Nyberg, Engnell, Nielsen). They argued that prophetic oracles circulated orally for generations before being written down, and that the written form is late and secondary. The emphasis on "Sitz im Leben" (setting in life) assumes oral performance as primary.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that writing existed from early times and that prophets wrote. But this doesn't mean ALL prophetic material was written by the prophets themselves. Oral performance and written composition could coexist — a prophet might speak oracles publicly and write some down, while disciples preserved others. The picture in Jeremiah 36 (dictation of a comprehensive scroll years into his ministry) suggests writing could be retrospective and selective. The "speech followed by writing" pattern Kaufmann observes could mean that some prophetic material was initially oral and only later committed to writing — by the prophet, but not simultaneous with the original utterance.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right to push back against the extreme oral-transmission model, which underestimated literacy and scribal culture in ancient Israel. His arguments against Nyberg are solid: Israel wasn't pre-literate Arabia; it was embedded in the literate cultures of Egypt and Mesopotamia. The explicit references to prophetic writing in Isaiah, Jeremiah, Habakkuk, and Ezekiel are real and can't be dismissed. However, Kaufmann's model may be too exclusively literary. Prophets probably DID speak publicly (the temple court, the city gate) AND write. Some oracles may have been composed in writing from the start; others may have been spoken first and written later. Jeremiah 36 depicts exactly this — Jeremiah verbally prophesied for 23 years before compiling a comprehensive scroll. That's both oral (initial delivery) and written (subsequent compilation). Kaufmann's emphasis on writing is a needed corrective to oral-transmission theory, but his denial of any significant oral phase may overreach. The more interesting question is why the clustering around the two falls: the existential crisis of the 8th and 6th centuries generated literature precisely because people needed to understand why God had destroyed what he had established.

**Strongest section:** The historical horizon analysis (Claims 3-4). Kaufmann's systematic survey of twelve prophetic corpora, demonstrating that each has a unified and limited historical horizon that wasn't artificially extended by later editors, is his most empirically rigorous argument. The silence about successor empires — especially Persia in books that would naturally mention it if late — is a genuine datum that standard redaction models struggle to explain. This section deserves more attention than it has received in critical scholarship.

**Weakest section:** Claim 1's dependence on Chapter IV. The assertion that classical prophecy "sprang from the soil of Israel's popular monotheism" is a promissory note. Kaufmann has established that the prophets *share vocabulary* with the early religion, but he hasn't demonstrated that the early religion was *monotheistic* in the popular sense he claims. This chapter works as a framing overview but doesn't independently support Kaufmann's most radical thesis.

**Methodological note — Kaufmann's method in this chapter:** This is Kaufmann at his most literary-critical, and it's where his method works best. Instead of making sweeping claims about the "fundamental idea" of Israelite religion, he examines specific formal features of the texts (perspective visions, historical horizons, patterns of revision) and tests them against predictions. The horizon analysis is genuinely falsifiable — if the books had been heavily revised, horizons would be extended; they aren't; therefore the revision model is questionable. This is closer to how critical scholarship should work. It's notable that Kaufmann's most convincing arguments come when he's closest to standard critical method, and his most contested ones when he's furthest from it.

**Cross-reference assessment:** This chapter sets up the entire prophetic section (Chapters XI-XIII) and should be read alongside Chapter XIII (The Prophecy of the Fall) for Kaufmann's reading of specific prophets. The claim that classical prophecy inherited monotheism (Claim 1) is the prophetic counterpart to Chapter IV's argument about popular religion. The historiosophy discussion (Claim 6) provides crucial context for understanding Second Isaiah, whose monotheism Smith (Origins Ch 10) reads as a creative *innovation* — exactly what Kaufmann denies. The compositional argument (Claims 3-4) challenges the form-critical assumptions underlying much of Smith's and Römer's treatment of the prophetic corpus.

**Unresolved tension:** Throughout this chapter, Kaufmann wants to have it both ways on the relationship between prophets and people. The prophets inherit their monotheism from the people (Claim 1). But the prophets also *transform* the people's eschatology (Claim 6), *repudiate* the power state that the people celebrate (lines 109-110), and suffer *violence* at the hands of the people and priests (lines 42-43). If the prophets and people share the same monotheistic faith, why the violence? Why the "rift" Kaufmann himself acknowledges? His answer — that the rift is about *practice* and *cultic consequences*, not *theology* — is coherent but strained. A rift deep enough to produce expulsion, imprisonment, and (in tradition) martyrdom suggests more than a disagreement about cultic implications. It suggests the prophets were calling the people to something the people didn't already possess. Kaufmann's framework can accommodate this as "rising above" popular religion rather than opposing it, but the line between "rising above" and "opposing" is thin enough that one wonders whether it's a distinction without a difference.

**The unanswered question:** If the prophets inherited their monotheism from the popular religion, where did the popular religion get it? Kaufmann has traced the chain of transmission (Torah → people → prophets) but hasn't explained the origin. That's Chapter VI (The Origins of Israelite Religion), which I need to read to complete the picture. Chapter X is successful as a *framing* chapter — it establishes that classical prophecy is a coherent, bounded literary phenomenon with genuine historical referents. Whether its theological content is *inherited* or *innovated* remains the question that divides Kaufmann from the entire critical tradition.
