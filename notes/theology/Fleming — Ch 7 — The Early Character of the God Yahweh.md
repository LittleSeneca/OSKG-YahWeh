# Fleming — Chapter 7: The Early Character of the God Yahweh

This concluding chapter asks: if Yahweh was originally the name of a Shasu people (not a god), and only later became a deity, what kind of god was he? Fleming argues that Yahweh's early character is best understood NOT through the standard Levantine categories (storm god like Baal, patriarch like El) but through analogies to South Arabian "political gods" — deities like Almaqah of Saba' and 'Amm of Qataban, who bind people and king into a single kinship-based community. These gods derive their power from their identification with a specific people, not from a primordial role in the natural world. Yahweh was a god of people without kings, allied for mutual defense — and only later, under the Israelite monarchy, acquired the trappings of storm god, warrior, and eventually sole God. The chapter also argues that we cannot confidently reconstruct an early Israelite "pantheon" — the evidence is too thin, and models derived from Ugarit and Mesopotamia may mislead.

---

## Claim 1: Yahweh was NOT originally a storm god or young warrior — these associations are secondary

**Author's claim:** "All of these associations, however archaic, came afterward" (p. 294). "I do not find Yahweh's associations with rain or storm to be demonstrated as original to his worship" (p. 255). "Every likeness to a major type is likely to be secondary, attached to Yahweh... as an extrapolation from his basic centrality to his own people" (p. 265).

**Evidence presented:**
- The storm imagery in Judg 5:4-5 (dripping clouds, quaking earth) belongs to the monarchic hymn, not the older battle account (Ch 4 conclusion)
- Psalm 29 is clearly a Baal hymn adapted for Yahweh — "Yahweh thus replaces Hadad or Baal, although it is not obvious when and against what religious background" (p. 281)
- The Mesha inscription shows Kamosh fighting for Moab without storm imagery — being a people's primary deity doesn't require storm god attributes
- The Kurkh Monolith shows Assur granting victory without storm imagery — "fighting for a people" doesn't make a "war god" (p. 282)
- The South Arabian political gods (Almaqah, 'Amm) lack nature-god profiles — they're defined by their identification with a people, not by cosmological function
- Yahweh's "young warrior" profile (Smith's second tier) is an artifact of measuring Yahweh against the Ugaritic pantheon, not independent evidence

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The claim is plausible and fits Fleming's overall model, but it's hard to prove a negative. The absence of storm/warrior features in the earliest evidence doesn't prove they weren't original — it could reflect the fragmentary nature of the evidence.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh was never originally a storm god, the entire Berlin hypothesis (Yahweh as local highland storm deity) loses its foundation, and the Midianite-southern-storm-god variant (van der Toorn, Smith, Römer) also falls.

**Who disagrees:** Van der Toorn (1999), Smith (2001a, 2004), Müller (2008), Pfeiffer (2017), Römer (2015) — all identify Yahweh's original character with storm or warrior features. This is close to a consensus position.

**Alternative reading:** Yahweh's storm features are too deeply embedded in the earliest poetry to be secondary. Psalm 29 adapts a Baal hymn precisely BECAUSE Yahweh was already a storm god — you don't borrow hymns for gods of completely different types.

**My assessment:** Fleming is pushing against a strong consensus here. His South Arabian analogies are genuinely illuminating and underutilized in the field, but they're analogies, not evidence about Yahweh specifically. The claim that storm features are "secondary" is plausible but would require a much fuller argument than this chapter provides.

## Claim 2: The best analogies for early Yahweh are South Arabian political gods (Almaqah, 'Amm) — deities who bind people and king through kinship

**Author's claim:** "In the end, the best analogies for thinking about Yahweh before Israel come from South Arabia" (p. 294). "The power of Almaqah and 'Amm derived from their complete identification with the people they unified and protected. The peoples of Saba' and Qataban were their family, 'the descendants of Almaqah' and 'the descendants of 'Amm'" (p. 295).

**Evidence presented:**
- Robin (2018) documented the pattern: each major South Arabian kingdom had a "great god" who bound communities together as "descendants" (wld) of that deity
- 'Amm of Qataban: the "Descendants of 'Amm" (wld 'm) are "a vast coalition of communes constituted by Qataban and led by its king" (p. 261)
- The divine name 'Amm is cognate with Hebrew 'ām ("people") — the god's very name means "Forefather" or "People" (p. 262)
- Almaqah of Saba' follows the same pattern: the "Descendants of Almaqah" assembled annually at Marib
- Kamosh and Moab show the same pattern in the Mesha inscription: slaughter devoted "to Kamosh and to Moab" (line 12), and the Bible's "people of Kemosh" ('am kĕmôš, Num 21:29; Jer 48:46) (pp. 263-264)
- The South Arabian evidence shows that the terminology of divine descent (wld 'm, wld 'lmqh) is old — "visible in the earliest documentation" — indicating "the kings latch onto conceptions that already exist" (p. 262)
- "The 'Descendants of 'Amm' would then have been such before kings" (p. 262) — the type predates monarchy

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The South Arabian evidence is genuinely illuminating and has been almost entirely neglected in Yahweh-origins scholarship. The structural parallels (people defined by divine descent, god and people joined in ritual, kinship terminology) are striking. But South Arabia is geographically distant and chronologically later (first millennium BCE) — the analogy may illuminate the pattern without proving historical connection.

**What's at stake:** This is Fleming's positive model for what Yahweh was before Israel. If correct, Yahweh was a "political god" in the South Arabian sense — a deity whose identity was inseparable from the people he unified, before any king claimed him.

**Who disagrees:** This is Fleming's own synthesis. No one has applied the South Arabian model to Yahweh before. The standard alternatives are: Yahweh as El-epithet (Cross), Yahweh as southern storm god (van der Toorn, Smith, Römer), or Yahweh as local highland storm god (Berlin hypothesis).

**Alternative reading:** The South Arabian pattern may reflect a different historical development — city-states coalescing into kingdoms under unifying deities. This may not apply to the mobile pastoralist Shasu setting that Fleming reconstructs for early Yahweh.

**My assessment:** This is the most creative contribution in the chapter. Fleming's knowledge of Mari and his willingness to range beyond the Levantine evidence pays off here. The South Arabian analogies are genuinely more apt than the Ugaritic ones for understanding a god defined by identification with a people. But the geographical and cultural distance is real, and the analogies are suggestive rather than probative.

## Claim 3: Mesopotamian analogies (Marduk, Assur, Itur-Mer) disprove van der Toorn's objection that a major deity cannot originate from identification with a limited people

**Author's claim:** "The most successful of great gods from Mesopotamia counter the objection that a deity rooted in identification with a limited people could not emerge as a major god. The actual histories of gods like Marduk and Assur demonstrate otherwise" (p. 265).

**Evidence presented:**
- Marduk: originally the god of Babylon — a single city — yet became "one of the great gods of ancient Mesopotamia" with the rise of the Old Babylonian kingdom (p. 256)
- Assur: god of the city Assur, sharing its name. "Deity and city share the name" (p. 257). "Already in the earliest second millennium... Assur was still just an important merchant town and its god a divine embodiment of its population" (p. 257)
- Itur-Mer at Mari: a god whose name takes the form of a full personal name, yet was "king of Mari" and presided over oaths — "this does not represent a type that prevented such prominence" (p. 256)
- Van der Toorn (1999: 914) had argued a major deity couldn't originate as a deified ancestor because "their worship tends to remain local." Fleming counters: Marduk, Assur, and Itur-Mer all began locally and became major.
- "With growth of social scale and developing political structures it is natural for a god identified with a particular people to be transformed in the process" (p. 265)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Mesopotamian counter-examples are well-documented and effectively refute van der Toorn's objection. Fleming doesn't need to prove Yahweh followed the same trajectory — only that the trajectory is possible.

**What's at stake:** This clears a major objection to Fleming's model. If Marduk could go from city-god to pantheon-head, Yahweh could go from people-god to sole God.

**Who disagrees:** Van der Toorn (1999) — but his objection was specifically about DEIFIED ANCESTORS, not about gods identified with peoples. Fleming somewhat blurs this distinction.

**Alternative reading:** Marduk and Assur became great through imperial conquest, not through inherent qualities. Yahweh had no empire — the comparison may not transfer.

**My assessment:** The Marduk/Assur analogies are effective against van der Toorn's specific objection, but they don't prove Fleming's positive model. They show that local gods CAN become great — not that Yahweh DID follow this path. The objection is cleared; the positive case still needs to be made.

## Claim 4: Yahweh and El were originally separate deities — El was Israel's original god, and Yahweh arrived later

**Author's claim:** "I conclude that the divine name did not originally pertain to El and that we must grapple with the reality of two key gods underlying the single God of late monarchic Judah and Judaism" (p. 284). "The first 'god of Israel' was El, as the deity in the personal name yiśrā'ēl" (p. 270, citing Smith 2001a: 142-45 and Lewis 2020b).

**Evidence presented:**
- The name "Israel" (yiśrā'ēl) likely means "May El contend" — El is the theophoric element, not Yahweh. "Such a connection would go back at least to the late 13th century, when Israel appears in the Merenptah stela" (p. 271)
- Shechem preserves El traditions without any reference to Yahweh: the temple of "El-Berith" (El of the Covenant) in Judg 9:46; Jacob's altar to "El, god of Israel" (Gen 33:20); and Rehoboam's assembly there (1 Kgs 12) — all under El's supervision (pp. 271-272)
- The Deir 'Alla plaster inscriptions (9th/8th c., east of the Jordan) record visions of Balaam by the "utterance of El" — "the presence and prominence of El, without concern for Yahweh, display the persistent centrality of El in this region as a god unto himself" (p. 269)
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud shows the convergence was underway by c. 800 BCE: blessings in the name of "Yahweh... and his asherah" assume Yahweh has absorbed El's consort (p. 269)
- At Jerusalem, Yahweh was a newcomer: "the narratives of Samuel and Kings envision Yahweh as a new arrival there, with David and his family" (p. 279). David took Jerusalem from Jebusites and brought Yahweh with him
- Jerusalem's pre-Israelite traditions associate the city with El Elyon, not Yahweh: Melchizedek is priest of El Elyon in Gen 14:18-20 (p. 278)
- The Mesha inscription (c. 840) already associates Israel with Yahweh — but this is Omride Israel, not earlier. "Nothing indicates or requires that Yahweh was a god of the El type" (p. 280)
- "Like Aššur at the city by that name, Yahweh may naturally have assumed the dominant role once a larger social and religious world came into play, and his identification with El may have resembled the eventual equation of Aššur with Marduk and Enlil in the ambitious Assyrian kingdom" (p. 280)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The separate-origin thesis is the default position of most scholars (Smith, van der Toorn, Römer, Lewis). The Deir 'Alla and Shechem evidence strengthens it considerably. Fleming's specific contribution is adding the Shasu evidence to the case and providing the Mesopotamian analogies for how such convergence happens.

**What's at stake:** If Israel's name contains El and its earliest cult sites are El sanctuaries, then Yahweh was a secondary arrival in Israel — exactly what Fleming's "Yahweh before Israel" thesis requires. The convergence of El and Yahweh was a political-theological process driven by monarchy.

**Who disagrees:** Cross (1973): Yahweh originated as a cultic epithet of El — they were always the same. De Moor (1997): Yahweh as "God of the Fathers" = El.

**Alternative reading:** Cross's theory explains why the Bible shows so little tension between Yahweh and El — if they were always identified, there was nothing to converge.

**My assessment:** Fleming is on solid ground. The separate-origin position is well-supported by the name "Israel," the Shechem traditions, and Deir 'Alla. The Shasu evidence adds weight but isn't necessary to the argument.

## Claim 5: The name "Israel" contains El, not Yahweh — and the archaeological/epigraphic evidence confirms El was the region's original high god

**Author's claim:** "If 'the god' in yiśrā'ēl is indeed El and not the generic 'god' of many such personal names, this would establish a particular link between El and Israel that goes back to the origin of the people's identity... In this relationship, Israel would have no unique claim on El but would rather claim participation in the interests of the major deity. At the moment when Israel received its name, no role for Yahweh need be supposed" (p. 271).

**Evidence presented:**
- The name "Israel" (yiśrā'ēl) contains the theophoric element 'ēl — most naturally read as the divine name El, not generic "god." Merneptah's Israel (c. 1207) was therefore an El-people, not a Yahweh-people
- Shechem was an El sanctuary: "the temple of El-Berith" (Judg 9:46), Jacob's altar to "El, god of Israel" (Gen 33:20), Rehoboam's coronation assembly there (1 Kgs 12) — three separate traditions all associating collective Israel with El at Shechem, with no role for Yahweh (pp. 271-272)
- The Deir 'Alla plaster texts (9th/8th c., east of the Jordan, within Israel's territory) record Balaam's visions by "the utterance of El" — entirely El-focused, with no mention of Yahweh. The texts are in Aramaic, not Hebrew, representing local tradition independent of both kingdoms' official cults (pp. 269-270)
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud (c. 800) shows the convergence process: "Yahweh of Samaria" and "Yahweh of Teman" appear alongside blessings invoking "his asherah" — Asherah was El's consort at Ugarit, so her pairing with Yahweh shows Yahweh absorbing El's attributes and family (p. 269)
- The KA 4.2 plaster text at Kuntillet 'Ajrud uses both names (El and Yahweh) with reference to the same deity — the convergence was underway but not yet complete (p. 269)
- Jerusalem's pre-Israelite traditions: Melchizedek is priest of "El Elyon, creator of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:18-20). David took Jerusalem from Jebusites — "the narratives of Samuel and Kings envision Yahweh as a new arrival there" (p. 279)
- Iron Age I archaeological evidence for cult sites in the region of earliest Israel: the "Bull Site" (bronze figurine on a high ridge east of Dothan) and the Mount Ebal enclosure — both in the territory between Shechem and the Jezreel Valley, the likely location of "little Israel" (p. 272)
- "Both Yahweh and El had political roles in the region. El may be represented in Israel's name, and collective Israel was defined in relation to El at Shechem; and however else he may have been present in the region, Yahweh undergirded an arrangement for mutual self-defense that reached a larger space" (p. 280)

**Confidence:** HIGH for the name "Israel" containing El. MEDIUM-HIGH for Shechem as an El sanctuary (the biblical texts are late but the convergence of three independent traditions is striking). MEDIUM for Deir 'Alla as evidence of persistent El worship (the texts are fragmentary and their relationship to Israelite territory is debated).

**What's at stake:** If Israel's very name invokes El, and its earliest cult sites are El sanctuaries, then Yahweh was a secondary god in Israel — exactly what Fleming's "Yahweh before Israel" thesis requires. The sequence is: El was the original high god of the region, Yahweh arrived among Shasu-related groups, and only under the monarchy (9th century) did the two converge.

**Who disagrees:** Cross (1973): El and Yahweh were always the same god. Those who read 'ēl in yiśrā'ēl as generic "god" rather than the proper name El.

**Alternative reading:** The 'ēl element in yiśrā'ēl could be generic ("God contends"), not the specific deity El. And the Shechem/Deir 'Alla evidence shows El worship PERSISTED alongside Yahweh worship, not that El was chronologically prior.

**My assessment:** The cumulative weight of the name "Israel," the Shechem traditions, Deir 'Alla, and Jerusalem's El Elyon background is strong. Fleming's specific contribution is showing how this convergence fits the broader pattern: just as Assur absorbed Enlil and Marduk as the Assyrian empire grew, Yahweh absorbed El as the Israelite kingdom expanded.

## Claim 6: We cannot reconstruct an early Israelite "pantheon" — the evidence is too fragmentary and models from Ugarit/Mesopotamia are imperial-scale projections

**Author's claim:** "In the decentralized social and political landscape of the early southern Levant, the idea of pantheon is more likely to lead astray than to guide" (p. 292). "When Yahweh came on the scene, was it taken for granted that he had to be fitted into a pantheon with El as head? Perhaps, but I wonder whether we have relied too much on the structures of major kingdoms to reconstruct the religion of small towns and a back country beyond" (p. 293).

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit had over 100 named deities receiving offerings at the zukru festival (p. 285) — but Ugarit was a wealthy city incorporated into the Hittite empire, not a model for the early Israelite highlands
- Deut 32:8-9 and Psalm 82, the key texts for reconstructing an Israelite pantheon, are late innovations: "the assignment of nations to gods is political and administrative, the act of a world-ruler in patrimonial mode" (p. 290)
- "Both the world oversight and the equation of Yahweh with Elyon may be understood not as prior traditions but as belonging to the setting of the author" (p. 291)
- These texts reflect imperial world-theology (Assyrian, Babylonian, Achaemenid) projected backward — "the novelty of the notion suggests innovation" (p. 290)
- "Where the social settings did not demand strict hierarchy and assignment of relationships, the gods would likewise not have been set in order" (p. 292)
- Schloen's "patrimonial" model assumes too much centralized authority — "the same variety of social configuration would have applied to the gods" (p. 292)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The caution is well-taken: we genuinely don't know how the divine world was structured in pre-monarchic Israel. But Fleming may overstate the case — the divine council imagery in the Bible (1 Kgs 22, Job 1, Isaiah 6) is too pervasive to dismiss entirely as late innovation.

**What's at stake:** This is a methodological caution, not a positive claim. Fleming is warning against overconfidence in pantheon reconstruction. If he's right, Smith's four-tier model is more speculative than usually acknowledged.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (2001a, 2004) and the Ugaritic-comparative school, who treat the divine council as a fundamental feature of West Semitic religion recoverable from biblical fragments.

**Alternative reading:** The divine council is attested across the ancient Near East (Ugarit, Mesopotamia, Israel) and is too widespread to be a late innovation. The specific form varies, but the concept is ancient.

**My assessment:** Fleming's caution is methodologically sound — we should acknowledge what we don't know. But I suspect he overcorrects. The divine council concept is deeply embedded and unlikely to be entirely late. The real question is not whether Israel had a concept of multiple gods in relationship, but what that relationship looked like in different periods and social settings.

## Chapter 7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Yahweh not originally storm/warrior god | MEDIUM | Deeply embedded storm imagery |
| 2: South Arabian political gods as best analogy | MEDIUM | Geographical/cultural distance |
| 3: Mesopotamian counter-examples to van der Toorn | HIGH | Distinction between ancestor-gods and people-gods |
| 4: Yahweh and El originally separate — El was Israel's original god | HIGH | Cross's counter-arguments |
| 5: Name "Israel" contains El; Shechem + Deir 'Alla confirm El's priority | HIGH/MEDIUM | Generic 'ēl reading; dating of Deir 'Alla |
| 6: Pantheon reconstruction unreliable | MEDIUM | Pervasiveness of divine council imagery |

**Strongest section:** The South Arabian analogies (pp. 257-264). This is genuinely new to the Yahweh-origins debate and opens up comparative possibilities that have been almost entirely neglected. The "Descendants of 'Amm" / "people of Yahweh" parallel is striking.

**Weakest section:** The "polytheism without pantheon" argument (pp. 284-293) is provocative but under-argued. Fleming raises important cautions but doesn't fully develop the alternative vision of how early Israelite polytheism might have worked.

**Overall:** This is the most synthetic and speculative chapter, but also the most original. Fleming is sketching a positive model for Yahweh's early character that doesn't depend on the standard Levantine categories. The South Arabian evidence deserves much more attention in the field. The chapter's modesty ("we cannot know") is appropriate to the fragmentary evidence.
