# Sommer, The Bodies of God — Introduction

## God's Body and the Bible's Interpreters

---

## Claim 1: The God of the Hebrew Bible HAS a body — this is the standard notion, not the exception

**Sommer's claim:** "The god of the Hebrew Bible has a body. This must be stated at the outset, because so many people, including many scholars, assume otherwise. The evidence for this simple thesis is overwhelming, so much so that asserting the carnal nature of the biblical God should not occasion surprise."

**Sommer's radically reframing thesis:** "What I propose to show in this book is that the startling or bizarre idea in the Hebrew Bible is something else entirely: not that God has a body — that is the standard notion of ancient Israelite theology — but rather that God has many bodies located in sundry places in the world that God created."

**Evidence presented — the embodied God in Genesis alone:**
- Genesis 1:26: "Let us make humanity in our form, according to our shape" — God and heavenly creatures HAVE bodies, and human bodies share their shape
- Genesis 2:7: God blows life-giving breath into Adam — action implying a mouth
- Genesis 3:8: Adam hears God "going for a stroll in the Garden of Eden at the breezy time of the day" — a being with legs
- Genesis 11:5: God "comes down" to look at the tower
- Genesis 18: God walks to Abraham's tent and converses

**Sommer's definition of "a body":** "Something located in a particular place at a particular time, whatever its shape or substance."

**Two types of divine embodiment distinguished:**

| Type | Description | Biblical Examples |
|------|-------------|------------------|
| **Non-material anthropomorphism** | God has a form but not material substance — a body of energy/light, not matter | P texts, Kaufmann's view |
| **Material anthropomorphism** | God's body has the same shape AND substance as human bodies | J texts — God walking, eating, talking face-to-face |

**On Exodus 33:20 ("No one can see Me and live"):**
> "In fact this text does not claim that God has no body for us to see; the point is rather that seeing God's body will lead immediately to death. (Similarly, the statement, 'One cannot touch a high-voltage wire and live,' does not mean that there is no such thing as a high-voltage wire; on the contrary, high-voltage wires are dismayingly, dangerously real. So is the embodied deity of the Hebrew Bible.)"

**The key verse — Exodus 33:11:** "Moses would speak with God face to face, as a man speaks with his friend." Not metaphor. Face to face. The chapter contains anthologized contradictory traditions about HOW DANGEROUS seeing God is — but ALL traditions agree God CAN be seen and HAS a body.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The biblical texts are unambiguous. Sommer's reading is straightforward — it's the denial of divine embodiment that requires interpretive gymnastics.

**What's at stake for faith:** The "spiritual," invisible, transcendent God of later Judaism and Christianity — the God who "has no body, no form" of Maimonides' Thirteen Principles — is not the God of the Hebrew Bible. The biblical God walked, talked, ate, could be seen (sometimes fatally), and had a back that Moses glimpsed. The disembodied God is a later philosophical development, not a biblical idea.

## Claim 2: Scholars have systematically avoided or denied the evidence for divine corporeality

**Sommer's claim:** "It is surprising that many scholars ignore or even deny the corporeality of the biblical God. Others acknowledge the evidence but attempt to minimize it or to claim that it is to be understood only symbolically."

**Evidence presented — three case studies in scholarly avoidance:**

1. **Gerstenberger** (*Theologies of the Old Testament*): A lengthy work that "highlights unexpected facets" of Israelite religion with strong Canaanite connections, yet "never mentions the embodied nature of Yhwh." Gerstenberger refers to Yhwh as "the invisible God" — completely ignoring the biblical evidence.

2. **Scarry** (*The Body in Pain*): A scholar of comparative literature who devotes a third of her book to analyzing biblical notions of body, yet "maintains that throughout the Old Testament God's power... are in part extreme and continual amplifications of the fact that people have bodies and He has no body." Sommer: "Scarry seems genuinely unaware of the fact that the Hebrew Bible contains not a single verse denying that God has a body."

3. **Eichrodt** (*Theology of the Old Testament*): Acknowledges anthropomorphism but dismisses it as "Canaanite influence" against which "pure Yhwhism fights violently." Blames "the great mass of the people" for the "inadequate conception" of embodied God. Sommer: "The claim that God's nonphysicality is 'left veiled'... is but a clever way of importing into the Hebrew scriptures a notion they lack."

**Sommer's diagnosis:** This avoidance stems from "medieval Christian supersessionism" — the idea that Judaism's God is corporeal and therefore primitive, while Christianity's God is spiritual and therefore advanced. Modern scholars, even Jewish ones, have unconsciously inherited this bias.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Sommer documents the avoidance persuasively.

## Claim 3: The book's thesis matters for Jewish-Christian dialogue and for understanding monotheism itself

**Sommer's claim:** Divine fluidity (multiple bodies, overlapping selves) is found in BOTH polytheistic cultures (Mesopotamia, Canaan) AND monotheistic ones (biblical Israel, later Judaism, Christianity), while it is absent in some polytheistic cultures (classical Greece). This means the polytheism/monotheism distinction is less fundamental than we think. The real distinction is between fluid and non-fluid conceptions of divinity — which cross-cuts the mono/poly divide.

**Confidence:** This is Sommer's argumentative framework. Plausible but awaits the evidence in Chapters 1-6.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| God has a body in the Hebrew Bible | VERY HIGH |
| Scholars have avoided/denied this | HIGH |
| God has MULTIPLE bodies — the book's main thesis | To be evaluated through Chapters 1-6 |

**Sommer's distinctive contribution:** He is not arguing against the consensus of Smith, Römer, and Dever. He ACCEPTS much of their historical reconstruction. But he argues that their model — polytheism → monotheism — is too simple. Early Israelite monotheism INCLUDED a fluid, multi-bodied God. This was a legitimate form of monotheism, not polytheism. The rejection of divine fluidity (by P and D) produced the transcendent God of later Judaism, but the fluid God survived in mystical traditions (kabbalah) and Christianity (the Trinity).

**The key meta-claim of Sommer's book:** "The notion of a God with multiple bodies and fluid selfhood is NOT polytheism. It is a different way of being monotheistic." This is the sophisticated counter-position that challenges the simple evolutionary narrative from polytheism to monotheism.
