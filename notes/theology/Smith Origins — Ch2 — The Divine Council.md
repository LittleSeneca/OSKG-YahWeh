# Smith Origins — Ch2 — The Divine Council

Smith maps the structure of the West Semitic divine council: its terminology, its composition (four tiers via Handy's model), and its Israelite reflexes. The chapter argues that Israel inherited a full four-tier pantheon, that Yahweh was originally a second-tier deity (a son of Elyon/El), and that monotheism resulted from the identification of El with Yahweh — collapsing tiers 1 and 2, reducing tiers 3 and 4 to derivative powers. The divine council structure survives monotheism essentially unchanged; what changes is the status of everyone below the top.

---

## Claim 1: Ugaritic assembly terminology reflects distinct divine groupings centered on specific gods, not a single generic "pantheon"

**Author's claim:** The root *phr* generates multiple expressions for divine assemblies, and the variation is not mere stylistic redundancy — it reflects real differences in referent. "The terminology of *phr* + divine name reflects different divine 'assemblies,' one belonging to El, a second to Baal, a third to Ditanu. None of these represents the pantheon as a whole." The general expression *phr 'ilm* ("assembly of the gods") might refer to the pantheon generically, but the specific constructions name particular patron deities and their households.

**Evidence presented:**

- *Phr 'ilm* ("assembly of the gods"): 1.47.29, 1.118.28, 1.148.9 — the most generic term, confirmed by Akkadian equivalent *puḫur ilāni* in RS 20.24. The -*m* on *'ilm* may indicate plural "gods" rather than the divine name El.

- *Phr bn 'ilm* ("assembly of the divine sons"): 1.4 III 14.

- *Phr m'd* ("assembly of the council"): 1.2 I 14, 15, 20, 31.

- **El-centered groupings:** *mpḥrt bn 'il* ("assembly of the sons of El," 1.65.3; cf. 1.40.25, 42), *dr 'il* ("circle of El," 1.15 III 19), *dr bn 'il* ("circle of the sons of El," 1.40.25, 33–34), *phr kbbm* ("assembly of the stars," 1.10 I 4, paralleling *bn 'il* and *dr dt šmm*).

- **Baal-centered:** *phr b'l* (1.162.17); the compound *dr 'il wphr b'l* ("circle of El and assembly of Baal," 1.39.7; 1.62.16; 1.87.18) implies the pantheon as a whole is the SUM of two parties — if *dr 'il* already constituted the whole pantheon, the addition of Baal's assembly would be redundant.

- **Ditanu-centered:** *phr qbs dtn* ("assembly of the collectivity of Ditanu," 1.15 III 15; cf. 1.161.3, 10).

- Non-divine uses of *phr* confirm it means "group" (1.23.57: "the assembly sings") and possibly the meeting-place (1.96.9–10).

- The term *dr* can mean "circle," "council," or "collectivity," based on *dr khnm* ("collectivity of priests") in 4.357.24.

- *'dt 'ilm* ("meeting of the gods") is confined to one section of Kirta (1.15 II 7, 11) — a hapax usage within the corpus.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The terminological distinctions are textually grounded and the compound *dr 'il wphr b'l* is particularly decisive: if El's circle were already the whole pantheon, appending Baal's assembly would be nonsensical.

**What's at stake:** If the Ugaritic texts preserve distinct council types (El's family council vs. Baal's assembly vs. the general pantheon), then flattening all of them into "the divine council" misreads the data. This matters for biblical parallels: when Psalm 82 mentions *'adat 'el*, which type of assembly is it invoking? Smith's answer (El's council of his sons) is more specific than Cross's broader definition.

**Who disagrees:** F. M. Cross offers a wider definition: wherever two or more deities with El are present, there the general divine assembly meets, even if council terminology is absent. Cross treats mythological scenes at El's abode as *pars pro toto* for the general pantheon.

**Alternative reading:** Cross's wider definition has heuristic value for identifying divine council *scenes* (not just explicit terminology). The mythological texts may presuppose a general assembly without naming it, and the terminological distinctions in ritual texts may reflect cultic/administrative groupings rather than distinct mythological realities. Smith acknowledges the issues are "admittedly complex and the data debatable."

**My assessment:** Smith's more cautious approach is better supported by the philological data. The compound *dr 'il wphr b'l* really is hard to explain if El's circle = the whole pantheon. But Cross is right that council *scenes* can exist without the explicit terminology — Smith concedes this implicitly when he discusses 1 Kings 22 and Isaiah 6. The distinction matters most for El's restricted family council (CAT 1.65), which seems genuinely distinct from the general assembly.

**Cross-reference:** Smith's argument here complements his Introduction claim that Ugaritic terminology is more precise than flattening labels like "Canaanite pantheon" imply. The multiple council types also support the family model developed in Ch3 — El has HIS household; Baal has HIS dependents; these are concentric, not identical.

## Claim 2: CAT 1.65 presents El's restricted family council — distinct from the broader pantheon — and may show Baal's secondary incorporation into El's household

**Author's claim:** CAT 1.65 "provides a restricted listing of those associated with El's council and family as opposed to the more general divine council." The cast of characters (El, the sons of El, TKMN and SHNM, Athirat, personified abstractions of El's qualities) belongs to "El's own household, not the company of the pantheon in general." Baal's presence (lines 10–11: Baal Sapan, Baal of Ugarit) may be a secondary incorporation reflecting Baal's importance for the Ugaritic dynasty.

**Evidence presented:**

- Lines 1–3: El (*'il*), the sons of El (*bn 'il*), the circle of the sons of El (*dr bn 'il*), the assembly of the sons of El (*mpḥrt bn 'il*). Note: *bn 'il* (not *bn 'ilm*) — the lack of -*m* marks *'il* as the singular divine name El, not plural "gods."

- Lines 4–5: TKMN and SHNM — the "pious sons" who hold drunken El's hand in 1.114 and appear in 1.17 I 30–31. Athirat joins them as El's spouse.

- Lines 6–11: Personified abstractions of El's qualities: "Mercy of El" (*ḥnn 'il*), "Constancy of El" (*nṣbt 'il*), "Well-being of El" (*šlm 'il*), the god ḪŠ (?), the god Adad, Baal Sapan, Baal of Ugarit.

- Lines 12–18: Weaponry of El — "By the spear of El" (*bmrḥ 'il*), "By the axe of El" (*bn't 'il*), "By the weapon of El" (*bṣmd 'il*), "By the strength of El" (*bdtn 'il*), "By the incandescence of El" (*bšrp 'il*), "By the firmness of El" (*bknt 'il*), "By the vigor of El" (*bgdyn 'il*).

- Line 19: "The sons of El" (*bn 'il*) repeated.

- The same limited group (minus Athirat) is the object of devotion in the ritual text 1.40.

- The weapons in lines 12–18 may suggest El's martial role, which has been disputed. Smith is cautious: *'il* could simply mean each weapon is "divine," in which case they might be Baal's weapons. But del Olmo Lete and Wyatt compare the weapons with ritual use at Emar and Mari — "perhaps such an allusion to weaponry in 1.65 suggests the ongoing martial role of El in Ugaritic thought, despite the ascendant position of Baal."

- Smith notes that most treatments of El-as-warrior have been characterized as "unfounded" due to poor evidence, but they overlook 1.65.12–14.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the restricted-council reading of 1.65 (textual evidence is strong). MEDIUM for El's martial role (the weapons could be Baal's, and *'il* could mean "divine" rather than "of El"). MEDIUM for Baal's secondary incorporation (plausible but inferential).

**What's at stake:** If CAT 1.65 shows El's restricted family council, it provides a Ugaritic *Sitz im Leben* for Israelite texts that distinguish between a broader divine assembly and a more intimate divine family. It also supports the claim (developed later) that El and Baal had distinct "households" — and that Yahweh's assumption of both roles (El's patriarchal authority + Baal's warrior profile) was a genuine theological innovation, not just a name-swap.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read CAT 1.65 as a general pantheon list rather than El's specifically — the presence of Baal and Adad could suggest a broader scope. Those who deny any martial role for El in Ugaritic texts (the "unfounded" camp). Cross's broader definition of the divine assembly would likely fold 1.65 into the general council category.

**Alternative reading:** CAT 1.65 might be a ritual text listing recipients of offerings, not a theological statement about council membership. The sequence could reflect ritual praxis (what gets sacrificed to whom) rather than conceptual grouping. The distinction between El's family and the general pantheon might be a modern imposition on a text that served different purposes.

**My assessment:** The restricted-council reading is persuasive. The absence of major second-tier deities (Anat, Athtart, Yamm, Mot, Shapshu, Yarih) — deities who appear in general pantheon lists — strongly suggests this is NOT the full pantheon. The inclusion of TKMN and SHNM (minor figures associated specifically with El's drunkenness and filial piety) further supports a household setting. However, the weaponry section's interpretation is genuinely ambiguous, and Smith's caution is appropriate. The Baal-as-secondary-incorporation reading is elegant but speculative.

## Claim 3: The Ugaritic divine assembly operates at four distinct tiers — highest authority, active deities, craftsman deities, and messenger deities (Handy's model)

**Author's claim:** Feasting scenes in the divine assembly "confirm deities from four levels in the pantheon." Smith adopts and elaborates L. K. Handy's four-tier model, with each tier having distinct functions, membership, and narrative roles.

**Evidence presented:**

**Tier 1 — Highest Authority (El and Athirat):**
- El presides, issues decrees, establishes corporate decisions. Cross and Mullen emphasize El's role in the general assembly.
- Athirat influences El's decisions — she petitions for Baal's palace (1.4 IV–V), may participate in selecting Baal's successor (1.6 I). This reflects the role of the royal wife who intercedes with her husband in political matters affecting her sons.
- Athirat's title *rbt* ("Lady") and the Akkadian epithet *rabītu* for royal wives at Ugarit support this reading.

**Tier 2 — Active/Major Deities (the seventy sons of Athirat):**
- Called *'ilm rbm* ("the great gods") — Mullen compares to Akkadian *ilū rabûtum*.
- Membership: Anat, Athtart, Athtar, YD'-YLHN, Shapshu, Yarih, Shahar, Shalim, and Baal (as an outsider who nonetheless calls El "father" — 1.3 V 35; 1.4 IV 47).
- Defining characteristics: (a) combat and conflict against cosmic enemies (Baal and Anat primarily; Athtar's martial ambition implied by his nomination to Baal's throne; Athtart's military character in Egypt); (b) association with nature or natural phenomena (Baal = storm, Yamm = sea, Mot = death, Shapshu = sun, Yarih = moon, Shahar and Shalim = dawn and dusk).
- Fertility is also a second-tier concern, but Smith cautions: "the language of identification of natural 'forces' with deities is reductionistic and potentially misleading." The terrestrial manifestation of second-tier deities occurs *in* natural realms, but nature is "rarely stressed in either proper names, prayers, or incantations." Without the Baal Cycle, Baal's warrior role would dominate scholarly assessments over his storm-god role.
- Consorts of second-tier gods are "rarely a matter of mythological speculation." Baal's spouse is unknown; his relationship to Anat is undefined. The exception (Nikkal wa-Ib, 1.24) involves a goddess outside the Ugaritic pantheon marrying an insider god (Yarih).

**Tier 3 — Craftsman Deities (Kothar wa-Hasis):**
- "Poorly represented in the Ugaritic texts" — essentially a single figure.
- Serves the upper two tiers: builds Yamm's palace (1.1 III; 1.2 III), makes Baal's weapon (1.2 IV), builds Baal's palace (1.4 V–VII).
- Occupies a "middle" position: his wisdom is superior to Baal's in some respects (1.4 V 45–48), yet he remains a servant — "the general factotum of the great gods."
- May be regarded as royalty (personal name *ktrmlk*), but his homes in Egypt and Crete (Caphtor) signal his place outside the Syro-Mesopotamian heartland.
- Smith suggests this tier "represents a particular development at Ugarit reflecting not only foreign trade...but also the important place of craftsmen in Ugaritic society."

**Tier 4 — Messenger/Servant Deities:**
- Messenger-gods (discussed further in Ch3), military retinues of major deities (*'il t'dr b'l*, "Baal's divine helpers" in 1.47.26 etc., possibly identifiable with Baal's meteorological vanguard in 1.5 V 7–9), and "divine workers" (*'inš 'lm*, literally "men of the gods," recipients of sacrifices in 1.41.27).
- Glyptic evidence: a storm-god accompanied by a figure holding weapons — possibly a member of Baal's retinue.
- Mullen notes that the Mesopotamian council "would not have included the lower tiers of the pantheon, such as workers or servants" — the West Semitic notion of assembly covers MORE levels than the Mesopotamian.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the four-tier structure as a heuristic model. MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific membership assignments. The data for tiers 1 and 2 is abundant; tier 3 relies heavily on one figure; tier 4 is documented but fragmentary. The Mesopotamian contrast (fewer levels in the assembly) is intriguing but based on Mullen's generalization.

**What's at stake:** The four-tier model provides the structural backbone for Smith's entire argument about Israelite convergence. If Israel originally shared this four-tier structure, then monotheism involved the *collapse* of specific tiers (3 and 4 reduced to derivative powers; tier 2 eliminated or absorbed) — it wasn't a wholesale rejection of polytheism but a restructuring from within. The model also enables precise comparison: what did Israel keep, modify, or discard?

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who prefer a bureaucratic model (Handy himself, following Weber) over the patrimonial household model Smith favors (see Claim 7). Those who doubt that Ugaritic religion was systematized enough to warrant a tier model. The specific placement of Baal as "outsider" in tier 2 — some would place him in a category of his own given his unique narrative prominence.

**Alternative reading:** The tiers might be a modern systematization of data that the Ugaritians themselves experienced as more fluid. Kothar's wisdom-superiority to Baal complicates a strict hierarchy. The boundaries between tiers 3 and 4 might be blurry (are "divine helpers" tier 3 or 4?). The model works better as a descriptive heuristic than as a claim about ancient cognitive categories.

**My assessment:** The four-tier model is genuinely useful and has become standard in the field for good reason. Smith's presentation is nuanced — he notes the caveats (Kothar's ambiguous rank, the nature/fertility caution, Baal's outsider status). The strongest evidence is for the tier 1/2 distinction (El presides; the seventy sons are the actors). Tier 3 is thin but Kothar really is distinct. The Mesopotamian contrast is important but needs more evidence than Mullen's generalization.

**Cross-reference:** Cross's work on the divine council focuses more on the *dynamics* of the council (El's decrees, the divine messenger, the prophetic vision) than on tier-structure. Smith's model systematizes what Cross describes narratively. The four tiers also map onto Schloen's patrimonial household model (Ch3): the patriarch and matriarch (tier 1), the children/heirs (tier 2), skilled household servants (tier 3), menial servants (tier 4).

## Claim 4: Pre-exilic Israel had the same four-tier pantheon structure as Ugarit, with Yahweh and Asherah as the divine couple at the top

**Author's claim:** "One can subject ancient Israelite texts to the same sort of analysis of divine tiers." Handy's model applies: Yahweh and Asherah form tier 1, the second tier is "vestigial" but attested, and tiers 3–4 underwent reduction and transformation.

**Evidence presented:**

**Tier 1 — Yahweh and Asherah:**
- The *asherah* in the Jerusalem temple (2 Kings 17:16; 21:7 — "the image of the asherah") was likely the goddess's symbol (tree or wooden pole) or the goddess herself.
- The eighth-century inscriptions from Kuntillet 'Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom mentioning "Yahweh and his asherah" — most scholars take this as evidence for a divine couple, whether "asherah" refers to the goddess or her cultic symbol (divine names generally don't take pronominal suffixes, supporting the symbol reading, but either way the implication is the same).
- Prophetic condemnations of asherah worship (eighth century onward) are taken as evidence that such worship was a real problem — "why condemn something unless it is a problem?"

**Tier 2 — Vestigial:**
- "There is no full-scale second tier represented in the extant biblical texts."
- Some names survive as generic nouns: Resheph (plague/pestilence), Deber (pestilence), Astarte (fertility?) — "at times little more than generic nouns."
- But in Habakkuk 3:5, Resheph and Deber appear as part of Yahweh's theophanic retinue — this "would seem to reflect already the reduction of other members of the second tier to Yahweh's servants similar to Baal's theophanic retinue described in CAT 1.5 V 6–9."
- The editing of later monotheists has likely erased many second-tier references.

**Tier 3 — Collapsed:**
- Smith doesn't provide extensive Israelite tier-3 evidence but implies it was absorbed or eliminated.

**Tier 4 — Transformed into angels:**
- Messenger-gods become "angels" (literally "messengers").
- Angels are "not regarded in later tradition as gods. Instead, they are powers that act only in the name of their patron god and only thanks to the power of that deity."
- Dead Sea Scrolls refer to angelic powers as *'lym* ("gods"), but "in the wake of the earlier telescoping of the pantheon and the collapse of its middle tiers, this word probably conveyed the sense of heavenly powers (under the One Power) rather than full-fledged deities."
- The Hodayot (1QH 7:28) asks "who is like You among the gods?" in the same words as Exodus 15:11 — but "the question in the Hodayot is devoid of its earlier polytheistic context."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Israel had a multi-tier divine world. MEDIUM-HIGH for Asherah as Yahweh's consort (the evidence is strong but the "symbol vs. goddess" debate complicates it). MEDIUM for the claim that tiers 3–4 were *once* more robust — this is necessarily inferential given the edited state of the biblical text.

**What's at stake:** This is the structural foundation for Smith's convergence model. If Israel's pantheon was always a truncated Yahweh-only court, the "collapse" narrative is unnecessary. If it was a full four-tier system, the monotheistic move was a genuine transformation of inherited structures. The asherah evidence is particularly contested because it directly challenges the traditional picture of Israelite religion as aniconic and monotheistic from early times.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue the asherah was only a cultic symbol, never a goddess (the suffix argument). Those who see Israelite religion as fundamentally distinct from "Canaanite" practice (Albright school, conservatives). Tigay's onomastic data (overwhelmingly Yahwistic names) could suggest popular monolatry was earlier and more widespread than Smith implies, reducing the scope of the pantheon.

**Alternative reading:** The four-tier structure might have been an elite/royal phenomenon at Ugarit — not necessarily replicated in highland Israel, which had a different social structure (rural, decentralized, no monarchy until later). The asherah might have been a localized Jerusalem phenomenon rather than pan-Israelite. The tiers might have existed in Israel as a *literary/theological* inheritance from Ugaritic-Canaanite culture without corresponding to lived religious practice.

**My assessment:** Smith's case for tier 1 (Yahweh + Asherah) is solid given the epigraphic and biblical evidence. The tier-2 evidence is frustratingly thin — Smith admits it's "vestigial" — but this is precisely what we'd expect if later editors systematically removed polytheistic references. The Resheph/Deber retinue in Habakkuk 3:5 is a genuinely important datum: it shows second-tier deities being demoted to Yahweh's servants, exactly as the model predicts. The angel-development argument is standard in the field and well-supported.

## Claim 5: Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8–9 preserve an older theology in which Yahweh is a son of Elyon/El, not the presider of the pantheon

**Author's claim:** Psalm 82 "preserves a tradition that casts the god of Israel in the role not of the presiding god of the pantheon but as one of his sons." Deuteronomy 32:8–9 (in its original LXX/DSS form) shows the same structure: Elyon allots nations to the divine sons, and Yahweh's portion is Jacob. This is an older polytheistic theology that later monotheistic editors suppressed.

**Evidence presented:**

**Psalm 82:**
- Verse 1: "God (*'ĕlōhîm*) stands in the divine assembly/assembly of El (*'ădat 'ēl*); among the divinities (*'ĕlōhîm*) He pronounces judgment." The figure of God (understood as Yahweh) takes his stand in the assembly — he is not described as presiding. The presider is left "decidedly mute or undescribed, probably the reason why it survived the later collapsing of the different tiers."
- Verse 6: "I myself presumed that You are gods, sons of the Most High (Elyon)." The other gods are condemned to death: "Yet like humans you will die, and fall like any prince."
- Verse 8: A prophetic voice calls on Yahweh to "arise, O God, judge the world; for You inherit all the nations." Yahweh is asked to assume the job of all gods — to rule their nations in addition to Israel.
- The name El in verse 1 "was understood in the tradition — and perhaps at the time of the text's original composition as well — to be none other than Yahweh and not a separate god called El." But the title Elyon ("Most High") in verse 6 "seems to denote the figure of El, presider par excellence not only at Ugarit but also in Psalm 82."

**Deuteronomy 32:8–9 (LXX/DSS version):**
- "When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance, when He divided up humanity, He fixed the boundaries for peoples, according to the number of the divine sons (*bĕnê 'ĕlōhîm*): For Yahweh's portion is his people, Jacob His own inheritance."
- The Masoretic Text reads "sons of Israel" (*bĕnê yiśrā'ēl*) instead of "divine sons" — E. Tov labels this an "anti-polytheistic alteration."
- The LXX/DSS version "shows Israelite polytheism which focuses on the central importance of Yahweh for Israel within the larger scheme of the world; yet this larger scheme provides a place for the other gods of the other nations in the world."
- The text is "mute about the god who presides over the divine assembly" but "does maintain a place for such a god who is not Yahweh."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH for the textual-critical argument (LXX/DSS vs. MT). HIGH for the theological interpretation (Yahweh as son, not presider). MEDIUM-HIGH that Psalm 82's presider was originally El/Elyon distinct from Yahweh (the text itself may already identify them).

**What's at stake:** This is arguably the single most important argument in Smith's book. If Yahweh was originally a second-tier deity — a son of El assigned to Israel as his inheritance — then Israelite monotheism was not a "fall" from original monotheism but an "ascent" of Yahweh from son to supreme god. The entire narrative of Israel's religious development is inverted. This has massive implications for biblical theology, the history of Israelite religion, and contemporary debates about monotheism's origins.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue El and Yahweh were always identified (Psalm 82's *'ĕlōhîm* is Yahweh, and *'ēl* is just a title). Conservative scholars who maintain Mosaic monotheism and treat these texts as rhetorical or poetic, not theological. Heiser (The Unseen Realm) accepts the divine council but argues Yahweh always presided — Psalm 82 is Yahweh judging subordinate *'ĕlōhîm*, not a son among sons. The MT's "sons of Israel" is defended by some as original (but this is a minority position in critical scholarship).

**Alternative reading:** Heiser's reading: Psalm 82 shows Yahweh as the HIGH god judging the lower gods — verse 1 has Yahweh standing IN the assembly, not AS a member. The assembly belongs to El because El IS Yahweh. Deut 32:8–9 could be read as Elyon = Yahweh, and the "divine sons" are subordinate heavenly beings, not co-equal gods. The fact that the text distinguishes Elyon from Yahweh is the very problem this reading tries to solve — and Smith's point is precisely that the distinction exists and was later erased.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading of Psalm 82 and Deut 32:8–9 is the most parsimonious explanation of the data. The MT's "sons of Israel" makes no sense in context (how does the number of Israel's sons determine the number of nations? — Israel didn't exist yet when nations were divided; there were 70 nations in the Table of Nations, matching the 70 sons of Athirat at Ugarit). The LXX/DSS reading solves this perfectly. Psalm 82:8's call for Yahweh to "inherit all the nations" only makes sense if he currently inherits only ONE (Israel). The evidence for a pre-monotheistic stage where Yahweh was subordinate to El is genuinely strong — and Smith will develop this further in Ch7.

**Cross-reference:** Cross's work on El as Israel's original god (the name "Israel" itself contains 'El, not Yahweh) dovetails perfectly with this claim. If El was Israel's original high god, and Yahweh was a separate deity who merged with him, Psalm 82 and Deut 32:8–9 preserve the "before" picture. Cross and Smith are fundamentally aligned here, though Cross might date the merger differently.

## Claim 6: The collapse of tiers 1 and 2 resulted from the identification of El with Yahweh — not from a loss of the second tier under an always-supreme Yahweh

**Author's claim:** "The collapse of the first and second tiers in the early Israelite pantheon likely was caused by an identification of El, the head of this pantheon, with Yahweh, a member of its second tier." This is the opposite of what we might assume: it wasn't that Yahweh was always at the top and the second tier was demoted; it's that Yahweh ROSE from the second tier by merging with the first-tier deity.

**Evidence presented:**

- Psalm 82 and Deut 32:8–9 (see Claim 5) show Yahweh as a son of Elyon, not the presider.
- By the eighth century, this identification was complete: "Yahweh assumed the position of presider" in Isaiah's vision (Isaiah 6) and especially 1 Kings 22:19 (Yahweh enthroned with the host of heaven).
- The Asherah shift provides a terminus: if Asherah was originally El's consort, she could only become Yahweh's consort AFTER El and Yahweh were identified. The Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscriptions (eighth century) attest "Yahweh and his asherah," so the identification was complete by then.
- "Prior to the eighth century such a 'world theology' suited the historical circumstances in Israel very well. In the world order there were many nations, and each had its own patron god." This worldview was cast as the divine patrimonial household in Deuteronomy 32.
- The change came in the eighth century when the neo-Assyrian empire "presented a new world order." Only then did Israel require a different "world theology" that "not only advanced Yahweh to the top but eventually eliminated the second tier altogether insofar as it treated all other gods as either non-entities or expressions of Yahweh's power."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The logic is compelling and the textual evidence for the earlier stage (Psalm 82, Deut 32) is strong. But the dating of the identification to the eighth century specifically relies on the asherah evidence (which is contested — see Claim 4) and on the dating of Isaiah 6 and 1 Kings 22 (which are debated). The neo-Assyrian trigger is Smith's own historical reconstruction and needs more argumentation (Ch7-10 will presumably provide it).

**What's at stake:** This is Smith's central argument about the MECHANISM of monotheism's emergence. It's not a philosophical discovery (Yahweh was always the only real god) nor a prophetic innovation (Moses/Elijah discovered monotheism) — it was a political-theological merger driven by changing world conditions. If Smith is right, monotheism was an accident of history, not an inevitability of revelation or reason.

**Who disagrees:** Albright school (Mosaic monotheism from the start). Scholars who date the El-Yahweh identification much earlier (Cross? — depends on how one reads his argument about Yahweh as the Divine Warrior who absorbs El's attributes). Those who see monotheism as developing from within Yahwism without an El-Yahweh merger (i.e., Yahweh was always supreme; monotheism was the logical extension of exclusive worship).

**Alternative reading:** The identification of El and Yahweh could have happened much earlier — perhaps even in the pre-monarchic period. If the name "Israel" contains El but the people worshipped Yahweh, the merger might be constitutive of Israelite identity itself, not an eighth-century innovation. The eighth-century evidence would then show the merger's AFTERMATH (the divine couple), not the merger itself. Smith's dating might be too late.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument is elegant and fits the available data well. The Psalm 82/Deut 32 evidence is genuinely strong for an early stage where Yahweh was NOT the high god. The neo-Assyrian trigger is plausible but needs the fuller argument from later chapters. I'm slightly uneasy about the specificity of the eighth-century dating — it relies heavily on the asherah inscriptions, which are themselves debated. But the overall direction of the argument (Yahweh rose from tier 2 to tier 1 by merging with El) is persuasive regardless of the precise dating.

**Cross-reference:** This claim is the hinge between Smith's Part I (structures) and Part III (monotheism's emergence). It's also where Smith most clearly departs from Heiser, who would accept the divine council but reject Yahweh's original subordination.

## Claim 7: The divine assembly is a patrimonial household, not a bureaucracy — contra Handy's Weberian model

**Author's claim:** The Ugaritic pantheon reflects a "patrimonial household" (Schloen's term, following Weber's category of patrimonialism) rather than a rational bureaucracy. "The Ugaritic presentation of the pantheon shows few major deities standing outside of kinship groups." The family model explains the data better than the bureaucratic model.

**Evidence presented:**

- Handy applies Weber's category of *bureaucracy* to the pantheon: monarchs and scribal schools functioned as bureaucratic government; some positions were held by people outside the kinship circle.
- Schloen counters: the monarchy was not a bureaucracy in Weber's terms but a patrimonial household with household workers. The pantheon reflects the larger patriarchal paradigm.
- Smith's evidence against the bureaucratic model:
  - Kothar wa-Hasis is the ONLY major god standing outside kinship groups — and he's an outsider whose abodes are in Egypt and Crete, not Syro-Mesopotamia.
  - "There is no typically bureaucratic divine figure such as a scribe in the Ugaritic pantheon nor any other figure apart from a divine craftsman who worked in the royal divine bureaucracy."
  - The number of family members in the pantheon far exceeds the number of bureaucratic functionaries.
- Biblical evidence for bureaucracy is also weak:
  - Handy places weight on "the satan" (Job 1–2) as a prosecuting attorney in the heavenly court. But "these are relatively few and the biblical material barely supports a general structure of bureaucracy."
  - Some individuation of angels might support bureaucracy, but the evidence is minimal.
- "Whereas the Ugaritic texts display a wide variety of relational concepts, the Israelite material manifest in the Bible shows far fewer, and what is largely left in the biblical material is the council of a single god."

**The Miller coda:** Smith engages P. D. Miller's reflections on the divine assembly:
- Miller: "The divine assembly is an image for speaking of a system for divine governance and order that is intimately involved with the world but not coterminous with it." This applies to both Ugarit and Israel.
- Miller: In Israel, the divine council deals with "the problem of the one and the many" — the "monotheistic thrust" results in "a radical centralization of divine power and reality in one deity in whom the complexity and plurality of the universe was not lost but ruled."
- Smith agrees but adds: this point "applies equally well to the divine assembly in the Ugaritic texts. The divine council mediates the problem of the one and the many in Ugaritic and Israelite language alike."
- Smith pushes back on Miller's implication that monotheism transforms the assembly: "monotheism requires one divine assembly headed by one divine ruler, but it makes little or no impact on the language of assembly in itself."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the patrimonial household model over the bureaucratic model. The absence of a divine scribe and the dominance of kinship terms are strong arguments. MEDIUM for the absolute distinction — some bureaucratic elements might exist within a primarily patrimonial framework.

**What's at stake:** The family vs. bureaucracy debate shapes how we understand the COHERENCE of polytheism. If the pantheon is a bureaucracy, its unity is functional/administrative — gods have jobs. If it's a family, its unity is relational/ontological — gods belong to each other. Smith's preference for the family model supports his larger claim (Introduction, Claim 6) that the divine family was polytheism's own form of "oneness." It also shapes the interpretation of monotheism: if the old unity was familial, the new unity (Yahweh alone) replaces relational belonging with singular sovereignty.

**Who disagrees:** Handy and scholars who find the bureaucratic model more illuminating. Those who see the family AND bureaucracy as complementary rather than competing models. Scholars who argue that the Ugaritic and biblical data are simply too thin to adjudicate between Weberian categories.

**Alternative reading:** The family and bureaucracy models needn't be mutually exclusive. The divine household could have bureaucratic functionaries within a familial structure — just as a royal court has both relatives and appointed officials. Kothar is a craftsman (bureaucratic function) but may be treated as royalty (*ktrmlk*). The binary opposition may be Smith's own construction.

**My assessment:** Smith's critique of the bureaucratic model is effective — the absence of a divine scribe is a genuinely telling point. But the family model also has limits: Baal is an "outsider" who nonetheless calls El "father" — is that familial or political? The language of divine kingship (Yahweh as king, the host as his court) has both familial and bureaucratic resonances. I think Smith is right that the family model is primary at Ugarit, but the Israelite evidence (Job's heavenly court, the satan as prosecutor) does tilt more bureaucratic than Smith acknowledges. The Miller discussion is valuable but somewhat tangential — it feels like Smith using Miller as a foil to articulate his own view.

## Claim 8: Monotheism preserves the divine council structure but empties lower tiers of independent divine status — all power becomes derivative of the One

**Author's claim:** "The Israelite presentation of the divine council differs little structurally from the Babylonian presentation of Marduk in Enuma Elish." The "monotheistic thrust" does not eliminate the council — it polarizes it into Ruler and ruled. What changes is not the structure but the STATUS of everyone below the top tier.

**Evidence presented:**

- Enuma Elish and the Hebrew Bible both present "a divine ruler surrounded by subservient divinities. Radical divine rulership manifest in these texts polarizes the divine polity into the ruler and his ruled."
- The Ugaritic Baal Cycle is actually MORE complex than either: "it manifests more complexity at the top and middle of the pantheon."
- Smith rejects the idea that Enuma Elish is "monotheistic" in structure: "this conclusion flies in the face of Enuma Elish's own reference to divinities other than Marduk as gods." Calling Babylonian polytheism monotheism "stretches the definition of monotheism beyond recognition."
- What IS distinctive about Israel: "All but the highest tier of divinities are reduced in status, no longer regarded as deities in any manner analogous to the head of the council; their power is entirely derivative and expresses the dominion of the Divine Head."
- The contrast between Babylon and Israel is political: "Babylon stands at the height of its political power, and its older deities pay homage to its newly exalted divine ruler. In contrast, Israel stands at the bottom of its political power and it exalts its deity inversely as ruler of the whole universe."
- "The imagery of the divine assembly represents for both monotheism and polytheism a pliable theological strategy for presenting order with the one and the many of divinity; this divine order may either mirror the conditions of the human world or it may oppose it."
- In Israel's case, it OPPOSES: Israel is politically powerless, so its god is cosmically all-powerful. The divine council becomes an expression of inverse exaltation.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the structural continuity claim (council structure survives monotheism). MEDIUM-HIGH for the "derivative power" characterization of angels/subordinate beings in Israelite thought. MEDIUM for the political contrast between Babylon (exalting Marduk at the height of power) and Israel (exalting Yahweh at the nadir of power) — this is a compelling reading but needs more comparative evidence.

**What's at stake:** If the divine council structure is common to polytheism AND monotheism, then the "monotheism vs. polytheism" binary is a difference of degree (how independent are the lower beings?) rather than kind (does a council exist at all?). This has major implications: (1) Israelite monotheism wasn't a rejection of the council — it was a radical reinterpretation of it; (2) the presence of a divine council in the Bible is not a "polytheistic hangover" but an integral part of monotheistic structure; (3) the real divide is between complex pantheons (Ugarit) and polarized ones (Babylon, Israel).

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat the biblical divine council as a vestige of polytheism that monotheism would ideally eliminate. Those who argue that the status-difference between Marduk's court and Yahweh's court is so great that the structural similarity is trivial. Heiser might say Smith concedes too much to the "monotheistic" label — if the Bible acknowledges other *'ĕlōhîm*, it's not monotheistic in the strict sense.

**Alternative reading:** The structural continuity might mask a functional discontinuity. In Enuma Elish, the lower gods retain agency — Marduk needs their acknowledgment. In the Hebrew Bible, angels have no independent agency — they are extensions of Yahweh's will. This isn't just "reduced status"; it's a different KIND of being. The structural similarity (ruler + court) is superficial; the ontological difference is fundamental.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument here is one of the chapter's most sophisticated. The recognition that Enuma Elish and the Hebrew Bible share a "ruler + ruled" structure is genuinely important and often overlooked. The Babylon-vs-Israel political contrast is elegant — gods mirror political realities, and when those realities invert, the theological response inverts too. Smith's caution about not calling Babylonian religion "monotheistic" is well-taken: structural similarity ≠ conceptual identity. The "derivative power" characterization is the strongest formulation I've seen of what makes biblical monotheism different from Babylonian polytheism without denying their structural commonality.

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Ugaritic assembly terminology reflects distinct divine groupings | HIGH | Cross's broader definition of assembly scenes |
| 2: CAT 1.65 = El's restricted family council; Baal secondary | MEDIUM-HIGH | Ritual-praxis reading rather than theological statement |
| 3: Four-tier pantheon (Handy model) | HIGH | Bureaucratic-model challenge; tier-boundary fuzziness |
| 4: Israel had same four-tier structure (Yahweh + Asherah at top) | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Asherah as symbol only" argument; Tigay's onomastic data |
| 5: Psalm 82 + Deut 32:8–9 show Yahweh as son of Elyon | VERY HIGH | Heiser's "Yahweh always presided" reading |
| 6: El-Yahweh identification caused tier collapse (8th c.) | MEDIUM-HIGH | Earlier dating of merger; neo-Assyrian trigger debatable |
| 7: Pantheon as patrimonial household, not bureaucracy | MEDIUM-HIGH | Family + bureaucracy as complementary, not competing |
| 8: Council structure survives monotheism; power becomes derivative | HIGH | Functional discontinuity masked by structural similarity |

**Strongest section:** Section 4 ("Israel and the Tiers of the Pantheon") — the Psalm 82 / Deuteronomy 32:8–9 analysis is the chapter's centerpiece and Smith's most important textual argument. The identification of the MT's "sons of Israel" as an "anti-polytheistic alteration" (citing Tov) is philologically decisive and theologically explosive.

**Weakest section:** Section 2 ("The Assemblies of El and Baal") — the CAT 1.65 discussion is necessarily tentative given the text's many difficulties (Smith acknowledges "the many difficulties involved in the interpretation of CAT 1.65"). The Baal's-assembly material is thin compared to El's.

**Most original contribution:** Claim 6 — that the collapse of divine tiers was caused by El-Yahweh identification (Yahweh RISING from tier 2), not by a pre-existing supreme Yahweh demoting the lower tiers. This inverts the intuitive reading of the data.

**Cross-reference connections:**
- Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*: Smith's four-tier model systematizes Cross's more narrative approach to the divine council. Both agree El was Israel's original god.
- Heiser's *The Unseen Realm*: Heiser would accept claims 1, 3, 4, 7, and 8 but reject claims 5 and 6 — for Heiser, Yahweh always presided; Psalm 82 is judgment, not promotion.
- Römer's *The Invention of God*: Compatible with Smith's overall trajectory but Römer emphasizes the political-institutional emergence of monotheism more than the structural-theological collapse Smith describes here.
- Tigay's *You Shall Have No Other Gods*: Tigay's onomastic evidence (predominantly Yahwistic names) provides a potential counterweight to Smith's picture of a robust four-tier pantheon in Israel — if most Israelites already named their children after Yahweh alone, how extensive was tier-2 worship?

**Smith's method in this chapter:** Philological precision (the *phr* terminology analysis) → textual exegesis (CAT 1.65, Psalm 82, Deut 32) → structural modeling (Handy's four tiers) → historical reconstruction (the El-Yahweh merger, the neo-Assyrian trigger). He moves from concrete (words) to abstract (models) to historical (causes). This is good method — the abstract claims are always anchored in specific texts.

**Unresolved tensions:**
1. The dating of the El-Yahweh merger. Smith suggests eighth century but the evidence (Isaiah 6, 1 Kings 22) shows the identification was COMPLETE by then — it could have happened earlier. Ch7 may address this.
2. The relationship between the divine council and the divine family (Ch3 topic). Smith gestures toward it but defers full treatment.
3. How much of the four-tier structure was Israel's LIVED religion vs. its literary inheritance. Smith treats the biblical texts as evidence for both, but the distinction matters for how we understand "popular religion" vs. "scribal theology."
