# Smith — Chapter 2: The Divine Council

Smith maps the Ugaritic divine council as a multi-tiered structure of assemblies — not one monolithic "pantheon" but several god-specific groupings centered on El, Baal, and Ditanu. He then traces how Israel inherited this four-tier structure, but collapsed it through convergence: El (tier 1) merged with Yahweh (originally tier 2), and the middle tiers telescoped, leaving only a single ruler with subservient powers. The chapter's most explosive claim: Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 preserve the older theology where Yahweh was NOT the head god, but one of El's sons.

---

## Claim 1: Ugaritic terminology reveals multiple distinct divine assemblies, not a single unified "pantheon"

**Author's claim:** "These expressions suggest a more particular organization than the pantheon as a whole, namely, various groupings centered around a specific divine figure." The "circle of El and the assembly of Baal" (dr 'il wpḫr b'l) in 1.39.7 "seem to refer to the pantheon as a whole as the sum of two parties named according to the two chief gods." This implies "dr 'il does not constitute the pantheon as a whole."

**Evidence presented:**
- Three categories of assembly language with *pḫr: (1) "assembly of the gods" (pḫr 'ilm, 1.47.29), (2) "assembly of the divine sons" (pḫr bn 'ilm, 1.4 III 14), (3) "assembly of the council" (pḫr m'd, 1.2 I 14, 15, 20, 31).
- God-specific assemblies: "assembly of the sons of El" (mpḫrt bn 'il, 1.65.3), "circle of El" (dr 'il, 1.15 III 19), "circle of El and the assembly of Baal" (dr 'il wpḫr b'l, 1.39.7), "assembly of Baal" (pḫr b'l, 1.162.17), "assembly of the collectivity of Ditanu" (pḫr qbs dtn, 1.15 III 15).
- CAT 1.65 provides a restricted listing of El's household, NOT the general pantheon: El, the sons of El, TKMN and SHNM, El and Athirat, followed by divinized qualities (Mercy, Constancy, Well-being of El) and weapons ("by the spear/axe/weapon of El"), then Baal Sapan and Baal of Ugarit. This "cast of characters... belongs to El's own household, not the company of the pantheon in general."
- Key technical distinction: bn 'il ("sons of El," 1.65) vs. bn 'ilm ("divine sons," elsewhere) — different referents. mpḫrt bn 'il ≠ pḫr 'ilm.
- Cross's wider definition (wherever two or more deities with El are present = divine assembly) is criticized: "there is no reason to assume that the mythological scenes describing El at his abode involve (even as pars pro toto) the general pantheon."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The philological evidence is meticulous. The distinction between bn 'il and bn 'ilm is a genuinely important observation that prior scholarship (including Cross) blurred.

**What's at stake:** If the Ugaritic pantheon was NOT a single unified body but multiple overlapping assemblies, then the "divine council" is not one thing. This matters for how we read Israelite divine council texts — was there ever an Israelite "pantheon" equivalent to the Ugaritic one, or were there multiple assemblies from the start? Smith's argument: Israel inherited a complex picture that it simplified, not a simple picture it corrupted.

**Who disagrees:** Cross defines the divine assembly more broadly and inclusively. Handy and Mullen tend to treat the pantheon as a more unified body. The distinction Smith draws between El's household assembly and the general pantheon is his own refinement.

**Alternative reading:** The multiple assembly terminology could reflect different literary contexts (myth vs. ritual vs. administrative) rather than genuinely distinct divine groupings. The same deities might appear in different lists under different rubrics without implying structural separation.

**My assessment:** Smith's philological case is tight. The distinction between bn 'il and bn 'ilm is real and important. The strongest point is CAT 1.65 — it clearly lists a restricted group that can't be the whole pantheon. But Smith may over-read the organizational implications: the different assembly terms might be functional subsets of a single imagined divine polity rather than evidence of genuinely separate institutions.

## Claim 2: The Ugaritic pantheon had four tiers: presiding couple, major deities (royal children), craftsman gods, and messenger/servant gods

**Author's claim:** The four-tier structure (following Handy, Mullen) provides a "heuristically helpful approach to the pantheon in Mesopotamia and Syria-Palestine."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Tier 1:** El presides; Athirat is consort who may influence decisions (petitions El for Baal's palace, 1.4 IV-V; participates in selecting Baal's successor, 1.6 I). Her title rbt ("Lady") parallels Akkadian rabîtu for royal wives.
- **Tier 2:** The seventy sons of Athirat (1.4 VI 46) — possibly designated 'ilm rbm ("great gods"). Major figures: Anat, Athtart, Athtar, YD'-YLHN, Shapshu, Yarih, Shahar, Shalim. Baal is an outsider but can call El "father" (1.3 V 35; 1.4 IV 47). Combat and natural phenomena are concentrated at this tier. Important caveat: nature is "rarely stressed in either proper names, prayers, or incantations" — Baal's warrior role is more prominent across genres than his storm-god role.
- **Tier 3:** Kothar wa-Hasis — craftsman, serves upper tiers, homes in Egypt/Crete (foreign), "general factotum" of the great gods. The only deity clearly at this level. Represents Ugarit's trade connections.
- **Tier 4:** Messenger-gods (discussed in Ch 3), military retinues, divine workers ('inš 'lm, literally "men of the gods," 1.41.27).
- Mesopotamian council may have covered fewer levels: Mullen notes lower tiers like workers/servants may not have been included in the Mesopotamian council proper.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The four-tier model is a heuristic, not an emic category. The evidence for tiers 1 and 2 is strong; tier 3 rests almost entirely on Kothar; tier 4 is a residual category.

**What's at stake:** The four-tier model becomes the template for understanding what Israelite monotheism ELIMINATED. When Smith says Israel "collapsed and telescoped" the pantheon, he means specifically: tiers 1-2 merged (El→Yahweh), tier 2 deities became either demons or Yahweh's servants, tier 3 disappeared, tier 4 became "angels." The model gives precision to what "convergence" actually means structurally.

**Who disagrees:** Handy, whose work Smith builds on, sees the model as more structurally rigid (bureaucratic). Schloen prefers a patrimonial household model. Scholars who see Mesopotamian influence rather than Ugaritic may find different tier structures.

**Alternative reading:** The four-tier model may impose an artificial order on data that is messier. Is Kothar really a "tier" by himself or just a unique figure? Are the "seventy sons" a fixed number or a literary convention? The model works descriptively but may over-systematize.

**My assessment:** The model is useful for what Smith needs it for: showing what Israelite monotheism eliminated. But it's clearly a modern scholarly construct. The strongest element is the tier 1/2 distinction — the presiding couple vs. the active warrior/nature deities. Tiers 3-4 are fuzzier. Smith acknowledges this implicitly by noting how little data supports tier 3.

## Claim 3: Israel inherited the four-tier pantheon — Yahweh + Asherah at the top — but it collapsed through convergence

**Author's claim:** "The paradigm of the pantheon went through a process of collapse and telescoping (aspects of a larger process of convergence in Israelite religion). There is no full-scale second tier represented in the extant biblical texts."

**Evidence presented:**
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions (8th century): "Yahweh and his asherah." Most scholars see these as evidence for Yahweh and Asherah as divine couple — tier 1.
- Temple asherah in Jerusalem: "the image of the asherah/Asherah" (2 Kings 21:7). Prophetic condemnations imply the worship existed — "why condemn something unless it is a problem?"
- Second-tier vestiges: Resheph and Deber appear in Habakkuk 3:5 as part of Yahweh's theophanic retinue — "already the reduction of other members of the second tier to Yahweh's servants similar to Baal's theophanic retinue in CAT 1.5 V 6-9."
- Angelic powers in Dead Sea Scrolls called 'lym ("gods") — but "in the wake of the earlier telescoping of the pantheon... this word probably conveyed the sense of heavenly powers (under the One Power) rather than full-fledged deities."
- Exodus 15:11 ("who is like You among the gods?") — when reused in the Hodayot (1QH 7:28), "the question does not carry the same freight... devoid of its earlier polytheistic context."

**Confidence:** HIGH that convergence occurred. MEDIUM that the Israelite pantheon had exactly the same four-tier structure as Ugarit's before convergence. The evidence for tier 2 in Israel is genuinely "vestigial."

**What's at stake:** This is the structural mechanism of monotheism's emergence. It's not that Israel "became monotheistic" as a philosophical decision — it's that the pantheon's structure collapsed. The identification of El with Yahweh (tier 1 + tier 2) pulled the whole structure down. What's left in the Bible is rubble, with occasional intact architectural fragments (Psalm 82, Deut 32:8-9).

**Who disagrees:** Tigay's onomastic evidence suggests widespread popular monolatry much earlier than Smith's model would predict — if most Israelites only named their children after Yahweh by the 8th century, the four-tier pantheon may have been more of a royal/scribal construct than a widely-held belief. Also, scholars who deny Asherah was a goddess (reading 'šrth as "his shrine/sanctuary" rather than "his Asherah").

**Alternative reading:** The Israelite pantheon may never have been as fully articulated as the Ugaritic one. "Vestigial" references might reflect Israel's AWARENESS of neighboring pantheons rather than its own internal pantheon structure. The four-tier model might be an Ugaritic phenomenon projected onto Israel.

**My assessment:** Smith's model is elegant but the Israelite side of the evidence is thin. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions are genuinely important, but they're two sites. The temple asherah evidence depends on how one reads 2 Kings. The model works better as an account of what Israelite scribes KNEW about pantheon structures (from Ugaritic/West Semitic heritage) and progressively rejected, rather than what Israelite popular religion actually practiced. That said, the convergence mechanism itself is independently attested by the El/Yahweh identification (see Claim 4).

## Claim 4: Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 preserve an older theology where Yahweh was originally in the SECOND tier — one of El's sons, not the head god

**Author's claim:** "In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god, but one of his sons. Accordingly, what is at work is not a loss of the second tier of a pantheon headed by Yahweh. Instead, the collapse of the first and second tiers in the early Israelite pantheon likely was caused by an identification of El, the head of this pantheon, with Yahweh, a member of its second tier."

**Evidence presented:**
- Psalm 82:1 — "God ('elōhîm) stands in the divine assembly/assembly of El ('ădat 'ēl)." Yahweh takes his stand in El's assembly, presumably as a prosecutor, not the presider. The presiding figure is either El or undescribed. The name El "was understood in the tradition... to be none other than Yahweh."
- Psalm 82:6 — the other gods are "sons of the Most High (Elyon)" — Elyon being a title of El (cf. El Elyon in Genesis 14:18-20). Yahweh is implicitly one of these sons, differentiated only by his prosecutorial role.
- Psalm 82:8 — Yahweh is called to "inherit all the nations" — to arrogate what belonged to the other divine sons.
- Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (LXX/DSS): "When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance... He fixed the boundaries for peoples, according to the number of the divine sons: For Yahweh's portion is his people, Jacob His own inheritance." The MT reads "sons of Israel" — "an anti-polytheistic alteration" (Tov). The older text has Elyon distributing nations to divine sons, with Yahweh receiving Israel.
- The theology: each nation has its own patron god; Yahweh is Israel's patron. The head god (Elyon/El) presides over all.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The MT alteration of Deut 32:8 is one of the most famous text-critical arguments in biblical studies. Tov's characterization as "anti-polytheistic alteration" is widely accepted. Psalm 82 is harder to dispute — Yahweh clearly stands IN the assembly, not above it.

**What's at stake:** This is the single most important claim in the chapter for Smith's overall argument. If Yahweh was originally tier 2, then biblical monotheism is not the survival of an original revelation but the result of a structural merger (El + Yahweh → Yahweh-El). This inverts the traditional narrative completely. It also explains why Yahweh has BOTH El characteristics (aged, wise, fatherly, council-head) and Baal characteristics (young warrior, storm-god, dragon-slayer).

**Who disagrees:** Evangelical scholars who maintain Mosaic monotheism (Heiser's "divine council" model keeps Yahweh as supreme from the beginning — the 'elōhîm in Psalm 82 are subordinate beings, not fellow tier-2 gods). Scholars who identify Elyon as Yahweh from the start (reading Psalm 82 as Yahweh judging lesser divine beings, not a separate El). Cross argues El and Yahweh were identified very early — so early that Psalm 82 already reflects the identification.

**Alternative reading:** Psalm 82 could be read as Yahweh (already identified with El/Elyon) judging lesser divine beings who have failed in their duties. In this reading, the "assembly of El" IS Yahweh's assembly, and 'ēl in verse 1 already refers to Yahweh. This is how later tradition certainly read it. The reference to "sons of Elyon" in v. 6 would then refer to Yahweh's sons, not El's. This reading is possible but requires ignoring the clear differentiation between the presider and the prosecutor.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading is the most natural one. The MT alteration of Deut 32:8 is the smoking gun — scribes changed the text BECAUSE it implied polytheism. That change only makes sense if the original reading was theologically problematic. Psalm 82's structure (Yahweh as prosecutor, not judge) is harder to explain away. The cumulative case is strong: early Israel knew a pantheon headed by El/Elyon with Yahweh as one member. The identification happened later, and the texts that preserve the older view survived because later readers re-interpreted El as Yahweh.

## Claim 5: The "world theology" changed with the neo-Assyrian empire — the old model (each nation has its own god) stopped making sense

**Author's claim:** "Other gods in their nations represented no threat to Israel and its patron god as long as they were not imported into Israel. As long as other gods did not affect worship of Yahweh in Israel, they could be tolerated as the gods of other peoples and nations. This state of affairs perhaps began to change in the eighth century when the neo-Assyrian empire presented a new world order."

**Evidence presented:**
- Pre-8th century: Deut 32:8-9 theology — each nation has its own divine son as patron. Israel's patron is Yahweh. The system works as long as boundaries are respected.
- 8th century: neo-Assyrian empire disrupts. Political boundaries collapse. "Only after this alteration of the world scene did Israel require a different 'world theology' that not only advanced Yahweh to the top but eventually eliminated the second tier altogether insofar as it treated all other gods as either non-entities or expressions of Yahweh's power."
- By the 8th century, El-Yahweh identification had occurred (since Asherah had transitioned from El's consort to Yahweh's consort by the time of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions).
- Texts like Isaiah 6 and 1 Kings 22:19 show Yahweh presiding over the divine council by this period.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that the neo-Assyrian crisis was a catalyst. The correlation (theological shift + imperial crisis) is plausible historically, but the causal mechanism is speculative.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism is partly a RESPONSE to imperial crisis, then it's not a natural theological evolution but a specific historical reaction. This means monotheism is contingent — under different political circumstances, Israelite religion might have remained polytheistic. It also means monotheism is, at its origins, a theology of RESISTANCE and HOPE, not triumphalism.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date monotheistic developments earlier (pre-8th century) or later (post-exilic). Those who see internal theological logic (not external political pressure) as the driver. Römer emphasizes the Assyrian crisis similarly; Albertz emphasizes the exilic crisis more.

**Alternative reading:** The shift from "each nation has its god" to "only Yahweh is God" might be driven by internal prophetic movements (Elijah, Hosea) rather than imperial politics. The neo-Assyrian correlation might be coincidental — the prophetic critique of other gods predates the Assyrian crisis.

**My assessment:** The correlation is real but the causation is underdetermined. Smith needs this claim to bridge the gap between "Israel had a pantheon" (Ch 2-4) and "monotheism emerged as rhetoric" (Ch 8-10). The neo-Assyrian crisis provides a plausible trigger, but Smith doesn't demonstrate mechanism — he asserts correlation. This will need the detailed historical argument of chapters 8-10 to bear weight.

## Claim 6: Monotheism doesn't eliminate divine council imagery — it reduces the status of council members

**Author's claim:** "Divine council language and scenes also appear in the 'priestly work' of the Pentateuch and post-exilic books (Zechariah and Daniel), which assumedly are monotheistic. In other words, monotheism requires that one divine assembly headed by one divine ruler, but it makes little or no impact on the language of assembly in itself."

**Evidence presented:**
- Isaiah 40 (opening of Second Isaiah) involves a divine council scene, yet this section contains "the greatest number of monotheistic statements in the Bible."
- Both Enuma Elish and the Hebrew Bible present "a divine ruler surrounded by subservient divinities." Radical divine rulership "polarizes the divine polity into the ruler and his ruled." The difference: in Israelite texts, "all but the highest tier of divinities are reduced in status, no longer regarded as deities in any manner analogous to the head of the council; their power is entirely derivative."
- The contrast with Babylon: "Babylon stands at the height of its political power, and its older deities pay homage to its newly exalted divine ruler. In contrast, Israel stands at the bottom of its political power and it exalts its deity inversely as ruler of the whole universe."
- P.D. Miller: the divine assembly "holds as one reality a monistic impulse in a pluralistic cosmic structure."

**Confidence:** HIGH that divine council imagery persists in monotheistic texts. This is textually undeniable — Isaiah 6, 1 Kings 22, Daniel 7, Zechariah 3 all have council scenes in indisputably monotheistic contexts.

**What's at stake:** This undermines the claim that monotheism is a radical break with polytheism. The STRUCTURE (ruler + court) persists; only the ONTOLOGICAL STATUS of the court members changes. Monotheism is a revaluation within an inherited structure, not a new structure. This is a key piece of Smith's argument that monotheism is "rhetoric" not a "stage."

**Who disagrees:** Heiser would say the divine council is consistent with "monotheism" properly understood (only one uncreated being; other 'elōhîm are created). Smith and Heiser actually agree that the structure persists — they disagree on what to call it.

**Alternative reading:** The persistence of council imagery could just be literary convention — poetic language inherited from polytheistic tradition but now "dead metaphor." Isaiah 40's "council scene" might not imply any real belief in other divine beings.

**My assessment:** Smith is right that the structure persists, and this is a genuinely important observation. But he may understate the ontological shift: going from "other gods exist but are subordinate" to "other heavenly beings exist but are NOT gods" IS a major conceptual change. Smith acknowledges this ("all but the highest tier... are reduced in status") but seems to want the structural continuity to carry more weight than the ontological discontinuity. Both are real.

## Claim 7: The family/patrimonial household model fits Ugaritic pantheon better than the bureaucratic model

**Author's claim:** Against Handy's bureaucratic model (using Weber): "The number of such divinities of bureaucracy in either Ugaritic or Israelite literature is minimal, whereas the number of family members in the pantheon in Ugaritic literature is high."

**Evidence presented:**
- Handy: pantheon as bureaucratic system with specialized offices. Satan in Job as "prosecuting attorney" — one of few biblical examples.
- Schloen: monarchy itself was a patrimonial household, not a bureaucracy in Weber's terms. Pantheon reflects patriarchal paradigm.
- Ugaritic evidence against bureaucracy: "there is no typically bureaucratic divine figure such as a scribe in the Ugaritic pantheon nor any other figure apart from a divine craftsman who worked in the royal divine bureaucracy." Kothar (tier 3) is the only possible example, and even he is an outsider/foreigner.
- Familial terminology dominates: "sons of El," "seventy sons of Athirat," divine marriage (Nikkal and Yarih), sibling relationships (Anat and Baal).
- Biblical material "barely supports a general structure of bureaucracy" — Satan, some individuated angels, but few examples.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The absence of bureaucratic figures in Ugaritic texts is striking. But the argument is partly from silence — absence of evidence for divine bureaucracy is not evidence of absence.

**What's at stake:** This sets up Chapter 3's core argument: the divine FAMILY provided polytheism's conceptual unity. If the pantheon is a family, its "oneness" is familial, not bureaucratic. Monotheism's "oneness" of a single ruler replaces the family's "oneness" of kinship.

**Who disagrees:** Handy (bureaucratic model). Scholars who see Mesopotamian influence (where bureaucratic divine structures ARE more evident) on Israelite rather than Ugaritic models.

**Alternative reading:** Family AND bureaucracy aren't mutually exclusive. A royal court IS both a household and an administrative body. The divine family might function bureaucratically without needing a "divine scribe." The distinction Smith draws may be sharper than the ancient reality.

**My assessment:** Smith's critique of Handy is fair — the Ugaritic texts really don't show a bureaucratic pantheon. But the family model has its own problems: if the pantheon is a family, why is there so much internal conflict (Baal vs. Yamm, Anat's violence, El's favoritism)? Smith addresses this in Ch 3 by noting the family includes conflict. The family model works better descriptively than the bureaucratic one, but it's still a model, not the thing itself.

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Multiple distinct assemblies, not one pantheon | HIGH | Functional subsets vs. structural divisions |
| 2: Four-tier pantheon structure | MEDIUM-HIGH | Heuristic convenience vs. emic reality |
| 3: Israel inherited four-tier pantheon; it collapsed | HIGH for convergence; MEDIUM for exact structure | Vestigiality vs. original thinness of Israelite pantheon |
| 4: Yahweh was originally tier 2; El presided | HIGH | Later readers reinterpreting El as Yahweh from the start |
| 5: Neo-Assyrian crisis drove theological shift | MEDIUM-HIGH | Correlation vs. causation; other drivers |
| 6: Council imagery persists in monotheism | HIGH | Ontological shift understated |
| 7: Family model > bureaucratic model | MEDIUM-HIGH | Argument from silence; models not mutually exclusive |

**Strongest section:** Section 4 (Israel and the Tiers of the Pantheon). The reading of Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 is masterful. The MT alteration of Deut 32:8 is a genuinely decisive piece of evidence that Smith deploys to maximum effect.

**Weakest section:** Section 3 (The Four Tiers). The tier structure is useful but feels taxonomic rather than analytical. Tier 3 (Kothar alone) is particularly thin.

**Most original contribution:** The argument that El's assembly (dr 'il, CAT 1.65) is NOT the same as the general pantheon. This distinction has been missed by earlier scholarship and has real implications for how we read biblical divine council texts.

**Cross-reference:** Claim 4 (Yahweh as original tier-2 god) is the key that unlocks Smith's Ch 7 argument about El as Israel's original god. Claim 5 (neo-Assyrian trigger) will need Ch 8-10 to flesh out. The family model preview (Claim 7) leads directly into Ch 3. Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* is the major dialogue partner throughout — Smith accepts Cross's basic framework but refines it at crucial points (the bn 'il vs. bn 'ilm distinction; the non-identity of El's household assembly with the general pantheon). Heiser's model keeps Yahweh supreme from the beginning — Smith's Psalm 82 reading directly challenges this.
