# Fleming — Chapter 5: The Name Yahweh

This chapter is a grammatical and onomastic investigation of the name Yahweh, reframed by Fleming's Ch 2 conclusion that Yhwʒ of Shasu-land was a people-name, not a divine name. The question shifts from "what does the divine name mean?" to "how would a Shasu people come to have a name that looks like a finite verb?" Fleming argues that Yhwʒ is best explained as a shortened (hypocoristic) personal name — a naming type well-attested in the Amorite onomasticon of the early second millennium. Israel itself is named the same way (yiśrā'ēl = "May El contend"). This chapter does not settle the etymology of the verbal root (hwy "to be" vs. _hwy "to live") but shifts the burden: a people-name derived from a personal name is straightforward; a divine name derived from the same requires special pleading.

---

## Claim 1: Yhwʒ of Shasu-land is best explained as a people-name derived from a shortened personal name
→ [[claim-yhw3-shasu-land-shortened-personal-name-not-divine]] | **fleming-ybi-5.1** | Confidence: medium
  Fleming's positive alternative to the divine-name reading: Yhwʒ fits the pattern of peoples named after personal names (Israel itself, Yamutbal). The typological argument is strong, but the specific claim depends on the verbal interpretation of the name.

## Claim 2: The Amorite Yahwi-/Yahwi- personal names provide the right onomastic context, but they're personal names, not divine names
→ [[claim-amorite-yahwi-names-onomastic-context-not-divine-names]] | **fleming-ybi-5.2** | Confidence: medium
  The Mari personal names with Ya-wi- and Ya-ah-wi- elements fit the naming type. The element never indicates a god. Key debate: hwy "to be" vs. ḥwy "to live" — unresolved but secondary to the typological point.

## Claim 3: Van der Toorn's objection (a major deity can't derive from a deified ancestor) dissolves once we recognize Yhwʒ was never a divine name
→ [[claim-van-der-toorn-objection-dissolved-two-step-model]] | **fleming-ybi-5.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  The two-step model (personal name → people-name → divine name) avoids the objection. A people giving its name to its god is a different phenomenon from a deified ancestor becoming a pantheon head.

## Claim 4: Cross's interpretation (Yahweh as liturgical epithet of El, "He brings armies into existence") cannot be sustained against the Shasu evidence
→ [[claim-cross-liturgical-epithet-model-unsustainable-shasu-evidence]] | **fleming-ybi-5.4** | Confidence: high
  Cross's model predates full integration of the Soleb Shasu evidence, which shows Yhwʒ as a people-name, not a liturgical formula. The model doesn't explain Yhwʒ. Supports: [[claim-yahweh-el-originally-separate-deities-el-israel-original-god]].

**Strongest section:** The typological argument that peoples named after personal names are common (Israel itself, Yamutbal, etc.) — this is Fleming's Mari expertise in action and it's persuasive (pp. 187-190).

**Weakest section:** The unresolved etymology debate (hwy vs. _hwy). Fleming acknowledges the uncertainty but doesn't fully resolve it. The chapter's argument doesn't actually require a specific etymology — it requires only the verbal FORM, not the verbal MEANING — but Fleming spends a lot of space on an issue he admits is secondary.

**Overall:** This is the most technical chapter and the one most dependent on specialist knowledge (Amorite onomastics, Egyptian phonology). It's narrower in scope than Ch 2-4 but makes an important methodological point: if you start with Yhwʒ as a people-name rather than a divine name, the onomastic questions become simpler and the objections to verbal derivation lose force.

**Cross-reference to Tigay:** [[Tigay — Chapter I — The Onomastic Evidence]] — Tigay's work on the frequency of Yahweh theophoric names in Iron Age Israel is the complementary onomastic investigation from the FIRST millennium. Fleming is working in the SECOND millennium. Together they bracket the onomastic question: where did the name come from (Fleming) and when did it become widely used (Tigay)?
