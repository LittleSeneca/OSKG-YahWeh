# Fleming — Chapter 5: The Name Yahweh

This chapter is a grammatical and onomastic investigation of the name Yahweh, reframed by Fleming's Ch 2 conclusion that Yhwʒ of Shasu-land was a people-name, not a divine name. The question shifts from "what does the divine name mean?" to "how would a Shasu people come to have a name that looks like a finite verb?" Fleming argues that Yhwʒ is best explained as a shortened (hypocoristic) personal name — a naming type well-attested in the Amorite onomasticon of the early second millennium. Israel itself is named the same way (yiśrā'ēl = "May El contend"). This chapter does not settle the etymology of the verbal root (hwy "to be" vs. _hwy "to live") but shifts the burden: a people-name derived from a personal name is straightforward; a divine name derived from the same requires special pleading.

---

## Claim 1: Yhwʒ of Shasu-land is best explained as a people-name derived from a shortened personal name, not as a divine name

**Author's claim:** "It is much more likely that the divine name Yahweh derives from the Shasu group without divine association than that the Shasu group already related to the eventual Israelite god" (p. 196). "The problem is not whether a major deity could be identified by a human ancestor but rather whether a people could be identified by a human personal name, a straightforward question with straightforward answer: yes, and fairly often" (p. 203).

**Evidence presented:**
- Peoples named after personal names are common: Israel itself (yiśrā'ēl, "May El contend"), Yamutbal/Emutbal ("The lord has died"), and numerous Ya-pre fixed Aramean group names (p. 187)
- The Yamutbal people of the early second millennium show the pattern clearly: a population identified by what was originally an individual's name, capable of defining a "land" (māt Yamutbalim) without being tied to a single settlement or king (pp. 189-190)
- "In the Mari material, none of the various peoples named by finite verbal forms is known to be equated with a deity" (p. 196)
- The hypocoristic (shortened) naming type is well-attested: Huffmon (1965) lists numerous Mari examples like I-ba-ás-si-ir, Ya-a-ar, Ya-ab-na-ah, etc. (p. 195)
- Shortened names "are sometimes found without any suffix, even vocalic. In this regard, they resemble many divine names and geographic names" (Huffmon, cited p. 195)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The typological argument (peoples can be named after personal names) is solid. But the specific claim that Yhwʒ IS such a shortened personal name depends on the verbal interpretation of the name, which remains debated. If Yhwʒ is a noun, the personal-name derivation is less obvious.

**What's at stake:** This is Fleming's positive alternative to the divine-name interpretation. If Yhwʒ was never a divine name, the question becomes: how did a people-name become a god-name? (He addresses this in Ch 6.) This inverts the standard approach.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone who treats Yhwʒ as a divine name or as a toponym derived from a divine name (Giveon, Ahituv, Römer, van der Toorn). Cross (1973) treated Yahweh as a liturgical epithet of El. Tropper (2017) argues it's a qatl-type noun.

**Alternative reading:** Yhwʒ IS a divine name — the Soleb list names a region after the god worshipped there (like "Beth-Yahweh"). The personal-name derivation is unnecessary.

**My assessment:** Fleming's typological argument is strong: peoples named after personal names are common, and the form of Yhwʒ fits the pattern. The weakness is that we can't PROVE it was a shortened personal name rather than something else (a noun, a place name, etc.). The argument is plausible but not demonstrable.

## Claim 2: The Amorite Yahwi-/Yahwi- personal names provide the right onomastic context, but they're personal names, not divine names

**Author's claim:** "Study of the Shasu name generally moves directly to the divine, assuming reference to the god later attached to Israel and leapfrogging the question of how to understand a human political entity on its own terms. This risks anachronism" (pp. 195-196). "The element never indicates a god, and it cannot be invoked as direct evidence for what would become the god of Israel" (p. 199).

**Evidence presented:**
- Extensive listing of Mari personal names with Ya-wi- and Ya-ah-wi- elements (pp. 197-198): Yawi-DINGIR, Yawi-ila, Yawi-Dagan, Yawi-Addu, Yawi-Erah, Yahwi-asar, Yahwi-nasi, Yawium, Yawiya
- ARM XXIII 448-51 proves that Ya-wi- and Ya-ah-wi- spellings can represent the same name/verb — no fixed separation between "to live" and "to be" (pp. 198-199)
- The element occurs with multiple deities (Dagan, Addu, Erah), not only with "the god" (ilu/El) — the element is not specifically tied to El (p. 199)
- Both Yawi- and Yahwi- forms appear with the storm god Addu (p. 199)
- Streck (1999, 2000) argued all these names use _hwy ("to live"), but the spelling variation in ARM XXIII doesn't settle which verb is intended
- Semantic parallels exist for hwy ("to be") in Akkadian (ibašši- names), Ugaritic (ykn- names), and Phoenician (ykn- names) (pp. 200-201)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Fleming's Mari expertise is on display — his own gathering of names from post-1965 publications adds substantially to Huffmon. But the key question (hwy "to be" vs. _hwy "to live") remains unresolved. Fleming acknowledges Streck's objection has force.

**What's at stake:** If the Amorite names all use "to live," they're less relevant to Yhwʒ. But Fleming's point is that even if the etymology is "to live," the naming TYPE (people named like persons) is what matters, not the specific root.

**Who disagrees:** Streck (1999, 2000) argues all Yahwi- names use _hwy "to live" — removing the Amorite evidence from relevance to Yahweh. Tropper (2017) argues Yhwʒ is a noun, not a verb at all.

**Alternative reading:** Streck is right — all the Amorite names mean "May DN live," and they have nothing to do with the divine name Yahweh, which has a different etymology entirely.

**My assessment:** Fleming is honest about the uncertainty. The key insight is methodological: stop treating Yhwʒ as a divine name and the onomastic question becomes simpler. Whether the root is "to be" or "to live" or something else, a people named after a verbal personal name is a common phenomenon. The divine-name question is secondary.

## Claim 3: Van der Toorn's objection (a major deity can't derive from a deified ancestor) dissolves once we recognize Yhwʒ was never a divine name

**Author's claim:** "When we shift the object of inquiry to Yhwʒ of Shasu-land in the Soleb inscription of Amenhotep III, and we set aside any consideration of deity in that name as reading back from a later phenomenon... the problem is not whether a major deity could be identified by a human ancestor but rather whether a people could be identified by a human personal name" (p. 203).

**Evidence presented:**
- Van der Toorn (1999: 914) acknowledged the theoretical possibility that Yahweh began as a deified ancestor but rejected it: "it is difficult to believe that the major Israelite deity... was originally a deified ancestor"
- Stol (1991) showed that Old Babylonian personal names like Ikrub-El, Ikšudum, and Ikūnum originated as deified ancestors — but these were minor family gods, not major pantheon deities
- Van der Toorn's objection is valid IF Yhwʒ was always a divine name. But if Yhwʒ was a people-name first, the objection doesn't apply
- "The difficulty shifts to explaining how the name for a people could come to be attached to a deity" (p. 203) — which is Ch 6's task

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The logical point is sound: van der Toorn's objection only matters if you're trying to derive a major deity directly from a personal name. Fleming's two-step model (personal name → people-name → divine name) avoids the objection.

**What's at stake:** This removes one of the main objections to deriving Yahweh from the Amorite onomasticon.

**Who disagrees:** Van der Toorn and anyone who treats Yahweh as always having been a divine name.

**Alternative reading:** The two-step model just pushes the problem back: how does a people-name become the name of a major deity? That's no easier than deriving a deity from an ancestor directly.

**My assessment:** Fleming is right that the two-step model changes the problem. A people giving its name to its god is a different phenomenon from a deified ancestor becoming a pantheon head. But he hasn't yet solved the problem — he's just relocated it. Ch 6 needs to deliver.

## Claim 4: Cross's interpretation (Yahweh as liturgical epithet of El, "He brings armies into existence") cannot be sustained against the Shasu evidence

**Author's claim:** "Efforts to explain Yahweh as derived from a liturgical formula for El, as elaborated by Frank Cross from the work of his teacher Albright, bypass the identification of the Shasu group with a human personal name and cannot convince" (p. 203).

**Evidence presented:**
- Cross (1973: 65-66) built on Albright's (1924, 1948, 1968) interpretation: Yahweh is a causative verbal form meaning "He brings [the heavenly hosts] into existence" — a liturgical epithet of El
- This interpretation predates full integration of the Soleb Shasu evidence
- The Shasu evidence shows Yhwʒ as a people-name, not a liturgical formula
- Fleming traces the genealogy of the idea: Albright (1924) → Albright (1948, review connecting to Yahweh Seba'ot) → Cross (1973, full elaboration)

**Confidence:** HIGH that Cross's interpretation doesn't account for the Shasu evidence. Fleming is simply right that Cross's model was developed before the Egyptian evidence was fully integrated and doesn't explain Yhwʒ as a people-name.

**What's at stake:** Cross's model has been enormously influential in American biblical scholarship. If it fails, one major alternative to the Midianite Hypothesis (still within a southern-origin framework) also fails.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone following Cross's interpretation. But Cross himself (d. 2012) couldn't respond to the Soleb evidence as Fleming reads it.

**Alternative reading:** The Shasu evidence doesn't disprove Cross — Yhwʒ COULD be a place name derived from the divine epithet, and the place gave its name to a Shasu group.

**My assessment:** Fleming is right that Cross's model doesn't account for the Egyptian evidence, but Cross's model was never built on the Egyptian evidence in the first place. The two operate in different evidentiary frames. The real question is whether Fleming's reading of the Egyptian evidence is compelling enough to overturn Cross.

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Yhwʒ as shortened personal name | MEDIUM | Noun-alternative (Tropper) |
| 2: Amorite names provide context | MEDIUM | Streck's "to live" only argument |
| 3: Van der Toorn's objection dissolved | MEDIUM-HIGH | Two-step model pushes problem back |
| 4: Cross's model unsustainable | HIGH | Cross never aimed to explain Shasu evidence |

**Strongest section:** The typological argument that peoples named after personal names are common (Israel itself, Yamutbal, etc.) — this is Fleming's Mari expertise in action and it's persuasive (pp. 187-190).

**Weakest section:** The unresolved etymology debate (hwy vs. _hwy). Fleming acknowledges the uncertainty but doesn't fully resolve it. The chapter's argument doesn't actually require a specific etymology — it requires only the verbal FORM, not the verbal MEANING — but Fleming spends a lot of space on an issue he admits is secondary.

**Overall:** This is the most technical chapter and the one most dependent on specialist knowledge (Amorite onomastics, Egyptian phonology). It's narrower in scope than Ch 2-4 but makes an important methodological point: if you start with Yhwʒ as a people-name rather than a divine name, the onomastic questions become simpler and the objections to verbal derivation lose force.

Fleming is working in the SECOND millennium. Together they bracket the onomastic question: where did the name come from (Fleming) and when did it become widely used (Tigay)?
