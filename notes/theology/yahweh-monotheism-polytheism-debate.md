# Was Yahweh Always One God? The Monotheism vs. Polytheism Debate

A deep-dive research project into the scholarly debate: was Yahweh originally one god among many in a polytheistic Canaanite pantheon, or was Israel always monotheistic? When did monotheism actually emerge?

---

## The Core Question

The debate is not just academic. It cuts to the heart of whether the Bible describes a God who *revealed* monotheism to humanity from the beginning, or a god who *evolved* into the sole deity through centuries of cultural, political, and theological pressure.

Three fundamental positions exist among scholars:

| Position | Key Claim | Key Proponents |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| **Evolutionary Polytheism → Monotheism** | Yahweh was one of many Canaanite gods; monotheism emerged gradually, crystallizing during/after the Babylonian Exile (6th c. BCE) | Mark S. Smith, John Day, William Dever, Israel Finkelstein, Francesca Stavrakopoulou, Ziony Zevit |
| **Divine Council / Early Monolatry** | Israel always worshipped only Yahweh, but acknowledged other divine beings (a "council") who were created/subordinate, not co-equal rivals | Michael Heiser, Benjamin Sommer, some evangelical scholars |
| **Early Monotheism** | Monotheism was present from Israel's earliest period, reflected in archaic poetry; references to "other gods" are rhetorical | Philip D. Stern, some confessional scholars |

Most scholars today hold **Position 1** (the evolutionary view), though Position 2 (Heiser's divine council) has gained significant popular traction. Position 3 is a minority view in critical scholarship.

---

## Position 1: The Evolutionary View (Scholarly Majority)

### What It Claims

Yahweh was not originally the sole God of the universe. He was a regional storm/war deity from the southern deserts (Edom/Midian/Teman) who was imported into Canaan, absorbed into the existing Canaanite pantheon, gradually took on the characteristics of El (the high god) and Baal (the storm god), fought for exclusive worship (monolatry), and eventually — under the trauma of the Babylonian exile — became the only God in existence (radical monotheism).

### The Timeline

| Period | Dates | What the Evidence Shows |
|--------|-------|------------------------|
| Late Bronze Age | 1550–1200 BCE | Earliest possible mention: "Shasu of Yhw" in Egyptian inscription at Soleb. Yahweh is a tribal deity of nomadic raiders south of Canaan. |
| Iron Age I | 1200–1000 BCE | Israelites emerge as a Canaanite subculture. Earliest biblical poetry (Song of Deborah, Song of the Sea) depicts Yahweh as a storm/war god from the south. No evidence of monotheism. |
| Iron Age II (early) | 1000–722 BCE | Yahweh becomes the national god of Israel (north) and Judah (south). Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (c. 800 BCE): "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah." El, Baal, and Asherah are still worshipped. |
| Iron Age II (late) | 722–586 BCE | Assyria destroys Northern Kingdom (722 BCE). Refugees flee south. Josiah's reform (c. 622 BCE) centralizes worship in Jerusalem, destroys competing shrines. Yahweh-alone party gains traction. Monolatry, not monotheism. |
| Babylonian Exile | 586–539 BCE | Temple destroyed. Jerusalem sacked. **The critical turning point.** Yahweh can no longer be a geographically-bound god. Radical monotheism emerges: Yahweh is God of the whole universe. Trito-Isaiah: "I am the LORD, there is no other." |
| Second Temple | 539 BCE–70 CE | Monotheism consolidates. But even now, not fully uniform — Elephantine papyri show 5th c. BCE Jews worshipping Yahweh alongside Anat. |

### Key Scholars and Their Arguments

#### Mark S. Smith (NYU, Skirball Professor)

**Books:** *The Early History of God* (1990, rev. 2002), *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001)

**Core argument:** Israelite religion was a subset of Canaanite religion. The Israelites didn't reject Canaanite deities — they absorbed them. Yahweh was not originally the head of the pantheon — El was. Over centuries, Yahweh was conflated with El (taking on El's epithets like *El Shaddai* and his consort Asherah), then absorbed Baal's storm imagery. Monotheism was a late development, emerging from monolatry under political pressure.

> **Smith's key evidence:**
> - Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (in the original DSS/LXX reading) depicts Elyon (the "Most High," i.e. El) parcelling out nations to his sons, with Yahweh receiving Israel as his portion. **This means Yahweh was originally a subordinate deity to El.**
> - Psalm 82 depicts God standing in a divine council and judging other gods — a text that only makes sense if the existence of other gods was once accepted.
> - Ugaritic texts (discovered 1928) show the Canaanite pantheon in detail: El as the high god, Baal as the storm god, Asherah as consort. The parallels with biblical language are so extensive that direct borrowing is the only plausible explanation.
> - The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions (8th c. BCE) explicitly mention "Yahweh and his Asherah" — showing that even late in the monarchic period, Yahweh was worshipped alongside a goddess.

**Smith's institutional weight:** Smith is Skirball Professor of Bible and Ancient Near Eastern Studies at NYU. His work is widely cited and represents what most scholars in the field consider settled ground.

#### John Day (Oxford, Emeritus Professor)

**Book:** *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000)

Day argues that Yahweh was originally a Midianite god, imported into Canaan, who gradually absorbed the characteristics and functions of El, Baal, and Asherah. He provides detailed textual analysis showing specific Canaanite mythological motifs that were transferred to Yahweh.

#### William G. Dever (University of Arizona, Archaeologist)

**Books:** *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), *Has Archaeology Buried the Bible?* (2020)

Dever approaches the question from archaeology. He argues that:
- Folk religion (what ordinary Israelites actually practiced) was thoroughly polytheistic well into the monarchic period
- The "official" religion of the Jerusalem priesthood was a minority view that only became dominant after the exile
- Asherah was widely worshipped as Yahweh's consort; the biblical polemics against her *prove* her popularity
- The vast number of female figurines found in Israelite archaeological sites attests to goddess worship

#### Israel Finkelstein (Tel Aviv University, Archaeologist)

**Book:** *The Bible Unearthed* (with Neil Asher Silberman, 2001)

Finkelstein is more of a minimalist on the historical reliability of the Bible, but his archaeological work supports the evolutionary view: the "United Monarchy" of David and Solomon was far smaller and less significant than the Bible describes, and the religious centralization under Josiah was a political-theological project, not a return to primitive purity.

#### Francesca Stavrakopoulou (University of Exeter)

**Book:** *God: An Anatomy* (2021)

Stavrakopoulou argues that even in the biblical texts, Yahweh was conceived as having a physical body — walking, talking, eating, smelling, having a penis. This corporeal, anthropomorphic deity looks nothing like the abstract, transcendent God of later theology. She traces how Yahweh's body was gradually "disappeared" through theological development.

### Evidence for Position 1

**1. Archaeological evidence of polytheistic Yahwism:**
- **Kuntillet Ajrud** (c. 800 BCE): Inscriptions reading "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" and "Yahweh of Teman and his Asherah." The grammar is fiercely debated, but the most straightforward reading acknowledges Asherah as Yahweh's consort.
- **Khirbet el-Qom** (8th c. BCE): "Blessed is Uriyahu by Yahweh... and by his Asherah."
- **Female figurines** throughout Israelite sites: widespread domestic worship of a goddess, likely Asherah.
- **Elephantine Papyri** (5th c. BCE): A Jewish community in Egypt worshipping Yahweh alongside the goddess Anat — centuries after monotheism supposedly emerged.

**2. Textual evidence of polytheistic origins:**
- **Deuteronomy 32:8-9:** The Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint preserve the original reading "according to the number of the sons of God" — Elyon (the Most High) divides the nations among his divine sons, and Yahweh receives Israel as his inheritance. The Masoretic Text (the standard Hebrew text) changed this to "sons of Israel" because the original was theologically embarrassing. **Even Michael Heiser, an evangelical scholar, agrees that "sons of God" is the original reading.**
- **Psalm 82:** "God stands in the divine assembly; he judges among the gods." The psalm depicts Yahweh addressing other *elohim* and condemning them to die "like men." This is a relic of polytheistic theology that the biblical editors preserved.
- **Exodus 15:11:** "Who is like you among the gods, O Yahweh?" — a rhetorical question that *presupposes the existence of other gods*, even if it asserts Yahweh's superiority.

**3. The Canaanite religious background:**
- Ugaritic texts (Ras Shamra, discovered 1928) reveal the Canaanite pantheon in detail. El is the aged high god; Baal is the young storm warrior; Asherah is El's consort. The parallels between Baal's mythology and Yahweh's portrayal in the Psalms are too extensive for coincidence. Psalm 29, for example, is widely regarded by scholars as a hymn originally composed for Baal, with Yahweh's name substituted.

**4. The pressure-cooker of exile:**
- For ancient peoples, gods were geographically bound. Yahweh "lived" in the Jerusalem Temple (the *bet Yahweh*, "house of Yahweh"). When the Temple was destroyed in 586 BCE and the people were exiled to Babylon, they faced a choice: abandon Yahweh for the gods of the land where they now lived, or radically reconceive Yahweh as a god not bound to geography. The Judean theologians chose the latter — and monotheism was born.

## Position 2: The Divine Council View (Michael Heiser)

### What It Claims

The "other gods" mentioned in the Hebrew Bible are real beings — but they are **created, subordinate divine beings** (the *bene elohim*, "sons of God"), not co-eternal rivals to Yahweh. Israel's religion was never polytheistic in the sense of worshipping multiple co-equal deities. Rather, Yahweh presided over a divine council of lesser beings. The development was not from polytheism to monotheism, but from an early acknowledgment of the council to a later (post-exilic) denial of its existence.

### Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023)

**Affiliation:** Executive Director of the School of Ministry, Celebration Church; PhD in Hebrew Bible and Semitic Languages from University of Wisconsin-Madison.

**Books:** *The Unseen Realm* (2015), *Demons* (2020)

**Core argument:** Heiser explicitly **rejects the evolutionary model**. He argues that:
1. The Hebrew Bible consistently presents Yahweh as utterly unique (*incomparable*, not merely *one among many*)
2. The *bene elohim* ("sons of God" / "divine beings") are created beings who serve Yahweh's council — they are not rivals for worship
3. Deuteronomy 32:8-9, properly understood, shows Yahweh (as a distinct person from Elyon, or identified with him) receiving Israel as his portion — but this doesn't mean he was a lesser deity. Heiser argues Yahweh IS Elyon.
4. The distinction is between the *unseen realm* (real divine beings who serve Yahweh) and *idols* (fake gods made of wood and stone). The Bible condemns idol worship but acknowledges the reality of the divine council.

> **Heiser's key distinction:** The Hebrew Bible uses *elohim* to refer to: (a) Yahweh, (b) the divine council members (*bene elohim*), (c) the spirits of the dead (1 Samuel 28:13), and (d) demons (*shedim*). The word *elohim* means "disembodied being from the spiritual realm" — it does NOT mean "co-equal deity worthy of worship." So when the Bible talks about "other gods," it's referring to real spiritual beings who are nonetheless infinitely inferior to Yahweh.

**Heiser on Deuteronomy 32:8-9:** Heiser agrees the original reading is "sons of God" (not "sons of Israel") and argues the Masoretic Text was altered. BUT he argues this doesn't prove polytheism — it proves a divine council where Yahweh allotted the nations to subordinate divine beings (the "princes" of Daniel 10). In Heiser's view, these beings later became corrupt and received worship they shouldn't have — which is the origin of pagan idolatry.

**Heiser on Psalm 82:** The "gods" being judged are the corrupt members of the divine council who have failed to rule the nations justly. They are sentenced to die "like men" — which proves they are not eternal, co-equal deities.

### Strengths of Heiser's View

1. **Takes the biblical text seriously** — doesn't dismiss the divine council language as "mythological baggage"
2. **Explains the data without requiring an evolutionary model** — the divine council is consistent from earliest to latest texts
3. **Agrees with the textual evidence** (Deut 32:8-9, Psalm 82) while interpreting it differently
4. **Accounts for Second Temple Jewish literature** where the divine council concept is well-attested

### Criticisms of Heiser's View

1. **It still requires explaining why the Bible speaks of "other gods"** — a problem for traditional monotheism whether you call them "gods" or "divine beings"
2. **Most critical scholars see Heiser as doing apologetics**, not disinterested scholarship. He starts from a confessional position and works backward.
3. **Heiser's reading of Deuteronomy 32:8-9 is strained.** The text most naturally reads as Yahweh (the son) receiving his inheritance from Elyon (the father). Identifying Yahweh with Elyon requires collapsing the distinction the text explicitly makes.
4. **The archaeological evidence for goddess worship** (Asherah, the female figurines) is harder to explain within Heiser's framework — it looks like actual polytheistic practice, not just acknowledgment of a divine council.
5. **Heiser dismisses the Ugaritic parallels** as merely "background context" when they strongly suggest direct borrowing and assimilation.

### Scholarly Reception

Heiser's *Unseen Realm* has been enormously popular among evangelical lay readers. Among critical scholars, his work is largely ignored — not because it's wrong about the textual data (he's right about Deut 32:8-9), but because his interpretive framework is seen as a sophisticated attempt to preserve orthodox theology in the face of evidence that points toward polytheistic origins. 

Some scholars, however, acknowledge that Heiser raised important questions about whether "monotheism" vs "polytheism" is even the right framework. Benjamin Sommer (Jewish Theological Seminary) in *The Bodies of God* (2009) similarly argues that ancient Israelite religion doesn't fit neatly into either category.

## Position 3: Early Monotheism (Minority View)

### What It Claims

Monotheism was part of Israelite religion from the beginning. The oldest biblical poems (Exodus 15, Deuteronomy 32, 2 Samuel 22 / Psalm 18) contain what Philip D. Stern calls "implicit monotheism" — rhetorical questions like "Who is like you among the gods?" that actually *deny* the existence of other gods by highlighting their powerlessness.

### Philip D. Stern (Biblical scholar)

Stern argues in a 2025 Biblical Archaeology Society article that:
- Exodus 15:11 ("Who is like You among the gods, O Yahweh?") is a rhetorical question whose implied answer is: *no one*. The "gods" lack divine attributes — they are "non-gods."
- 2 Samuel 22:32 / Psalm 18:31 ("Who is a god besides Yahweh?") makes the same move — nobody is god except Yahweh.
- Deuteronomy 32:39 ("See now that I, I am He, and there is no god besides Me") is the clearest statement of monotheism in any ancient literature, and the Song of Moses is dated by many scholars to the 12th-11th centuries BCE.

### Problems with Position 3

1. **Rhetorical questions that assert incomparability are not the same as denying existence.** "Who is like you among the gods?" can mean "You're the greatest among the gods" (henotheism), not "There are no other gods" (monotheism). Ancient Near Eastern literature is full of hymns that praise one god as supreme without denying other gods exist.
2. **The dating of these poems is disputed.** Even if the Song of the Sea is archaic, the version we have has been edited. Stern's dating of Deuteronomy 32 to the 12th-11th centuries BCE is at the early end of scholarly estimates.
3. **Archaeological evidence cuts against early monotheism.** If Israel was monotheistic from the 12th century, why do we find goddess figurines, Asherah inscriptions, and other polytheistic practices throughout the monarchic period?
4. **This view is held by very few critical scholars.** It's primarily a confessional position.

## The Archaeological Evidence: A Closer Look

### Kuntillet Ajrud (c. 800 BCE)

A way-station/caravanserai in the Sinai desert. The site produced inscriptions on pottery and plaster mentioning:

- "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah"
- "Yahweh of Teman and his Asherah"

**The debate centers on the grammar.** The Hebrew phrase *lyhwh šmrn wl'šrth* could mean:
- "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" (Asherah as personal name = the goddess)
- "Yahweh of Samaria and his asherah" (asherah as common noun = the cult object, a wooden pole)

Mainstream scholars (Smith, Day, Dever) argue for the goddess interpretation. Conservative scholars argue for the cult object interpretation. The accompanying drawings show a bull-like figure and what appears to be a seated figure playing a lyre — possibly Yahweh and Asherah, though this is also debated.

**My assessment:** The cult-object interpretation is strained. The parallel construction ("Yahweh of Samaria AND his asherah") uses the possessive suffix, which is more natural for a personal name ("his Asherah") than a cult object ("his wooden pole"). The most straightforward reading — and the one most scholars accept — is that these Israelites worshipped Yahweh alongside a goddess.

### The Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE)

The Moabite king Mesha brags about defeating Israel and looting "the vessels of Yahweh." This is the earliest clear inscription mentioning Yahweh as the national god of Israel. It confirms Yahweh was associated with Israel, but doesn't tell us whether other gods were also worshipped.

### Elephantine Papyri (5th c. BCE)

A Jewish military colony in Egypt maintained a temple to Yahweh (or "Yahu" as they wrote it). The papyri mention "Anat-Yahu" — the goddess Anat paired with Yahweh. They also refer to other deities: Eshem-Bethel, Herem-Bethel, and Anat-Bethel. This is **centuries after the exile** and **centuries after monotheism supposedly emerged** — and these Jews were still polytheistic.

This is a major problem for both the early monotheism view and the "monotheism emerged in the exile" view. It suggests the transition was far messier and more gradual than either position acknowledges.

## Where Scholars Agree (Even When They Disagree)

Despite the heated debate, there is actually substantial common ground across the scholarly spectrum:

1. **The Hebrew Bible contains language that acknowledges the existence of other divine beings.** Psalm 82, Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (original reading), and numerous other passages describe a divine council. This is not seriously disputed — the debate is about what it *means*.

2. **The Masoretic Text of Deuteronomy 32:8 was altered.** The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve "sons of God"; the MT has "sons of Israel." Both Heiser (evangelical) and Smith (critical) agree on this. The alteration was made because the original was theologically problematic.

3. **Israelite religion changed significantly over time.** Even conservative scholars acknowledge development — they just date monotheism earlier. Everyone agrees the religion of the Second Temple period looks different from the religion of the Judges period.

4. **The terms "monotheism" and "polytheism" are too blunt for the data.** As Debra Scoggins Ballentine notes in her 2022 *Religion Compass* article, "many scholars prefer alternative concepts such as monolatry, henotheism, or one-deity discourse." The reality was more complex than either label captures.

5. **There was a "Yahweh-alone" party (the Deuteronomists and prophets) and a "Yahweh-plus-others" popular religion.** The Bible preserves the perspective of the Yahweh-alone party that eventually won — but the archaeological record shows the other side was the majority for most of Israel's history.

6. **The Babylonian exile was a theological watershed.** Whether you believe monotheism *began* there or merely *crystallized* there, everyone agrees the exile forced radical theological innovation.

## The State of the Evidence: My Assessment

Having reviewed the evidence, here's where I land:

### What the evidence strongly supports:

- **Yahweh was not originally the sole God.** The earliest biblical texts, the archaeological record, and the Canaanite religious context all point to a world where multiple divine beings were acknowledged.

- **Israelite religion emerged from a Canaanite matrix.** The linguistic, cultural, and religious parallels with Ugarit are too extensive for this to be coincidence.

- **The Babylonian exile was the pressure cooker that produced radical monotheism.** When the Temple was destroyed and the people were exiled, Yahweh had to become a universal God or cease to be relevant. The Judean theologians chose the former — a brilliant theological move.

- **The process was messier than either side's narrative suggests.** Elephantine shows polytheistic Yahwism surviving into the 5th century BCE. The transition was gradual, contested, and never total.

### What the evidence does NOT support:

- **That Israel was monotheistic from the beginning (Position 3).** The archaeological evidence for polytheistic practice is overwhelming. If early Israel was monotheistic, the people apparently didn't get the memo.

- **That there was no development at all.** Even Heiser's divine council view acknowledges that later Judaism denied the council's existence. Something changed.

### Where I'm still uncertain:

- **What to make of Heiser's divine council framework.** It's a more sophisticated framework than simple "polytheism → monotheism," and it takes the biblical texts seriously. But it's hard to shake the sense that it's an apologetic project that starts with the conclusion and works backward.

- **Whether "polytheism" is even the right word for what early Israelites believed.** If they acknowledged other divine beings but only worshipped Yahweh, that's monolatry, not polytheism. And if Heiser is right that the *bene elohim* were created beings, it's something closer to monotheism with a heavenly court. The categories we're using may not map well onto ancient reality.
