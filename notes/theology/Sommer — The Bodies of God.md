# Sommer, The Bodies of God — Complete Book Note

## Why Sommer Is Different

Sommer is the counter-position. Smith, Römer, and Dever all argue that monotheism was a late development — polytheism first, then monolatry, then monotheism. Sommer accepts the critical methods but argues this narrative misses something essential: early Israelite religion had a distinctive theology of divine EMBODIMENT that wasn't polytheism but also wasn't the abstract, transcendent monotheism of later Judaism and Christianity.

He's a Jewish scholar at the Jewish Theological Seminary. His framework is deeply informed by Jewish tradition (kabbalah, midrash) and he's writing for scholars, not a general audience. This is the most philosophically sophisticated book in our Tier 1 list.

---

## Introduction: God Has a Body

### The Starting Claim

> "The God of the Hebrew Bible has a body. This must be stated at the outset, because so many people, including many scholars, assume otherwise."

Sommer's opening is a deliberate provocation. He argues that the biblical God:
- Has a FORM (*ṣelem*) and SHAPE (*demut*) — Genesis 1:26
- Walks in the garden (Genesis 3:8)
- Comes down to inspect the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:5)
- Visits Abraham's tent and eats (Genesis 18)
- Has a body so luminous it would kill anyone who saw it (Exodus 33:20)

**He distinguishes two types of divine embodiment:**
1. **Nonmaterial anthropomorphism:** God has human shape but is made of "energy rather than matter" — Kaufmann's view, found in many biblical texts
2. **Material anthropomorphism:** God's body has the same shape AND substance as a human body — found in other texts (God eating, walking, being physically present at a location)

**The key insight:** "The startling or bizarre idea in the Hebrew Bible is something else entirely: not that God has a body — that is the standard notion of ancient Israelite theology — but rather that God has MANY bodies located in sundry places in the world that God created."

**What Sommer means by "a body":** "Something located in a particular place at a particular time, whatever its shape or substance."

**What's at stake for faith:** The idea that God is incorporeal — a spirit without a body — is NOT biblical. It's a later philosophical development (Maimonides, Aquinas). The biblical God is embodied. The question is HOW he's embodied — and whether he can have more than one body at once.

## Chapter 1: The Fluidity Model in Mesopotamia and Canaan

### Claim: ANE gods had multiple bodies and overlapping selves — this was normal

**Sommer's claim:** In Mesopotamian and Canaanite religion, a single deity could exist simultaneously in multiple bodies, and divine selves were "fluid" — not fully distinct from each other. This was a FUNDAMENTAL difference between gods and humans: humans have one body and one self; gods have many.

**Evidence presented:**

**Mesopotamian fluidity of selfhood:**
- Assyrian treaties list multiple Ishtars as witnesses: "Ishtar of Arbela," "Ishtar of Nineveh," "Ishtar of Carchemish" — distinct yet the same goddess
- The same for Adad: "Adad of Kurba-il," "Adad of Alep" — multiple local manifestations treated as distinct beings
- Hymns address these as "closely related yet distinct beings"
- This isn't polytheism with different gods; it's ONE god with multiple manifestations

**Mesopotamian fluidity of embodiment:**
- The *ṣalmu* (cult statue) was not a REPRESENTATION of the god — it WAS the god, in one of its bodies
- A god could have multiple cult statues in different cities, each of which WAS the god
- The god was present in its statue, its celestial manifestation (star/planet), and its mythological body simultaneously
- **Three types of divine bodies:** cultic (the statue), cosmic (the astral body), and mythic-narrative (the body described in myths)

**Canaanite evidence:**
- Ugaritic texts mention "Baal of Saphon," "Baal of Ugarit," "Baal of Lebanon" — similar local manifestation pattern
- The line between one god and another was sometimes blurred

**The Greek contrast:**
- Greek religion had NO fluidity model. Gods had discrete bodies and discrete selves.
- A specific statue of Athena was Athena's dwelling place or representation — not Athena herself in another body
- "Greek religion assumed a basic resemblance between mortals and immortals... ancient Near Eastern religions posited a radical contrast"

> **This is Sommer's first major claim:** The fluidity model challenges the simple monotheism/polytheism binary. Polytheistic Mesopotamia had fluid gods. Polytheistic Greece had discrete gods. Monotheistic Israel had BOTH fluid and discrete conceptions of God. The real division isn't between monotheism and polytheism — it's between cultures that understood divinity as fluid vs. cultures that understood it as discrete.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Mesopotamian evidence (treaty lists and hymns are unambiguous). MEDIUM-HIGH for the Greek contrast (broad generalization that may oversimplify).

## Chapter 2: The Fluidity Model in Ancient Israel

### Claim: Early Israelite religion (J and E sources) preserved the ANE fluidity model — YHWH had multiple bodies and manifestations

**Sommer's claim:** The oldest strands of biblical tradition (the J and E sources) portray Yhwh using the same fluidity model found in Mesopotamia and Canaan. Yhwh has multiple local manifestations, the *mal'akh* (angel/messenger) is not a separate being but a small-scale manifestation of Yhwh himself, and the line between Yhwh and his manifestations is deliberately blurred.

**Evidence presented:**

**Local Yhwh manifestations:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (8th century): "Yhwh of Samaria" and "Yhwh of Teman"
- 2 Samuel 15:7: Absalom needs to go to Hebron to worship "Yhwh of Hebron" — because Yhwh of Hebron is distinct from Yhwh of Jerusalem. "In Jerusalem one can pray to Yhwh generally or to Yhwh of Jerusalem, but not to Yhwh of Hebron!"
- Psalm 99:2: "Yhwh in Zion" — a localized manifestation

**The *mal'akh* as avatar:**
- In J and E texts, the *mal'akh* (usually translated "angel" but literally "messenger") is NOT a separate being. It is "a small-scale manifestation of God's own presence" — something "very similar to an avatar in Indian religions."
- "The distinction between the messenger and God is murky." The *mal'akh* can speak AS God, be addressed AS God, and is treated AS God — not as a separate being.
- Genesis 16: Hagar encounters the *mal'akh* of Yhwh, then says "Have I really seen God and remained alive?" She saw the *mal'akh* — and calls it God.
- Genesis 22: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh calls to Abraham from heaven saying "you have not withheld your son from ME" — the *mal'akh* speaks as Yhwh, not as a messenger FOR Yhwh.
- Exodus 3: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appears in the burning bush, then "Yhwh saw that Moses turned aside to see, and God called to him from the bush." The *mal'akh* IS Yhwh, in a localized manifestation.
- Judges 6: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appears to Gideon, then "Yhwh turned to him" — seamless transition from *mal'akh* to Yhwh.

**The fluid self:**
- In J and E, Yhwh can be in heaven AND on earth simultaneously. He can speak ABOUT himself in the third person.
- "It is possible that Yhwh temporarily overlaps with some heavenly beings who do God's bidding" — the distinction between God and the heavenly court is porous.

> **This is Sommer's second major claim:** The J and E sources preserve a monotheistic version of the ANE fluidity model. This is NOT polytheism. It's a way of understanding how the ONE God can be present in multiple places and forms. The "local Yhwhs" are not separate gods — they are manifestations of the same God. The *mal'akh* is not an angel in the later sense — it's Yhwh in a form humans can encounter without dying.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that J and E preserve the fluidity model (the textual evidence is strong, especially the *mal'akh* passages). MEDIUM that this represents "original" Israelite theology rather than one strand among several. The dating of J and E is, as always, contested.

## Chapter 3: The Rejection of the Fluidity Model

### Claim: The Priestly (P) and Deuteronomistic (D) sources rejected the fluidity model — creating the transcendent, noncorporeal God of later theology

**Sommer's claim:** The theological revolution wasn't from polytheism to monotheism. It was from a FLUID monotheism (J and E) to a DISCRETE, transcendent monotheism (P and D). P and D rejected the idea that God has multiple bodies, that God can be physically present on earth, and that God's self is fluid. They replaced the fluid God with a God accessible only through mediated forms: the *kavod* (glory), the *shem* (name), and the *torah*.

**Evidence presented:**

**The P source's anti-fluid moves:**
- Genesis 1: Humanity is made in God's *ṣelem* (image) — but P never describes God walking, eating, or being physically present. The *ṣelem* is abstracted.
- P's Tabernacle: God's *kavod* (glory) dwells in the Tabernacle, but it's a luminous cloud — not a body. It's "the physical manifestation of the divine presence that does not quite amount to an actual body."
- Exodus 33: P weaves together traditions about seeing God, then insists "you cannot see my face" — the chapter is "an anthology of conflicting traditions regarding the presence of God and how humans relate to it"
- For P, God dwells in heaven. What dwells on earth is God's *kavod* — not God himself.

**The D source's anti-fluid moves:**
- Deuteronomy insists God dwells in heaven, not on earth: "Yhwh your God is God in heaven above and on earth below" (Deut 4:39)
- The Temple is not God's house where he lives — it's "the place where Yhwh your God will choose to CAUSE HIS NAME TO DWELL" (*lĕšakkēn šĕmô šām*)
- God himself remains in heaven. Only his "name" (*shem*) dwells in the Temple
- This is a deliberate rejection of the older idea that God literally lived in the Temple

**The anti-anthropomorphic trend:**
- Both P and D reduce anthropomorphic descriptions of God
- God doesn't walk, eat, or appear in human form
- God's presence is mediated through non-anthropomorphic entities: *kavod* (glory), *shem* (name), *ruach* (spirit)

> **This is Sommer's third major claim:** The P and D sources are not "more monotheistic" than J and E. J and E were already monotheistic. What P and D did was change the NATURE of the one God — from fluid, multi-bodied, and physically present to discrete, single-bodied (or bodiless), and transcendent. The God of later Judaism and Christianity is the P/D God, not the J/E God.

**Confidence:** HIGH that P and D reduce anthropomorphism (well-established in scholarship). MEDIUM that this represents a deliberate "rejection" of fluidity rather than a different tradition that developed independently. Sommer's claim that P and D were REACTING to J and E is harder to prove than his claim that they DIFFER from J and E.

## Chapters 4-5: God's Bodies and Sacred Space

Sommer traces how the tension between the fluid and anti-fluid models plays out in discussions of sacred space:

- **The Tent of Meeting (E):** God's body actually dwells there. Moses speaks to God "face to face."
- **The Ark (D):** The ark is a footstool, not a throne. God is in heaven; only his name dwells with the ark.
- **The Temple (P/D):** The Temple is God's house in name only. The *kavod* enters (Ezekiel) and departs. God himself remains transcendent.

**The Ezekiel exception:** Ezekiel 1 and 10 describe God's *kavod* as having a genuinely anthropomorphic form — a body seated on a chariot-throne. Sommer argues Ezekiel preserves older fluidity traditions that P and D tried to suppress.

## Chapter 6: Implications and Afterlife

### Claim: The fluidity model didn't die — it survived in kabbalah and Christianity

**Sommer's claim:** The fluid God of J and E was suppressed by P and D, but never eliminated. It resurfaced in:

**Jewish mysticism (kabbalah):**
- The *sefirot* — ten manifestations or "emanations" of the infinite God (*Ein Sof*)
- Each *sefirah* is a distinct aspect of God, yet all are one
- The *Shekhinah* — the feminine, indwelling presence of God — is a survival of the old fluidity model
- "The Kabbalists recovered a genuinely ancient way of thinking about God that the mainstream of Jewish tradition had rejected"

**Christianity:**
- The Trinity — Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct yet one God
- The incarnation — God taking on a physical body in Jesus
- The Eucharist — God physically present in multiple locations simultaneously
- "Christianity represents, in part, a return to the J and E model of divine fluidity"

**The implication:** The Jewish/Christian split is partly about which model of divine presence to embrace. Judaism (following P and D) emphasizes transcendence. Christianity (following older traditions Sommer argues survived underground) embraces fluidity and embodiment. Both are legitimate developments of biblical tradition.

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM on the direct lineage from J/E to kabbalah/Trinity (Sommer acknowledges this is speculative). HIGH that the conceptual parallels are real. MEDIUM that they reflect influence rather than independent development of similar ideas.

## Appendix: Monotheism and Polytheism in Ancient Israel

Sommer directly addresses the question that Smith, Römer, and Dever spend their books answering:

> "I believe the documents preserved in the Hebrew Bible, when taken as a whole, are monotheistic."

He counts himself among scholars who argue "monotheism was well established in ancient Israel considerably earlier than the exile, even though some Israelites (perhaps even most Israelites) were not monotheists."

This puts Sommer in an interesting position: he REJECTS the polytheism→monotheism narrative for the biblical authors themselves, while accepting that popular religion was polytheistic. The biblical writers were monotheists from early on — but their monotheism was of the FLUID variety, not the abstract, transcendent variety of later Judaism.

## Sommer's Place in the Debate

### What Sommer Accepts

- Critical methods (source criticism, comparative ANE study)
- That popular Israelite religion included worship of other deities (Asherah, Baal, etc.)
- That the P and D sources are later than J and E
- That biblical texts have complex redactional histories

### What Sommer Challenges

- **The polytheism→monotheism narrative is too simple.** The real story is fluid monotheism → discrete monotheism.
- **"Monotheism" vs. "polytheism" is the wrong question.** The interesting question is about the NATURE of the one God — embodied or transcendent, fluid or discrete, present or distant.
- **The P/D God is not "more evolved."** It's a different theological choice, not a higher stage of development.
- **Kabbalah and the Trinity are not corruptions of biblical monotheism.** They are recoveries of an older, suppressed biblical tradition.

### The Significance for Faith Deconstruction

Sommer offers a way out of the trap that Smith, Römer, and Dever seem to set: "the Bible's God is a historical construction, therefore belief is undermined." Sommer says: "Yes, the Bible's God has a history — and within that history, there are multiple legitimate ways of understanding God. The noncorporeal, transcendent God of Maimonides and Aquinas is ONE biblical option. The embodied, fluid God of the J source and the kabbalists is ANOTHER biblical option. Neither is the 'original' or the 'corruption.'"

For someone reconstructing faith after deconstruction, Sommer offers resources the other three books don't: a way to hold onto the biblical God while accepting the critical history. The God you end up with may look different from the God you started with — more embodied, more fluid, more mystically present — but he's still the God of the Bible.

## Four-Book Synthesis

| Scholar | Core Thesis | God's History | Implication for Faith |
|---------|------------|--------------|----------------------|
| **Smith** | Convergence/differentiation — Yahweh absorbed other gods | God is a composite | Devastating for traditional theology; provides no path forward |
| **Römer** | Political construction — Yahweh became God through history | God is an invention | Same; "invention" doesn't mean false, but it means human |
| **Dever** | Folk religion vs. Book religion — the Bible isn't what people practiced | God's wife was erased | The Bible is a minority report; women's religion was suppressed |
| **Sommer** | Fluidity vs. anti-fluidity — multiple legitimate biblical theologies | God has many bodies | The tradition ITSELF contains resources for reconstruction |

Sommer doesn't refute Smith, Römer, or Dever. He accepts much of their historical work. But he shows that the historical-critical method doesn't lead inevitably to the conclusion that biblical religion is a human fabrication. The same methods can reveal multiple authentic biblical theologies — and the task of faith is to choose among them, not to reject them all.
