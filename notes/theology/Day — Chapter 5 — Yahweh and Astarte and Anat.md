# Day — Chapter 5: Yahweh and the Goddesses Astarte and Anat (and the Queen of Heaven)

Day covers two major Canaanite goddesses and the Queen of Heaven debate. His key arguments: Astarte was Baal's consort (likely the "Queen of Heaven"), Anat was a warrior goddess who functioned as Yahweh's consort at Elephantine, and both goddesses were sufficiently associated with Yahweh (through the Yahweh-Baal equation) that they could serve as divine consorts in syncretistic Israelite religion.

---

## Claim 1: The vocalization "Ashtoreth" is a deliberate scribal distortion (vowels of *bošet* "shame")

**Author's claim:** "It is generally accepted that the vocalization of the name of the goddess *ʻaštōret* in the Old Testament is a deliberate scribal distortion of an original *ʻaštart*" (p. 129). The distorted vowels reflect *bošet* "shame," the same polemical device used for Baal in personal names.

**Evidence presented:**
1. Extra-biblical forms uniformly presuppose Astarte (Greek *Astartē*, Akkadian *Ištar*) — not Ashtoreth
2. The same *bošet* distortion pattern operates on Baal names (Ish-bosheth for Eshbaal, etc.) — "It is virtually certain that the distorted vocalization reflects the vowels of the Hebrew word *bošet*" (p. 129)
3. Molech's vowels are probably similarly distorted (should be *mōlek* or *melek*)
4. **Against Cooper's objection** (that *a→o* is natural vowel shift, like *dāgān→dāgôn*): (a) We should expect *ʻaštōrt* not *ʻaštōret* — extra-biblical forms lack the vowel between r and t; (b) Greek sources (all late) consistently render *Astartē*, not a form with o — surprising if the change had already occurred; (c) The Syrian goddess Atargatis (conflation of Astarte + Anat) preserves the *a* vowel (p. 130)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is settled scholarship. The *bošet* pattern, already demonstrated in Chapter 3 for Baal names, extends to Astarte.

**What's at stake:** Every time you read "Ashtoreth" in the OT, you're reading a theological insult. The scribes weren't just recording a goddess name — they were deforming it. This is the same scribal strategy applied to Baal-zebub and Molech.

**Who disagrees:** A. Cooper (1990) argues the vocalization is authentic vowel shift, not polemical. Day refutes efficiently.

**My assessment:** Day's rebuttal of Cooper is effective. The absence of any *o* vocalization in Greek transcriptions is decisive — if the name naturally shifted to Ashtoreth in Hebrew, Greek sources (which postdate the Hebrew Bible) should show some trace of it. They don't.

## Claim 2: Shamgar ben Anat = "son of Anat" as an honorific military title, not a patronymic

**Author's claim:** Shamgar ben Anat (Judg 3:31; 5:6) is "metaphorically described as 'son of (the goddess) Anat'" — the term functions as "an honorific military title" (p. 135). Anat was a warrior goddess, and other Canaanites called *bn ʻnt* were warriors.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Against the "resident of Beth-Anat" theory:** (a) *bn ʻnt* is found in Canaanite contexts where a place name isn't credible; (b) "it is much more usual for a person's ancestry than his or her place of origin to be cited in the Old Testament"; (c) we'd expect *bēt-hāʻănātî* for a Beth-Anat resident, not *ben-ʻănāt* (p. 134-135)
2. **Against Fensham's "Hanaean" theory:** The Mari Hanaeans are "about five hundred years prior to the time of Shamgar ben Anat" (p. 135)
3. **Positive evidence:** (a) Ugaritic texts attest *bn ʻnt* for individuals (KTU² 4.307.6; 4.320.4); (b) Two inscribed ARROWHEADS owned by warriors bear *bn ʻnt* — "which fits the honorific military explanation" (p. 135-136). One from El-Khadr reads *ʻbdlbʼt bn ʻnt*; another from the Beqaʻ valley has *bn bn ʻn[t]*
4. Anat was a warrior goddess — the military title makes cultural sense

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The arrowhead evidence is the strongest single point — warrior weapons inscribed with "son of Anat" make the military-title reading natural. But the evidence is circumstantial, not explicit.

**What's at stake:** If correct, this reveals that Anat was sufficiently known in early Israel that "son of Anat" could function as a military honorific — implying cultural familiarity with the goddess beyond what the OT text explicitly acknowledges.

**Who disagrees:** Albright, Alt, Noth, Mazar — "resident of Beth-Anat" (majority older view). Day's counter-arguments are solid.

**My assessment:** The arrowhead evidence tips the balance decisively. It's hard to imagine a "resident of Beth-Anat" inscribing that location on a weapon. "Son of [warrior goddess]" as a military title makes immediate sense analogously to "son of Mars" or similar martial epithets.

## Claim 3: Anat-Yahu at Elephantine confirms Anat functioned as Yahweh's consort

**Author's claim:** "It seems indubitable that the goddess Anat, in the form of Anat-Yahu, did function as Yahweh's wife amongst the Jews at Elephantine in the fifth century BCE" (p. 144). This probably reflects pre-exilic Israelite syncretism going back to the Northern Kingdom.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Anat-Yahu is a deity:** Albright's attempt to read *ʻnt* as "providence" or "sign" was "disproved by the fact that a deity Anat-Bethel is attested c. 675 BCE in the treaty between Esar-haddon of Assyria and Baal, king of Tyre" (p. 144). If Anat-Bethel is a deity, so is Anat-Yahu
2. **Anat as Baal's consort:** Despite challenges from P.L. Day and N. Walls, "other evidence seems to make a good, cumulative case." Baal is constantly paired with Anat and Astarte at Ugarit. In the Egyptian contest of Horus and Seth, Anat and Astarte are "both explicitly stated to be Seth's (i.e. Baal's) wives" (p. 143)
3. **Baal-Yahweh equation permits Anat-Yahweh:** If Yahweh = Baal (Chapter 3), and Anat = Baal's consort, then Anat could serve as Yahweh's consort
4. **Origin of this syncretism:** Day prefers a pre-exilic Northern Kingdom origin, since Bethel (Jer 48:13) is the Northern Kingdom's deity, and Anat-Bethel at Elephantine pairs Anat with Bethel. "I prefer to see the notion of Anat as Yahweh's wife as going back to pre-exilic Israel, rather than as a later accretion" (p. 145)
5. **Hos 14:9 (ET 8):** Day sees a word play on Anat (and Asherah) in Hosea — from the 8th c. Northern prophet — implying Anat was known as a consort of Yahweh then (p. 145)

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Anat-Yahu deity identification at Elephantine. MEDIUM for tracing it to pre-exilic Northern Israel.

**What's at stake:** Elephantine is a genuine anomaly — a Jewish community in 5th c. BCE Egypt worshipping Yahweh alongside a goddess explicitly called "Anat of Yahu." This is polytheistic Yahwism surviving CENTURIES after monotheism supposedly emerged in the exile. Either the exile didn't produce uniform monotheism, or Elephantine preserves a pre-reform religion.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith and K. van der Toorn argue Anat worship was unknown in pre-exilic Israel and that it arose in Egypt or post-722 Samaria. Day: they "exaggerate the extent to which Anat was absent in the first-millennium BCE Mediterranean world" (p. 145).

**Alternative reading:** Elephantine was an isolated, unrepresentative military colony. Anat-Yahu may reflect syncretism that developed in Egypt among diaspora Jews rather than authentically pre-exilic Israelite religion.

**My assessment:** Day's rebuttal of Albright's "providence" reading is decisive. The Esar-haddon treaty evidence for Anat-Bethel as a deity is unimpeachable. But the chain from Elephantine BACK to pre-exilic Israel is speculative — we simply don't know when or where the Anat-Yahu syncretism originated. Day's preference for Northern Kingdom origin is plausible but not proven.

## Claim 4: The "Queen of Heaven" in Jeremiah is most likely Astarte (possibly in syncretism with Ishtar)

**Author's claim:** "Although certainty is not possible, the best case can be made for the Queen of Heaven being Astarte" (p. 150). Possibly "Astarte in syncretism with her Mesopotamian equivalent Ishtar" (p. 150).

**Evidence presented (eliminating alternatives):**
- **Shapash** (sun goddess — Dahood): "gained no support." Based on a dubious translation of Ugaritic *knm* as "cakes" (p. 146-147)
- **Asherah** (Koch, Keel/Uehlinger): Nothing in first-millennium texts "associates her particularly with the heavens at all" (p. 147). Asherah is the West Semitic equivalent of... nothing Mesopotamian — Astarte, not Asherah, is the West Semitic equivalent of Ishtar (p. 147)
- **Anat:** The Hermopolis letter pairs "temple of Bethel and temple of the Queen of Heaven" — suggesting Anat-Bethel as consort of Bethel. But the Deuteronomist "never once polemicizes against her cult elsewhere" — Asherah and Astarte are the goddesses the Deuteronomist targets. Anat seems less prominent in first-millennium Palestine (p. 148)
- **Ishtar** (Schrader, Rudolph, Weinfeld): The Mesopotamian goddess IS called "Queen of Heaven" (*šarrat šamê*) and *kawwānîm* (cakes) is an Akkadian loanword (*kamānu*) attested for Ishtar offerings. BUT the text makes clear this was "deep-seated among both the ordinary people and rulers of Judah" — "a popular folk custom... not some recently implanted cult from Assyria" (p. 149)

**Evidence presented (for Astarte):**
1. Called "Lady of Heaven" in New Kingdom Egypt (alongside Anat, Ishtar, Qudshu)
2. **Most frequently associated with heavens in first millennium:** Eshmunazar inscription (KAI 14): *ʻštrt šmm ʼdrm* "Astarte of the Highest Heavens" (p. 150)
3. **Aphrodite Ourania = Astarte:** Two Delos inscriptions by Ascalonites address "the heavenly Aphrodite, Astarte of Palestine." Herodotus (1.105) says the most ancient temple of Aphrodite Ourania was in Ascalon — indicating Palestinian origin (p. 150)
4. **Cake offerings attested:** Kition tariff (4th c. BCE) mentions cakes baked for the "Holy Queen" (though different Hebrew word)
5. **Deuteronomistic polemic:** The Deuteronomist (to whom Jer 7 and 44 belong) explicitly polemicizes against Astarte elsewhere (1 Kgs 11:5, 33; 2 Kgs 23:13) — but never against Anat or Shapash
6. **Plus Ishtar syncretism:** The Akkadian loanword for "cakes" (*kawwānîm*) suggests some Mesopotamian influence. Ackerman's view: "not simply Astarte, but Astarte in syncretism with her Mesopotamian equivalent Ishtar" (p. 150)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Day's elimination of alternatives is methodical and persuasive. The Astarte conclusion isn't certain but is the best-supported. The Ishtar syncretism nuance is appropriately cautious.

**What's at stake:** Jeremiah 44 is the most detailed description of popular goddess worship in the OT. Identifying the goddess tells us which Canaanite deity had the deepest hold on ordinary Judean religious practice — and it was most likely Astarte, with cakes, incense, and drink offerings provided primarily by women.

**Who disagrees:** Ishtar proponents (Schrader, Rudolph, Weinfeld, Held). Asherah partisans (Koch, Keel/Uehlinger). Anat advocates (Vincent, Albright, Porten, Cogan).

**My assessment:** Day's case is strongest on two points: (1) Astarte is the only candidate the Deuteronomist actually polemicizes against elsewhere, and (2) the Aphrodite Ourania/Astarte identification is well-attested in the first millennium. The cake-offering vocabulary being Akkadian is the strongest counter-point for Ishtar, and Day handles this by adopting Ackerman's "syncretism" solution — a reasonable compromise.

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Ashtoreth = deliberate *bošet* distortion | VERY HIGH | Cooper's vowel-shift theory (refuted) |
| Shamgar ben Anat = honorific military title | MEDIUM-HIGH | Could still be geographical or literal patronymic |
| Anat-Yahu = Anat as Yahweh's consort at Elephantine | HIGH (for Elephantine); MEDIUM (for pre-exilic Israel) | Van der Toorn/Smith: late accretion, not pre-exilic |
| Queen of Heaven = Astarte (+ Ishtar syncretism) | MEDIUM-HIGH | Ishtar identification has strongest philological evidence; Astarte has strongest cultural fit |

**Strongest section:** The Queen of Heaven identification. Day systematically evaluates all five candidates, gives each its due, and reaches a differentiated conclusion. This is Day at his best — methodical, fair to alternatives, appropriately cautious.

**Weakest section:** The Anat-Yahu → pre-exilic Northern Kingdom chain. Day's preference is plausible but the evidence from Elephantine can't tell us WHEN the syncretism developed. The Hosea 14 wordplay is too weak to carry this weight alone.

**Most important implicit argument:** The cumulative picture across Chapters 2, 3, and 5 shows that Yahweh had THREE possible consorts in syncretistic Israelite religion — Asherah (through El equation), Anat (through Baal equation), and Astarte (through Baal equation + Queen of Heaven cult). This isn't a single goddess tradition but MULTIPLE competing consort traditions, which helps explain why the Deuteronomistic polemic needed to be so comprehensive.
