# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 1

## Deities in Israel in the Period of the Judges

---

## Claim 1: El was the original god of Israel; Yahweh was imported later

**Smith's claim:** "The original god of Israel was El." Yahweh was a southern warrior-god from Edom/Midian/Teiman who entered Israelite religion secondarily, perhaps through trade routes. Over time, El and Yahweh were identified, and El's characteristics, titles, and cult sites were absorbed into Yahweh.

**Evidence presented:**
- **The name "Israel":** Contains *'ēl*, not Yahweh. If Yahweh had been the original god, the group would be named something like *Yiśrâ-yāhû*. This suggests El was the original chief deity.
- **Genesis 49:24-25:** A series of El epithets ("Bull of Jacob," "Shepherd, Stone of Israel," "El, your Father," "Shadday") separate from the mention of Yahweh in verse 18.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8-9:** The "smoking gun." Elyon (a title of El) distributes nations to his divine sons; Yahweh receives Israel as HIS portion. This preserves a stage when Yahweh was subordinate to El — one of his sons.
- **No biblical polemics against El:** Unlike Baal and Asherah, El was never condemned. He wasn't rejected — he was absorbed. This suggests the identification happened early and peacefully.
- **El's name became generic:** *'ēl* became the common noun for "god." Joshua 22:22 uses *'ēl 'ĕlōhîm yhwh* — "El, God, Yahweh."
- **Exodus 6:2-3:** Explicitly identifies El Shadday with Yahweh. "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." Smith reads this as an honest admission: the patriarchs worshipped El, not Yahweh.
- **El traits transferred to Yahweh:** Aged bearded figure enthroned amidst divine council → "Ancient of Days" (Daniel 7). "Father of years" → "Eternal God" (Deut 33:27). "Kind El, the Compassionate" → "Merciful and gracious God" (Exod 34:6). Healing god, tent dwelling, dream-visions, cosmic waters — all El traits now applied to Yahweh.
- **The Shechem example:** The local god was *'ēl bĕrît* ("El of the covenant," Judg 9:46). This title appears in a Late Bronze Age Ugaritic text (KTU 1.128). When Shechem's cult became Yahwistic, Yahweh simply took El's place and El's title. "The old religious lore of a deity such as El was inherited by the Yahwistic priesthood."

**Confidence:** HIGH that El and Yahweh were originally distinct. VERY HIGH that Deut 32:8-9 originally read "sons of God" (DSS/LXX), not "sons of Israel" (MT). MEDIUM that Yahweh was imported from the south — the evidence is suggestive (old poetry has him coming from Edom/Seir/Paran) but not conclusive.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is foundational. If El was the original god and Yahweh was a later import who absorbed El's identity, then Yahweh is not the eternal Creator God of Christian theology — he's a composite figure formed from at least two earlier deities. The "God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" was, historically, El — not Yahweh. Exodus 6:2-3 essentially admits this. Traditional theology treats this as progressive revelation (God revealed his true name Yahweh later). Smith treats it as evidence of distinct deities later merged.

**Who disagrees:** Heiser argues Yahweh IS Elyon in Deut 32:8-9 — they're the same deity, and the "sons of God" are created divine beings, not rival gods. Kaufmann argued Israelite monotheism was original. Conservatives argue the patriarchs worshiped Yahweh under the name El Shadday (progressive revelation, not distinct deities). See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins|the full directory]].

**Alternative reading:** Exodus 6:2-3 can be read as progressive revelation, not evidence of distinct deities. The name Israel with *'ēl* could reflect El as a generic term for "god" rather than the proper name of a distinct Canaanite deity. Smith's reading is plausible but not the only plausible reading.

**My assessment:** The cumulative case is strong. The Israel/El naming, Deut 32:8-9, the lack of El polemic, and the systematic transfer of El's traits to Yahweh form a pattern that's hard to explain as coincidence. The weakest link is the southern import thesis — we have plausible mechanisms (trade, the Kenite hypothesis) but no direct evidence of HOW Yahweh moved from Edom to Israel. If this specific claim is wrong, the larger point (El and Yahweh merged) can still be correct — they could have merged within Canaan rather than through import.

## Claim 2: Baal and Yahweh coexisted without conflict in the Judges period; the conflict was a ninth-century development retrojected backward

**Smith's claim:** The Deuteronomistic editors of Judges (8th century or later) projected the ninth-century Baal-Yahweh conflict backward into the Judges period. In the Judges period itself, Baal and Yahweh probably coexisted without major tension. The Baal-names in Saul's Yahwistic family (Eshbaal, Meribbaal, alongside Jonathan) suggest *ba'al* could function as a title for Yahweh or that Baal was acceptable in Yahwistic circles.

**Evidence presented:**
- Judges' redaction postdates 722 BCE (Judges 18:30 mentions "the captivity of the land"). The anti-Baal polemic in Judges 2-3 is part of a literary framework imposed centuries later.
- Saul's family had BOTH Yahwistic names (Jonathan) AND Baal names (Eshbaal, Meribbaal). "Why would a Yahwistic family give Baal names, if Baal were a god inimical to Yahweh?"
- Later scribes altered these names: Eshbaal → Ishboshet, Meribbaal → Mephiboshet, Jerubbaal → Jerubbeshet (substituting *bôšet*, "shame," for *ba'al*). The alterations prove the names became embarrassing — they were NOT originally so.
- The name *bĕ'alyāh* ("Yah is Lord") and the seal *ywb'l* ("Yaw is Lord") suggest *ba'al* could simply mean "Lord" and function as a title for Yahweh.
- "Baal was probably not the threat in the Judges period or the tenth century that later tradents considered him. It was the traumatic events of the ninth century and afterwards that shaped the perspective of the tradents."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the redaction dating (Judges finalized after 722 is widely accepted). MEDIUM on the coexistence claim — it explains the data neatly but requires treating the Judges narratives as unreliable for religious history while using them for political history. There's a selectivity problem.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Smith is right, the biblical picture of Israel constantly "falling into" Baal worship during the Judges period is a theological construct, not history. The "cycles of apostasy" in Judges are literary, not historical. This undermines the Bible's own explanation for why Israel needed kings and prophets.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives would argue the Judges narratives preserve genuine historical memory of Baal conflict. The Baal-names could reflect syncretism (some Israelites had already compromised) rather than peaceful coexistence. The redaction dating doesn't prove the content is fabricated — editors can faithfully transmit older traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The Baal-names in Saul's family might reflect exactly what the Bible says: some Israelites (even royal families) had apostatized. The names are evidence of the problem, not evidence that there was no problem. Smith's reading requires the biblical narrative to be systematically misleading — which is possible but needs more evidence than he provides here.

**My assessment:** Smith's strongest point is the name alterations (Eshbaal → Ishboshet). You don't censor what was never there. The alterations prove Baal-names existed in early Israelite tradition and later became unacceptable. But Smith's conclusion that this means "peaceful coexistence" goes beyond the evidence. The names could reflect genuine syncretism that the later editors correctly identified as problematic. Smith is extracting a specific conclusion (no conflict) from evidence that supports a range of possibilities.

## Claim 3: Israel's early poetry shows convergence — multiple deities' imagery applied to Yahweh

**Smith's claim:** The oldest stratum of biblical poetry (Genesis 49, Deuteronomy 33, Judges 5, Psalm 18, Psalm 29, Psalm 68) mixes imagery from El, Baal, and possibly Asherah and applies it all to Yahweh. This "convergence" happened early and was not polemical — it was creative theological synthesis.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Deuteronomy 33:26-27:** "Who rides through the heavens" = Baal storm language. "The eternal God" (*'ĕlōhê qedem*) = El language. Both in adjacent verses, both applied to Yahweh.
- **Psalm 18:14-16:** "Yahweh thundered... the Most High (*'elyôn*) uttered his voice... the channels of the sea (*'ăpîqê mayim*) were seen." Baal thundering + El's title + El's cosmic waters — all conflated.
- **Genesis 49:25-26:** A series of paired divine epithets including possible goddess language (*šādayim wārāḥam*, "Breasts-and-Womb," echoing Ugaritic titles of Asherah and Anat). The goddess language was NOT later applied to Yahweh — it was dropped.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the imagery is shared. MEDIUM on the dating of these poems — Smith dates them to the early monarchy or pre-monarchic period, but this is contested. If they're later, the convergence might be later too.

**What's at stake for faith:** The poetry that sounds most "monotheistic" to modern readers (Yahweh as cosmic king, thunderer, ancient one) is actually the most SYNCRETISTIC — it's built from pieces of other gods. The majestic language of the Psalms is borrowed from Baal hymns. This doesn't make it false, but it makes it derivative.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue shared language reflects common ANE idiom, not borrowing. Poetic imagery is like language — everyone in the region used similar metaphors for the divine without implying theological equivalence.

**My assessment:** The parallels are too specific for "common idiom." When Psalm 29 uses the exact structure of a Baal hymn (sevenfold voice, progression from sea to mountains to temple, enthronement over the flood), "coincidence" is harder to believe than "borrowing." The convergence thesis is one of Smith's strongest arguments.

## Claim 4: Asherah as a goddess is poorly attested in the Judges period; the asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol

**Smith's claim:** The asherah (wooden pole/tree symbol) was present in early Israelite religion, but it probably functioned as a symbol within Yahweh's cult, not as a representation of a separate goddess. The goddess Asherah may have been known earlier (Genesis 49:25 possibly preserves her epithets), but by the Judges period, her cult had merged into Yahweh's.

**Evidence presented:**
- Asherah does NOT appear as a theophoric element in Hebrew names
- Judges 6 focuses on Baal, not Asherah — only the asherah pole is mentioned
- Genesis 49:25 may preserve goddess epithets (*šādayim wārāḥam*) paired with El's epithets, suggesting an early stage when a goddess was invoked alongside El — but this language was not later applied to Yahweh
- The Taanach cult stand (10th c.) shows a tree (asherah), a bull deity, and a goddess figure (probably Astarte, not Asherah)

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Smith at his most cautious — and possibly his most wrong. The majority of scholars disagree with him. He himself concedes "I may be wrong." Flag as: Smith's conclusion here is a judgment call based on how much weight you give to different pieces of ambiguous evidence.

**What's at stake for faith:** See Foreword/Preface note — same assessment. If Asherah WAS a goddess and Yahweh's consort, the biblical God had a wife. Smith resists this conclusion even though it would strengthen his larger argument.

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone. See the full list in the Foreword/Preface note.

**My assessment:** Smith is probably wrong here — the majority interpretation (Asherah was a goddess) has better cumulative evidence. But his caution is methodologically instructive. He's not willing to claim more than the evidence supports, even when the claim would help his case. That's a mark of intellectual honesty worth noting.

## Claim 5: Israel's pantheon was typical for the region

**Smith's claim:** First-millennium Levantine states (Ammon, Moab, Edom, Phoenician city-states) all had small pantheons of 10 or fewer deities headed by a national god. Israel's configuration (Yahweh as national god, El/Baal/Asherah as subordinate or absorbed deities, divine council) was unremarkable. The transition from second-millennium pantheons of 200+ deities (Ugarit) to first-millennium pantheons of ~10 was a regional trend. Israel participated in it — and eventually took it to its logical extreme (one god).

**Evidence presented:** Survey of Ammon (Milkom, El, ~10 deities), Moab (Chemosh, Ashtar-Chemosh), Edom (Qaws, possibly El and Baal), Byblos (Baal Shamem, Baalat Gebal, ~8), Sidon (Eshmun, Astarte, ~6), Tyre (Bethel, Baal Shamem, Melqart, Astarte, ~8-10).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The data is sparse — we have far fewer texts from these cultures than from Ugarit. "10 or fewer deities" might reflect gaps in the evidence, not actual pantheon size. Smith acknowledges this: "deriving historical claims on the basis of the actually attested texts... is highly problematic."

**What's at stake for faith:** This contextualizes Israelite religion. Israel wasn't uniquely polytheistic (as the Bible's polemic implies) or uniquely monotheistic (as traditional theology claims). It was typical for its time and place. What WAS unique was taking the regional trend toward fewer gods to its conclusion: zero gods besides Yahweh.

**My assessment:** The comparative data is genuinely useful for countering both "Israel was always monotheistic" and "Israel was uniquely depraved in its polytheism." Neither is true. Smith's regional survey is one of the most underappreciated sections of the chapter.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 1's Overall Strength

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| El was original god; Yahweh imported | MEDIUM-HIGH | Schmid's late dating (if texts are Persian, this reconstruction collapses) |
| Baal coexisted peacefully in Judges period | MEDIUM | Alternative reading of Baal-names as genuine syncretism |
| Early poetry shows convergence | HIGH | Dating of the poems (if late, convergence is late too) |
| Asherah was symbol, not goddess | LOW | Majority scholarly disagreement |
| Pantheon typical for region | MEDIUM | Sparse comparative data |

**The single biggest threat to this entire chapter:** Konrad Schmid's argument that the Pentateuch and early poetry are Persian-period compositions. If Smith is wrong about the dating of his sources, the entire reconstruction of "early Israelite religion" is built on texts that don't describe early Israelite religion. Smith is aware of this challenge (he mentions it in the preface) but doesn't resolve it.
