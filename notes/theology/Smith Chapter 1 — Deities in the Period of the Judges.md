# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 1

## Deities in Israel in the Period of the Judges

This is the evidence chapter. Smith builds his case that early Israelite religion included El, Baal, Asherah, and Yahweh — not as "foreign syncretism" but as indigenous Israelite practice.

---

## Section 1: Israel's "Canaanite" Heritage

> **Core claim:** "Early Israelite culture cannot be separated easily from the culture of 'Canaan.'" The distinction between "Israelite" and "Canaanite" that the Bible insists on is not supported by the material evidence.

### The Archaeological Case

Smith presents a systematic case for cultural continuity:

1. **Scripts:** Both linear and cuneiform alphabetic scripts appear in highlands AND coast/valleys during both Late Bronze and Iron I.

2. **Language:** "Canaanite and Hebrew so closely overlap that the ability to distinguish them is premised more on historical information than linguistic criteria." Isaiah 19:18 calls Hebrew "the language of Canaan" (*śěpat kěna'an*).

3. **Material culture:** Items once thought distinctively Israelite — the four-room house, collared-rim store jar, hewn cisterns — "are now attested on the coast, in the valleys, or in Transjordan."

4. **Burial patterns:** Multiple primary burials in caves continued from Late Bronze through Iron Age. Bench tombs appeared first on the coast, then in the highlands.

5. **Cultic terminology:** Sacrificial terms are shared with Ugaritic/Phoenician — *zebah*, *šělāmîm*, *minḥah*, *kālîl*, *'ōlāh*. Cultic personnel terms too: *kōhen* (priest), *qādēš* (cultic functionary), *rb khnm* / *hakkōhēn haggādôl* (chief priest). Even the "tent of meeting" (*'ōhel mô'ēd*) has Canaanite prototypes.

> **This is a devastating empirical case.** Smith isn't just asserting cultural continuity — he's cataloguing specific parallels across every category of evidence.

### The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE)

The earliest extrabiblical mention of Israel. It distinguishes between "Israel" (written with the people determinative) and "Canaan" (written with the land determinative). Smith notes this distinction but argues it doesn't imply cultural separation — just that an Egyptian scribe categorized them differently. "Israel as a people was established by 1200 B.C." but culturally continuous with Canaan.

### The Evidence Problem

Smith is honest: "It is at present impossible to establish, on the basis of archaeological information, distinctions between Israelites and Canaanites in the Iron I period." The biblical evidence is "similarly problematic" — narrative material about Iron I was written "at least two or three centuries" later.

> Smith also notes that Israel absorbed MANY groups: Gibeonites, Jerahmeelites, Kenites, descendants of Rahab, Caleb the Kenizzite, and Canaanite cities like Hepher and Tirzah. The ethnic picture was complex, not binary.

### The Religious Continuity

Inscriptions from Canaan (before Israel emerged) attest to the same deities: El (Qubur el-Walaydah, 12th c.), Asherah (Lachish ewer, 13th c., with titles *rbt* "lady" and *'lt* "goddess"), Anat (El-Khadr arrowhead, c. 1100, "son of Anat"), Baal (Taanach and El-Amarna letters, 15th-14th c.).

**The biblical record confirms these continued in Israel during the Judges period:** El (*'ēl bĕrît* at Shechem, Judges 9:46), the asherah (Judges 6:25-26), Baal names (Jerubbaal, Eshbaal, Meribbaal), Anat (Shamgar ben Anat, Judges 5:6 — though no cult for Anat is attested).

> **Smith's crucial move:** "In various ways, El, Baal, and Asherah (or at least the symbol named after her, the asherah) were integrally related to Yahweh and the cult of this deity during the period of the Judges." These weren't foreign competitors — they were PART of Yahwism.

## Section 2: Yahweh and El

> **Core claim:** "The original god of Israel was El."

### The Two Pieces of Evidence

1. **The name "Israel"** contains *'ēl*, not Yahweh. If Yahweh had been the original god, the group would be named something like *Yiśrâ-yāhû*. The name suggests El was the original chief god.

2. **Genesis 49:24-25** presents El epithets separate from Yahweh (mentioned separately in v. 18).

### Deuteronomy 32:8-9 — The Smoking Gun

Smith presents this as a "literary vestige of the initial assimilation":

> "When the Most High (*'elyôn*) gave to the nations their inheritance... he fixed the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of divine beings. For Yahweh's portion is his people, Jacob his allotted heritage."

**Smith's reading:** Elyon (a title of El) distributes nations to his divine sons. Yahweh — originally a warrior-god from Sinai/Paran/Edom/Teiman — receives Israel as HIS portion. This preserves a stage when **Yahweh was a subordinate deity to El**, one of his sons.

> "Perhaps due to trade with Edom/Midian, Yahweh entered secondarily into the Israelite highland religion."

### The Assimilation of El to Yahweh

Over time, El and Yahweh were identified. Smith notes three key facts:

1. **There are no biblical polemics against El** — unlike Baal and Asherah, El was never condemned. He was absorbed, not rejected.

2. **El's name became a generic noun for "god"** (*'ēl*). Joshua 22:22 uses *'ēl 'ĕlōhîm yhwh* — "El, God, Yahweh" — showing the progression from proper name to generic noun to Yahwistic identification.

3. **Exodus 6:2-3** explicitly identifies the old god El Shadday with Yahweh: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." Smith reads this as an honest admission: the patriarchs worshipped El (a Canaanite god), not Yahweh.

### The El Traits Absorbed by Yahweh

Smith catalogues the specific characteristics that transferred from El to Yahweh:

| El's Traits | Applied to Yahweh |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Aged, bearded, enthroned figure | "Ancient of days" (Daniel 7), enthroned in divine council (1 Kings 22:19; Isaiah 6) |
| "Father of years" (*'ab šnm*) | "Eternal God" (*'ĕlōhê qedem*, Deut 33:27) |
| "Kind El, the Compassionate" | "Merciful and gracious God" (Exodus 34:6) |
| "Father of humanity" | Father of Israel (Deut 32:6) |
| Healer | Yahweh as healer (Gen 20:17; Num 12:13) |
| Dwelling in a "tent" (*qrš*) | Tabernacle with *qĕrāšîm* |
| Dwelling amid cosmic waters | Yahweh's abode amid waters (Ps 47:5; Ezekiel 47) |
| Divine council of holy ones | Yahweh's heavenly court |
| Dream-visions | Yahweh appears in dreams |
| Creates (*qny*) and establishes (*kwn*) | Deut 32:6 applies both verbs to Yahweh |

**Deuteronomy 32:6-7 is a case study:** "Is he not your father, who created you (*qānekā*), who made you and established you? Remember the days of old (*'ôlām*), consider the years of many generations." Every phrase echoes El's Ugaritic epithets. "The people, for example, are 'senseless' (*lō' ḥākām*), unlike El" — the text uses El's traits as a foil.

### The Shechem Example

At Shechem, the local god was *'ēl bĕrît* ("El of the covenant" — Judges 9:46). This title appears in a Late Bronze Age Ugaritic text (KTU 1.128). When the cult of Shechem became Yahwistic, "it inherited and continued the El traditions of that site." Yahweh simply took El's place and El's titles.

> "Ezekiel 16:3a proclaims accordingly: 'Thus says the Lord God to Jerusalem: Your origin and your birth are of the land of the Canaanites.'" Even the Bible admits it, in Smith's reading.

### Tigay's Onomastics — Addressed Again

Smith engages Tigay directly: of 557 Israelite names with divine elements, 557 are Yahwistic, 77 use *'l*, a handful use *b'l*, none use Anat or Asherah. Smith argues the *'l* names "historically reflect the identification of Yahweh and El" — there WAS no separate El cult by the time these inscriptions were written, because El had been absorbed. He uses Tigay's own data to support his case.

## Section 3: Yahweh and Baal

> **Core claim:** In the Judges period, Baal and Yahweh probably coexisted without major conflict. The later polemic (Judges 2-3, the Deuteronomistic redaction) retrojects ninth-century conflicts backward.

### The Redaction Problem

The book of Judges received its final form in the 8th century or later. The "cyclic pattern" (Israel sins → God punishes → Israel cries out → God sends a judge) is a literary framework imposed by later editors. The polemic against Baal in Judges 2-3 is part of this later framework.

> Judges 18:30 dates the redaction to after "the day of the captivity of the land" — either 722 (fall of northern kingdom) or 587. Smith favors 722. So the anti-Baal polemic is an **8th-century perspective projected backward.**

### The Names Evidence

The early material preserves Baal names that later editors found embarrassing:

| Original Name | Meaning | Later Alteration | Altered Meaning |
|--------------|---------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Jerubbaal | "May Baal contend" | Jerubbeshet | "Shame" (*bôšet*) |
| Eshbaal | "Man of Baal/Lord" | Ishboshet | "Man of shame" |
| Meribbaal | "Baal is advocate" | Mephiboshet | "From the mouth of shame" |

**Smith's key insight:** These names belonged to **Saul's Yahwistic family.** Jonathan (a Yahwistic name) was Saul's son. Eshbaal and Meribbaal were also in Saul's lineage. "Why would a Yahwistic family give Baal names, if Baal were a god inimical to Yahweh?"

**Three possibilities:**
1. *Ba'al* was a title for Yahweh ("Lord"), not a separate deity
2. Baal was acceptable in royal Yahwistic circles
3. Both

Smith leans toward "both." The name *bĕ'alyāh* ("Yah is Lord") and the seal *ywb'l* ("Yaw is Lord") suggest *ba'al* could function as a title. But the fact that later editors found the names scandalous suggests the situation was more complex than just synonymy.

> "Baal was probably not the threat in the Judges period or the tenth century that later tradents considered him. It was the traumatic events of the ninth century and afterwards that shaped the perspective of the tradents."

This is crucial: **the Baal-Yahweh conflict is real, but it dates to the 9th century (Ahab/Jezebel), not the Judges period.** The Deuteronomists projected ninth-century trauma backward.

## Section 4: Yahweh and Asherah

> **Core claim:** Asherah as a distinct goddess is poorly attested in the Judges period. The symbol (the asherah) was present, but it may have been part of Yahweh's cult, not a separate deity's.

**Smith's caution is striking here.** He argues:
- Asherah does NOT appear as a theophoric element in Hebrew names (unlike El, Baal, and Yahweh)
- Judges 6 focuses on Baal, not Asherah
- The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to the symbol, not necessarily the goddess
- "It may be argued that her symbol was part of the cult of Yahweh in this period, but it did not symbolize a goddess"

### Genesis 49:25 — Possible Goddess Language

Smith discusses the intriguing proposal by Vawter, Freedman, and O'Connor that Genesis 49:25 contains a pair of goddess epithets: *šādayim wārāḥam* ("Breasts-and-Womb"). In Ugaritic texts, *rḥm* ("womb") is a title of Anat and Asherah. The pairing of female epithets with El's male epithets in the same passage would represent an early stage where a goddess (probably Asherah) was invoked alongside El.

**But Smith's conclusion is characteristically cautious:** This language "did not refer to the goddess later in the cult of Yahweh." The female imagery persisted but was no longer attached to a distinct goddess. The Taanach cult stand (10th c.) may show a goddess (Astarte, not Asherah) alongside a bull-deity and an asherah tree — but it's not clear this represents Israelite practice specifically.

## Section 5: Convergence of Divine Imagery

This is the payoff section. Smith demonstrates his convergence model through three poetic texts that **mix El and Baal imagery and apply both to Yahweh.**

### Deuteronomy 33:26-27

> "There is none like God, O Jeshurun, who rides (*rōkēb*) through the heavens to your help... The eternal God (*'ĕlōhê qedem*) is your dwelling place."

**"Rides through the heavens"** = Baal storm language (Baal is the "cloud-rider"). **"Eternal God"** (*'ĕlōhê qedem*) = El language (El is the "father of years," the ancient one). Both applied to Yahweh in adjacent verses.

### Psalm 18 (2 Samuel 22):14-16

> "Yahweh also thundered in the heavens, and the Most High (*'elyôn*) uttered his voice... Then the channels of the sea (*'ăpîqê mayim*) were seen."

**"Thundered in the heavens"** = Baal theophany. **"Most High"** (*'elyôn*) = El's epithet. **"Channels of the sea"** (*'ăpîqê mayim*) echoes the Ugaritic description of El's dwelling at the "springs of the two rivers // channels of the double-deeps."

### Genesis 49:25-26

Combines El, Asherah (possibly), and Baal imagery in a single blessing applied to Yahweh (or to the deity who would later be identified as Yahweh).

> **Smith's conclusion:** "Imagery regularly applied to El and Baal in Northwest Semitic literature was attributed to Yahweh at a relatively early point in Israel's religious history. Moreover, in applying this imagery to Yahweh, these passages combine or conflate the imagery of more than one Canaanite deity."

---

## Section 6: Convergence in Israelite Religion

Smith steps back to reflect on what convergence means as a religious development.

**The pre-monarchic pantheon:** Genesis 49:25-26 possibly suggests three deities: El, Asherah, and Yahweh. Baal adds a fourth. "This situation changed by the period of the early monarchy. Yahweh and El were identified, and at some point, devotion to the goddess Asherah did not continue as an identifiably separate cult."

**But Smith hedges:** "Because of the incomplete picture of this period, perhaps it should be concluded that Israel was more polytheistic in the period of the Judges." He's acknowledging that his reconstruction might be MINIMIZING the polytheism.

**Factors pushing toward monolatry:** The aniconic tradition, the Ten Commandments, polemics against "other gods," and denials of other deities. But these are mostly later developments — Smith describes them as features that "helped to mold ideas of monolatry," not as evidence that monolatry existed in the Judges period.

**The El Shadday example:** The *shadday* epithet was traditional to Transjordan (attested at Deir 'Alla). It was assimilated first as a title of El, then as a title of Yahweh. This shows "the further assimilation of this title into the repertoire of epithets for Yahweh" — a process of absorption that occurred over centuries.

---

## Section 7: Israel and Its Neighbors

> **Core claim:** "Israelite religion in its earliest form did not contrast markedly with the religions of its Levantine neighbors in either number or configuration of deities. Rather, the number of deities in Israel was relatively typical for the region."

Smith surveys Ammon (Milkom, El, ~10 deities), Moab (Chemosh, Ashtar-Chemosh), Edom (Qaws, possibly El and Baal names), and the Phoenician city-states (Byblos, Sidon, Tyre — each with ~8-10 deities). Israel's pantheon of Yahweh, El, Baal, Asherah, and the divine council is unremarkable in context.

**The big picture:** Second-millennium Ugarit had 200+ deities. First-millennium Levantine states had 10 or fewer. Israel was participating in a regional trend toward fewer deities — what would eventually become monotheism was an extreme version of a wider development.

> Smith closes: "During the period of the Judges, Yahweh held hegemony over a complex religion that preserved some old Canaanite components through an identification with El, a continuation of the concepts of the divine council and partially divinized ancestors, a coexistence with Baal, and perhaps an early toleration for Asherah and subsequent assimilation of her cult and symbol, the asherah. **This state of affairs was not to hold for the period of the monarchy.**"

## Key Takeaways from Chapter 1

### What Smith Proves (within his framework)

1. **Cultural continuity is overwhelming.** Script, language, pottery, architecture, burial, cultic terminology — every category of evidence shows Israelite culture derived from Canaanite culture.

2. **El was the original god of Israel.** The name "Israel" and Genesis 49:25-26 point to an El-worshipping group that later adopted Yahweh.

3. **Deuteronomy 32:8-9 preserves the transition.** Yahweh was originally one of El's sons, receiving Israel as his inheritance.

4. **Yahweh absorbed El completely.** El's titles, traits, and cultic sites were transferred to Yahweh. El was never polemicized against — he was simply absorbed.

5. **Baal coexisted with Yahweh in the Judges period.** The conflict was a ninth-century development retrojected backward by later editors.

6. **Asherah's status is ambiguous.** The symbol was present, but whether a distinct goddess cult existed is unprovable with current evidence.

7. **Convergence was the mechanism.** Israel didn't just reject other gods — it absorbed them. Yahweh became the composite of El, Baal, and Asherah's attributes.

### What Smith Acknowledges He Can't Prove

1. The pre-8th century picture is largely conjectural.
2. Israel may have been MORE polytheistic than his reconstruction suggests.
3. The social and political forces driving convergence are largely unknown.
4. Regional variation is invisible in the surviving evidence.
