# Lewis — Chapter 7: The Iconography of Divinity — Yahweh

Lewis examines the tension between the Hebrew Bible's rampant literary anthropomorphism (Yahweh has hands, feet, back, face, nostrils) and its archaeological near-silence on male divine images. He argues against the thesis that an anthropomorphic cult statue of Yahweh stood in the Jerusalem Temple, surveys possible theriomorphic representations (bulls, Bes figures at Kuntillet ʿAjrud), and devotes extensive space to the aniconic and abstract traditions: fire, radiance (_kābôd_), Name theology, and sacred emptiness. The chapter is the book's longest and most detailed, with the aniconic/abstract sections taking up more space than the anthropomorphic/theriomorphic ones — reflecting Lewis's view that abstract representation was the dominant Israelite mode.

## Claim 1: NO anthropomorphic cult statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple
→ [[claim-no-anthropomorphic-yahweh-statue-jerusalem-temple]] | **lewis-ocg-7.1** | Confidence: high
  Nine-point rebuttal of the Niehr/Uehlinger/van der Toorn statue thesis. Strongest arguments: (1) Temple vessel inventories (2 Kgs 25) list pillars, basins, snuffers, lampstands — but no divine image. (2) Lethal-God-sightings tradition is inconsistent with routine statue-viewing. (3) Na'aman: no single object found that can be "unequivocally interpreted as an anthropomorphic cult statue of a god."
  Contradicts: [[claim-yhwh-statues-images-prohibition-late-synthesis]], [[claim-divine-image-prohibition-late-exilic]], [[claim-seals-coins-depict-yhwh-anthropomorphic]].

## Claim 2: Dever's observation stands — no provenanced Iron Age male divine figurines exist
→ [[claim-no-iron-age-male-divine-figurines-demonstrates-aniconism]] | **lewis-ocg-7.2** | Confidence: very-high
  Established archaeological fact: no representations of a male deity in terra cotta, metal, or stone in clear Iron Age contexts. Contrast with hundreds of female Judean pillar figurines eliminates preservation bias. "Some degree of ideological motivation" drove the male absence.
  Supports: [[claim-no-anthropomorphic-yahweh-statue-jerusalem-temple]]. Contradicts: [[claim-seals-coins-depict-yhwh-anthropomorphic]].

## Claim 3: Theriomorphic Yahweh images (bulls, Bes figures) are possible but none definitive
→ [[claim-theriomorphic-yahweh-images-bulls-bes-possible-not-definitive]] | **lewis-ocg-7.3** | Confidence: low
  Kuntillet ʿAjrud Bes figures alongside inscription are the most intriguing but may be from two separate times. Bull Site bronze is "best candidate for theriomorphic El," not Yahweh. Jeroboam's bulls likely El images. Taanach cult stand's empty space suggestive but inconclusive.
  Contradicts: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-cult-object-symbolized-goddess]] (Lewis's caution cuts against both confident identifications).

## Claim 4: Abstract traditions (fire, kābôd, Name, sacred emptiness) are the dominant mode of representing Yahweh
→ [[claim-abstract-traditions-fire-kabod-name-emptiness-dominant-yahweh-mode]] | **lewis-ocg-7.4** | Confidence: high
  Four modes form a coherent typology: fire (burning bush, Sinai, Carmel), kābôd radiance (P, Ezekiel), Name theology (Deuteronomy's šakkēn šēm), sacred emptiness (invisible enthronement on cherubim). Ayn Dara temple footprints show invisible enthronement was a known ANE concept. Not late theological inventions — deep roots in Israelite religion. This is Israel's genuine distinctive contribution.

## Chapter 7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| No anthropomorphic Yahweh statue in Jerusalem Temple | HIGH | Future discovery could overturn; silence arguments are inherently weak |
| Dever's observation still stands — no Iron Age male figurines | VERY HIGH | Wood images wouldn't preserve; new discoveries possible |
| Theriomorphic Yahweh images possible but none definitive | LOW-MEDIUM | Kuntillet ʿAjrud interpretations remain contested |
| Abstract traditions were the dominant mode | HIGH | Popular religion may have differed from textual record |

**Strongest sections:** The rebuttal of the "statue in the Temple" thesis (meticulous, point-by-point, convincing). The typology of abstract traditions (fire/radiance/Name/emptiness) — this is genuinely original synthesis. The discussion of lethal God-sightings as evidence for aniconism (integrating Hendel's anthropological insight).

**Weakest section:** The divine couple discussion (Seyrig seal, Jeremias figurines, Jerusalem potsherd) — this material is fragmentary and inconclusive, and Lewis's treatment reflects that. The section exists more to show he's aware of the arguments than to advance a positive case.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Aniconism is real, ancient, and multifaceted — not just Deuteronomistic propaganda
- Literary anthropomorphism and physical aniconism coexisted without apparent contradiction
- The absence of male divine figurines is a genuine archaeological fact requiring explanation
- Abstract representation (fire, radiance, Name) was Israel's creative solution to the problem of divine presence
- The debate over a Yahweh statue remains polarized, but the weight of evidence favors aniconism

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the necessary complement to Ch5 (El iconography). Where El's iconography is largely defined by what's present (enthroned bronzes, masseboth, bulls), Yahweh's iconography is defined by what's absent. Stavrakopoulou's *God: An Anatomy* is the inverse approach — she emphasizes the rampant anthropomorphism of the texts where Lewis emphasizes the aniconism of the artifacts. The tension is productive: Israelites imagined God with a body but refused to depict that body in physical form.
