# Lewis — Chapter 7: The Iconography of Divinity — Yahweh

Lewis examines the tension between the Hebrew Bible's rampant literary anthropomorphism (Yahweh has hands, feet, back, face, nostrils) and its archaeological near-silence on male divine images. He argues against the thesis that an anthropomorphic cult statue of Yahweh stood in the Jerusalem Temple, surveys possible theriomorphic representations (bulls, Bes figures at Kuntillet ʿAjrud), and devotes extensive space to the aniconic and abstract traditions: fire, radiance (_kābôd_), Name theology, and sacred emptiness. The chapter is the book's longest and most detailed, with the aniconic/abstract sections taking up more space than the anthropomorphic/theriomorphic ones — reflecting Lewis's view that abstract representation was the dominant Israelite mode.

---

## Claim 1: There was NO anthropomorphic cult statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple — the arguments for one do not survive scrutiny

**Lewis's claim:** "It seems more likely that we should take the biblical tradition at face value in its assertion that there was not an anthropomorphic statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple." He systematically rebuts the seven arguments of Niehr, Uehlinger, Becking, van der Toorn, and others who argue for a Yahweh statue.

**Evidence against the statue thesis:** (1) Uniformity is a bad comparative method — Israel was different. (2) Image-ban texts prove images existed, but not necessarily of Yahweh in Jerusalem. (3) "Coalition of concealment" is too sweeping — individual texts must be examined case by case. (4) Anthropomorphic language need not imply a physical statue — metaphors are the "most simple and most plausible explanation" (contra Niehr). (5) "House of Yahweh" doesn't require a resident image — modern houses of worship function without them. (6) The Nimrud prisms' "gods in whom they trusted" is historically suspect — Na'aman shows the author freely manipulated sources; the Khorsabad annals omit the divine image claim. (7) Vessel inventories from the Temple's destruction list pillars, basins, shovels, snuffers, lampstands — but no divine image. "If the divine image of Yahweh was such a preeminent focal point, would not we expect its specific mention?" (8) The lethal-God-sightings tradition (Hendel) makes no sense if people regularly viewed Yahweh's statue. (9) Archaeological evidence: "I cannot find a single object that was found in a cultic site in the kingdoms of Israel or Judah and that could be unequivocally interpreted as an anthropomorphic cult statue of a god" (Na'aman). Mettinger: "Do we have even one three-dimensional representation of Yhwh in metal or terracotta that may be classified as a cultic image? The answer is no."

**Confidence:** HIGH. Lewis makes a compelling cumulative case. The strongest single argument is the absence of ANY mention of a Yahweh image in the exhaustive Temple vessel inventories (2 Kgs 25). The second strongest is the lethal-God-sightings tradition — it's theologically inconsistent with routine statue-viewing.

**What's at stake:** The entire nature of Israelite aniconism. If there was no Yahweh statue, Israelite religion IS genuinely distinctive in the ancient Near East on this point — not a "Deuteronomistic concealment" but a real feature of pre-exilic cult. If the statue-thesis is right, the aniconic tradition is late theological propaganda.

**Who disagrees:** Niehr, Uehlinger, Becking, van der Toorn, Berlejung, Dietrich and Loretz — a who's who of continental scholarship. This is one of the most polarized debates in the field.

**Alternative reading:** There may have been a statue in the First Temple that was removed or destroyed before the Deuteronomistic editors got to the text. The silence is the product of successful erasure, not absence. The Arad massebah shows aniconism in the provinces — but Jerusalem, as the royal capital, may have been different.

**My assessment:** Lewis wins this debate on current evidence. The burden of proof is on those claiming a Yahweh statue, and they haven't met it. The vessel inventory argument is especially telling — Nebuchadnezzar's looters catalogued bronze snuffers but not a divine statue? The statue-thesis requires special pleading.

## Claim 2: Dever's 1983 observation still stands — no provenanced Iron Age male divine figurines exist, and the absence demands explanation

**Lewis's claim:** Dever's statement that "no representations of a male deity in terra cotta, metal, or stone have ever been found in clear Iron Age contexts" has "become the starting point for all scholars wrestling with the iconic/aniconic debate." The two exceptions Dever noted (Hazor bronze, Gezer stick figure) have been eliminated. The absence is particularly striking given the abundance of female figurines and the wealth of Late Bronze Age male figurines.

**Evidence:** All the striding/smiting male figurines (Megiddo, Tell Balâṭah) are Late Bronze or transitional LB/Iron I. The contrast with female figurines (hundreds of Judean pillar figurines) is stark. The economic argument (precious metals were melted down) can't explain the absence in terra-cotta. "That we have female divine figures in terra-cotta but not male ones suggests some degree of ideological motivation driving the latter's absence." Albertz and Schmitt suggest this is a broader trend — aniconism for major national deities across the southern Levant.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the absence of provenanced Iron Age male divine figurines. MEDIUM-HIGH on the ideological explanation — it makes sense but can't be definitively proven.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite aniconism was real practice or just elite rhetoric. The archaeological silence on male figurines is the strongest evidence that aniconism affected actual cult, not just theology.

**Who disagrees:** Uehlinger argues his catalogue of data is "definitely sufficient to make the hypothesis [of an anthropomorphic divine image of Yahweh] more reasonable and probable than its opposite." But he also admits "we are still unable to identify beyond a doubt a Yahweh image as such."

**Alternative reading:** Wooden images — which the image-ban texts frequently mention — wouldn't survive archaeologically. The absence could be a preservation bias rather than evidence of aniconism. But this doesn't explain the terra-cotta gap.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most important archaeological facts about Israelite religion, and Lewis handles it well. The contrast with female figurines is the key point — if all divine images were simply not preserved, we'd expect female ones to be missing too. They're not. Something specific was going on with male deity representation.

## Claim 3: Theriomorphic representations — especially the Kuntillet ʿAjrud Bes figures, the Bull Site bronze, and Jeroboam's bulls — are the most likely candidates for Yahweh images, but none is definitive

**Lewis's claim:** Yahweh may have been represented theriomorphically — through bull imagery (Jeroboam's calves, the Bull Site bronze, the Taanach cult stand) and through Bes/Bes-like figures (Kuntillet ʿAjrud pithos A). The Kuntillet ʿAjrud standing Bes figures are particularly intriguing because they appear alongside the "Yahweh of Samaria and his asherah" inscription. But Lewis is cautious: none of these can be definitively identified as Yahweh.

**Evidence:** The Kuntillet ʿAjrud pithos A has three figures (two standing Bes-like, one seated lyre player) alongside the inscription. Some scholars identify the Bes figures as representing Yahweh and the seated figure as Asherah. Lewis is skeptical: "There is no need to equate the drawings with the inscription, especially if they come from two separate times, as seems likely." The Bull Site bronze zebu bull is "the best candidate for a theriomorphic representation of Israelite El" — not necessarily Yahweh. Jeroboam's bulls at Bethel and Dan (1 Kgs 12:28-33) are more likely El images than Yahweh images (as argued in Ch5). The Taanach cult stand's bull calf and empty space (for an invisible deity?) is suggestive but inconclusive. The bull-headed warrior stela from Bethsaida is likely the Aramean moon god, not Yahweh.

**Confidence:** LOW on any specific theriomorphic Yahweh image. MEDIUM that Yahweh was sometimes represented theriomorphically.

**What's at stake:** The nature of popular Yahwism. If Yahweh was represented by bulls and Bes figures at sites like Kuntillet ʿAjrud, then "aniconic Yahwism" was an elite/Jerusalemite preference, not a universal Israelite practice.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who identify the Kuntillet ʿAjrud Bes figures as Yahweh and the seated lyre player as Asherah (e.g., Dever, Hadley). Scholars who see Jeroboam's bulls as Baal images rather than El or Yahweh.

**My assessment:** Lewis is appropriately cautious. The Kuntillet ʿAjrud iconography is genuinely ambiguous. His refusal to force identifications is methodologically correct — but also means the theriomorphic evidence remains tantalizing rather than probative.

## Claim 4: The aniconic and abstract traditions (fire, kābôd radiance, Name theology, sacred emptiness) represent the dominant mode of representing Yahweh — and this is Israel's genuine distinctive contribution

**Lewis's claim:** The abstract traditions — Yahweh represented by fire (burning bush, Sinai theophany, Elijah on Carmel), by radiance/glory (_kābôd_ in P and Ezekiel), by his Name (_šēm_ in Deuteronomy/DtrH), and by sacred emptiness (invisible enthronement on cherubim) — represent "the dominant mode of representing Yahweh." These traditions are not late theological inventions but have deep roots in Israelite religion.

**Evidence:** Fire traditions: Moses' burning bush (Exod 3), Sinai theophany (Exod 19), pillar of fire (Exod 13:21), Elijah's Carmel contest (1 Kgs 18), fiery anthropomorphisms (Yahweh's mouth breathes fire, nostrils snort smoke). Kābôd traditions: P's tabernacle narrative (Exod 40:34-38), Ezekiel's elaborate vision (Ezek 1), Isaiah's temple vision (Isa 6). Name theology: Deuteronomy's insistence that Yahweh causes his Name to dwell (_šakkēn šēm_) in the chosen place (Deut 12:5, 11; 14:23; 16:2, 6, 11), replacing the notion of Yahweh himself dwelling there. The Name "resides" in a way that avoids embodied limitation. Sacred emptiness: Yahweh "enthroned upon the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4; 2 Sam 6:2) but invisibly — the cherubim throne is empty. The Ayn Dara temple in Syria has gigantic footprints leading into the inner sanctum — a physical representation of an invisible deity.

**Confidence:** HIGH that abstract traditions existed and were widespread. MEDIUM-HIGH that they represent the dominant mode — they dominate the textual evidence, but popular practice may have differed.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite aniconism was a genuine innovation or just retroactive theological spin. Lewis argues it was real, ancient, and multifaceted — not a single Deuteronomistic party line but multiple independent traditions converging on the idea that Yahweh shouldn't be depicted physically.

**Who disagrees:** The "statue in the Temple" school (Niehr, Uehlinger) sees these abstract traditions as late apologetics covering up earlier iconic practice. Stavrakopoulou emphasizes the radical anthropomorphism of the texts and sees the aniconic traditions as one voice among many.

**Alternative reading:** The abstract traditions weren't dominant in practice — they dominated the SURVIVING texts because those texts were edited by aniconic parties. Popular religion may have been more iconic than our sources admit.

**My assessment:** Lewis's treatment of the abstract traditions is the most valuable part of this chapter. The four modes (fire, kābôd, Name, emptiness) form a coherent typology that shows aniconism wasn't one thing — it was multiple creative solutions to the problem of representing an invisible god. The Ayn Dara footprints are a particularly striking comparator: Israel wasn't alone in imagining invisible deity, but it developed the idea further than anyone else.

## Chapter 7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| No anthropomorphic Yahweh statue in Jerusalem Temple | HIGH | Future discovery could overturn; silence arguments are inherently weak |
| Dever's observation still stands — no Iron Age male figurines | VERY HIGH | Wood images wouldn't preserve; new discoveries possible |
| Theriomorphic Yahweh images possible but none definitive | LOW-MEDIUM | Kuntillet ʿAjrud interpretations remain contested |
| Abstract traditions were the dominant mode | HIGH | Popular religion may have differed from textual record |

**Strongest sections:** The rebuttal of the "statue in the Temple" thesis (meticulous, point-by-point, convincing). The typology of abstract traditions (fire/radiance/Name/emptiness) — this is genuinely original synthesis. The discussion of lethal God-sightings as evidence for aniconism (integrating Hendel's anthropological insight).

**Weakest section:** The divine couple discussion (Seyrig seal, Jeremias figurines, Jerusalem potsherd) — this material is fragmentary and inconclusive, and Lewis's treatment reflects that. The section exists more to show he's aware of the arguments than to advance a positive case.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Aniconism is real, ancient, and multifaceted — not just Deuteronomistic propaganda
- Literary anthropomorphism and physical aniconism coexisted without apparent contradiction
- The absence of male divine figurines is a genuine archaeological fact requiring explanation
- Abstract representation (fire, radiance, Name) was Israel's creative solution to the problem of divine presence
- The debate over a Yahweh statue remains polarized, but the weight of evidence favors aniconism

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the necessary complement to Ch5 (El iconography). Where El's iconography is largely defined by what's present (enthroned bronzes, masseboth, bulls), Yahweh's iconography is defined by what's absent. Stavrakopoulou's *God: An Anatomy* is the inverse approach — she emphasizes the rampant anthropomorphism of the texts where Lewis emphasizes the aniconism of the artifacts. The tension is productive: Israelites imagined God with a body but refused to depict that body in physical form.
