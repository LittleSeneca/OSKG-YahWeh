# Keel & Uehlinger — Summary and Conclusion

## Word and Picture, Focal Points, Open Questions, Theological Perspectives

The concluding chapter synthesizes the 1,300-year iconographic survey into a coherent narrative arc and reflects on the relationship between word and image in the study of ancient Israelite religion. It also identifies open questions and offers theological perspectives — rare in a work of this archaeological rigor.

---

## Claim 1: Names are conventional and provide limited information about a deity's essence — images capture the functional "constellation" that names obscure

**Authors' claim:** "The name identifies the male or female who is addressed in the cult, in hymns, and in petitions... But, like everything linguistic, names are artificial, as a rule... Names give only general information, or none at all, about the essence of the deity addressed and worshiped."

The book's final methodological reflection: names (Asherah, Baal, Yahweh) are poor guides to what a deity actually was. "Yahweh" may etymologically mean "he blows" (Knauf 1984), but "no one would want to maintain that this etymology... expresses the dominant aspect of the God of Israel." Images capture the deity's functional "constellation" — what the deity DID, how the deity related to other powers, what role the deity played in the symbol system.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Egyptian tree goddess appears from the 18th through 21st Dynasties: same image, same function — but different names (Nut, Neith, Isis, Ma'at)
- "What did 'Asherah' mean? Did the term always mean the same thing? If so, what: a natural tree, an artificial tree, a pole, an anthropomorphic image?... It is quite improbable that names like 'Asherah,' 'Baal,' or 'Yahweh' always referred to the same singular reality or concept"
- A good identity card combines word AND picture — the picture reproduces the specific "physiognomic constellation," the name classifies and individualizes

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the methodological capstone of the entire project.

**What's at stake:** This justifies the entire iconographic approach. If names are unreliable guides to divine identity, then text-only reconstructions of Israelite religion are building on sand. The iconographic "constellation" approach is not just supplementary — it's methodologically more fundamental.

**Who disagrees:** Text-centric scholars who privilege names and narratives over images. But Keel & Uehlinger's Egyptian example (same goddess, different names, same iconography) is hard to dispute.

**My assessment:** This is the most important methodological claim in the book, and it's placed in the conclusion for a reason. The entire Asherah debate — goddess or symbol? — is partly a problem of asking the wrong question. The name "Asherah" attached to different realities at different times. The iconography shows us WHAT she was (tree, blessing entity, mother goddess) in ways the name alone cannot.

## Claim 2: The 1,300-year arc: from erotic divine couples to aniconic solar/lunar Yahwism to text-centered orthodoxy

**Authors' summary of the developmental arc:**

*Middle Bronze Age IIB (ca. 1800-1550)*: "Interaction of the Sexes." Goddesses dominant, often naked with emphasized gender characteristics. The weather god and goddess interact to produce fertility. Warlike aspects subordinate. The "Canaanite religion" of biblical polemic has its clearest iconographic expression here.

*Late Bronze Age (ca. 1550-1250/1150)*: "Political and Warrior Aspects take the Upper Hand." Egyptian colonialism reshapes the iconography. The naked erotic goddess retreats, replaced by the tree. She becomes clothed, associated with celestial/warlike elements. Young warriors, chariots, and the striking pharaoh dominate. "Women assume the role of servants." The goddess is demoted — transferred to cheaper terra-cotta media.

*Iron Age I (ca. 1250/1150-1000)*: "The Hidden God." Amun becomes invisible (present only as cryptogram). Deities in the foreground are warriors (Seth-Baal, Reshef). The goddess appears only through substitute symbols (tree, nursing mother animal). Domination is the determinative theme.

*Iron Age IIA (ca. 1000-925)*: "Breaking Through to a New Era." Anthropomorphic deities very rare. Substitute entities (horse, branch, suckling animal, worshiper) replace the goddess. The tree loses its goddess association and becomes a symbol of kingship. "All the decoration in the Solomonic temple is predicated upon the idea that this is the place of divine order and blessing, but the motifs are not gender specific or else are associated with a 'male' royal god."

*Iron Age IIB (ca. 925-720/700)*: "The Power of Blessing in the Service of 'Male' Deities and Royalty." Solar symbolism dominant. The tree is guarded by griffins and cherubs — connected with kingship. Only at Kuntillet Ajrud do we find transparent goddess references (the tree, nursing cow). "Guardian lions... no longer have anything to do with the goddess."

*Iron Age IIC (ca. 720-600)*: Astralization, goddess revival in pillar figurines, and orthodox aniconic reaction compete simultaneously. The "Queen of Heaven" represents a repersonalized Asherah. Elite seals become image-free. Text begins to replace image (Ketef Hinnom amulets).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The arc is documented across 8,500 seals and countless other artifacts.

**What's at stake:** This is the master narrative. The development is not: polytheism → monotheism. It's: erotic divine couples → warrior gods → hidden high god → solarized Yahweh → goddess revival + aniconic orthodoxy → text-centered religion. The path to biblical monotheism was not a straight line.

**My assessment:** The arc is convincing because it's not a theory imposed on the data — it emerged from cataloguing 8,500 seals period by period. The most striking finding is how EARLY the aniconic turn begins (Iron IIA) and how LATE the goddess revival occurs (Iron IIC). The biblical narrative (preexilic polytheism → exilic monotheism) is wrong in both directions: aniconism is older, and goddess worship is more recent, than the Bible suggests.

## Claim 3: The relationship between Yahweh and Asherah was never a divine couple — but the goddess did come back

**Authors' claim:** Throughout the entire Iron Age, "we have not been able so far to find any solid evidence of any partner relationship in the sources."

The book's verdict on the central question: the iconographic evidence from 8,500 seals, hundreds of figurines, and dozens of inscriptions shows NO divine couples in Iron Age Israel/Judah. The asherah was a cultic symbol subordinate to Yahweh (Iron IIB), and when she was repersonalized as a goddess in Iron IIC, she was worshiped independently (as "Queen of Heaven"), not as Yahweh's consort.

**Evidence presented (cumulative across the book):**
- MB IIB: divine couples depicted (weather god + naked goddess) — the "Canaanite" pattern
- LB: couples become rare; goddess retreats
- Iron I-IIA: no couples; goddess becomes transparent (tree)
- Iron IIB: "his asherah" = cultic symbol, not goddess; NO pair imagery
- Iron IIC: pillar figurines = goddess revival; but still NO divine couples in Judahite iconography
- "If Yahweh did at some time have a partner, it would probably have been at the time of Manasseh" — and even then, the evidence is absent

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The absence of divine couple imagery is well-documented. But the functional reality may have differed from the iconographic record — people may have experienced the asherah/pillar goddess AS Yahweh's partner even if official iconography didn't depict them as a couple.

**What's at stake:** The popular "Yahweh had a wife" narrative is iconographically unsupported for Iron Age Israel/Judah. Yahweh had a cultic symbol that mediated his blessing (Iron IIB) and there was a powerful mother goddess worshiped in domestic contexts (Iron IIC). But the two were never visually paired. The "wife" language imports a human marriage category into a divine relationship that was more complex.

**My assessment:** This is the book's most important and most controversial conclusion. I find it convincing on the evidence but I think Keel & Uehlinger understate the experiential reality. If you're a Judean woman in the 7th century praying to a pillar figurine for fertility, and you also pray to Yahweh for protection, the relationship between those two divine powers may FEEL like a divine couple regardless of whether seals depict them together. The iconographic record shows us the OFFICIAL symbol system. Folk religion may have operated differently.

## Claim 4: Open questions — the book's honest acknowledgment of what remains unknown

**Authors' list of open questions:**
- The exact relationship of Yahweh to the solar and lunar characteristics he absorbed: was Yahweh identified WITH the sun/moon, or did he merely acquire their attributes?
- The degree to which Assyrian religious policy actively promoted astral cults vs. the degree to which local populations adopted them voluntarily
- The function of the horse-and-rider figurines — "Host of Heaven" or something else?
- The relationship between official state religion and family/private piety — how much overlap, how much tension?
- The dating and interpretation of the rosette stamps (Josiah's administrative seals)
- Whether the aniconic tendency was primarily theological or primarily practical/administrative

**Confidence:** N/A — these are questions, not claims.

**What's at stake:** The honest acknowledgment of uncertainty is what separates Keel & Uehlinger from more polemical scholars. They're not trying to WIN an argument — they're trying to establish what the evidence can and cannot support. This is methodological integrity at its best.

**My assessment:** The open questions are well-chosen. The solar/lunar Yahweh question is particularly important — did Yahweh BECOME the sun god, or did he just borrow solar imagery? The evidence supports borrowing (the solar motifs are Egyptian, not indigenous), but the line between borrowing and identification is blurry in ancient religion.

## Claim 5: Theological perspective — the aniconic turn enabled universal monotheism

**Authors' theological reflection:** "It remains to be considered whether the shape of the head supports in the graves of Ketef Hinnom... might lead one to consider whether the Q-shaped symbol that can characterize the shape of the womb... might be a late remnant of the idea that the earth is symbolically the mother's womb."

The book ends with a surprisingly theological coda. The aniconic turn, the replacement of goddess figurines with text amulets, and the "shining of Yahweh's countenance" replacing the asherah's blessing — all these represent a genuine religious transformation. The motherly Asherah is "far removed from the piety documented in this grave." But the Q-shaped head supports in Jerusalem tombs may preserve "a dim reflection of a mythological theme, used now in the context of a piety that is decidedly unmythological."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Ketef Hinnom graves: no terra-cottas of doves, goddesses, or riders — only text amulets and a few Egyptian/Phoenician protective pendants
- Yahweh's "shining countenance" replaces the asherah's blessing — but "Yahweh's own 'countenance' now appears in the place of the countenance of the asherah"
- The Q-shaped head supports in Jerusalem tombs may be "a late remnant of the idea that the earth is symbolically the mother's womb" (Job 1:21, Ps 139:15) — the goddess has been absorbed into the earth itself
- "The blessing of Yahweh essentially amounted to something more than social prestige even in preexilic times. It could mean hope for those who had died"

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM for the head support interpretation (Keel & Uehlinger themselves are tentative). MEDIUM for the theological trajectory.

**What's at stake:** The aniconic turn was not just an artistic preference. It was a theological transformation: the personal, embodied, gendered divine presence (goddess with breasts, god with phallus) was replaced by an invisible, universal, text-mediated divine presence. This made monotheism possible — a God who cannot be seen can be everywhere. But it also made the divine less accessible, less embodied, less female.

**My assessment:** This theological coda is brief but profound. The entire 1,300-year arc culminates in a paradox: the triumph of aniconic Yahwism made universal monotheism possible, but it also erased the female divine from the official symbol system. The goddess didn't die — the pillar figurines show she was more popular than ever in the 7th century. She was suppressed. And the suppression was successful enough that by the time the Hebrew Bible was finalized, she had become a tree, a pole, a polemical target — everything except what she had been for 1,300 years: a goddess.

## Book-Level Assessment

**What Keel & Uehlinger achieved:** The first comprehensive diachronic survey of Israelite/Palestinian religion based on the full iconographic record — 8,500 seals, hundreds of figurines, dozens of cult stands, ivories, and amulets. This is the evidence base that text-centric scholars (Smith, Cross, Day) were missing. It is the archaeological counterpart to Dever's work — more systematic, less polemical, and with better chronological control.

**What shifts for the Truth Project:**
1. **Smith vindicated on Asherah as cult symbol.** The grammatical argument (no possessive suffix on proper names) is supported by the iconographic evidence Smith never used. "His asherah" = cultic symbol.
2. **Dever partially corrected.** The pillar figurines ARE evidence of goddess worship — but in the 7th century (Iron IIC), not the 9th-8th (Iron IIB). Dever's dating was wrong; his interpretive instinct (these matter) was right.
3. **Solar and lunar Yahwism are the missing links.** The absorption of Egyptian solar theology (Iron IIB) and Mesopotamian lunar theology (Iron IIC) into Yahwism is the developmental mechanism that Smith's convergence thesis describes but cannot ground in material evidence. Keel & Uehlinger ground it.
4. **The aniconic turn begins centuries earlier than the exile.** Iron IIA already shows the decline of anthropomorphic deities. Iron IIC shows the triumph of image-free seals among the elite. The "missing statue" was not removed — it was never there.
5. **Monotheism was not a revolution but a gradual, contested process.** The simultaneous existence of pillar figurine piety, astral cults, and aniconic Yahwism in 7th-century Judah shows that "monotheism" was one option among several — and not the most popular one. It won because its adherents survived the exile and wrote the history.

**The book's limitation:** The focus on iconography means Keel & Uehlinger cannot fully address the experiential dimension of religion. We see what people MADE and WORE — not what they FELT or BELIEVED. A pillar figurine in a 7th-century house tells us the goddess was present. It doesn't tell us whether the woman who held it thought of the goddess as Yahweh's wife, his subordinate, or an independent power. The iconographic record is the most objective evidence we have. But religion is always more than its material remains.
