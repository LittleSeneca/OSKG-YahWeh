# Lewis — Chapter 3: Methodology

Lewis lays out the disciplines needed to reconstruct ancient Israelite religion, surveys the nature of the source material (texts, material culture, ancient Near Eastern comparanda), and articulates his methodological commitments. The chapter's most distinctive move is its adoption of the "Propp Principle" — radical open-mindedness in the face of extremely limited data — which gives Lewis's entire project a tone of measured agnosticism that distinguishes it from more confident syntheses like Smith's or Cross's.

---

## Claim 1: The definition of "religion" is a perennial problem with no solution — and the two most important modern developments are (a) recognizing internal religious pluralism and (b) focusing on non-elite religion

**Lewis's claim:** "No field ever moves forward because a good number of people agree on the definition of some central concept" (quoting Catherine Bell). He identifies two "notable developments": Albertz's "internal religious pluralism" (royal vs. household religion; Zevit's plural "religions") and the post-Annales turn toward non-elite communities — "kitchen religion" in Saul Bellow's phrase — that had been "slighted in the past in favor of the religion of the privileged."

**Evidence:** Lewis cites Albertz (1978, 1994), Zevit (2001 — note the plural "Religions"), Stavrakopoulou and Barton (2010 — "Religious Diversity"), and the feminist archaeological work of Meyers. He quotes Bell dismissing definition-hunting as unproductive. He also cites the failure of earlier scholars to find a single essence of sacrifice — quoting Evans-Pritchard (14+ motivations), Bourdillon ("any general theory of sacrifice is bound to fail"), Hendel, and Milgrom.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on pluralism and non-elite focus as major developments. MEDIUM on whether defining religion is truly pointless — useful working definitions exist; the problem is insisting on ONE definition.

**What's at stake:** If Lewis is right, then any book on "Israelite religion" (singular) is already misleading. His own subtitle uses the singular but he insists it's used "collectively." The real question is whether his own book succeeds at representing pluralism or defaults to synthesizing elite Yahwism.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a working definition of religion (e.g., Dever's "belief in and response to a supernatural power"). Confessional scholars who see Israelite religion as fundamentally unified despite surface diversity.

**My assessment:** Lewis's pluralism is methodologically sound and aligns with the best recent work (Smith, Stavrakopoulou, Dever). The tension between this commitment and his focus on El and Yahweh (two elite male deities) will be a recurring theme throughout the book.

## Claim 2: "Israelite" is a heuristic umbrella term — Israel and Judah had genuinely different religions, but our textual sources (filtered through Judahite editors) make them impossible to cleanly separate

**Lewis's claim:** "By following the convention of using the name 'Israelite' as an umbrella term, I do not mean to homogenize distinctly different Israelite and Judean religions into a unified whole. The impossibility of so doing should be obvious." But he adds: "Our textual sources are a nightmare for the historian of religion who thinks she/he can separate northern/Israelite traditions from texts... that have been transmitted, reimagined, and even reworked by southern/Judean hands."

**Evidence:** Lewis quotes Daniel Fleming: "All of [the Bible's] contents have been filtered through Judahite assumptions." He notes Schniedewind's sociolinguistic argument that northern dialect features were "collected, edited, and [re-]written in Jerusalem and Judah" using standardized Judean Hebrew. He cites Fleming's 2012 attempt to recover "the legacy of Israel in Judah's Bible" but calls the methodological hurdles "high."

**Confidence:** HIGH that the term is heuristic. HIGH that northern and southern religions differed. MEDIUM-HIGH that they can't be separated at all — some scholars (Fleming, Römer) have made progress.

**What's at stake:** Whether we can ever know what northern Israelite religion looked like, or whether everything we think we know is actually Judahite retrojection. This matters enormously for questions like: Did the northern kingdom worship El separately from Yahweh? Was the golden calf at Bethel an El symbol or a Yahweh symbol?

**Who disagrees:** Fleming would say more can be recovered than Lewis admits. Römer uses the Elijah-Elisha cycles and Hosea to reconstruct northern Yahwism. The maximalist school would argue the biblical picture of northern apostasy is basically accurate.

**My assessment:** Lewis is appropriately cautious. The default assumption should be that our picture of "Israelite" religion is Judah-centric. His chapters on El (4-5) will need to wrestle with whether El traditions are northern or pan-Israelite.

## Claim 3: Epigraphy has "rewritten the history of ancient Israelite religion" — it provides dated, non-biblical evidence that is more reliable than the Hebrew Bible's heavily edited text

**Lewis's claim:** "The field of epigraphy has rewritten the history of ancient Israelite religion... The simple fact alone that the earliest attestation of the divine name Yahweh comes from the ninth-century BCE Moabite inscription of King Mesha underscores the need for historians of Israelite religion to control the Northwest Semitic epigraphic record." He also notes that Zevit argues "Israelite religion is most approachable through its manifestations in physical evidence discovered in archaeological excavations [including] inscriptions and drawings."

**Evidence:** Lewis runs through the epigraphic evidence for El (Proto-Sinaitic, Ugarit, Deir ʿAlla, Kuntillet ʿAjrud), El Elyon (Sefire, Karatepe, Philo of Byblos), El Shaddai (Ugarit, Deir ʿAlla), Baal, Yahweh (Shasu, Mesha, Kuntillet ʿAjrud, Khirbet el-Qom, Ketef Hinnom, Arad, Lachish), and Yahweh as Holy One (Kuntillet ʿAjrud). He also notes the ability to date inscriptions using archaeological context and script typology, in contrast to biblical texts where dating is fiercely contested.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the importance of epigraphy. HIGH on specific readings — though Lewis himself notes in Ch. 4 that even leading epigraphists (including his teacher Cross) have made unsustainable readings.

**What's at stake:** If epigraphy is primary, biblical texts become secondary — contextualized and corrected by inscriptions rather than the other way around. This inverts the traditional hierarchy of evidence.

**Who disagrees:** Biblical maximalists who trust the Hebrew Bible's historical framework. Literary critics who see the biblical text as self-contained and not needing external corroboration.

**Alternative reading:** Epigraphy and biblical texts are complementary, not hierarchical. Each has strengths (epigraphy: date, provenance; biblical texts: narrative context, theological depth). The best scholarship uses both.

**My assessment:** Lewis's epigraphic emphasis is one of the book's greatest strengths. Chapters 4-7 will be thick with epigraphic data, and this is where Lewis (as a Cross student with Northwest Semitic training) has a genuine comparative advantage over scholars like Römer or Smith who are more text-centric.

## Claim 4: Albertz revolutionized onomastics by showing that personal names reflect personal piety, not state religion — and this shift is as important as any methodological development in the field

**Lewis's claim:** Albertz "argued that [questions about pantheon reduction and Yahweh's dominance] were sorely misguided, as they largely ignored the social context of personal names." Instead, "the verbs and nouns in the large majority of personal names were expressions of personal piety. Rather than reflecting matters of state and priestly cult, the petitions, thanksgivings, hopes for salvation, confessions, and concerns about the birth process that are found in names had more to do with personal religion and life cycle events."

**Evidence:** Lewis cites Albertz's three defining works (1978, 1994, 2012 with Schmitt). He also cites Tigay's cautions: onomastic habits change slowly, so names can persist after devotion shifts; political pressure can produce Yahwistic names for polytheistic families (e.g., Ahab's children). Pardee's genre-specific analysis shows different divine names appear in different text types — mythological vs. ritual vs. onomastic. Lewis notes that "Asherah" appears only once as a theophoric element in Ugaritic personal names despite a vibrant cult attested in other genres.

**Confidence:** HIGH that Albertz's approach is methodologically superior to simple distribution-counting. MEDIUM-HIGH on how much we can actually recover — the cautions from Tigay and Pardee are significant.

**What's at stake:** If Albertz is right, onomastics tell us about family religion, not about what percentage of Israelites were Yahwists. The long-standing debate about whether Israelites were mostly polytheistic or mostly Yahwistic (Tigay vs. Dever, essentially) changes shape: names answer a different question than the one most scholars have been asking.

**Who disagrees:** Tigay (who Lewis respects and cites) would say onomastics CAN be used for distribution analysis if properly controlled. Hess has used onomastics to argue for widespread Yahweh worship. The debate is about controls, not about whether names contain religious information.

**My assessment:** This is genuinely important. Lewis's treatment of onomastics is more sophisticated than most comparable works (Dever's, for instance, uses onomastics more crudely). The Albertz-Pardee-Tigay triangulation is the right framework.

## Claim 5: The "Propp Principle" — we have at most 5% of the data, and parsimonious reconstructions are usually "a failure of imagination, not a triumph of induction"

**Lewis's claim:** Lewis endorses William Propp's argument that "we are obliged to adopt as provisional truth the most likely and parsimonious reconstruction" is a "pernicious" scholarly axiom. Propp argues: "We can and must take into account the 95 percent of information hidden from our view, the sea bottom connecting solitary islands of data. The only sensible response to fragmented, slowly but randomly accruing evidence is radical open-mindedness."

**Evidence:** Lewis gives examples: the Hebrew Bible is silent on incantations, yet Ketef Hinnom amulets prove they were used. The Pentateuch and Deuteronomistic History are silent on cultic singers, yet they were "a constituent feature of temple worship throughout the ancient Near East" (Knoppers). Statistical arguments about word distribution assume near-complete datasets when we have "a very small dataset."

**Confidence:** HIGH that our dataset is extremely limited. MEDIUM on whether parsimony is "pernicious" — Occam's razor exists for a reason, and "radical open-mindedness" can shade into "anything is possible."

**What's at stake:** If the Propp Principle is right, most confident historical reconstructions in the field are overconfident. This includes much of what Lewis himself argues in Chapters 4-10. There's a productive tension between Lewis's endorsement of Propp and his willingness to make positive claims.

**Who disagrees:** Smith, Cross, and Dever all make confident reconstructions that Propp would call "failures of imagination." The minimalist school (Thompson, Lemche) would say Propp doesn't go far enough — if we have 5%, we should be radically skeptical, not just open-minded.

**My assessment:** This is the most intellectually honest passage in the book so far. Lewis is essentially saying: "I'm going to argue for X, but I could be wrong, and here's why." That's the right tone for a reference work. The question is whether he maintains this epistemic humility in the substantive chapters or defaults to confident assertion.

## Claim 6: The Hebrew Bible IS an ancient Near Eastern text — treating it as separate from its cultural environment is a fundamental category error

**Lewis's claim:** Quoting Peter Machinist: "a phrase such as 'the Bible and the ancient Near East' assumes that the former was not a part of the latter." Lewis argues that "the Hebrew Bible is at its core an ancient Near Eastern text that emerged out of an ancient Near Eastern world" and that this obvious point is "embraced" far less than it should be, especially in religious institutions that require "less and less Hebrew and Aramaic of their students."

**Evidence:** Lewis notes that even within secular universities, post-historical-critical approaches (feminist, post-colonial) have grown at the expense of studying the Hebrew Bible as an ancient Near Eastern production. He's not arguing against those approaches — he says they're "important" — but insisting they shouldn't come "at the expense of" contextual study.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is definitionally true. The Hebrew Bible was written in Hebrew and Aramaic by people living in Iron Age Israel and Judah, using literary conventions shared with Ugaritic, Akkadian, and Egyptian literature.

**What's at stake:** Whether the Hebrew Bible should be read primarily as Scripture (for communities of faith) or as an ancient artifact (for historians). Lewis is clearly on the artifact side, but he's generous enough to acknowledge that both approaches have value.

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars who read the Bible primarily as divine revelation that transcends its cultural context. Kaufmann would be the extreme example. More moderately, scholars like Sommer argue for reading the Bible both ways simultaneously.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right and this should be uncontroversial. But it's not — which tells you something about the state of the field. His point about declining language requirements in seminaries and religious studies departments is well-taken and depressing.

## Claim 7: Modern comparative method has moved past "parallelomania" and "trait-list" distinctiveness — the real work is in studying configurations of traits

**Lewis's claim:** Today's comparativists follow Talmon: "our concern should be with difference as much as with likenesses." They avoid Sandmel's "parallelomania" (overdoing supposed similarities) AND Kaufmann's categorical distinctiveness. Instead, following Machinist, they study "configurations of traits and ideas to understand the distinctiveness of ancient Israel — or, for that matter, any culture." The goal is not to find a single trait present in Israel but absent elsewhere, but to see how Israel combined and reconfigured traits shared across the ancient Near East.

**Evidence:** Lewis cites Talmon (1978), Sandmel (1962), and Machinist (1991). He notes that "establishing traits is an elusive business" because "as the increasing volume of archaeological discoveries makes clear, some correspondence always seems to be waiting to be found somewhere in the ancient Near East." He also cites the shared pattern of divine identity formation: Tyre chose Melqart, Sidon chose Eshmun, Moab chose Chemosh, Edom chose Qos, Ammon chose Milcom, Israel chose Yahweh. The pattern is shared; the specific god is contingent.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is the consensus position in the field.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israel is "unique" or "just another ancient Near Eastern culture." Lewis's answer: both. The configuration is distinctive; the individual traits are not.

**Who disagrees:** Only the extremes. Pan-Babylonianists (everything comes from Mesopotamia) and Kaufmann-style apologists (nothing does). Everyone else operates within this framework, even if they emphasize continuity (Smith) or distinctiveness (Cross) more.

**My assessment:** This is a mature methodological statement. It's also a corrective to how Cross is sometimes read — Lewis shows that Cross himself was arguing for both continuity AND novelty, not just continuity. The "configuration" approach is genuinely useful and avoids the sterile "was Israel unique?" debate.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Defining religion is impossible; pluralism and non-elite focus are key | VERY HIGH | Some working definition is still needed for operational purposes |
| "Israelite" is heuristic — Israel/Judah can't be cleanly separated | HIGH | Fleming and others are making progress on recovery |
| Epigraphy has rewritten the field | VERY HIGH | Forgeries and limited corpus constrain conclusions |
| Albertz's onomastic revolution | HIGH | Tigay/Pardee cautions limit what onomastics can tell us |
| Propp Principle — 5% data, radical open-mindedness | HIGH | Can shade into excessive agnosticism; parsimony has genuine value |
| Hebrew Bible IS an ancient Near Eastern text | VERY HIGH | Confessional approaches disagree but on different grounds |
| Modern comparative method: configurations, not traits | VERY HIGH | This is consensus; the debate is about specific configurations |

**Strongest section:** The Propp Principle discussion (lines 740-752). This is the intellectual core of Lewis's methodology. He's essentially arguing that most scholarship in the field is overconfident, and he's right. The question is whether he can maintain this epistemic humility in practice.

**Weakest section:** The treatment of archaeology is surprisingly thin (lines 762-770). Lewis essentially says "Zevit already covered sacred architecture, so I won't" and defers to colleagues. For a book that promises to integrate text and material culture, this is a significant gap. Dever would say this is exactly the problem — text scholars claim to integrate archaeology but then relegate it to a few paragraphs.

**What Lewis signals by his methodology:**
- He's more epistemologically cautious than Smith, Cross, or Dever
- He's genuinely interdisciplinary but his heart is in texts and epigraphy, not dirt archaeology
- He wants to use source criticism without being bound by it — the JEDP/heuristic compromise is pragmatic
- He values onomastics more than most historians of religion and integrates it more sophisticatedly
- His debt to Cross (typological method, epigraphy, NW Semitic philology) is evident but not slavish
- The Propp Principle creates productive tension: Lewis will make arguments while acknowledging they might be wrong

**Cross-references:** This chapter's methodology pairs with Dever's Ch. III (which is more archaeologically focused), Smith's introduction to *Origins* (which is more programmatic about monotheism), and Sommer's introduction (which is more theologically engaged). Lewis's onomastic discussion should be read alongside Tigay's introduction, which uses onomastics differently. The Propp Principle resonates with the minimalist school's skepticism but Lewis is clearly not a minimalist — he thinks positive reconstructions are possible, just uncertain.
