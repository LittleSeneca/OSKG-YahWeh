# Kaufmann — Introduction

Kaufmann lays out his entire project in a few pages: a fundamental critique of Wellhausen's evolutionary model, and an assertion that Israelite monotheism was an original, popular, national creation — not a late elite development. The introduction is a declaration of war on the critical consensus.

---

## Claim 1: The Wellhausen consensus persists despite collapsed foundations

**Author's claim:** Biblical scholarship continues to adhere to Wellhausen's evolutionary conclusions even though "both the evidence and the arguments supporting this structure have been called into question and, to some extent, even rejected." Scholars admit the grounds have crumbled but won't abandon the conclusions.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann doesn't enumerate the specific critiques here — he's writing for an audience that knows them. He references "the critique of Wellhausen's theory which began some forty years ago" (i.e., since roughly the 1920s). The introduction functions as a promissory note — the evidence for this claim is the entire book.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the observation that Wellhausen's documentary hypothesis has faced serious challenges; LOW on Kaufmann's claim that scholars concede the foundations have crumbled while irrationally clinging to conclusions. This is a caricature of the field — scholars like von Rad and Noth weren't mindlessly clinging to Wellhausen; they modified and developed the paradigm.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's entire project. If Wellhausen's framework is actually robust, Kaufmann's "fundamental critique" is attacking a straw man. If he's right that the paradigm persists on inertia, then the field needs the comprehensive alternative he's offering.

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical establishment of Kaufmann's day. But more specifically: Wellhausen's actual arguments about the development of centralized worship, the evolution of sacrifice, and the dating of P remain debated on their merits, not just on inertia. Römer (Introduction) engages these questions directly and concludes the evolutionary model is substantially correct, even if Wellhausen's specific datings need adjustment.

**Alternative reading:** The persistence of Wellhausen's framework isn't irrational inertia — it's that subsequent modifications (form criticism, tradition history) refined rather than refuted the basic evolutionary insight. The paradigm shifted but the direction of development (from pluralism to monotheism) held.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is partly right — there IS a tendency in biblical scholarship to treat Wellhausen's conclusions as settled even when the arguments for them have been qualified. But he overstates the collapse. The evolutionary model isn't a house with crumbled foundations; it's a house that's been renovated so many times the original blueprint is hard to recognize, but the basic structure (monotheism developed gradually, the Torah reached its final form late) is still standing. I need to see whether Kaufmann's alternative accounts for the data BETTER, or just differently.

---

## Claim 2: The Torah is pre-prophetic, not post-exilic

**Author's claim:** "But its basic postulate — that the priestly stratum of the Torah was composed in the Babylonian exile, and that the literature of the Torah was still being written and revised in and after the Exile — is untenable. The Torah — it will be shown — is the literary product of the earliest stage of Israelite religion, the stage prior to literary prophecy." The Torah's sources "are demonstrably ancient — not, in part, not in their general content, but in their entirety, even to their language and formulation."

**Evidence presented:** Again, this is a promissory note — Chapters V and VI will make this case. Kaufmann accepts source criticism (JE, P, D are distinct) but reverses the sequence: P is EARLIEST, not latest. He claims the Torah was "sealed and canonized" before prophetic literature was even ordered.

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Kaufmann's most radical historical claim, and it contradicts not just Wellhausen but the entire documentary tradition. The linguistic evidence for P's lateness (Late Biblical Hebrew features) is substantial. Kaufmann will need extraordinary evidence to overturn it.

**What's at stake:** Everything. If P is pre-prophetic, then monotheistic law predates the prophets. The prophets aren't innovators — they're traditionalists calling Israel back to a monotheism it always had. This inverts the entire critical narrative.

**Who disagrees:** Everyone in the critical tradition. Even scholars sympathetic to Kaufmann's overall project (Tigay, Sommer) don't follow him on P's dating. The linguistic and historical arguments for exilic/post-exilic P are among the most robust results of biblical criticism.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann may be right that SOME material in P is ancient (ritual texts, priestly traditions) even if the final redaction is late. This is actually the moderate position many scholars hold — P preserves old material in a late framework. Kaufmann's "entirety, even to language" claim goes far beyond what the evidence can support.

**My assessment:** This is the hill Kaufmann is willing to die on, and it's probably where he's weakest. The critical dating of P rests on multiple converging lines (linguistics, institutional development, theological concepts). Kaufmann's alternative would need to explain ALL of those better than the consensus. I'm skeptical he can, but I need to read Chapters V-VI carefully to see if he has arguments I haven't encountered.

## Claim 3: Israelite religion was absolutely different from paganism from the beginning

**Author's claim:** "We shall see that Israelite religion was an original creation of the people of Israel. It was absolutely different from anything the pagan world ever knew; its monotheistic world view had no antecedents in paganism. Nor was it a theological doctrine conceived and nurtured in limited circles or schools... It was the fundamental idea of a national culture, and informed every aspect of that culture from its very beginning."

**Evidence presented:** The introduction only sketches the argument. Kaufmann points to two types of evidence: (1) the Bible's consistent misrepresentation of paganism as fetishism — which he takes as evidence that Israelites genuinely didn't understand pagan religion, not that they polemically distorted it; (2) "the peculiar biblical conceptions of sin, impurity, sacrifice, magic, dreams, prophecy and inspiration, priest and prophet, and the blanket prohibition of divination, unparalleled in the pagan world."

**Confidence:** LOW for the "from the very beginning" claim; MEDIUM-HIGH that Israelite religion developed distinctive features. The archaeological evidence (figurines, inscriptions, standing stones) strongly suggests popular religion was more continuous with Canaanite practice than Kaufmann allows.

**What's at stake:** The entire nature of the Yahweh phenomenon. If Kaufmann is right, Israelite religion is a genuine revolution — a radical break with everything that came before. If he's wrong, it's one more example of how religions evolve from polytheism through henotheism to monotheism, with Israel being unusual primarily in the thoroughness of its eventual break.

**Who disagrees:** Effectively every scholar we've read except Tigay (on onomastics) and Sommer (on the fluidity model). Smith's entire corpus is an argument for continuity. Dever's archaeological work directly contradicts the claim that popular religion was fundamentally different. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism from polytheistic roots.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion WAS distinctive — we don't need to deny that. But distinctiveness doesn't require absolute difference "from the very beginning." The evidence suggests a gradual differentiation: early Israelite religion shared substantial features with Canaanite religion (El worship, divine council, possibly Asherah), with monotheism emerging as a hard-fought position over centuries.

**My assessment:** This is where the rubber meets the road. Kaufmann's position is falsifiable: if we find evidence that early Israelites worshipped a pantheon, had a consort for Yahweh, or conceived of God in mythological terms, his "absolute difference from the beginning" claim fails. And we HAVE found that evidence — at Kuntillet Ajrud, in the figurines, in the Ugaritic parallels. The question is whether Kaufmann has a way of accounting for this evidence that doesn't collapse his thesis, or whether he simply dismisses it as "popular deviance" from an already-existing normative monotheism. I suspect the latter, and I need to watch for it.

## Claim 4: Biblical "idolatry" was not genuine mythological polytheism

**Author's claim:** "Despite appearances, Israel was not a polytheistic people. Careful examination of the biblical testimony to Israel's 'idolatry' yields an altogether new assessment of this idolatry. It was not genuine, mythological polytheism; foreign elements did not play a substantial, creative role in the formation of the popular culture."

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann signals his signature argument here — that when Israelites "worshipped idols," they weren't importing the full pagan mythological apparatus. They were engaging in a degraded, fetishistic practice that didn't involve actual belief in the myths, theogonies, or divine biographies that characterized genuine paganism.

**Confidence:** LOW. This requires Kaufmann to argue that the archaeological and biblical evidence for polytheistic practice in Israel represents something fundamentally different from the same practices in neighboring cultures. That's a heavy evidentiary burden.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right, the entire "Israel was polytheistic" narrative collapses. Israel's idolatry was a superficial, contentless imitation of pagan forms — not evidence of genuine polytheistic belief.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (Did God Have a Wife?) documents thousands of figurines and argues they represent genuine popular religion. Smith (Early History of God, Origins) traces the mythological content that Israelites DID share with their neighbors. Römer documents the epigraphic evidence for Yahweh's pairing with Asherah.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is onto something REAL but overstates it. Israelite religion WAS distinctive in its aniconic tendency, its rejection of theogony, and its eventual monotheism. But the distinction wasn't present "from the very beginning" — it developed over time, and the biblical texts retroject later norms onto earlier periods.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most interesting move, and I need to watch how he handles the specific evidence. His claim that biblical polemic consistently misrepresents paganism as fetishism IS an observation worth taking seriously — the prophets do seem to mock idols as mere wood and stone rather than engaging with pagan theology. But the question is whether this reflects Israelite ignorance of paganism (Kaufmann's claim) or deliberate polemical strategy (the critical consensus). The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, which pair Yahweh with Asherah in a blessing formula, are particularly hard for Kaufmann to explain away as "not genuine polytheism."

## Claim 5: Israelite religion was a popular, national creation — not an elite doctrine

**Author's claim:** Israelite monotheism "was not a theological doctrine conceived and nurtured in limited circles or schools" but "the fundamental idea of a national culture" from the beginning.

**Evidence presented:** None in the introduction — this is a thesis statement. The evidence will presumably come in Chapters IV (The Religion of the People) and throughout the historical survey.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-LOW. The claim that Israelite monotheism was popular rather than elite runs counter to the biblical texts themselves, which CONSTANTLY depict the prophets as a minority calling an unwilling people back to YHWH. If monotheism were genuinely popular, why does the entire prophetic corpus consist of rebuke for idolatry?

**What's at stake:** Whether the Bible's own picture of Israel's religious history (constant apostasy, prophetic rebuke) is essentially accurate or fundamentally misleading. Kaufmann wants to say: the Bible's picture is CORRECT that monotheism was original, but the "apostasy" was a superficial phenomenon, not a reversion to genuine polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves, if read straightforwardly. The Deuteronomistic History depicts a people constantly falling into worship of other gods. The prophets thunder against precisely this. Kaufmann has to argue that all this testimony is about something OTHER than genuine polytheism.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical picture of constant apostasy IS accurate — Israel WAS polytheistic through most of the monarchy, and monotheism was indeed an elite prophetic position that only became the popular norm after the Exile. This reading has the virtue of taking both the biblical texts and the archaeological evidence at face value.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is doing something genuinely clever here — he's accepting the biblical picture of early monotheism but reinterpreting the evidence of "apostasy" as something less than genuine polytheism. Whether this works depends entirely on whether he can characterize the archaeological and biblical evidence for polytheistic practice in a way that's intellectually honest. The danger is that he simply DEFINES genuine polytheism in a way that excludes the Israelite evidence by fiat.

## Claim 6: Method — the Bible's internal consistency is as conclusive as material remains

**Author's claim:** "Especially significant are the inferences to be drawn from the ideological constants of the literary corpora, from the leading motifs of the various strata, from the several crystallizations of the historical tradition. These are the literary equivalent of geological stratification, and are as conclusive as the material remains of buildings, inscriptions, artifacts, and pottery."

**Evidence presented:** He lists as examples: the biblical polemic against idolatry consistently misrepresenting paganism as fetishism; the peculiar biblical conceptions of sin, impurity, sacrifice, magic, dreams, prophecy; the blanket prohibition of divination; the fact that national collapse led to the COMPLETE extirpation of Israelite idolatry (contrast with other nations that lost political independence but kept their gods).

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. His geological metaphor is rhetorically powerful but methodologically questionable. "Ideological constants" can reflect the perspective of the final redactors, not the original historical reality. The consistency he observes might be the product of deuteronomistic editing, not historical fact.

**What's at stake:** Whether Kaufmann's method can produce reliable historical conclusions at all. If he's reading the Bible's theological consistency as historical evidence, he's doing theology, not history.

**Who disagrees:** The entire historical-critical method. Source criticism exists precisely because the Bible's surface consistency masks multiple voices and developmental layers. Kaufmann is essentially arguing that the consistency IS the data, which inverts the critical project.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's "ideological constants" reflect the theological perspective of its final editors, who systematically reshaped older traditions to conform to monotheistic norms. The consistency is real — but it's the consistency of editorial theology, not of historical witness.

**My assessment:** This is where Kaufmann's method is most vulnerable. He's treating the Bible's theological coherence as evidence of historical reality. But coherence can be produced by editors as easily as by events. The question isn't whether the Bible is internally consistent on these points — it is. The question is whether that consistency tells us about the period DESCRIBED or the period of the DESCRIBERS. Kaufmann's "geological stratification" metaphor assumes the former; critical scholarship demonstrates the latter.

## Claim 7: Scholarship reads the evidence backwards — "in the light of paganism"

**Author's claim:** "Yet, as if in stubborn defiance of the very nature of our evidence, biblical scholarship seeks to read it... in the light of paganism!" The critical method assumes Israelite religion must have developed like other ancient Near Eastern religions, and interprets anomalous data as late editorial insertions rather than evidence of genuine distinctiveness.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann points to the tendency to dismiss any monotheistic-sounding passage in early texts as a later insertion, creating a self-confirming paradigm where early texts can't be monotheistic because monotheism is late, and texts are dated late because they're monotheistic.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann has identified a genuine circularity in some critical methodology. The argument that "this passage sounds monotheistic, therefore it must be late" DOES assume what it's trying to prove. But Kaufmann has his own mirror-image circularity: assuming early monotheism and dismissing contrary evidence as popular deviance.

**What's at stake:** The methodological framework within which all the evidence is evaluated. If Kaufmann is right about the circularity, the entire critical dating scheme needs re-examination — not necessarily reversal, but at least reopening.

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition would say that dating isn't based on theological content alone but on multiple converging lines of evidence (language, institutions, historical references, literary dependencies). The circularity Kaufmann identifies is real but not as damning as he claims, because the dating scheme doesn't rest on a single pillar.

**Alternative reading:** The circularity is a necessary feature of historical reconstruction with limited data. All historical work involves interpretive frameworks that shape how evidence is read. The question is which framework accounts for MORE of the data with FEWER ad hoc adjustments.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's best point in the introduction. There IS a tendency in critical scholarship to assume that anything monotheistic-sounding must be late, and to date texts accordingly. Tigay's onomastic study is powerful precisely because it breaks this circularity — it uses an independent dataset (personal names) to test the question. But critiquing the circle doesn't prove Kaufmann's alternative is correct. It only shows the question should be genuinely open. And on the evidence we've accumulated (Kuntillet Ajrud, the figurines, Ugaritic parallels), the critical framework accounts for MORE data than Kaufmann's revolutionary model does.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Wellhausen persists on inertia | MEDIUM-HIGH | Critics did modify Wellhausen, didn't just cling |
| 2: Torah is pre-prophetic | LOW | Linguistic evidence for late P |
| 3: Absolute difference from beginning | LOW | Kuntillet Ajrud, figurines, Ugaritic parallels |
| 4: Idolatry was not genuine polytheism | LOW | Archaeological evidence of actual polytheistic practice |
| 5: Monotheism was popular, not elite | MEDIUM-LOW | Biblical texts themselves depict prophets as minority |
| 6: Internal consistency = geological strata | LOW-MEDIUM | Consistency can be editorial, not historical |
| 7: Scholarship is circular | MEDIUM | Real circularity exists; but doesn't prove Kaufmann's alternative |

**Strongest section:** The methodological critique of circularity in critical dating (Claim 7). Kaufmann puts his finger on a genuine problem.

**Weakest section:** The promissory nature of the entire introduction. Kaufmann asserts radical conclusions with almost no evidence presented. The entire book rests on chapters we haven't read yet.

**Methodological note:** Kaufmann writes like a systematic theologian doing history. His method is to identify the "fundamental idea" of Israelite religion and then interpret all evidence through that lens. This is the opposite of the inductive method he criticizes in others. He accuses critical scholars of reading the data "in the light of paganism," but he reads it in the light of an assumed monotheistic revolution. Both approaches have their circularities. The question is which circularity is smaller.
