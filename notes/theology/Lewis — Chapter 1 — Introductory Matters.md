# Lewis — Chapter 1: Introductory Matters

Lewis frames the entire project: a reference work on the God who becomes foundational to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, approached as a historian of religion rather than a theologian. He surveys what a comprehensive volume *would* require, admits his own volume cannot cover it all, and argues that "divinity" is the best lens through which to study Israelite religion selectively. The chapter ends with a roadmap of his seven "tastings" (Chapters 4-10).

---

## Claim 1: The historian of religion's approach is fundamentally different from the theologian's — and this distinction shapes everything that follows

**Lewis's claim:** "My approach is that of a historian of religion rather than that of a theologian. Specifically, I write as a historian of the religion of ancient Israel as it is understood within its ancient Near Eastern context." He emphasizes his training in "Near Eastern languages and civilizations (not theology, as close readers will see)." His department at Johns Hopkins includes Assyriology, Egyptology, Northwest Semitics, archaeology, and art history — not systematic theology or divinity.

**Evidence presented:** Lewis doesn't argue this point with evidence; he asserts it as his disciplinary identity and methodological positioning. The evidence is in the book's execution: its heavy reliance on epigraphic data, iconographic analysis, archaeological reports, and comparative ancient Near Eastern texts alongside (not subordinate to) the Hebrew Bible.

**Confidence:** HIGH that this is Lewis's actual approach. The question is whether the distinction is as clean as he claims. A historian of religion who works at a secular university still makes interpretive choices shaped by unstated philosophical commitments — every scholar does. Lewis is *less* theological than, say, Heiser or even Cross (who was at Harvard Divinity), but the line between "history of religion" and "theology" is blurrier than his framing suggests.

**What's at stake:** Whether the volume can claim an Archimedean point outside confessional commitments. If Lewis smuggles in theological assumptions without acknowledging them (e.g., about what counts as "religion," about the primacy of texts over practice), his "historian of religion" stance is partly rhetorical.

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars (Heiser, Waltke, Provan) would argue that bracketing theology distorts the subject matter — ancient Israelites understood their religion *theologically*, and to study it purely as a historical phenomenon is to miss its internal logic. Conversely, more theoretically sophisticated scholars (Asad, McCutcheon, Smith J.Z.) might argue that Lewis's "historian of religion" stance is still too Protestant in its assumptions about belief over practice.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis is making a pragmatic claim, not a philosophical one. He means: "I will not assume the truth of biblical claims, and I will use the full toolkit of historical-critical scholarship." That's defensible without claiming a clean break from theology.

**My assessment:** Lewis is on solid ground for his target audience (scholars of ancient Near Eastern religion, not seminarians). The real test is whether he maintains this stance consistently — especially in Chapter 10 on holiness, where the temptation to slip into theological description is strongest. His insistence is more useful as a signal of method than as a claim about disciplinary purity.

---

## Claim 2: A truly comprehensive volume on ancient Israelite religion is impossible in a single book — therefore selectivity is required, and the author must state his criteria

**Lewis's claim:** "The subject of the present volume, ancient Israelite religion, is far too large to be covered in a single volume. This is especially true if one desires to probe its various topics with the depth of research they deserve." He then walks through what comprehensiveness would require: full treatment of every male and female deity, sacred time and space, the full range of cultic practice, all religious personnel, incantation specialists, wisdom literature, and aesthetic dimensions.

**Evidence:** The list itself is the evidence. Lewis names roughly 22 male deities, 11+ female deities, dozens of festival types, at least 12 varieties of offerings, 12+ types of incantation specialists, multiple priestly groups (Aaronids, Levites, Mushites, Zadokites, Shilohnites, etc.), and multiple literary corpora. Zevit's 2001 volume, which focuses primarily on sacred architecture and epigraphy, already runs to 700+ pages. Albertz's two-volume history covers 600+ pages.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is publicly verifiable — no single volume has ever achieved comprehensive coverage. Even the massive reference works (e.g., *Religions of the Ancient World* edited by Johnston, the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*) are multi-author edited collections.

**What's at stake:** Lewis is preemptively defending himself against the criticism that his book is incomplete. By openly acknowledging what he's NOT covering (goddesses, cultic calendars, incantation, wisdom literature in depth), he makes his selectivity explicit rather than implicit. This is methodologically honest and lets readers decide if the tradeoffs are acceptable.

**Who disagrees:** No one seriously disagrees with the claim that comprehensiveness is impossible. The debate is about whether Lewis's chosen focus (divinity, especially El and Yahweh) is the *right* selection — or whether it overweights elite/theological concerns at the expense of popular religion.

**Alternative reading:** The "impossibility" claim is partly rhetorical. Other scholars have produced synthetic volumes (Römer, Smith, Albertz) that achieve reasonable coverage. Lewis's choice to focus on divinity reflects his interests and expertise, not an objective necessity.

**My assessment:** This is honest framing. Lewis tells you exactly what he's doing and why. The real question is whether his seven "tastings" are representative enough — and that can only be judged after reading them all.

---

## Claim 3: Studying ancient Israelite religion through the "lens of divinity" is the best organizing principle, because divinity connects the historical, sociological, cultic, ideological, and aesthetic dimensions

**Lewis's claim:** "By using divinity, I do not mean to narrow the topic theologically; rather, writing as a historian of religion, I intend to explore divinity as a window to the historical, the sociological, the performance of cult, the ideological, and the aesthetic." He aligns himself with Smith and van der Toorn, who also use divinity as a conceptual category, and cites Schwemer on Hittite cult and Kugel on the centrality of gods in ancient Near Eastern consciousness.

**Evidence:** Lewis marshals three supporting voices:
- **Schwemer (2016):** Hittite cult was organized around the gods — their houses, their care, their favor. "The observance of the cult is regarded as a prerequisite of the gods' favour."
- **Kugel (2011):** "The peoples [of the ancient Near East] had always deferred, and referred, to God or the gods in all things." How someone thinks about God "has very much to do with how that person conceives of himself or herself."
- **Smith (2001b):** "To understand divinity is to extrapolate from the human condition... and to understand humanity is in turn to make some sense of divinity."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that divinity is a *good* organizing principle. HIGH that Lewis's cited authorities support it. But "best" is harder to defend — other principles (sociology as in Albertz, material culture as in Zevit, literary genre as in Niditch) have distinct advantages.

**What's at stake:** The entire structure of the book. If divinity is the wrong lens, the book misses what's most important about Israelite religion. If it's the right lens, Lewis has found the thread that ties together El worship, Yahweh's origins, iconography, and characterization.

**Who disagrees:** Zevit would argue for material culture (archaeology) as the more objective starting point. Albertz would say social structure (family → tribe → state) reveals more about how religion actually functioned. Niditch would foreground individual religious experience.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis isn't actually claiming divinity is "best" in absolute terms — he's saying it's the lens he's chosen and it has demonstrable advantages. The other lenses are complementary, not competing.

**My assessment:** Lewis's choice is well-defended and internally coherent. His real contribution (judging from the roadmap) will be integrating iconographic and epigraphic evidence with textual analysis — and divinity is the natural category for that integration. The test is whether the lens of divinity illuminates or obscures the religion of ordinary Israelites who may not have been theologically sophisticated.

## Claim 4: The Hebrew Bible preserves "opposing voices" that must be taken seriously — Israelite religion was not monolithic

**Lewis's claim:** Throughout Chapter 1, Lewis emphasizes plurality. He notes "ongoing negotiations over sacerdotal power, status, and cultic privilege," the variety of priestly groups with different claims to holiness, and how "each tradition (e.g., Priestly Source, Holiness Code, Ezekiel, Deuteronomy, Deuteronomistic History, and Chronicles) has its own nuances." In the chapter roadmap, he promises to show "opposing voices with regard to restricting and expanding holiness."

**Evidence:** Lewis cites specific examples: P says *only* Aaronids are holy; H extends holiness to the entire community; prophets could be symbiotic with or adversarial to priests; royal religion coexisted with family religion; incantation specialists operated despite being "often deemed illicit."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This claim is beyond dispute in critical scholarship. The Bible is an anthology, not a unified treatise, and it preserves competing traditions. Even conservative scholars acknowledge surface tensions (they just resolve them differently).

**What's at stake:** Lewis is signaling that he won't harmonize contradictory evidence — he'll let the tensions stand. This separates him from confessional approaches and from older scholarship that treated "Israelite religion" as a unified system.

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars would say the tensions are resolvable through careful exegesis and that Lewis overstates the diversity. Heiser would argue that apparent polytheism is actually a divine council of subordinate beings, not competing cults.

**My assessment:** Lewis's emphasis on plurality is standard critical procedure and aligns with Smith, Römer, Dever, and essentially the entire post-1970s scholarly consensus. What will matter is whether he fairly represents the internal logic of each tradition or reduces them all to power struggles.

## Claim 5: The book's seven "tastings" (Chapters 4-10) represent a defensible selective coverage of Israelite religion, centered on El and Yahweh

**Lewis's claim:** The seven chapters cover: (1) El worship, (2) El iconography, (3) Yahweh's origin, (4) Yahweh iconography, (5) Yahweh as warrior and family god, (6) Yahweh as king and judge, (7) Yahweh as holy one. This "distillate" captures core elements while leaving much aside (goddesses, calendrical festivals, wisdom literature, incantation, etc.).

**Evidence:** Lewis justifies each chapter's inclusion: El worship is fundamental because "El" appears in the name *Israel*; iconography is essential because ancients thought about gods visually, not just textually; Yahweh's origin is the central historical question; characterization of Yahweh covers the major metaphors through which Israelites understood their god.

**Confidence:** HIGH that these are important topics. MEDIUM that they constitute a *representative* selection — notably absent are goddess worship (especially Asherah, which gets only passing mention in this roadmap), ancestor cult, divination, and the full range of popular religious practice.

**What's at stake:** If Lewis's selection skews too heavily toward elite, textual, and theological concerns, the book misrepresents Israelite religion as more systematic and less embodied than it was. Stavrakopoulou's *God: An Anatomy* (2021) argues the opposite: that Yahweh was understood in radically embodied, physical terms. Lewis's iconographic chapters may address this, but the roadmap doesn't suggest a focus on the body per se.

**Who disagrees:** Dever would say archaeology of popular practice is missing. Stavrakopoulou would say the physical body of God is underrepresented. Albertz would say family and personal piety need more space. Zevit would want more material culture.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis isn't claiming this is a *balanced* coverage — he's claiming it's a *deep* coverage of selected topics. A single author can't do everything, and Lewis's topics are genuinely central (Yahweh, El, iconography). The missing topics are covered by other scholars.

**My assessment:** Lewis's roadmap is honest about what's included and excluded. The real criterion is whether what he DOES cover is done with sufficient depth and rigor to justify the omissions. For a reference work, this is defensible. But readers should supplement with Dever (for archaeology of popular religion), Stavrakopoulou (for embodiment), and Albertz (for social history).

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Historian vs. theologian distinction | HIGH | Unstated philosophical commitments; boundary blurrier than claimed |
| Comprehensiveness impossible | VERY HIGH | Nothing — this is obvious |
| Divinity as organizing lens | MEDIUM-HIGH | Alternative lenses (sociology, archaeology, embodiment) may be equally valid |
| Opposing voices in Hebrew Bible | VERY HIGH | Only confessional harmonization disputes this |
| Seven tastings as representative selection | MEDIUM-HIGH | Missing goddess religion, popular practice, ancestor cult |

**Strongest part:** Lewis's frank admission of what he's NOT covering. This is methodologically refreshing — too many scholars pretend their book covers everything. The walkthrough of what comprehensiveness would require (lines 250-308) is a valuable map of the field in itself.

**Weakest part:** The "historian of religion vs. theologian" framing. Lewis protests too much. His acknowledgments page thanks Frank Moore Cross (Harvard Divinity) and concludes with a doxology to "the God who holds in his hand our every breath" (Dan 5:23). The tension between his methodological secularity and his personal piety is human and honest — but the framing doesn't fully acknowledge it.

**What this chapter does:** It's a traditional "here's what I'm doing and why" introduction. It doesn't advance substantive historical claims. It sets expectations and defines terms. That's appropriate for an introductory chapter of a reference work.

**Cross-references:** Lewis's organizing principle (divinity as lens) echoes Smith's introduction to *Origins of Biblical Monotheism* but with more explicit engagement with Zevit, Albertz, and Niditch as alternative approaches. His emphasis on plurality aligns with Römer's *Invention of God* and Dever's insistence on the diversity of Israelite practice. His methodological positioning as a historian of religion contrasts with Heiser's confessional approach and Cross's more theologically-inflected history.
