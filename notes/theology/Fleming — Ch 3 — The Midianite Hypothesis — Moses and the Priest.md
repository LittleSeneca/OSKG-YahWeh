# Fleming — Chapter 3: The Midianite Hypothesis — Moses and the Priest

This chapter dismantles the biblical prose foundation of the Midianite Hypothesis. Fleming traces the hypothesis to its true creator (Tiele, not Ghillany), shows how it transformed from a Kenite/Rechabite argument into a Moses-Midian argument, then systematically demonstrates that none of the key biblical texts (Exodus 3, 6, 18; Genesis 4:26; the Kenite connections) actually support the idea that Israel borrowed Yahweh worship from southern peoples. His conclusion: the Midianite Hypothesis must function without the Midianites. The biblical prose evidence for southern affinity is real — but it's about population kinship, not religious borrowing.

---

## Claim 1: Cornelis Tiele (1872), not Friedrich Ghillany (1862), was the true creator of the Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis

**Author's claim:** "Tiele certainly proposes a new way to understand the origin of Yahweh in relation to the peoples of the inland deserts... he advances the idea with consciousness of its novelty: only two alternatives had been considered before him, that Yahweh came from Egypt and that Ya(hweh) was a Canaanite deity acquired after conquest" (p. 92). "Tiele should be regarded as the creator of what became the Midianite Hypothesis" (p. 94).

**Evidence presented:**
- Ghillany's 1862 work (under pseudonym Richard von der Alm) did NOT propose Yahweh came from the desert — he argued Yahweh came from Phoenicia via the god Yao, in Samuel's time (p. 91)
- Tiele's 1872 Dutch work self-consciously presented the desert-origin idea as novel
- Tiele built his case from Rechabites → Kenites → Moses' father-in-law, with the Rechabite "zeal for Yahweh" as the starting point
- The hypothesis was Kenite, not Midianite: Tiele "has little interest in the story of Jethro as Midianite priest" (p. 94)
- Tiele's spatial reference was "the wider desert" as an alternative way of life, not specifically the south
- German scholars (Stade, Budde) picked up the idea without crediting Tiele, transforming it into a Moses-centered Midianite form
- Blenkinsopp (2008: 132) had attributed priority to Ghillany based on Holzinger (1900), who was apparently mistaken

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is a historiographical claim, verifiable by consulting the primary sources. Fleming has done the legwork in the original Dutch and French editions.

**What's at stake:** Understanding the hypothesis's origins reveals how much it mutated. Tiele's original version was more modest, less Moses-centric, and not specifically southern. The transformation into a Moses-Midian-Sinai hypothesis was a later development that loaded theological freight onto a more careful comparative-religion argument.

**Who disagrees:** Blenkinsopp (2008) and the scholarly tradition that credits Ghillany.

**Alternative reading:** Priority disputes don't affect the substance of the hypothesis — what matters is the mature form, not the first sketch.

**My assessment:** Fleming's historiographical work is meticulous and illuminating. The transformation from Tiele's Kenite/Rechabite argument to the later Midianite/Moses form matters because it shows how biblical scholars progressively centered the hypothesis on the Exodus narrative — the very narrative Fleming argues cannot support it.

## Claim 2: Exodus 6:2-3 and Exodus 3 do not preserve historical memory of Yahweh being unknown before Moses — they are schematic texts bridging Genesis and Exodus

**Author's claim:** "No matter the literary-historical system, there is no good case that Exodus 3 and 6 represent such ancient religious realities" (p. 107). "Both texts make Moses central even to knowing the name by which this god must be approached" (p. 127). "The texts contradict the assumption in Genesis that Israel's ancestors knew Yahweh by name, but with Moses' privileged authority at stake, it appears that the writer of this section in Exodus has added the revelation to its already powerful claim" (p. 107).

**Evidence presented:**
- Exodus 6:2-8 is Priestly (P), widely considered a relatively late composition that self-consciously connects Genesis ancestors with Moses (pp. 107-108)
- The text's language and concepts are thoroughly Priestly: El Shadday, covenant, land promise, "I am Yahweh"
- The declaration "by my name Yahweh I was not known to them" (6:3) serves to make the exodus moment uniquely transformative: "The name goes with the deliverance, and the deliverance will confirm the name" (p. 109)
- Exodus 3:1-4:18 is an insertion linking Genesis promises and the Joshua conquest — the strongest links to the ancestors appear precisely in the section where the divine name is revealed (3:6-17)
- In European scholarship, this text is widely considered post-Priestly or post-Deuteronomistic — a bridge text (Schmid 2010, Berner 2010)
- "Neither text offers a matrix that preserves fixed ancient traditions for religious origins with roots in historical realities" (p. 117)
- The texts address "perceived narrative problems" — how to reconcile ancestor stories where Yahweh is known with the need to make Moses the mediator of something new

**Confidence:** HIGH. Fleming's literary analysis is strong and aligns with broad European scholarship on the post-exilic dating of key Pentateuchal bridge texts. The schematic function of these passages is well-established.

**What's at stake:** If Exodus 3 and 6 don't preserve ancient memory, the entire Midianite Hypothesis loses its biblical anchor. The claim that "Israel didn't always know Yahweh" depends entirely on these texts. Without them, there's no biblical basis for Israel having "acquired" Yahweh from anyone.

**Who disagrees:** Documentary Hypothesis defenders who treat Exodus 3 (E) and 6 (P) as preserving genuinely old traditions from monarchic-period sources. Also, scholars like Römer who accept later dating but still find historical memory embedded in the texts.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the texts as we have them are late, they could preserve older traditions. The very oddness of the claim that the ancestors didn't know Yahweh argues for its antiquity — a late writer wouldn't invent a problem they'd then need to solve.

**My assessment:** Fleming's case is strong. The schematic character of these texts is visible on their face — they're doing theological bridge-work. The alternative reading (oddness = antiquity) has some force, but Fleming's literary analysis shows the "oddness" is exactly what you'd expect from a writer trying to connect two originally separate narrative complexes.

## Claim 3: Exodus 18 does not portray Jethro as a priest of Yahweh introducing the god to Israel — Jethro is responding to demonstrated power, not initiating worship

**Author's claim:** "Without the expectation that the people of Israel only learned of Yahweh by name in connection with the exodus, Jethro's meeting with Moses would never have suggested a Midianite origin for the god" (p. 120). "Neither Exodus 2 nor 18 supplies any basis for finding biblical memory in any form for non-Israelite worship of Yahweh" (p. 123).

**Evidence presented:**
- Jethro's blessing ("Blessed be Yahweh") is a response to Moses' report of deliverance — his acclamation "Now I know that Yahweh is greater than all the gods" (v. 11) is based on new experience, like Rahab in Joshua 2 (pp. 119-120)
- The "priest of Midian" title in 2:16 is given without any deity specified (unlike Potiphera "priest of On") — it's a marker of social standing, not religious function (p. 123)
- In Exodus 2, the priest performs no sacred act and expresses no religious commitment — the title may simply mean "leader of the Midianites" (p. 123)
- Jethro's sacrifice in 18:12 uses "Elohim" (God), not "Yahweh" — ambiguous framing
- The chapter seems designed to resolve the problem of Moses' foreign marriage, not to explain Yahweh's origin: "it appears that the Midianite marriage was a problem to solve in known narrative, not a creation for Exodus 18" (p. 120)
- The entire chapter is probably a late addition resolving the loose end of Moses' Midianite wife before the Sinai law-giving
- Exodus 2:16-22 (the well scene) is itself possibly secondary to the Moses story (Berner 2010)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Fleming's reading of Jethro's response as reactive rather than initiatory is textually sound. The observation that "priest of Midian" lacks a specified deity is a genuinely sharp point. But the counter-reading (Blenkinsopp, Budde) that Jethro "initiates the action" has some textual basis — Jethro does make the sacrifice, and Aaron and the elders come to him.

**What's at stake:** Exodus 18 is the single prose text most directly linking a non-Israelite "priest" to Yahweh worship. If Jethro isn't introducing Yahweh but merely acknowledging him, the Midianite Hypothesis cannot claim Exodus 18 as evidence.

**Who disagrees:** Budde (1899): Jethro expresses "proud joy that his God, Yahweh, the God of Kenites, has proved himself mightier than all other gods." Rowley (1950): "the first incorporation of the Israelite elders into the worship of Yahweh." Blenkinsopp (2008): "Jethro is the principal actor; he initiates the action." Römer (2015): "the cult of Yahweh is founded by a Midianite priest."

**Alternative reading:** Jethro's role as priest and his initiative in sacrifice, combined with the unusual portrayal of a non-Israelite leading worship, suggests a genuine tradition of Midianite Yahwism that the biblical editors could not entirely suppress.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is more textually careful than the traditional Midianite Hypothesis reading. But he may overstate the case. The text IS ambiguous. Jethro's "Now I know" could mean "I, the priest of Yahweh, now see my god's power demonstrated" as easily as "I, a Midianite priest, now recognize Yahweh's superiority." Fleming's insistence on the reactive nature of Jethro's response is correct; whether that settles the question is less clear.

## Claim 4: The Kenite/Cain connection does not support Yahweh's origin among Kenites — Genesis 4:26 is another schematic text

**Author's claim:** "Here again we have no evidence that Yahweh was a Kenite god — no interest in Yahweh at all, except by extrapolation from Moses' 'father-in-law' in Exodus 18" (p. 125). "The interpretation of Yahweh's first worship in Gen 4:26 represents the guiding hand of a fellow scholar, not the passing on of near-forgotten lore — from before the great flood!" (p. 126).

**Evidence presented:**
- Genesis 4:26 ("Then it was begun to call on the name of Yahweh") is part of a bridge between the Cain material (4:1-24) and the Priestly genealogy (ch. 5)
- David Carr (forthcoming) reads it as "non-Priestly" but still part of a late compositional effort to trace Yahweh worship back to primeval times
- "The passage envisions an early approach to Yahweh long before Moses, in contrast to P" (Carr, cited p. 127)
- The Kenites share their name with Cain, but the Cain narrative in Genesis 4 is an etiology of civilization, not a religious origin story
- The connection between Cain and Kenites is never developed in the Bible — no biblical text says "the Kenites worshipped Yahweh because they descend from Cain"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Kenite element was always the weakest part of the Midianite Hypothesis and Fleming dispatches it efficiently.

**What's at stake:** Low for the overall argument — even Blenkinsopp's four-part summary of the hypothesis treats the Kenite/Cain connection as supplemental. But Fleming's point is methodological: every alleged biblical support for the hypothesis dissolves on close reading.

**Who disagrees:** Blenkinsopp (2008: 141), who reads Gen 4:26 as marking "a decisively new stage in religious history." Tiele's original formulation built heavily on the Kenite-Rechabite connection.

**Alternative reading:** The Cain-Kenite connection preserves genuine genealogical memory of a southern tribe associated with early Yahweh worship, even if the Genesis 4 text itself is late.

**My assessment:** This is Fleming's easiest target and he hits it cleanly. Gen 4:26 is transparently a theological notice about primeval Yahwism, not a historical report about Kenite religion.

## Claim 5: The Bible DOES preserve genuine tradition about kinship with southern/inland peoples — but this is about population affinities, NOT religious borrowing

**Author's claim:** "The Midianites and the Kenites themselves are another matter entirely. Jethro — or Reuel, or Hobab — represents just one instance of a recurring theme in biblical accounts of origins and early days, when the predecessors of the biblical peoples manifest diverse lines of affinity and kinship with the inhabitants of the inland back country" (p. 128). "This phenomenon constitutes the kind of 'tradition' that is no creation of monarchic or post-monarchic scribes" (p. 128).

**Evidence presented:**
- Diverse texts show southern/inland kinship: Esau as Jacob's twin (Gen 25), friendly Kenites (1 Sam 15:6, 27:10, 30:29), Moses' Midianite marriage, Deuteronomy's "your brother" Edomite (Deut 23:8)
- These traditions "creep into widely varied texts with a panoply of particulars" — suggesting genuine tradition, not scribal invention
- But NONE of them suggests Yahweh was borrowed from these peoples
- Tiele's original insight was about Israel AS a desert people, not about borrowing a god FROM desert peoples
- "Tiele's explanation of Yahweh's origin... depended first of all on the notion that Israel began as a desert people" (p. 129)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The observation that kinship traditions are widespread and varied is correct and important. But whether these all point to a single "collective memory" or represent different traditions from different periods is harder to assess.

**What's at stake:** This is the positive payoff of the chapter. Fleming isn't just destroying the Midianite Hypothesis — he's salvaging what it got right: Israel did have real affinities with inland/southern peoples. The error was interpreting those affinities as evidence for religious borrowing.

**Who disagrees:** The Midianite Hypothesis camp, who see the kinship traditions AS evidence for religious transmission.

**Alternative reading:** Kinship and shared cult typically go together in the ancient Near East. If Israel was genuinely kin to Edom and Midian, shared worship of Yahweh (or a proto-Yahweh) is the most natural inference.

**My assessment:** This is Fleming's most constructive move in the chapter. The tradition of southern/inland kinship is real and important. Fleming's interpretation (population affinities, not religious borrowing) is more cautious and probably more defensible than the traditional reading. But he may be drawing too sharp a line between "kinship" and "shared cult" — in the ancient world, these were often inseparable.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Tiele as true MH creator | HIGH | Priority dispute, not substance |
| 2: Exod 3, 6 as schematic texts | HIGH | Documentary Hypothesis defenders |
| 3: Exod 18 doesn't show Midianite Yahwism | MEDIUM-HIGH | Textual ambiguity — Jethro's initiative |
| 4: Kenite/Cain connection unsupportive | HIGH | None — weakest MH element |
| 5: Kinship traditions real, not religious | MEDIUM-HIGH | Ancient kinship/cult inseparability |

**Strongest section:** The literary analysis of Exodus 3 and 6 as schematic bridge texts (pp. 106-117). This is careful, well-sourced, and devastating to the traditional Midianite Hypothesis. Fleming shows that the "unknown name" motif serves compositional needs, not historical memory.

**Weakest section:** The Exodus 18 analysis, which is good but slightly over-argued. Fleming's reading is more plausible than the traditional one, but the text IS genuinely ambiguous about Jethro's relationship to Yahweh, and a defender could reasonably disagree.

**Cross-reference to Römer:** [[Romer — Chapter 3 — Moses and the Midianites]] takes the opposite approach — Römer treats Exodus 3 and 18 as preserving genuine memory of Midianite contribution to Yahweh's cult. Fleming directly engages Römer's reading (pp. 105-106) and reaches the opposite conclusion. The divergence between these two scholars on the same texts makes this the sharpest point of conflict in the current debate.

**Cross-reference to Cross:** [[Cross — Preface + Part I — Canaanite Religion and El]] — Cross built his southern league concept partly on the same prose traditions Fleming deconstructs. If Exodus 3/6/18 don't support southern Yahwistic origins, Cross's model loses one of its pillars.
