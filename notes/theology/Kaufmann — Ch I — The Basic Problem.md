# Kaufmann — Chapter I — The Basic Problem

Kaufmann opens his book with what he presents as a bombshell observation — so obvious once stated, yet somehow overlooked by every scholar before him: the Bible is utterly ignorant of the nature of pagan religion. Across centuries of polemic, law, and narrative, biblical writers consistently misrepresent paganism as mere fetishism (worship of wood and stone) rather than engaging with the living gods, myths, and cosmic dramas that constituted actual ancient Near Eastern paganism. For Kaufmann, this is not a polemical tactic — it is genuine ignorance, and it reveals that Israelite religion was non-mythological from its earliest beginnings. This chapter is Kaufmann's most original contribution and the foundation on which his entire reconstruction rests. It is also — despite its genuine insights — the most methodologically vulnerable chapter in the book.

---

## Claim 1: The Bible displays a "strange fact" — utter ignorance of the nature and meaning of pagan religion

**Author's claim:** "If one examines the biblical account of the origins of Israelite monotheism and the story of its battle with and eventual triumph over paganism, he will discover a strange fact: the Bible is utterly unaware of the nature and meaning of pagan religion" (lines 3-6). Despite centuries of fighting "idolatry" and producing abundant polemical literature, "No one, apparently, has ever doubted this assumption or criticized it in the light of the data" (lines 14-15). Scholars universally assume the biblical age was intimately acquainted with paganism — Kaufmann argues the data shows the opposite.

**Evidence presented:** The entire chapter is the evidence for this claim. Kaufmann surveys the biblical corpus — narratives, prophetic polemics, and legal codes — and argues that nowhere does the Bible engage with pagan religion as pagans knew it. Named gods (Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh, etc.) are "mere names" stripped of mythological content (line 98). The Bible's anti-idolatry polemic attacks only the worship of manufactured objects, never the belief in living gods with mythological biographies.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the *observation* (the Bible's polemic really does focus overwhelmingly on fetishism); LOW on the *explanation* (that this reflects genuine ignorance rather than polemical strategy or editorial selection).

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right about the observation, every scholar working on Israelite religion needs to explain this pattern. If he's right about the explanation, the entire evolutionary model — which assumes Israel gradually shed pagan beliefs it once held — collapses.

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone in the critical tradition assumes the biblical writers knew pagan religion and polemically misrepresented it. Mark S. Smith, in *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, argues extensively that Israelite religion shared mythological categories with Ugaritic/Canaanite religion — the divine council, theomachy (divine combat), the divine family. Thomas Römer (*The Invention of God*) traces the gradual emergence of monotheism from polytheistic roots and treats biblical anti-idolatry polemic as a late development, not evidence of early distinctiveness. William Dever's archaeological work documents extensive continuity between Israelite and Canaanite material religion.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical writers knew pagan religion perfectly well and chose to misrepresent it. Reducing pagan gods to "wood and stone" is a deliberate polemical strategy — a form of satirical degradation that refuses to dignify pagan beliefs by engaging with them seriously. This is the standard critical reading: the prophets weren't ignorant; they were rhetorically effective. See especially the satirical tone of Isaiah 44:9-20, which Kaufmann himself quotes at length (lines 426-441) — the humor depends on the audience recognizing the absurdity of idol-making, which in turn suggests familiarity with how idols were actually made and worshipped.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann has identified a genuinely interesting pattern that deserves more attention than most scholars give it. The Bible really IS strangely silent about pagan mythology — it never names Marduk killing Tiamat, never describes Baal's battle with Yamm, never mentions the death and resurrection of Baal. For a corpus supposedly engaged in a life-or-death struggle with paganism, this silence is notable. BUT — and this is a massive but — Kaufmann's leap from "the Bible doesn't describe pagan mythology" to "Israelites didn't know pagan mythology" is a non-sequitur of staggering proportions. The Bible is elite literature produced by a specific scribal/theological class with a specific agenda. Its silence about pagan mythology tells us about the interests and rhetorical strategies of its authors, not about what ordinary Israelites knew or believed. Kaufmann treats the Bible as a transparent window onto Israelite consciousness rather than as a carefully constructed theological document. This is the foundational methodological flaw of the entire book.

## Claim 2: The Bible consistently characterizes paganism as fetishism — the worship of "wood and stone"

**Author's claim:** "Observe now what is said regarding the worship of the 'host of heaven.' Several of the named gods—Ashtoreth (Ishtar), Bel, Marduk, Nebo, etc.—are known from pagan sources to have been astral deities, yet not once does the Bible connect them with the worship of the 'host of heaven'" (lines 139-142). The Bible treats the "host" and the "idols" as two distinct categories — never linking named gods to their natural or mythological functions. Across all biblical corpora, "the sole argument advanced against pagan religion is that it is a fetishistic worship of 'wood and stone'" (lines 292-293).

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann provides an extensive survey:

- **Narratives:** The Dagon story (1 Sam 5) is the model — Dagon is a statue that falls and breaks; not even the Philistines suggest the fall of the image portends anything for the living god (lines 227-242).
- **Prophets:** Amos speaks of gods that Israelites "made" for themselves (line 320). Hosea parallels "Baals" with "graven images" (line 333). Isaiah's *Götterdämmerung* is the twilight of "silver and gold images" — "he makes no allusion to polytheistic beliefs" (lines 351-352). Jeremiah calls the gods "stone and wood" (line 365), "no-gods" (line 366). Even Ezekiel, "fascinated as he is by erotic symbolism, never once utilizes the sexual themes of mythology" (lines 406-407).
- **Second Isaiah:** The classic polemic against idolatry (Isaiah 44:9-18) heaps mockery on the folly of making gods from the same wood used to cook dinner — but "has not a word about the gods or their myths" (line 449). He never contrasts YHWH with the "contentious, lustful deities of the pagans" (line 450).
- **Laws:** "There is no law in the Pentateuch interdicting the belief in pagan gods or the telling of their myths" (lines 467-468). The standing prohibition concerns "making" and worshipping idols (lines 471-473).
- **Deuteronomy:** The folly of idolatry is that its gods "see not, and hear not, and eat not, and smell not" (lines 498). "Not that they are gluttonous and drunkards—but that they 'eat not'!" (lines 500-502).

**Confidence:** HIGH on the descriptive claim — Kaufmann's survey of the biblical data is accurate. The Bible's anti-idolatry polemic really does focus overwhelmingly on the fetishism argument, and really does not engage with pagan mythology in any substantive way. This is a real pattern that any theory of Israelite religion must account for.

**What's at stake:** This is Kaufmann's empirical foundation. If the pattern is real (it is), the question becomes: what explains it? Kaufmann's explanation (genuine Israelite ignorance of paganism) competes with alternative explanations (polemical strategy, monotheistic editing, the Bible reflecting elite rather than popular religion).

**Who disagrees:** No one seriously disputes the *pattern* — the Bible's polemic really is focused on fetishism. Scholars disagree about what explains it. Dever would say the Bible's elite authors deliberately caricatured popular religion they found embarrassing. Smith would say the monotheistic editors suppressed mythological material and replaced it with the fetishism argument. Römer would say the fetishism polemic is a late (exilic/post-exilic) development that retrojects later theology onto earlier periods.

**Alternative reading:** The fetishism argument is rhetorically effective precisely because image-worship was a real, visible, and salient aspect of pagan cult — not because Israelites didn't know about the myths. The prophets attack what ordinary Israelites could SEE: the statues, the rituals, the cult objects. Attacking the mythological theology behind them would have been less viscerally effective. Moreover, the fetishism polemic is a form of satirical hyperbole — like a modern atheist mocking the Eucharist as "cannibalism." The mockery doesn't mean the mocker doesn't understand transubstantiation; it means they've chosen a reductive frame for polemical purposes.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's strongest descriptive contribution. The pattern he identifies is real, pervasive, and genuinely puzzling. The Bible DOES seem strangely incurious about what pagans actually believed. But Kaufmann's explanation assumes that what the Bible *doesn't say* is evidence of what Israelites *didn't know* — and this only works if you treat the Bible as comprehensive ethnographic reportage rather than theological polemic. The fact that the Bible doesn't describe Marduk killing Tiamat doesn't tell us Israelites didn't know the *Enuma Elish*; it tells us the biblical authors didn't think that information served their rhetorical purposes. Silence in polemical literature is weak evidence for ignorance.

## Claim 3: The biblical ignorance of paganism is genuine — not a product of polemical strategy or monotheistic editing

**Author's claim:** "These phenomena go too deep and are too pervasive to be explained merely as monotheistic reworking" (lines 185-186). "Nothing can make plausible the suppression of a polemic against polytheistic beliefs, had such a polemic been in existence" (lines 505-507). Kaufmann argues that if the biblical writers knew pagan mythology, they would have used it — monotheism had no reason to suppress mythological material entirely. Later Judaism and Christianity "saw no harm in stories of God's battles with rebellious angels" (line 193); they could have treated pagan gods as demons or evil spirits. The total absence of such engagement indicates genuine ignorance, not editorial suppression.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann offers several arguments:

1. **Monotheism didn't require total silence about other gods:** "While monotheism could not acknowledge the divinity of the pagan gods, it need not have denied them legendary roles. We have seen that occasionally the Bible does allow them the status of demons; these might have been permitted to play the part of evil spirits or enemies of YHWH" (lines 186-190).
2. **Later Jewish and Christian polemics DID engage with mythology:** "Greek thinkers in their attacks upon the popular religion gave due attention to its fetishistic aspect, but they did not permit this to distract them from combating the popular myths. Nor did the later Jewish and Christian polemics rest content with the fetishistic argument only" (lines 537-540). The biblical approach is different — and earlier — than what we find in Hellenistic Judaism and early Christianity.
3. **Polemical strategy would have exploited mythological material:** "If our author had but dipped into the treasury of Babylonian myths, what a mine of material he would have found for his satires: gods who are born and die, who procreate, who eat, drink, and sleep, who make war on their mother, and crowd like flies around the sacrifice" (lines 452-456). That Second Isaiah never uses this material — when it would have been devastatingly effective — suggests he didn't know it.
4. **The writers attribute their own viewpoint to pagans:** "It is abundantly clear that the writers naively attribute their own viewpoint to the idolaters" (lines 516-517). When Sennacherib boasts of defeating the gods of the nations, the biblical writer has him saying "he cast them into the fire" — "no hint that Sennacherib ascribes his triumphs to the god Ashur who triumphed over the gods of these nations" (lines 527-528).

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann's third argument (why didn't Second Isaiah use mythological material?) is his strongest — the satiric potential of Babylonian mythology really IS obvious, and a writer who knew it would presumably use it. But his other arguments are weaker than they appear.

**What's at stake:** The entire explanatory framework. If the ignorance is genuine, then Israelite religion was non-mythological from the start. If it's editorial/polemical, then the evolutionary model stands — the Bible's silence about paganism is a product of successful monotheistic suppression of Israel's own pagan past.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus treats biblical silence about pagan mythology as a product of editorial activity. The Deuteronomistic editors systematically reshaped older traditions to conform to monotheistic norms. Smith (*Origins*, Ch. 1 and throughout) documents precisely the mythological categories that biblical texts share with Ugaritic literature — but are often demythologized or polemically transformed. The presence of mythological *fragments* (Leviathan, Rahab, the divine council) in biblical texts suggests the material WAS known but was being suppressed or transformed — not that it was unknown.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical writers knew pagan mythology but made a deliberate theological choice to avoid dignifying it through engagement. Mocking Marduk by name would have acknowledged Marduk as a meaningful entity — better to reduce all pagan gods to the undifferentiated category of "wood and stone." This is not ignorance; it's a sophisticated rhetorical strategy of delegitimation through redefinition. The fetishism argument is *more* effective at undermining pagan religion than engaging with mythology would be, because it denies paganism any intellectual content whatsoever.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's strongest point here is the contrast with later Jewish and early Christian polemics — they DID engage with pagan mythology (Philo, Justin Martyr, etc.), while the Hebrew Bible does not. This is a real historical difference that requires explanation. However, Kaufmann's argument from silence is methodologically fragile. The biblical authors might have known pagan mythology and chosen not to use it for reasons Kaufmann hasn't considered: (a) naming pagan gods might have been considered ritually dangerous or contaminating; (b) the fetishism argument might have been more effective with their actual audience; (c) the biblical writers might have been engaging with the *popular* face of paganism (image worship) rather than its *elite* face (mythology) because that's what most Israelites encountered. The fact that the biblical writers don't do what Kaufmann thinks they *should* have done is not strong evidence that they *couldn't* have done it.

## Claim 4: The henotheism/monolatry framework is the wrong question — Israelite religion was never mythological polytheism

**Author's claim:** "It is commonly assumed that the religion of YHWH began as henotheism or monolatry, recognizing him as sole legitimate god in Israel, but acknowledging the existence of other national gods... This view is founded on the tacit assumption that the pagan gods were conceived of identically by both Israelite and pagan" (lines 42-51). Kaufmann rejects this framework entirely: "We cannot ask whether it was during the preprophetic or prophetic age that the religion of YHWH came to deny the reality of the foreign gods. The Bible nowhere denies the existence of the gods; it ignores them" (lines 578-581).

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann's evidence is the entire survey he's conducted — the Bible's consistent characterization of paganism as fetishism rather than mythological polytheism. The henotheism-to-monotheism model assumes that early Israelites believed in the existence of other national gods (Chemosh for Moab, Milcom for Ammon, etc.) as real, living deities — and that monotheism developed by gradually denying their reality. Kaufmann argues the Bible never depicts Israelites holding this belief. The "other gods" are always idols — manufactured objects — not living deities from the Canaanite or Mesopotamian pantheons.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the *critique* of the henotheism framework (it really does oversimplify); LOW on Kaufmann's *positive* claim that Israel never passed through a polytheistic/henotheistic stage.

**What's at stake:** The entire developmental narrative. If Kaufmann is right, Israelite religion was *sui generis* from the beginning — not one more example of the henotheism-to-monotheism trajectory we see elsewhere in the ancient world (e.g., Asshur in Assyria, Marduk in Babylon). Israel didn't *evolve* to monotheism; it *began* with a radically different conception of deity.

**Who disagrees:** The henotheism/monolatry framework is standard in critical scholarship. Smith (*Origins*, Ch. 6-8) explicitly traces this development. Römer (*Invention*, Ch. 4-5) documents the emergence of monolatry in the 9th-8th centuries BCE. The Mesha Stele (9th c. BCE) shows that Moabites conceived of Chemosh exactly as Kaufmann says Israelites didn't conceive of YHWH — as a national god who could be angered and whose favor determined national fortunes. The implication is that early Israel likely thought similarly.

**Alternative reading:** The henotheism framework captures something real — early Israel DID acknowledge the existence of other national gods. Biblical texts like Judges 11:24 (Jephthah acknowledging Chemosh gave land to the Ammonites), Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (YHWH receiving Israel as his portion when Elyon divided the nations), and Psalm 82 (God judging the divine council) preserve traces of an earlier worldview in which YHWH was one god among many. The Bible's later characterization of all foreign gods as mere idols reflects the theological position of its final editors, not the beliefs of early Israelites.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right that the henotheism/monolatry framework can be reductive — it imposes Greek-derived categories (henotheism was coined by Max Müller in the 19th century) onto Israelite religion and assumes that what "acknowledging other gods exist" meant to Israelites was the same as what it meant to pagans. His point that the Bible *ignores* rather than *denies* other gods is genuinely interesting. But he overreaches. The biblical texts DO preserve traces of a worldview in which other gods were real — even if the final editors worked hard to suppress this. The fact that Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (in its original form, preserved in the LXX and DSS) describes Elyon apportioning the nations to the "sons of God" and YHWH receiving Israel is devastating to Kaufmann's thesis. Someone in Israel's tradition knew a polytheistic framework, and the text survived long enough to be edited into monotheistic form.

## Claim 5: No foreign god is ever an active, living character in biblical narrative — only YHWH acts

**Author's claim:** "Just as no foreign god is active in the creation stories, so no god other than YHWH ever appears at work in Israel's early history or in the battles between Israel and its neighbors. YHWH fights Israel's enemies, but no god ever appears as his living antagonist" (lines 202-206). When the Bible describes YHWH "judging" the gods of Egypt or battling Bel and Nebo, "it is always idols that are meant" (line 206).

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann surveys the narratives:

- **Creation (Gen 1-11):** "Precisely in the creation legends (Gen. 1-11), where the bulk of mythological matter is imbedded, paganism is entirely absent; primeval man knows only the god YHWH" (lines 180-182).
- **The Exodus:** YHWH "works judgments" on the "gods of Egypt" (Exod 12:12; Num 33:4) — but these are idols, not living deities.
- **The Dagon story (1 Sam 5):** The model narrative. Dagon is a statue that falls, breaks, and loses its head and hands. "We hear nothing of Dagon proper, Dagon the living god; not even the Philistines are said to suggest that the fall of the image portends evil for the god" (lines 237-239). Even the Philistines are depicted as sharing the biblical view that their god IS the statue.
- **Sennacherib's boasts:** The Assyrian king is depicted as saying he "cast [the gods] into the fire" (line 522) — a fetishistic conception. "There is, of course, no hint that Sennacherib ascribes his triumphs to the god Ashur who triumphed over the gods of these nations" (lines 527-528).
- **Prophetic judgments:** Jeremiah interprets "Bel is shamed, Merodach dismayed" with "her images are shamed, her idols dismayed" (lines 218-219). The gods are their images.
- **The humiliation of idols:** "It is characteristic that instead of fleeing, the pagan gods must be borne away on pack animals, or are carried off into exile with their priests" (lines 224-226).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive observation (the Bible really does depict foreign gods primarily as passive statues); LOW on the inference that this reflects Israelite belief rather than literary convention.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right that even the *narratives* — not just the polemics — depict pagan gods as mere statues, then the biblical worldview is fundamentally different from the pagan one at the level of basic storytelling. This would support the claim that Israelite religion was sui generis.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (*Origins*, Ch. 2) shows that the divine council imagery (1 Kings 22, Isaiah 6, Job 1-2) depicts living divine beings — not idols — even if they're subordinate to YHWH. Lewis (*Origin*, Ch. 6) documents that early Israelite religion conceived of other divine beings. The Bible DOES contain active non-YHWH divine beings — they're just not the *named* gods of the nations. The "sons of God" in Gen 6:1-4, the divine council in Psalm 82, the Satan in Job — these are living supernatural agents.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical narratives' treatment of foreign gods as statues is consistent with the monotheistic theology of their authors, who systematically demoted all other divine beings and denied the reality of foreign gods specifically. But traces of an older view survive — Jephthah's acknowledgment of Chemosh (Judges 11:24), the divine council, the "sons of God" taking human wives. Kaufmann's generalization that "no foreign god is ever active" is true of the final form of the text but ignores the vestiges of an older worldview that the final editors couldn't fully erase.

**My assessment:** The Dagon story is genuinely striking and deserves the attention Kaufmann gives it. It really is remarkable that even the Philistines in this story seem to share the biblical view of Dagon as a statue rather than a living god — this suggests the story was composed entirely from within an Israelite conceptual framework, with no attempt at "native informant" perspective. This supports Kaufmann's claim that the biblical writers genuinely couldn't imagine paganism from the inside. BUT — this tells us about the literary conventions of biblical narrative, not about what ordinary Israelites believed. The Dagon story is a theological satire, not a documentary. Its depiction of Philistine religion tells us about Israelite theology, not Philistine religion. Kaufmann's willingness to treat narrative theology as historical data is his consistent weakness.

## Claim 6: The golden calf story is the Bible's "classic representation" of Israelite idolatry — and it depicts fetishism, not mythological polytheism

**Author's claim:** "In this portrayal of Israel's prototypal sin we have a classic representation of the biblical view of idolatry. The sin is not that the people represent YHWH in the figure of an ox" (lines 264-265). Rather, "They do not give their allegiance to a living god, one of the gods of the nations or of their own ancient pantheon, but to an anonymous image, just now fashioned out of their own trinkets. In this calf, this idol that was not the image of a god, but a god itself, the Bible embodies its conception of Israelite idolatry as fetishism" (lines 270-274).

**Evidence presented:**

- **The golden calf (Exod 32):** The people demand a "god who will go before them" (line 260). Aaron makes a "molten calf" (line 261). The people cry "This is your god, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (lines 262-263). Kaufmann's key interpretive move: they're not worshipping a known deity (Baal, El) through an image — they're worshipping the image itself as a newly created god.
- **Nebuchadnezzar's image (Dan 3):** A late story (Persian/Hellenistic) showing the same conception persists: "idolatry is the worship of an anonymous idol, an idol that represents no god at all, but is itself to be worshiped" (lines 280-281).
- **Continuity across periods:** "Even as late as Persian and Hellenistic times the Jewish attitude toward paganism was determined by the belief that the pagans worshiped idol-fetishes" (lines 282-284).

**Confidence:** LOW on the historical claim (that Israelite idolatry was actually fetishism); MEDIUM on the literary claim (that this is how the Bible *portrays* Israelite idolatry).

**What's at stake:** The nature of Israelite "apostasy." If Kaufmann is right, when Israelites "worshipped idols" they weren't reverting to Canaanite polytheism — they were engaging in a degraded, contentless fetishism that had no intellectual or mythological substance. This allows Kaufmann to accept the Bible's picture of constant apostasy while denying that this apostasy involved genuine polytheistic belief.

**Who disagrees:** The golden calf narrative is widely recognized as a polemic against Jeroboam's calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28-29), which were almost certainly YHWH cult images — not "anonymous idols." The biblical writer is retrojecting the sin of the northern kingdom back to Sinai to delegitimize it. Dever (*Did God Have a Wife?*) argues that the archaeological evidence shows Israelites DID worship Canaanite deities (Baal, Asherah) alongside YHWH — not anonymous fetishes. Römer (*Invention*, Ch. 8-9) documents that the "calves" of the northern kingdom were likely YHWH images, not foreign gods.

**Alternative reading:** The golden calf story is a polemical construct designed to delegitimize the northern kingdom's calf iconography. The author deliberately frames the sin as fetishism — worshipping "the work of hands" — because that's the standard biblical polemic. But the historical reality: Jeroboam's calves were YHWH images, and the people who worshipped before them understood themselves to be worshipping YHWH (the god who "brought you up out of the land of Egypt"), not anonymous fetishes. Kaufmann takes the biblical polemic at face value as historical description.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's method in microcosm — and it reveals the problem. He reads Exodus 32 as a transparent window onto Israelite religious practice, when it's clearly a theological polemic with a specific political agenda (delegitimizing the northern cult). The narrative's portrayal of idolatry as fetishism is evidence of how the *author* wanted to characterize apostasy, not evidence of what apostasy actually looked like. Kaufmann's willingness to treat the Bible's own categories as historical data is both his greatest strength (he takes the text seriously) and his greatest weakness (he doesn't ask whether the text might be polemically distorting reality).

## Claim 7: The prophetic corpus is unanimous — not one prophet engages with pagan mythology

**Author's claim:** "We search in vain: not one word have the prophets for mythological beliefs, not once do they repudiate them" (lines 312-313). "Their whole condemnation revolves around the taunt of fetishism" (lines 316-317).

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann surveys prophet by prophet:

- **Amos:** "Hardly mentions the belief in gods... fails entirely to express himself on the nature of the polytheism which he allegedly leaves behind" (lines 318-323).
- **Hosea:** Even in chapters 1-3, which reflect the Baal worship of Jezebel's age, "none of the distinctive mythological features of the Canaanite Baal are mentioned" (lines 328-329). Hosea 11:2 parallels "Baals" with "graven images" (line 333). Idolatry is "the worship of 'the work of hands'" (line 343).
- **Isaiah:** His *Götterdämmerung* "is thus the twilight of silver and gold images; he makes no allusion to polytheistic beliefs" (lines 351-352).
- **Micah:** "And I will cut off your graven images and your pillars out of your midst; and you shall no more worship the work of your hands" (lines 354-356).
- **Jeremiah:** He "amply sets forth his conception of pagan religion: it is the worship of wood and stone" (lines 361-363). "The 'other gods' are not the mythological beings of authentic paganism, nor even demons, but the handiwork of men" (lines 363-365). His most complete statement: the nations will eventually say "Shall a man make for himself gods, they being no gods?" (lines 368-369).
- **Ezekiel:** Despite mentioning the "lamenting of Tammuz" (line 377), "he has not a word to say about the myths of Tammuz or any other god... He, too, characterizes pagan religion as fetishism. His favorite epithet for the gods is *gillulim* (dung-pellets)" (lines 390-394). "It is most remarkable that Ezekiel, fascinated as he is by erotic symbolism, never once utilizes the sexual themes of mythology" (lines 405-407).
- **Second Isaiah:** The most extensive anti-idolatry polemic in the Bible (Isa 44:9-18) "plays on one theme only: the monstrous folly of believing that idols can be gods" (lines 421-422). Kaufmann asks pointedly: if Second Isaiah knew Babylonian mythology, "what a mine of material he would have found for his satires" — yet he never uses it (lines 452-456).

**Confidence:** HIGH on the descriptive claim (the prophetic polemic really IS overwhelmingly about fetishism); MEDIUM on whether this pattern characterizes ALL prophets or admits exceptions.

**What's at stake:** This is the empirical center of gravity for Kaufmann's entire argument. If the prophetic record — across centuries, across different prophets, across different historical contexts — consistently avoids engaging with pagan mythology, this needs explanation. Kaufmann's explanation (they didn't know it) is one possibility; editorial homogenization, rhetorical strategy, and theological avoidance are others.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars acknowledge the pattern but explain it differently. The prophets were addressing an Israelite audience that was practicing syncretistic worship — combining YHWH worship with Canaanite religious practices. The prophets' target wasn't Babylonian mythology; it was the popular syncretism of their own people. Moreover, the prophetic books as we have them have been edited by Deuteronomistic and post-exilic redactors who may have homogenized the anti-idolatry rhetoric. What we're reading may be the redactors' standardized polemic, not the original prophetic message.

**Alternative reading:** The prophetic anti-idolatry polemic reflects a specific historical reality: Israelite syncretism primarily involved adopting Canaanite *practices* (high places, standing stones, Asherah poles, child sacrifice) rather than adopting Canaanite *mythology* as a belief system. The prophets attacked what people were actually *doing*, not what they *believed*. The fetishism argument made sense because the visible target of prophetic ire was cult objects and ritual practices. The prophets weren't ignorant of mythology; they were focused on praxis.

**My assessment:** The Ezekiel/Tammuz passage (Ezek 8:14) is a significant problem for Kaufmann, and his treatment of it is weak. He asks: "Did Ezekiel or his contemporaries know the myth of the death of youthful Tammuz, the beloved of Ishtar? Or did they know only the pagan rites that Ezekiel mentions?" (lines 378-380). This is special pleading — he's suggesting Ezekiel saw women weeping for Tammuz but somehow didn't know why. More damagingly, he suggests "Is it certain that they were Israelites, and not rather pagan priestesses of the royal cult?" (lines 385-386). This is an ad hoc rescue — when the Bible DOES mention pagan practice, Kaufmann suggests the participants might not have been Israelites, or might not have understood the mythology behind their own rites. This is the kind of special pleading that undermines his thesis. If Israelites could adopt pagan rites without understanding the mythology, then the Bible's silence about mythology doesn't prove Israelites didn't practice pagan rites — which is precisely the evolutionary view Kaufmann is trying to refute.

## Claim 8: The Pentateuchal laws confirm the fetishism pattern — and all sources agree

**Author's claim:** "The Pentateuch also represents pagan religion as mere fetishism, and again there is no difference between sources; all agree in their view of idolatry" (lines 464-466). 

**Evidence presented:**

- **No law against belief or myth-telling:** "There is no law in the Pentateuch interdicting the belief in pagan gods or the telling of their myths" (lines 467-468). The only name-related prohibition is against using their names in oaths (Exod 23:13; line 470-471). The standing prohibition concerns "making" and worshipping other gods (lines 471-473).
- **Images of YHWH treated as "other gods":** "Images of the YHWH cult are assumed to be the product of foreign influence, and a cult involving them is regarded as no worship of YHWH at all, but of 'other gods'" (lines 482-484). This is a crucial point — even when Israelites made images intending them to represent YHWH, the Bible categorizes this as idolatry.
- **Deuteronomy especially clear:** The two categories of pagan cult objects are "the idols and the host of heaven" (line 489). The gods of the nations are "the handiwork of man," "wood and stone" (lines 493-494). The folly of idolatry is "that its gods 'see not, and hear not, and eat not, and smell not'" (line 498).
- **All sources agree:** "There is no difference between sources" — whether J, E, P, or D, the conception is the same (line 465). This uniformity across Pentateuchal sources is itself evidence, for Kaufmann, that the fetishism view is not a late development but an original feature of Israelite religion.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive claim (the laws really do focus on manufacture and worship of objects); LOW on the inference that agreement across sources proves antiquity.

**What's at stake:** For Kaufmann, the agreement of all Pentateuchal sources on the fetishism view is evidence that this view is ancient and original — not a late editorial imposition. If the fetishism view were a late development, we'd expect to find traces of an earlier, more mythologically-engaged view in some sources. The uniformity suggests the fetishism view is foundational.

**Who disagrees:** Source critics would note that the Pentateuchal sources as we have them have all been edited by later hands — the "agreement" Kaufmann observes might reflect the work of redactors who harmonized the sources' views on idolatry. The Documentary Hypothesis doesn't claim the sources had *different* theologies of idolatry; it claims they were composed at different times and reflect different institutional settings. Moreover, the fact that the laws prohibit "making" other gods rather than "believing in" them might reflect the practical nature of ancient Near Eastern law codes (which regulate behavior, not belief), not a distinctive Israelite theology.

**Alternative reading:** The Pentateuchal focus on "making" rather than "believing" reflects the nature of ancient law as behavioral regulation, not theological treatise. Ancient Near Eastern law codes don't typically legislate belief — they legislate practice. The prohibition on "making" idols is a practical prohibition on a visible, actionable behavior. The absence of a law against "believing in" pagan gods doesn't tell us Israelites didn't believe in them; it tells us the law regulated behavior, not thought. Furthermore, the uniformity across sources may reflect the fact that the final redactors of the Pentateuch imposed a consistent anti-idolatry framework on materials that originally may have been more diverse.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's observation about the Pentateuchal laws is descriptively accurate — they really do focus on the manufacture and worship of cult objects. But his inference that this reflects original Israelite theology rather than the practical nature of ancient law is questionable. "You shall not make for yourself a graven image" is a behavioral prohibition, not a theological statement about the nature of pagan gods. The legal focus on practice over belief is what we'd expect from any ancient law code, regardless of the underlying theology.

## Claim 9: The Bible shows no awareness of the connection between named gods and their natural/mythological functions

**Author's claim:** "Had we only the Bible, we should know nothing of the real nature of the 'gods of the nations'" (lines 93-95). Named gods like Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh, Milcom, Bel, Nebo, and Amon are "mere names" stripped of mythological content. "No god is ever styled according to his function or place in the pantheon, as so often occurs in the literatures of Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Canaan" (lines 131-133).

**Evidence presented:**

- **No functional epithets:** Gods are identified only by their nations — "Ashtoreth, god of the Sidonians," "Milcom, the abomination of the Ammonites," "Chemosh, the abomination of Moab" (lines 130-131). Never as "Baal, rider of the clouds" or "Ashtoreth, goddess of love and war."
- **No sexual differentiation:** "Nor is the sexual differentiation of the gods ever alluded to; gods and goddesses are both comprised under the masculine rubric *elohim* (e.g., 'Ashtoreth, the god of [*elohe*] the Sidonians'), there being, in fact, no word in biblical Hebrew for 'goddess'" (lines 134-137).
- **Astral deities not connected to the "host of heaven":** Ashtoreth (Ishtar), Bel, Marduk, and Nebo are known from pagan sources to have been astral deities, "yet not once does the Bible connect them with the worship of the 'host of heaven'" (lines 141-142). The "host" and the "idols" are always treated as distinct categories (lines 143-148).
- **The "queen of heaven" is not identified with Ashtoreth:** Jeremiah 44:17ff mentions the "queen of heaven" (apparently the moon), but "is never identified in the Bible with Ashtoreth or any other deity that the Bible knows by name" (lines 150-151).
- **Ezekiel doesn't link sun worship to Shamash:** Ezekiel sees elders bowing eastward to the sun (Ezek 8:16) but "fails to link this solar cult with that of the 'idols of Israel'... Nor does he give any hint that this deified sun bears any of the personal mythological traits of the Assyro-Babylonian Shamash" (lines 152-155).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive observations — Kaufmann has identified a genuine textual phenomenon. The Bible really does treat named pagan gods in a remarkably flat, de-mythologized way.

**What's at stake:** This is one of Kaufmann's most striking observations, and it deserves more scholarly attention than it has received. The biblical treatment of named pagan gods really IS different from how pagan texts treat their own gods — and this difference requires explanation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars might argue that the Bible's flattening of named gods reflects the monotheistic framework — once you believe YHWH is the only real God, other "gods" are ontologically demoted to mere names or demons. The Bible's treatment doesn't reflect ignorance; it reflects theological conviction. Additionally, some biblical texts DO preserve functional/mythological characterizations — Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (Elyon dividing nations), Psalm 82 (divine council), and the Ugaritic parallels to biblical imagery (El's council, Baal's battle with Sea/Yamm) suggest the biblical authors inherited and transformed mythological categories they certainly knew.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's de-mythologized treatment of named pagan gods reflects a deliberate theological strategy: deny pagan deities any substantive reality by refusing to describe them in terms that would acknowledge their mythological identity. Calling Ashtoreth "goddess of love and war" would grant her a domain, a function, a reality. Calling her "god of the Sidonians" reduces her to a national marker. This is theological polemic, not ethnographic ignorance. The very fact that the Bible knows the *names* of pagan gods means someone in Israel knew enough about pagan religion to identify them — the names didn't invent themselves.

**My assessment:** This is one of Kaufmann's best and most underappreciated observations. The Bible's treatment of named pagan gods really is strangely flat. There IS something genuinely distinctive about how the biblical texts handle foreign deities — they're reduced to national identifiers with no mythological content. But Kaufmann's explanation (genuine ignorance) is less persuasive than the alternative (theological delegitimation through demythologization). The fact that the Bible knows the names of these gods at all presupposes contact with cultures that worshipped them. The question is why the Bible strips them of content — and the answer is likely theological: to acknowledge their mythological identity would be to grant them a reality the biblical authors refused to concede.

## Claim 10: The basic problem is that Israelite religion is non-mythological — and this is what makes it unique

**Author's claim:** "It is precisely this non-mythological aspect that makes it unique in world history; this was the source of its universal appeal" (lines 588-589). "The Bible's ignorance of the meaning of paganism is at once the basic problem and the most important clue to the understanding of biblical religion. It underscores as nothing else can the gulf that separates biblical religion from paganism" (lines 590-594).

**Evidence presented:** This is the culminating claim of the chapter, synthesizing the evidence he's presented. Kaufmann argues that "fossil-remains of ancient myths cannot obscure the basic difference between Israelite religion and paganism" (lines 586-588). The Bible is not just monotheistic — it's non-mythological. There are no theogonies, no divine biographies, no cosmic dramas involving multiple gods. Even the mythological fragments (Rahab, Leviathan, the dragon) feature only YHWH as the active divine being — "There are no active foreign gods" (line 170).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the claim that Israelite religion is "non-mythological" in a distinctive way; LOW on the claim that this was true "from the beginning" and makes Israelite religion absolutely unique.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's ultimate thesis. He's not just arguing for early monotheism — he's arguing that Israelite religion is categorically different from every other ancient religion, not just in its number of gods but in its entire conceptual framework. If he's right, comparative study of Israelite religion within its ancient Near Eastern context is fundamentally misguided — the similarities are superficial, the differences are essential.

**Who disagrees:** The entire comparative project of modern biblical scholarship. Smith (*Origins*, throughout) demonstrates that Israelite religion shares fundamental categories with Ugaritic/Canaanite religion: the divine council, theomachy, the divine family, anthropomorphic deity, temple as divine dwelling. These aren't superficial similarities — they're structural. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism precisely *through* the transformation of these shared categories. Day documents specific mythological borrowings and transformations throughout the Hebrew Bible.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion IS distinctive — but distinctiveness doesn't require absolute categorical difference. Israelite religion shared fundamental categories with its neighbors while developing these categories in increasingly distinctive directions. The non-mythological character Kaufmann observes is the *endpoint* of a centuries-long process, not the starting point. The Bible's non-mythological character is an achievement, not an inheritance. This reading preserves Kaufmann's insight about Israelite distinctiveness while accounting for the evidence of continuity that his absolutist framework can't accommodate.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right that there's something genuinely distinctive about the biblical conception of deity, and the comparative approach can sometimes flatten this distinctiveness into sameness. Israelite religion DID move in a direction that was unusual in the ancient world. But Kaufmann's insistence on ABSOLUTE difference "from the beginning" forces him into a framework that can't accommodate the evidence of continuity. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (YHWH and his Asherah), the figurines, the Ugaritic parallels — these are not "fossil remains" that can be dismissed as insignificant. They're evidence that early Israelite religion was more continuous with its environment than Kaufmann allows. The "non-mythological" character of the Bible is a literary-theological accomplishment, not a primitive inheritance. Kaufmann has mistaken the destination for the point of departure.

**Strongest section:** The empirical survey of the biblical data (Claims 2, 5, 7, 9). Kaufmann has identified a genuine and underappreciated pattern: the Bible's anti-idolatry polemic really IS overwhelmingly focused on fetishism, the treatment of named pagan gods really IS remarkably flat, and the absence of engagement with pagan mythology really IS striking. These observations deserve more scholarly attention than they've received, and any adequate theory of Israelite religion must account for them. Kaufmann's descriptive work is excellent — even scholars who reject his conclusions should engage with his data.

**Weakest section:** The leap from descriptive observation to causal explanation. Kaufmann repeatedly makes the same methodological move: the Bible doesn't discuss X → therefore Israelites didn't know X. This argument from silence is almost always invalid, because (a) the Bible is polemical literature, not ethnography; (b) polemicists have strategic reasons for what they do and don't discuss; (c) the final form of the biblical text reflects centuries of editing by scribes with a monotheistic agenda. Kaufmann acknowledges none of these complicating factors. He treats the Bible as a transparent window onto Israelite consciousness, which is methodologically naive.

**The Ezekiel/Tammuz problem:** This is the chapter's most revealing moment. When the biblical text DOES mention a pagan mythological practice (women weeping for Tammuz in Ezek 8:14), Kaufmann's response is revealing: maybe they weren't Israelites, maybe they didn't understand the myth behind the rite. This is textbook special pleading — when the evidence fits his thesis, he cites it; when it doesn't, he explains it away. The Tammuz passage is a small crack in Kaufmann's edifice, but it reveals the structural weakness of his method. If Israelites could adopt pagan mourning rites without understanding the mythology, then clearly Israelites DID have contact with and adopt pagan practices — which is precisely the syncretism the evolutionary model predicts. Kaufmann's attempt to quarantine this evidence shows the strain his absolutist framework places on the data.

**Methodological note:** Kaufmann's method in this chapter is essentially circular in a subtle way. He assumes that the Bible's portrait of paganism is accurate — that when the Bible says pagans worship "wood and stone," this reflects what Israelites genuinely believed about paganism. Then he uses this "accurate" portrayal as evidence that Israelites didn't know real paganism. But if the Bible's portrait of paganism is a polemical caricature (as most scholars would argue), then the Bible's ignorance is an artifact of its own rhetoric, not evidence about Israelite knowledge. Kaufmann never considers the possibility that the Bible is LYING about paganism — that it KNOWS paganism is more sophisticated than fetishism but deliberately reduces it to absurdity for rhetorical effect. This seems like an obvious alternative that a sophisticated reader should at least consider.

**Final assessment:** Chapter I is simultaneously Kaufmann's most brilliant and most frustrating chapter. The empirical observations are genuine and important. The Bible's treatment of foreign gods IS strangely flat; the anti-idolatry polemic really DOES focus overwhelmingly on fetishism; the absence of serious engagement with pagan mythology IS noteworthy. Kaufmann has put his finger on a real puzzle that evolutionary models need to address. But his explanation — genuine Israelite ignorance, rooted in a primordial non-mythological religion — is a massive overreach. The data supports the observation of a distinctive pattern; it does not support the inference of categorical difference from the beginning. Kaufmann has discovered a genuine anomaly for the evolutionary model, but he's mistaken it for a refutation.
