## Chapter 5: God's Bodies and Sacred Space (2): Difficult Beginnings

### Claim: The fluidity/anti-fluidity tension was present from the beginning
→ [[claim-fluidity-anti-fluidity-tension-present-from-beginning-not-chronological]] | **sommer-bog-5-6.1** | Confidence: high
  The tension between fluid and anti-fluid theologies wasn't a tidy chronological development. It was present from early Israelite religion onward. Some very early texts show anti-fluid tendencies; some later texts preserve fluidity. The Bible is an anthology that preserves THE DEBATE, not a single resolution. Some Psalms combine fluid and anti-fluid language in the same poem. The redactors who combined J/E and P/D into the Torah deliberately preserved both theologies rather than harmonizing.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-je-sources-preserved-ane-fluidity-model-yhwh-multiple-manifestations]], [[claim-priestly-deuteronomistic-sources-rejected-fluidity-model]], [[claim-bible-contains-two-theologies-in-tension-fluid-vs-anti-fluid-monotheism]]

## Chapter 6: The Afterlife of the Fluidity Model

### Claim 1: Fluidity survived in kabbalah — the *sefirot* are God's bodies
→ [[claim-kabbalistic-sefirot-structural-parallel-ane-fluid-divinity]] | **sommer-bog-5-6.2** | Confidence: medium
  The kabbalistic doctrine of the ten *sefirot* — emanations of the infinite God (*Ein Sof*) — represents a structural rediscovery of the ancient fluidity model. The *sefirot* are distinct from each other and from *Ein Sof*, yet they ARE God — structurally identical to ANE gods with overlapping selves. Kabbalah was controversial precisely because it revived a theology that mainstream rabbinic Judaism had suppressed (Maimonides: "God has no body, no form").
  **Depends on:** [[claim-ane-gods-fluid-selves-multiple-local-manifestations]], [[claim-fluidity-model-survived-kabbalah-christianity]], [[claim-priestly-deuteronomistic-sources-rejected-fluidity-model]]

### Claim 2: The Trinity is fluid monotheism — not polytheism
→ [[claim-trinity-fluid-monotheism-not-polytheism-sommer]] | **sommer-bog-5-6.3** | Confidence: medium
  "The doctrine of the Trinity represents a particular version of the theology of divine fluidity." Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: three persons, one God. Three bodies, one divine self — structurally parallel to ANE one-deity-multiple-manifestations. Judaism perceives the Trinity as polytheistic because rabbinic Judaism internalized anti-fluid monotheism so deeply that ANY multiplicity in God looks like polytheism. Sommer's provocative implication: the Trinity is a legitimate monotheistic development; Judaism and Christianity developed different possibilities within the biblical inheritance.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-ane-gods-fluid-selves-multiple-local-manifestations]], [[claim-fluidity-model-survived-kabbalah-christianity]], [[claim-priestly-deuteronomistic-sources-rejected-fluidity-model]]

### Claim 3: Sommer's overall reframing of the polytheism/monotheism debate
→ [[claim-sommer-reframes-polytheism-monotheism-evolution-as-fluid-embodiment-shift]] | **sommer-bog-1-3.1** | Confidence: high
  The standard narrative: Polytheism → monolatry → monotheism. Sommer's reframing: Fluid monotheism → anti-fluid monotheism. Israel always had ONE God, but the nature of that God's embodiment changed. J/E: one God, many bodies. P/D: one God, no body. Kabbalah/Trinity: one God, many modalities/persons. Sommer accepts Smith/Romer/Dever's historical data but disputes that the biblical texts are polytheistic, that the evolution was about NUMBER of gods, or that later monotheism is "more advanced."
  **See also:** [[claim-bible-contains-two-theologies-in-tension-fluid-vs-anti-fluid-monotheism]] — the two-theologies thesis this reframing grounds.

## Appendix: Monotheism and Polytheism in Ancient Israel

### Claim: The Hebrew Bible is monotheistic — taken as a whole
→ [[claim-monotheism-well-established-before-exile-biblical-authors]] | **sommer-bog-app.1** | Confidence: depends on definitions
  The canonical texts teach that Yhwh alone is God. The evidence for "polytheism" is mostly from what some Israelites practiced — not what the canonical texts teach. Popular practice and canonical theology are different things. Sommer stands WITH Smith/Romer/Dever on the historical-critical method, but rejects their conclusion that Israelite religion was polytheistic. For Sommer, early Israelite religion was FLUID MONOTHEISM — one God with many manifestations.

**Sommer's contribution to the four-book synthesis:** He provides the theological sophistication the other three lack. Smith, Romer, and Dever trace WHAT happened. Sommer asks: what did it MEAN to the people who wrote the texts? His answer: they weren't polytheists becoming monotheists. They were monotheists whose God changed from having many bodies to having none.
