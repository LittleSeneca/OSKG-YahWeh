## Chapter 5: God's Bodies and Sacred Space (2): Difficult Beginnings

### Claim: The fluidity/anti-fluidity tension was present from the beginning

**Sommer's claim:** The tension between fluid and anti-fluid theologies wasn't a tidy chronological development. It was present from early Israelite religion onward. Some very early texts show anti-fluid tendencies; some later texts preserve fluidity. The Bible is an anthology that preserves THE DEBATE, not a single resolution.

**Evidence:** Some Psalms combine fluid and anti-fluid language in the same poem. The editorial process that produced the Pentateuch deliberately preserved BOTH J/E (fluid) and P/D (anti-fluid) — creating the canon's inherent theological tension.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The textual evidence of co-existing theologies is clear.

## Chapter 6: The Afterlife of the Fluidity Model

### Claim 1: Fluidity survived in kabbalah — the *sefirot* are God's bodies

**Sommer's claim:** The kabbalistic doctrine of the ten *sefirot* — emanations/manifestations of the infinite God (*Ein Sof*) — represents a rediscovery of the ancient fluidity model. The *sefirot* are distinct from each other and from *Ein Sof*, yet they ARE God. "The kabbalists rediscovered the old idea, though probably without realizing what they were doing."

**The *sefirot* as bodies:**
- Each *sefirah* is a particular aspect of divinity: Wisdom, Understanding, Mercy, Judgment, etc.
- They interact with each other, flow into each other, form a dynamic system
- This is structurally identical to the ANE conception of gods with overlapping selves
- The difference: kabbalah is rigorously monotheistic — the *sefirot* are not "gods" but divine modalities

**Why kabbalah was controversial:** Mainstream rabbinic Judaism had fully embraced anti-fluid monotheism (Maimonides: "God has no body, no form"). Kabbalah revived a theology the rabbinic tradition had suppressed.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the structural parallel. MEDIUM on whether kabbalists were consciously recovering ANE patterns (Sommer says probably not).

### Claim 2: The Trinity is fluid monotheism — not polytheism

**Sommer's claim:** "The doctrine of the Trinity represents a particular version of the theology of divine fluidity." Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: three persons, one God. Three bodies (the Son's incarnation; perhaps the Father's in some traditions), one divine self.

**The implication for Jewish-Christian dialogue:**
> "The Trinity is not polytheism. It is a legitimate monotheistic development of the ANE fluidity model. Judaism rejected this model in its mainstream; Christianity embraced it. Both are monotheistic — they just developed different possibilities within the biblical inheritance."

**Why Jews perceive the Trinity as polytheistic:** Judaism internalized anti-fluid monotheism so deeply that ANY multiplicity in God looks like polytheism. But from the ANE perspective, the Trinity is perfectly intelligible as fluid monotheism.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is a theological claim, not a historical one. Christians have their own theological language for the Trinity that doesn't map perfectly onto Sommer's categories.

### Claim 3: Sommer's overall reframing of the polytheism/monotheism debate

**The standard narrative:** Polytheism → monolatry → monotheism. Israel gradually reduced the number of gods from many to one.

**Sommer's reframing:** Fluid monotheism → anti-fluid monotheism. Israel always had ONE God, but the nature of that God's embodiment changed. J/E: one God, many bodies. P/D: one God, no body (only Glory/Name). Kabbalah and Trinity: one God, many modalities/persons.

**What Sommer does NOT dispute:**
- Israelite religion had polytheistic elements in popular practice (Dever's evidence)
- Yhwh absorbed characteristics of other gods (Smith)
- Monotheism developed over time (Römer)

**What Sommer DOES dispute:**
- That the biblical texts themselves are polytheistic
- That the evolution was primarily about the NUMBER of gods rather than the NATURE of God's presence
- That later monotheism is "more advanced" than earlier fluid versions

## Appendix: Monotheism and Polytheism in Ancient Israel

### Claim: The Hebrew Bible is monotheistic — taken as a whole

**Sommer's argument:** The canonical texts teach that Yhwh alone is God. The evidence for "polytheism" is mostly from what some Israelites practiced — not what the canonical texts teach. Popular practice and canonical theology are different things.

**The key distinction:**
- **What Israelites believed/practiced:** Diverse, often polytheistic (Dever's evidence is real)
- **What the biblical texts teach:** Yhwh alone is God
- **What the texts ALSO teach:** Yhwh had multiple bodies and fluid selfhood

**Sommer's position on the scholarly directory's "counter-positions":** He stands WITH Smith/Römer/Dever on the historical-critical method, but he rejects their conclusion that Israelite religion was polytheistic. For Sommer, early Israelite religion was FLUID MONOTHEISM — one God with many manifestations.

**Confidence:** Sommer's position is internally coherent but depends on several contestable interpretive moves (defining "monotheism" broadly enough to include fluidity; distinguishing canonical teaching from popular practice; reading J/E as monotheistic).
