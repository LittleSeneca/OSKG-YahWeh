# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter X: Summary and Conclusion

The final chapter is part recapitulation, part methodological manifesto, and part theological reflection. It restates the book's central argument — that seal amulets provide the best available evidence for reconstructing the religious symbol system of Canaan/Israel — and then does something surprising: it concludes with a confession. The suppression of Canaanite religion by the deuteronomists is compared to Christian anti-Judaism, and the authors call their own tradition to "humility and to change their ways." This is not a dispassionate academic conclusion. It's a theological statement from within the tradition.

---

## Claim 1: Pictures are NOT more ambiguous than words — they're BETTER at capturing the complexity of religious concepts, which are constellations, not propositions

**Author's claim:** "People who have never concerned themselves with the unique possibilities of words and pictures usually think that pictures are vague and ambiguous, whereas words are precise and clear. It is easy to demonstrate that this judgment is rash" (lines 4-7). "When the issue concerns constellations and relationships, pictures are far more potent than words. So constellations of images depict the essence of numerous narrative structures that expand on their themes but can be reduced to the essential narrative once again" (lines 88-91).

**Evidence presented:**
- The passport photo analogy: a photograph captures physiognomic relationships (forehead-to-nose, nose-to-eyes) that words cannot describe with comparable precision
- Names are "artificial" and "rest upon conventions" that can change easily — "Criminals change their names like shirts" (lines 25-26)
- The Egyptian tree goddess appears with the same form and function across centuries but can be labeled with different names (Nut, Neith, Isis, Ma'at). The NAME tells you almost nothing; the ICONOGRAPHY tells you the function.
- "Yahweh" may have originally meant "he blows" (Knauf) but "no one would want to maintain that this etymology... expresses the dominant aspect of the God of Israel" (lines 38-44)
- "Asherah" — does the term mean a natural tree, an artificial tree, a pole, an anthropomorphic image, a naked goddess, a clothed enthroned lady, a heavenly personal being, or a mix? The name alone cannot answer; only iconography in context can.

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is a philosophical argument, not an empirical one, but it's well-made. The point about names being arbitrary labels while iconography captures functional roles is fundamental to their entire methodology.

**What's at stake:** This is the theoretical justification for the entire book. If pictures are inherently more ambiguous than words, then the text-priority of biblical scholarship is justified. If pictures are actually BETTER at capturing the "constellation" of divine functions and relationships, then ignoring iconography isn't just a gap — it's a systematic distortion.

**Who disagrees:** Text-priority scholars who treat the Hebrew Bible as the primary (or only) source for Israelite religion. The entire guild of biblical theologians who work from texts alone.

**Alternative reading:** Pictures may be better at showing WHAT a deity looks like and what MOTIFS cluster around it, but texts are better at showing what people BELIEVED about the deity — theology requires propositions, not just images.

**My assessment:** Their argument is strongest when applied to specific religio-historical questions (like "was Asherah a goddess or a symbol?") and weakest when extended to theology in general. But for their purposes — reconstructing the religious symbol system of ancient Palestine — the iconographic priority is justified.

---

## Claim 2: The standard reconstruction of Israelite religion is structurally flawed because it relies on two corpora (Ugaritic texts and the Hebrew Bible) that are both geographically and chronologically distant from Iron Age Palestine

**Author's claim:** "The one-sided orientation toward the world of hearing (and reading) has led to the situation that the religious history of Palestine (ca. 1800-500) has been reconstructed predominantly on the basis of two lexical corpora: the texts of Ugarit (especially the mythology) and the Hebrew Bible, and this situation continues" (lines 136-141). "Ugarit is about 400 km (240 miles) from Jerusalem... The production of the Ugaritic texts ended about 1200, thus about the mid-point of the period that concerns us here, and at a time when not a single biblical text had yet been written" (lines 144-152).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Ugaritic textual corpus (14th-13th c. BCE) is 400 km north of Jerusalem and ends around 1200 — making it contemporaneous with the Late Bronze Age, not the Iron Age when Israelite religion actually developed
- The Hebrew Bible was "recopied, adapted, and purged over the centuries" — even preexilic fragments are embedded in post-exilic redactions
- "Trying to make sense of the symbol system of ninth- or seventh-century Palestine with the aid of texts from Ugarit is extremely problematic. Frequently, these can offer nothing more than 'parallels'" (lines 153-156)
- The biblical texts cannot produce consensus reconstructions "given the contemporary views about the uniqueness and age of these texts" (lines 161-162)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The geographic and chronological gaps are indisputable. The question is whether those gaps matter — and their entire book is the argument that they do.

**What's at stake:** If accepted, this critique undermines the methodology of Smith, Cross, Day, and the entire Ugaritic-studies-to-Hebrew-Bible pipeline. It doesn't mean Ugaritic texts are irrelevant, but it means they cannot be the PRIMARY evidence for Iron Age Israelite religion.

**Who disagrees:** Everyone who built their career on Ugaritic-Hebrew Bible comparisons. Smith (who acknowledges the missing "puzzle pieces"), Cross, Clifford, Miller, Coogan. They would argue that Ugarit preserves the closest available West Semitic mythology and that the conceptual continuity across the Late Bronze/Iron Age transition justifies the comparison.

**Alternative reading:** The authors overstate the problem. Ugaritic religion and Israelite religion share a common Canaanite heritage. The geographic distance is real but the cultural distance may be less than they claim. And we simply don't have Iron Age Canaanite mythological texts — Ugarit is the best we've got.

**My assessment:** This is the book's most aggressive methodological claim, repeated here with full force. It's partly a turf war (iconographers vs. text scholars) but the critique is legitimate. The problem is that without Ugaritic texts, we lose the only detailed mythological narratives from the West Semitic world. The iconography can show us WHICH symbols clustered together but not the STORIES told about them. The ideal approach — as Keel & Uehlinger themselves practice — uses both, with iconography as the primary chronological anchor and texts as supplementary.

## Claim 3: There was never a distinctive "Yahweh iconography" — Yahweh was depicted THROUGH the imagery of other deities, not with his own unique visual vocabulary

**Author's claim:** "We do not believe that there was an actual Yahweh iconography in Israel and Judah. This does not mean that Israelite and Judean women and men would not have recognized an appropriate portrayal of their god in one or another of the pictures presented here" (lines 764-768).

**Evidence presented:**
- Throughout the book, they have identified solar motifs (winged disk, scarab, falcon), celestial motifs (four-winged figures, stars), and royal motifs (cartouche, lion, cherub throne) as the visual vocabulary of Yahwism
- But none of these are UNIQUE to Yahweh — they're borrowed from Egyptian, Phoenician, and Syrian repertoires
- The four-winged Baal figure becomes interchangeable with the four-winged scarab (sun god symbol) — the SAME image could represent either Baal or the sun god, and in Israel either could represent Yahweh
- "Yahweh's 'essence,' described by an assortment of widely varying images that have been brought together, was quite open to expansion" (lines 790-794)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the logical conclusion of their entire survey. If Yahweh had a distinctive iconography, they would have found it. They didn't.

**What's at stake:** This is a different way of saying what Smith calls "convergence" and what Römer describes as Yahweh's "absorption" of other gods' traits. The iconographic evidence shows that Yahweh didn't REPLACE other gods' imagery — he ABSORBED it. The same solar disk that represented Re in Egypt and Baalshamem in Phoenicia represented Yahweh in Israel. The visual vocabulary was shared; only the name was different.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who think they CAN identify Yahweh-specific iconography — e.g., the "Yahweh and his Asherah" reading of the Kuntillet Ajrud pithos (rejected in Ch VII), or attempts to identify specific seal motifs as Yahwistic.

**Alternative reading:** The lack of distinctive Yahweh iconography could reflect an aniconic tendency rather than absorption — Yahweh was deliberately NOT depicted, and the solar/royal motifs were generic divine symbols, not Yahweh-specific ones. Keel & Uehlinger would partially agree with this — they see the aniconic trend as real — but they'd say it coexisted with the absorption of other deities' imagery.

**My assessment:** This is one of the book's most important conclusions. It means the question "what did Yahweh look like?" is the wrong question. The right question is "which existing divine images did Yahweh worshippers use to represent THEIR god?" The answer: the sun, the winged protective powers, the royal lion, the blessing tree. Yahweh looked like whatever the "Most High God" looked like in the contemporary Levantine symbol system.

## Claim 4: The external evidence "confirms neither the view that there was a fully developed Mosaic monotheism nor the popular view that late and even very late dates for the literature are justified"

**Author's claim:** "These few biblical correlations have shown that the external evidence confirms neither the view that there was a fully developed Mosaic monotheism nor the popular view that late and even very late dates for the literature are justified" (lines 784-786).

**Evidence presented:**
- Their correlations between iconography and biblical texts have shown that many biblical motifs (solar imagery in Psalms, cherub thrones in Isaiah, the "Lord of Heaven" concept) have Iron Age IIB iconographic parallels — they're not late Persian or Hellenistic inventions
- But they also show that these motifs were SHARED with neighboring cultures — they're not uniquely Israelite innovations from a Mosaic age
- The truth is between the two extremes: the biblical texts preserve genuine Iron Age religious concepts, but those concepts were part of a shared Levantine symbol system, not a unique revelation

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is an important hedge against both maximalist and minimalist positions, but their evidence for the "not too late" side is less systematically presented than their evidence for the "not uniquely Israelite" side.

**What's at stake:** This positions the book between the "biblical minimalists" (who date everything late) and the "albrightians" (who trust the biblical chronology). The iconographic evidence supports a middle path: the religious concepts are genuinely old (Iron IIB, 9th-8th c.) but they're not uniquely Israelite.

**Who disagrees:** Both extremes. Maximalists would reject the shared-culture framing. Minimalists might push for even later dates than Keel & Uehlinger allow.

**Alternative reading:** The correlations between iconography and biblical texts could be coincidental rather than causal — similar motifs appearing independently in different cultures. Keel & Uehlinger reject this as anachronistic: "the purely aesthetic use of figurative elements is recent."

**My assessment:** This is a reasonable position but it's stated rather than argued in the conclusion. The evidence for it is scattered across Chapters VI-IX. A more systematic treatment of biblical-iconographic correlations would strengthen the claim.

## Claim 5: Yahweh's identity was "open at the back" — the Thomas Mann metaphor captures how Yahweh absorbed and overflowed into the identities of other deities

**Author's claim:** Quoting Thomas Mann's Joseph and His Brothers: "the old man's ego was not quite clearly demarcated, that it opened at the back, as it were, and overflowed into spheres external to his own individuality both in space and time" — and adding: "we would add here, is the deity" (lines 798-804). "Anyone who examines the pictorial world of Palestine and Israel from the Middle Bronze Age through the Iron Age will have no trouble seeing how deep the roots go, and even the little roots as well, that describe Yahweh's power, his 'colorfulness,' and the rich variety of the ways he shows himself" (lines 812-816).

**Evidence presented:**
- The entire diachronic survey of the book serves as evidence for this claim
- Yahweh absorbed: the bull of El, the solar disk of Re, the winged protectors of Baalshamem, the blessing tree of Asherah, the cherub throne of the Phoenician king
- Each absorption added to Yahweh's "colorfulness" without requiring a new name or a new image

**Confidence:** This is a theological-metaphorical claim, not an empirical one. But as a SYNTHESIS of the empirical evidence, it's beautiful and apt.

**What's at stake:** This is the book's deepest theological intuition. Yahweh's power is not in his uniqueness but in his ABSORPTIVE capacity. The "open ego" metaphor captures what Smith calls convergence and what the biblical texts themselves perform: Yahweh becomes whatever the other gods were, and in becoming them, renders them obsolete.

**Who disagrees:** Theological exclusivists who insist on Yahweh's radical otherness from Canaanite religion. Also scholars who emphasize conflict and polemic (the "Yahweh alone" movement as rejection) rather than absorption.

**Alternative reading:** The "absorption" model might be too irenic. The biblical texts don't just absorb — they MOCK, POLEMICIZE, and SUPPRESS. The "open ego" metaphor captures the absorptive side but not the violent side of Yahwistic differentiation.

**My assessment:** The Thomas Mann quote is a genuinely inspired choice. It captures the paradox: Yahweh is both utterly distinctive (the "jealous" God who tolerates no rivals) and completely composite (made of pieces borrowed from every neighboring deity). The "open ego" resolves this paradox: Yahweh's distinctiveness IS his absorptiveness. No other god in the Levantine pantheon absorbed so much from so many while maintaining a single name and identity.

## Claim 6: The deuteronomistic suppression of Canaanite religion is structurally parallel to Christian anti-Judaism — and both traditions need to confront this

**Author's claim:** "Judean religion encountered a situation in the seventh and sixth centuries before Christ that was comparable to Christian anti-Judaism... It believed that it could establish its own identity only by outlawing everything Canaanite and by calling for the destruction of such items as the holy stones... that their ancestors had once erected" (lines 838-848). "Part of the tragic history of the Judeo-Christian history of guilt is the fact that the people who later became the victims of the holocaust reported in a central passage in their own religious writings that a major preoccupation of theirs was the elimination of other groups in order to ensure the purity and sanctity of their own religious community" (lines 849-854).

**Evidence presented:**
- Deuteronomy 7:2-6, 16, 24-26 — the command to destroy Canaanite cult objects
- The "holy stones" (masseboth) that the ancestors erected (Gen 28:18, 22) became "Canaanite" and were outlawed (Deut 7:5; 12:5)
- The pattern: a religious community defines its identity by vilifying and destroying what came before, then later becomes the victim of the same dynamic
- The comparison to Christian anti-Judaism (1 Thess 2:15; John 8:42-44) and the Holocaust

**Confidence:** This is a moral-theological claim, not a historical one. As historical analysis, the comparison between deuteronomistic suppression of Canaanite religion and Christian anti-Judaism is provocative but debatable. As a theological confession from within the tradition, it's powerful.

**What's at stake:** This is the ethical payoff of the entire book. If the iconographic evidence shows that Canaanite religion was not a foreign corruption but the ROOT SYSTEM of Israelite religion, then the deuteronomistic polemic against "Canaanite" practices was a self-mutilation — a tradition cutting off its own roots and calling them weeds. The authors are calling for the tradition to acknowledge this.

**Who disagrees:** Traditionalists who maintain the deuteronomistic distinction between "pure" Yahwism and "corrupt" Canaanite religion. Also Jewish scholars who might object to the comparison between deuteronomistic polemic and Christian anti-Judaism as unfair or anachronistic.

**Alternative reading:** The deuteronomists weren't suppressing their own roots — they were purifying a tradition that had genuinely been corrupted by syncretism. The "Canaanite" practices they opposed were not ancestral Israelite religion but foreign imports from Phoenicia and Assyria.

**My assessment:** This conclusion is remarkably honest for scholars working in a Catholic university context (Fribourg). They're not just describing the evidence — they're drawing moral consequences from it. The comparison to the Holocaust is handled with care (they call it "relatively speaking, a less harmful fall into sin") but it's still striking. This is what distinguishes Keel & Uehlinger from purely descriptive scholarship: they have a theological stake in the outcome.

## Chapter X Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Pictures better than words for constellations | HIGH | Overstatement — words do some things pictures can't |
| 2: Ugarit + Bible = structurally flawed basis | HIGH | Already argued throughout the book |
| 3: No distinctive Yahweh iconography | HIGH | Future discovery of a labeled Yahweh image |
| 4: Evidence rejects both Mosaic monotheism and extreme minimalism | MEDIUM-HIGH | Needs more systematic biblical correlation |
| 5: Yahweh's "open ego" — absorptive identity | N/A (theological) | Philosophical disagreement about divine identity |
| 6: Deuteronomistic suppression parallels anti-Judaism | N/A (moral-theological) | Historical anachronism objection |

**Strongest section:** The Thomas Mann "open ego" passage (§248). This is the most theologically sophisticated moment in the book and captures the central finding better than any academic formulation could.

**Weakest section:** The period-by-period recapitulation (§§231a-243) is useful as a reference but adds little new analysis.

**Most surprising section:** The feminist-theological conclusion with its Holocaust comparison. This is not what you expect from a book about stamp seals.

**The book's final word:** "Here all are called to humility and to change their ways" (line 854). The last sentence is not an academic conclusion but a call to repentance — addressed to the tradition that produced both the Hebrew Bible and the scholars studying it.
