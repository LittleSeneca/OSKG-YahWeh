# Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion

This is the chapter where Kaufmann tells his positive story. Having argued that monotheism predates the classical prophets, he must now explain WHERE it came from. His answer: a revolutionary revelation to Moses at Sinai — the "monotheistic revolution." This is Kaufmann at his most theologically transparent: the "creative genius" explanation is a secularized version of revelation, and his historical method is at its most circular. But the chapter is also genuinely insightful in places — particularly his recognition that the patriarchs were NOT monotheists (an honest concession that creates problems for his larger thesis), and his clear articulation of the qualitative difference between Israelite and pagan narrative.

---

## Claim 1: Apostolic prophecy is uniquely Israelite and coeval with monotheism

**Author's claim:** The "apostolic prophet" — a messenger SENT by God to a people, bearing divine commands, not answering human inquiries — is an Israelite creation with no true pagan parallel. This institution arose simultaneously with monotheism. Moses is the archetype; the "send" verb (שׁלח) is the heart of the prophetic call. Pagan analogues (Mari prophets, ecstatics) are fundamentally different: they lack ideological continuity, moral passion, and the sense of being commissioned by a single absolute divine will rather than embodying a personal charism.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann surveys the prophetic call narratives (Moses, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel), noting the dominance of "send" language. He contrasts with pagan manticism where the prophet answers men's inquiries rather than bearing God's unsolicited word. He engages the Mari texts in a lengthy footnote (pp. 215-216) and dismisses Noth's claim that they level the qualitative difference — pagan messenger-prophets are "peripheral," lacking the ideological and moral content of Israelite prophecy.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the descriptive observation that Israelite prophetic literature emphasizes divine sending in a distinctive way; MEDIUM-LOW on the claim of absolute uniqueness. The Mari texts DO show messengers sent by gods with unsolicited messages — Kaufmann's dismissal is too quick.

**What's at stake:** If apostolic prophecy is unique to Israel and coeval with monotheism, then the monotheistic revolution happened early and was institutionalized from the start. If the institution developed gradually from Canaanite/ANE models, the evolutionary picture holds.

**Who disagrees:** Noth and subsequent scholarship on Mari prophecy see genuine continuity. The Mari messengers of Dagan and Adad deliver unsolicited divine messages with moral content (rebuke for neglecting offerings and building projects). Kaufmann's distinction between "peripheral" pagan messenger-prophecy and "central" Israelite apostolic prophecy may reflect the Bible's own self-presentation more than historical reality.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite prophecy DID develop distinctive features — ideological continuity, moral passion, historical consciousness — but these developed OVER TIME, not from a single Mosaic revolution. The shift from clairvoyant/seer to messenger-prophet was gradual, and early Israelite prophecy (the bands of ecstatics, the "sons of the prophets") looked much more like its Canaanite environment than the classical prophets did.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right that Israelite prophecy developed something genuinely novel. But his insistence that this novelty was present "from the very beginning" in Moses flattens the developmental evidence. The earliest Israelite prophets (Deborah as judge, the ecstatic bands in 1 Samuel) look much more like their ANE environment than like Isaiah or Jeremiah. Kaufmann's Moses is a retrojection of later prophetic ideals onto a founding figure.

## Claim 2: The patriarchs were NOT monotheists — and the Bible itself attests to this

**Author's claim:** Despite the Bible's surface presentation of Abraham as a monotheist, the patriarchal narratives contain "indirect" testimony that Israelite monotheism is post-patriarchal. Three pieces of evidence: (1) No apostolic prophecy in Genesis — no patriarch is "sent" with a mission; (2) No battle with idolatry — the patriarchs never reproach their neighbors for idolatry, and there is "no religious contrast between the patriarchs and their surroundings"; (3) The name YHWH was unknown to the patriarchs (Exod. 3:13ff; 6:2f), confirmed by the absence of the YHWH element in early Hebrew names.

**Evidence presented:** The three points above are genuinely textual observations. Kaufmann also notes that the people — the "real bearers of historical monotheism" — did not inherit their faith from the patriarchs; even biblical tradition doesn't claim the Israelites knew or worshipped YHWH in Egypt.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the descriptive observation; this is one of Kaufmann's most honest and valuable moves. He sees what the text actually says and doesn't force it into his larger thesis. The absence of YHWH in patriarchal names, the Exod. 6:3 statement that the patriarchs knew God as El Shaddai not YHWH, and the lack of anti-idolatry polemic in Genesis are all real features of the text that critical scholarship also notes.

**What's at stake:** This claim partially undermines Kaufmann's "absolute difference from the beginning" thesis from the Introduction. If the patriarchs weren't monotheists, then Israelite religion didn't begin as something "absolutely different." It BECAME different — with Moses. Kaufmann is forced to admit there was a "before" that looked continuous with its environment. This concession actually aligns him more with the evolutionary model than he'd like to admit — he just compresses the evolution into a single generation and a single person.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish and Christian readers who take Genesis at face value. Also, scholars who argue the patriarchal narratives contain genuine memories of El worship that WAS proto-Israelite religion (see [[Cross — Preface + Part I — Canaanite Religion and El]], [[Day — Chapter 1 — Yahweh and El]]).

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the patriarchs worshipped El, not YHWH — but wrong that this was "not yet monotheism." The question is whether El worship in the patriarchal period was polytheistic (El as head of a pantheon) or already tending toward monolatry/henotheism. Cross and Day argue that El was the original high god of Israel, and that YHWH was later identified WITH El — meaning the patriarchal "El" was continuous with later Israelite religion, not a different religion entirely. Kaufmann's sharp break at Moses may be overdrawn.

**My assessment:** This is the most intellectually honest moment in the chapter. Kaufmann sees the textual evidence that the patriarchs didn't know YHWH and didn't fight idolatry, and he admits it rather than explaining it away. The problem is that this admission creates a tension he never fully resolves: if Israelite religion was a radical NOVUM with no antecedents, what do we do with the patriarchal El worship that clearly IS an antecedent? Kaufmann's answer ("it wasn't monotheism yet") is descriptive but doesn't explain the continuity — why did Moses' God get identified as the "God of the fathers" if there was no genuine continuity?

## Claim 3: Moses was a creative genius — the monotheistic revolution originated with a single person

**Author's claim:** The spiritual revolution that produced Israelite monotheism "must have been, like all similar events in history, the working of a creative genius and leader of men. Following the biblical saga, we call this pioneer creative spirit by the name Moses." Moses "must have been the first to conceive" the monotheistic idea. The birth of every original idea is "a mystery which defies attempts to account for it."

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann argues by elimination: since monotheism has no roots in paganism, and since it appears in Israel suddenly, a single creative mind must have originated it. He compares Moses to Mohammed — another figure who effected a rapid religious revolution among tribal peoples. The cultural environment (Egyptian technical magic, Hebrew kahin-seer traditions, Canaanite ecstatic prophecy) provided the FORMS through which the new idea expressed itself, but not the CONTENT.

**Confidence:** VERY LOW. This is not an explanation — it's a placeholder for one. "Creative genius" is what you say when you've ruled out evolutionary explanations but don't want to say "miracle." Kaufmann is one step away from saying "God revealed it to him" — and indeed, he almost says this: "He was sent; his God revealed himself to him and let him hear his voice. Intuitive insight took the shape of a prophetic vision."

**What's at stake:** The entire historical credibility of Kaufmann's project. If the origin of Israelite monotheism is a mystery that "defies attempts to account for it," then Kaufmann has abandoned historical explanation for something closer to theology. A historian who says "this can't be explained" about their central explanandum has stopped doing history.

**Who disagrees:** Everyone doing historical-critical work on Israelite origins. Smith traces a gradual differentiation from Canaanite polytheism. Römer traces the step-by-step emergence of monotheism through the monarchy and exile. Even scholars sympathetic to Kaufmann's emphasis on Israelite distinctiveness (Tigay, Sommer) don't resort to "creative genius" as an explanatory category.

**Alternative reading:** The "revolution" was actually a gradual process that took centuries. What Kaufmann attributes to a single Mosaic moment was the accumulated work of prophets, priests, scribes, and reformers over generations. The biblical tradition compressed this long development into a founding moment and attached it to Moses. Kaufmann mistakes the compression for the event itself. The comparison with Mohammed actually undermines Kaufmann: we HAVE contemporary sources for Mohammed and can trace the development of his ideas. For Moses, we have only legends written centuries later.

**My assessment:** This is where the theological commitments become most visible. Kaufmann's "creative genius" is functionally equivalent to "divine revelation" — it's an uncaused cause that explains everything while explaining nothing. The chapter reads like a secularized version of traditional Jewish belief, with "creative genius" standing in for "God spoke to Moses." The structure is identical: Moses received something new, transmitted it to the people, and the people were transformed. Kaufmann's contribution is to translate this into historical-sounding language without actually providing a historical mechanism.

## Claim 4: Israel was monotheistic from the desert period — idolatry was always deviance, not the norm

**Author's claim:** The Torah portrays the desert period as monotheistic. The golden calf was either dedicated to YHWH (not a foreign god) or a fetishistic god-image. The Baal Peor apostasy was partial and punished. "The people as a whole never once suggest exchanging their God for another." National sin presupposes a monotheistic people — idolatry is "a national sin" precisely because Israel is the covenant people of YHWH. "By making Israel enter a covenant with the one God, [Moses] made it a monotheistic people."

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann's key argument is that the stories of desert idolatry are internally inconsistent with the claim that Israel was polytheistic — they depict apostasy as aberration, not norm. The golden calf narrative (Exod. 32) says only 3,000 out of 600,000 were punished. The Baal Peor narrative (Num. 25) commands judges to execute only "those of your men who joined Baal Peor," implying partial participation. The narrative speaks of national sin because the covenant makes the whole people answerable, not because all sinned.

**Confidence:** LOW. Kaufmann is taking legendary narratives composed centuries after the events and reading them as historical reportage. The desert stories are not eyewitness accounts — they're theological narratives produced by later writers who WERE monotheists and who projected their monotheism back onto the founding period.

**What's at stake:** Whether the desert period was actually monotheistic or whether this is a later theological retrojection. If Kaufmann is right, the critical model (monotheism emerged gradually during the monarchy) is wrong. If the critical model is right, Kaufmann is reading theology as history.

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical tradition. The desert narratives were composed and edited during the monarchy and exile. Their monotheistic character reflects the theology of their AUTHORS, not the religion of the historical desert period. The archaeological evidence from the settlement period (1200-1000 BCE) shows continuity with Canaanite religious practice, not a monotheistic revolution.

**Alternative reading:** There WAS a distinctive religious identity that formed in the pre-monarchic period — perhaps a loose confederation bound by covenant to YHWH. But this was likely henotheistic (YHWH as patron god of the confederation, other gods acknowledged but not worshipped) rather than monotheistic. The desert narratives preserve a memory of this formative period but reinterpret it through later monotheistic lenses.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reading of the narratives as evidence of early monotheism is methodologically circular. He takes the stories' own theological assumptions (Israel is a monotheistic people, therefore apostasy is aberration) as evidence for the historical reality those assumptions describe. But those assumptions are exactly what needs to be tested. The narratives could equally well reflect a LATER community's attempt to understand how their ancestors could have fallen into idolatry if the covenant was always monotheistic. Kaufmann's reading assumes what it needs to prove.

## Claim 5: The "new divine drama" — history replaced myth

**Author's claim:** Israelite religion created a "new kind of divine saga" in which the conflict is not between gods (mythological drama) but between the will of God and the defiance of man (historical-moral drama). "With the transference of the divine drama from the realm of myth to the realm of history, pagan mythology evaporated." The Exodus legends — Pharaoh's defiance of YHWH — are the prototype. "There is no polemic in the Bible against pagan myth, for when historical saga was born, the sources of myth dried up and it withered away."

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann points to the structure of the Exodus narrative: YHWH is opposed not by other gods but by a defiant pagan king. The conflict is between divine will and human will. Nature is the instrument of God, not the arena of divine combat. The Bible doesn't argue against myth; it simply replaces it with a different kind of story.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive observation; LOW on the historical claim that this happened all at once with Moses. The distinction between mythological and historical narrative is one of Kaufmann's genuinely valuable insights. But does it describe the ORIGIN of Israelite religion or its MATURE FORM?

**What's at stake:** If the "new divine drama" emerged with Moses, then Israelite religion was a genuine revolution from the start. If it developed gradually, then early Israelite narrative may have been more mythological than the Bible's final form suggests.

**Who disagrees:** Smith would argue that early Israelite religion DID share mythological features with Canaanite religion (divine council, combat myths, theomachy) and that the "historicization" of myth was a gradual process. Psalm 82 (God judging the divine council), the Chaoskampf motifs in Job and Isaiah, and the Ugaritic parallels all suggest that mythological thinking persisted in Israel long after Moses.

**Alternative reading:** The move from myth to history WAS a genuine Israelite innovation — but it happened over centuries, not in a single Mosaic moment. Early Israelite literature (the Song of the Sea, the Song of Deborah, various Psalms) retains mythological features. The thoroughgoing historicization of narrative is a product of the Deuteronomistic school and the exilic period. Kaufmann's insight about the DISTINCTIVENESS of the final product is valid; his claim about its SUDDEN emergence is not.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann at his best descriptively and his worst historically. He has identified something genuinely distinctive about biblical narrative — the replacement of divine combat with historical conflict, the moralization of the divine drama. This IS a real difference between the Bible and, say, the Baal Cycle or Enuma Elish. But he compresses a centuries-long development into a single founding moment. The early poetry of Israel — the Song of the Sea with its "Who is like you among the gods, YHWH?", the Song of Deborah with its cosmic participants — shows that mythological thinking didn't "wither away" instantly. It persisted alongside and within the historical narrative, gradually being transformed.

## Claim 6: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is baseless

**Author's claim:** The theory that Moses derived Yahwism from the Kenites or Midianites (via Jethro) has no support in the biblical text. The Bible consistently distinguishes a "prophetic-only" sanctity south of Beersheba from a "cultic + prophetic" sanctity north of it — no patriarch builds altars south of Beersheba, no Israelite ever returns to Sinai/Horeb to worship, and the desert period is remembered as cultless (Amos 5:25; Jer. 7:22). Jethro is priest "of Midian," not "of YHWH." Moses discovers the holy mountain unwittingly; Jethro told him nothing about it. If the Midianites really worshipped YHWH, "there is no reason why the biblical tradition should have obscured the fact."

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann's strongest argument is his survey of the consistent biblical distinction between southern (revelation-only) and northern (cultic) sacred geography. He also notes that Israel never entered Midianite territory, that Kadesh plays no role in later Israelite worship, and that the Kenites joined Israel and its God — not the reverse.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the negative case (the biblical text doesn't support the Kenite hypothesis); LOW on the positive historical claim (therefore the hypothesis is false). Kaufmann is basically arguing from silence in the biblical text — but the biblical text was produced by Yahwists who had every reason to obscure foreign origins for their God.

**What's at stake:** The Kenite hypothesis is one of the few critical theories that actually SUPPORTS Kaufmann's non-evolutionary model. If Moses imported YHWH from Midian, that IS a kind of revolution — an external introduction of a new god to Israel, not an organic evolution from Canaanite religion. Kaufmann paradoxically REJECTS the theory that would help his case, because his theological commitments require that YHWH be absolutely new — not borrowed from anywhere.

**Who disagrees:** A substantial scholarly tradition (see [[Fleming — Ch 3 — The Midianite Hypothesis — Moses and the Priest]], [[Lewis — Chapter 6 — Origin of Yahweh]], [[Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin and Moses]]). The Egyptian topographical lists mentioning "Yhwʒ of Shasu-land" (see [[Fleming — Ch 2 — Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land]]) place a Yahweh-like name in the region of Edom/Midian in the 14th-13th centuries BCE — exactly where the Bible locates Moses' Midianite connection. The biblical texts themselves contain traces: Jethro presides at a sacrifice (Exod. 18:12), Moses' father-in-law is called both "Jethro the priest of Midian" and "Hobab the Kenite," and the Kenites appear as Yahwists in later Israelite tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The Kenite hypothesis may be correct in a modified form. Perhaps Moses DID encounter YHWH through Midianite contacts, but transformed the deity from a local mountain/storm god into the sole sovereign God of a covenant people. This would preserve both the historical connection to Midian AND the genuinely innovative character of Mosaic Yahwism. The biblical tradition's obscuring of the Midianite connection would then reflect later orthodox discomfort with foreign origins — exactly the kind of theological revision Kaufmann should expect, given his own theory about how the Torah was composed.

**My assessment:** This is where Kaufmann's theological commitments most clearly distort his historical judgment. He rejects the Kenite hypothesis not because the evidence is weak (it's actually reasonably strong for ANE history), but because admitting foreign origins for YHWH would compromise the absolute novelty he needs. If Moses got YHWH from Jethro, then the "monotheistic idea" wasn't a creatio ex nihilo in Moses' mind — it had antecedents. Kaufmann needs Moses to be the absolute origin point, so he must reject any theory that gives YHWH a pre-Mosaic history. But the Egyptian evidence for "Yhwʒ of Shasu-land" (which Kaufmann, writing in 1960, may not have fully appreciated) makes it increasingly difficult to maintain that YHWH had no existence before Moses. The irony is that a modified Kenite hypothesis — Moses transforms a Midianite YHWH into something radically new — would actually preserve much of Kaufmann's revolutionary picture while accommodating the epigraphic evidence. His blanket rejection of the hypothesis weakens rather than strengthens his case.

## Claim 7: The name YHWH — origin unknown, significance clear

**Author's claim:** "All that can be known beyond doubt is the value of the name since the time that Moses brought it to the Israelite tribes. Henceforth, YHWH was the symbol of the monotheistic idea." The etymology is irrelevant; what matters is that from Moses onward, YHWH functioned as the name of the one sovereign God who is both universal Lord and national patron.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann doesn't engage the etymological debate (from hyh "to be"? from hwh "to blow"? causative "he causes to be"?). He simply asserts that the name's "value" — its function as the monotheistic symbol — is what matters, and this was established by Moses.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the functional observation; LOW on the implication that pre-Mosaic meaning is irrelevant. The name YHWH's function as a monotheistic symbol in the biblical period is clear. But WHERE THE NAME CAME FROM matters enormously for Kaufmann's thesis. If YHWH was a pre-existing deity (as the Shasu-land evidence suggests), then Moses didn't create the name — he reinterpreted it.

**What's at stake:** Whether YHWH is a Mosaic creation or a pre-Mosaic deity that was transformed. If the latter, Kaufmann's "absolute beginning" claim is falsified.

**Who disagrees:** The philological and historical scholarship that sees the name's origins as highly significant. The Shasu-Yhwʒ connection ([[Fleming — Ch 2 — Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land]], [[Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin and Moses]]) suggests a pre-Israelite YHWH cult in the south. The biblical tradition itself preserves a memory of this in the Midianite connection.

**Alternative reading:** YHWH was a deity from the southern regions (Edom/Midian/Seir) who was adopted by early Israel — perhaps through Moses, perhaps through a longer process of contact. The name's original meaning (whatever it was) became secondary to its new function as the name of Israel's covenant God. The identification of YHWH with El (the old patriarchal god) enabled the merger of southern Yahwism with northern El worship.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's refusal to engage the origins of the name is strategically necessary for his thesis but intellectually unsatisfying. If YHWH existed before Moses as a deity in the southern regions, then the "monotheistic revolution" wasn't the invention of a new God but the radical reinterpretation of an existing one. This is actually a MORE plausible historical model — religious revolutions rarely create entirely new deities; they transform existing ones. Kaufmann's insistence on absolute novelty forces him to bracket the very evidence that might help explain how the revolution actually happened.

## Claim 8: The Exodus and covenant — historical kernel beneath legendary embellishment

**Author's claim:** There is a historical core to the Exodus and Sinai traditions. The tribes, several thousand strong, escaped from Egypt during the reign of Merneptah (late 13th century BCE). The route led from northern Egypt into the desert of the isthmus, not to the southern Sinai peninsula. The covenant at Sinai involved a ceremony with twelve pillars, sacrifice, blood-sprinkling, and the proclamation of laws — the core of which was the exclusive worship of YHWH. The "book of the covenant" (Exod. 20:19-23:33) and the stone tablets represent genuine early traditions.

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann uses the Merneptah Stele (which mentions "Israel" as a people in Canaan circa 1208 BCE) to date the Exodus to the late 13th century. He identifies Raamses with Tanis in the northern delta and argues the tribes escaped through the isthmus toward Lake Sirbonis. He speculates that an Egyptian pursing force was lost in the treacherous swamps — a natural event interpreted as divine deliverance. He reconstructs the covenant ceremony from Exodus 24.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the general historical plausibility of a small-scale exodus; LOW on the details of the covenant ceremony. Some sort of escape from Egypt by a group that later became part of Israel is plausible. But the covenant ceremony Kaufmann reconstructs is drawn from a text composed centuries later — we have no independent access to what happened at "Sinai."

**What's at stake:** Whether the covenant at Sinai — the founding moment of Kaufmann's monotheistic revolution — has any recoverable historical content, or whether it's entirely a later theological construct.

**Who disagrees:** Minimalists would deny any historical Exodus. But even among scholars who accept some historical kernel, the Sinai covenant is usually seen as a later theological framework imposed on diverse traditions, not a recoverable historical event. The law collections in the Torah reflect the concerns of the monarchy period, not the desert.

**Alternative reading:** A small group of escaped slaves brought the story of deliverance by YHWH into Canaan, where it merged with traditions of other groups (those already in the land, those who never went to Egypt). The covenant idea developed later — perhaps modeled on Hittite suzerainty treaties — as a way of conceptualizing the relationship between YHWH and Israel. The Sinai setting may reflect memories of a southern mountain sanctuary where YHWH was originally worshipped, not a historical legislative event.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reconstruction of the Exodus is actually reasonably sober by critical standards — he accepts a small-scale historical event, naturalistic explanations for the "miracles," and a limited route. His problem is the covenant. He takes Exodus 24 as a basically reliable memory of a historical ceremony, but everything we know about how covenant texts functioned in the ANE suggests the Sinai covenant narrative is a literary-theological construct, not a report. The very concept of a "covenant" between a deity and an entire people — with stipulations, blessings, and curses — finds its closest parallels in Hittite and Assyrian political treaties of the Late Bronze and Iron Ages. This suggests the covenant FORM was applied to the Moses tradition LATER, not that Moses actually conducted a covenant ceremony with written stipulations. Kaufmann is right that SOMETHING happened — but what happened was probably much simpler than what Exodus 24 describes.

## Chapter VI Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Apostolic prophecy uniquely Israelite | MEDIUM | Mari parallels; developmental evidence within Israel |
| 2: Patriarchs were NOT monotheists | HIGH | Actually undermines Kaufmann's "absolute difference from beginning" |
| 3: Moses as creative genius | VERY LOW | Not an explanation; theology masquerading as history |
| 4: Israel monotheistic from desert period | LOW | Circular reasoning; reads theology as history |
| 5: History replaced myth | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptive); LOW (historical) | Retention of mythological features in early biblical poetry |
| 6: Kenite hypothesis baseless | MEDIUM (negative); LOW (positive) | Shasu-Yhwʒ evidence; biblical traces of Midianite connection |
| 7: Name YHWH — origin irrelevant | MEDIUM | Pre-Mosaic YHWH evidence falsifies "absolute beginning" |
| 8: Historical Exodus + covenant | MEDIUM (Exodus); LOW (covenant details) | ANE treaty parallels suggest covenant form is late |

**Strongest section:** Claim 2 (the patriarchs weren't monotheists) — Kaufmann's most honest piece of textual observation, and one that ironically creates productive tension with his larger thesis. Also Claim 5 descriptively — the distinction between mythological and historical narrative is a genuine insight, even if Kaufmann misdates its emergence.

**Weakest section:** Claim 3 (Moses as creative genius) — this is where the theological scaffolding is most visible. "Creative genius" is a secularized way of saying "divine revelation," and it explains nothing. Kaufmann simultaneously insists that monotheism had "no roots in paganism" while acknowledging that its cultural FORMS (Egyptian magic, Hebrew kahin-seership, Canaanite ecstasy) all came from the environment. The content/form distinction won't bear the weight he places on it.

**Methodological assessment:** This chapter reveals the fundamental circularity of Kaufmann's method. He argues that the Torah is early and reliable, then uses the Torah's account of Moses to reconstruct the origins of Israelite religion, then uses THAT reconstruction to confirm that the Torah's picture is essentially correct. The biblical text is both the object of investigation and the primary source of evidence — with no external controls. When Kaufmann says "we call this pioneer creative spirit by the name Moses," he's being more candid than he realizes: he's following the biblical saga, not doing independent historical investigation.

The chapter is also notable for what it DOESN'T address. Kaufmann never explains how Moses' monotheistic idea spread from one man to an entire people — the mechanism is simply asserted (covenant ceremony, prophetic authority). He never addresses the problem of transmission: how did the Mosaic revolution survive the centuries between Moses and the earliest biblical texts? He never engages the archaeological evidence that would later (Kuntillet Ajrud, the Judean pillar figurines) complicate his picture of a monotheistic popular religion. Some of this is understandable given the book's date (1960), but the methodological problem is deeper than missing data.

**Theological commitments:** Kaufmann is writing as a Jewish theologian using historical tools, not as a historian examining Jewish theology. His Moses is a secularized version of the traditional Moses — the recipient of revelation, the founder of the covenant, the archetypal prophet. What Kaufmann adds is the language of "creative genius" and "historical transformation." But the structure is identical: a single man, a single moment, a single revelation, a transformed people. This is faith seeking historical expression, not history testing faith.

**Comparison with Smith:** The contrast between Kaufmann and Mark S. Smith is the deepest methodological divide in the study of Israelite religion. Smith (see [[Smith Origins — Ch7 — El Yahweh and the Original God of Israel]]) sees gradual differentiation: Israelite religion emerged slowly from Canaanite polytheism, with YHWH absorbing the functions and attributes of other deities over centuries. Kaufmann sees radical revolution: a single creative mind produced something "absolutely different" in a single generation. Smith's model has the advantage of fitting the archaeological and epigraphic evidence. Kaufmann's model has the advantage of fitting the Bible's own self-presentation. The question is which you trust more as a historical source — and Kaufmann's answer is clear. Whether that answer is methodologically defensible is the central question his project raises.

**Note on the Kenite hypothesis irony:** The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis that Kaufmann rejects would actually STRENGTHEN his case for a non-evolutionary origin. If Moses imported YHWH from Midian, that IS an external introduction — a revolutionary break with Canaanite religious evolution. Smith's model of gradual differentiation from Canaanite polytheism is much harder to maintain if YHWH came from OUTSIDE Canaan. Kaufmann rejects the Kenite hypothesis because he needs YHWH to be absolutely new, not borrowed. But a borrowed-then-transformed YHWH would actually be a more historically plausible version of his "revolutionary" model. This is a case where theological commitments (YHWH must be a Mosaic creation) prevent Kaufmann from embracing historical evidence that would support his larger argument against evolutionary gradualism.
