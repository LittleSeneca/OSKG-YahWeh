# Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion

This is the chapter where Kaufmann tells his positive story. Having argued that monotheism predates the classical prophets, he must now explain WHERE it came from. His answer: a revolutionary revelation to Moses at Sinai — the "monotheistic revolution." This is Kaufmann at his most theologically transparent: the "creative genius" explanation is a secularized version of revelation, and his historical method is at its most circular. But the chapter is also genuinely insightful in places — particularly his recognition that the patriarchs were NOT monotheists (an honest concession that creates problems for his larger thesis), and his clear articulation of the qualitative difference between Israelite and pagan narrative.

## Claim 5: New Divine Drama — History Replaced Myth
→ [[claim-new-divine-drama-history-replaced-myth]] | **kaufmann-ri-6.5** | Confidence: medium
  Kaufmann's best descriptive insight: biblical narrative replaces divine combat (Marduk vs. Tiamat, Baal vs. Yam) with historical-moral conflict (Pharaoh vs. YHWH). But he compresses centuries of development into a single Mosaic moment; early poetry (Song of the Sea, Song of Deborah) retains mythological features. ## Claim 6: Kenite/Midianite Hypothesis Is Baseless
→ [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-baseless]] | **kaufmann-ri-6.6** | Confidence: medium
  Biblical text doesn't support the theory — Jethro is priest "of Midian" not "of YHWH," no patriarch builds altars south of Beersheba, desert period remembered as cultless. But the biblical text was produced by Yahwists who had every reason to obscure foreign origins. Contradicts: [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]], [[claim-midianites-historical-people-northwest-arabia]], [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]], [[claim-yahweh-originated-south-midian-edom]], [[claim-bible-acknowledges-yhwh-foreign-origin]]. Irony: the Kenite hypothesis would actually SUPPORT Kaufmann's non-evolutionary model (external introduction = revolutionary break).

## Claim 7: Name YHWH — Origin Unknown, Significance Clear
→ [[claim-yhwh-name-origin-irrelevant-significance-mosaic]] | **kaufmann-ri-6.7** | Confidence: medium

## Claim 8: Historical Exodus and Covenant Kernel Beneath Legendary Embellishment
→ [[claim-exodus-historical-kernel-merneptah-covenant-legendary]] | **kaufmann-ri-6.8** | Confidence: medium

## Chapter VI Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Apostolic prophecy uniquely Israelite | MEDIUM | Mari parallels; developmental evidence within Israel |
| 2: Patriarchs were NOT monotheists | HIGH | Actually undermines Kaufmann's "absolute difference from beginning" |
| 3: Moses as creative genius | VERY LOW | Not an explanation; theology masquerading as history |
| 4: Israel monotheistic from desert period | LOW | Circular reasoning; reads theology as history |
| 5: History replaced myth | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptive); LOW (historical) | Retention of mythological features in early biblical poetry |
| 6: Kenite hypothesis baseless | MEDIUM (negative); LOW (positive) | Shasu-Yhwʒ evidence; biblical traces of Midianite connection |
| 7: Name YHWH — origin irrelevant | MEDIUM | Pre-Mosaic YHWH evidence falsifies "absolute beginning" |
| 8: Historical Exodus + covenant | MEDIUM (Exodus); LOW (covenant details) | ANE treaty parallels suggest covenant form is late |

**Strongest section:** Claim 2 (the patriarchs weren't monotheists) — Kaufmann's most honest piece of textual observation, and one that ironically creates productive tension with his larger thesis. Also Claim 5 descriptively — the distinction between mythological and historical narrative is a genuine insight, even if Kaufmann misdates its emergence.

**Weakest section:** Claim 3 (Moses as creative genius) — this is where the theological scaffolding is most visible. "Creative genius" is a secularized way of saying "divine revelation," and it explains nothing. Kaufmann simultaneously insists that monotheism had "no roots in paganism" while acknowledging that its cultural FORMS (Egyptian magic, Hebrew kahin-seership, Canaanite ecstasy) all came from the environment. The content/form distinction won't bear the weight he places on it.

**Methodological assessment:** This chapter reveals the fundamental circularity of Kaufmann's method. He argues that the Torah is early and reliable, then uses the Torah's account of Moses to reconstruct the origins of Israelite religion, then uses THAT reconstruction to confirm that the Torah's picture is essentially correct. The biblical text is both the object of investigation and the primary source of evidence — with no external controls. When Kaufmann says "we call this pioneer creative spirit by the name Moses," he's being more candid than he realizes: he's following the biblical saga, not doing independent historical investigation.

The chapter is also notable for what it DOESN'T address. Kaufmann never explains how Moses' monotheistic idea spread from one man to an entire people — the mechanism is simply asserted (covenant ceremony, prophetic authority). He never addresses the problem of transmission: how did the Mosaic revolution survive the centuries between Moses and the earliest biblical texts? He never engages the archaeological evidence that would later (Kuntillet Ajrud, the Judean pillar figurines) complicate his picture of a monotheistic popular religion. Some of this is understandable given the book's date (1960), but the methodological problem is deeper than missing data.

**Theological commitments:** Kaufmann is writing as a Jewish theologian using historical tools, not as a historian examining Jewish theology. His Moses is a secularized version of the traditional Moses — the recipient of revelation, the founder of the covenant, the archetypal prophet. What Kaufmann adds is the language of "creative genius" and "historical transformation." But the structure is identical: a single man, a single moment, a single revelation, a transformed people. This is faith seeking historical expression, not history testing faith.

**Comparison with Smith:** The contrast between Kaufmann and Mark S. Smith is the deepest methodological divide in the study of Israelite religion. Smith (see [[Smith Origins — Ch7 — El Yahweh and the Original God of Israel]]) sees gradual differentiation: Israelite religion emerged slowly from Canaanite polytheism, with YHWH absorbing the functions and attributes of other deities over centuries. Kaufmann sees radical revolution: a single creative mind produced something "absolutely different" in a single generation. Smith's model has the advantage of fitting the archaeological and epigraphic evidence. Kaufmann's model has the advantage of fitting the Bible's own self-presentation. The question is which you trust more as a historical source — and Kaufmann's answer is clear. Whether that answer is methodologically defensible is the central question his project raises.

**Note on the Kenite hypothesis irony:** The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis that Kaufmann rejects would actually STRENGTHEN his case for a non-evolutionary origin. If Moses imported YHWH from Midian, that IS an external introduction — a revolutionary break with Canaanite religious evolution. Smith's model of gradual differentiation from Canaanite polytheism is much harder to maintain if YHWH came from OUTSIDE Canaan. Kaufmann rejects the Kenite hypothesis because he needs YHWH to be absolutely new, not borrowed. But a borrowed-then-transformed YHWH would actually be a more historically plausible version of his "revolutionary" model. This is a case where theological commitments (YHWH must be a Mosaic creation) prevent Kaufmann from embracing historical evidence that would support his larger argument against evolutionary gradualism.
