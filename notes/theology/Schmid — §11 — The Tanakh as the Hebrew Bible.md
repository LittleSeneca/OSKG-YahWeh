# Schmid — §11: The Tanakh as the Hebrew Bible

This is the section where Schmid stakes his controversial claim: the Torah formed in the PERSIAN PERIOD (5th-4th centuries BCE). The canon's three-part structure (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) was a slow development, not an original design. The Torah is the oldest and most important part — and its formation was likely driven by Persian imperial authorization of local laws. This dating has massive implications for the Truth project: if Schmid is right, the Pentateuch's narratives about Israel's origins are Persian-period theological constructs.

---

## Claim 1: The Hebrew Bible as a fixed canon is a post-biblical phenomenon — the texts are "pre-biblical" and become biblical only through canonization

**Schmid's claim:** "Historically speaking, the Hebrew Bible in the sense of a complete list of holy writings, set in terms of its textual inventory, constitutes a post-biblical phenomenon. Or, to formulate it the other way around, the writings contained in the Bible, are, strictly speaking, pre-biblical and only become biblical through the process of canonization."

**Evidence presented:**

- Qumran manuscripts show textual fluidity: "the texts of the Hebrew Bible were not fixed down to the letter at the turn of the era"
- Different versions of the same biblical book existed with "small variations" at Qumran
- The inventory of "biblical" books was not set: at Qumran, Jubilees and Enochic literature "do not appear to have been less important than what later turned out to be included in the standard inventory"
- The term "canon" for the collection of biblical books is first attested for the Christian Bible in the 4th century CE; in Judaism, it only became common in the wake of David Ruhnken's 1768 work
- The acronym "Tanakh" is first attested in the Middle Ages

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Qumran evidence for textual fluidity is well-established. The late dating of canon terminology is not controversial.

**What's at stake:** If the canon is a post-biblical phenomenon, then the canon's theological authority is a function of reception history, not an inherent property of the texts. This is Schmid's liberation move: he can study the theology OF the texts without being bound by the canon's normative claims ABOUT the texts.

**My assessment:** This is standard critical scholarship — not Schmid's innovation, but essential groundwork. It establishes that the "Bible" as a fixed entity is a later construction applied to texts that were fluid in both form and status during their composition.

## Claim 2: The Synod of Jamnia is an academic fallacy — there was no council that decided the canon

**Schmid's claim:** "The scholarship of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries often brought the verifiable evidence of a canon from Josephus and the book of 4 Ezra together with the assumption of a synod in Jamnia, which supposedly decided upon this canon. In 1871 Heinrich Grätz interpreted statements from the Mishnah and Talmud as supporting such a synod. While Jamnia did in fact become a center of Jewish scholarship after 70 CE, there was neither a synod that took place there, nor was the canonical nature of the writings of the Hebrew Bible as a whole discussed, but only the status of Qoheleth and Song of Songs."

**Evidence presented:**

- Grätz's (1871) misinterpretation of Mishnaic/Talmudic statements
- Only Qoheleth and Song of Songs were debated at Jamnia — not the canon as a whole
- Citation of Günter Stemberger, "Jabne und der Kanon," JBTh 3 (1988): 163-74

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The Jamnia synod is now universally recognized as a scholarly myth. Stemberger's work is definitive.

**What's at stake:** The Jamnia myth supported the idea that the canon was established by authoritative decision. Without Jamnia, the canon's formation was a gradual, decentralized process. This supports Schmid's "center and periphery" model of canon formation rather than a "council decision" model.

**My assessment:** Schmid is correct to flag this, though it's now consensus. The Jamnia myth persists in popular literature despite being debunked.

## Claim 3: The canon was originally TWO parts ("Law and Prophets"), not three — the tripartite structure developed later

**Schmid's claim:** "The canon of the Hebrew Bible at the turn of the era appears to have been conceived primarily as a two-part textual entity. The three-part structure that later became familiar is not yet recognizable."

**Evidence presented:**

- Ben Sira's prologue (ca. 132 BCE): "the Law and the Prophets and the [other writings] that followed them" — "other" is an open, generic category, not a defined "Writings" section
- "So my grandfather Jesus...was himself also led to write something pertaining to instruction and wisdom" — Sirach's grandson could ADD to the "other books" category; it wasn't closed
- Qumran texts: "Moses and the Prophets" (1QS 1.1-2; CD 5.21-6.2; 4QDibHam)
- New Testament: "the Law and the Prophets" (Luke 16:16, 29, 31; 24:27; Acts 26:22; 28:23)
- The Psalms were sometimes included: 4QMMT "the book of Moses [and] the book[s of the pro]phets and into Davi[d's psalms]"; Luke 24:44 "the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms"
- But 11QPsa 27.11 identifies David's psalms as "prophecy" — suggesting Psalms were counted as PROPHETS, not a separate third category
- The Ketuvim crystallized AFTER the closure of Nevi'im — a "melting pot of further authoritative literature after the closure of the Nevi'im"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The textual evidence for a two-part canon in the Second Temple period is strong and consistent across multiple sources (Ben Sira, Qumran, NT).

**What's at stake:** The three-part canon (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) is often assumed to be original. If it's a later development, then the canonical shape reflects evolving theological judgments, not an original design. The Ketuvim are the "leftovers" — the books that didn't fit the Torah-Prophets framework.

**My assessment:** Solid. The convergence of Ben Sira (pre-Maccabean), Qumran (Hasmonean), and NT (1st century) witnesses to a two-part canon is persuasive. The tripartite structure was a post-70 CE development as rabbinic Judaism formalized its scripture.

## Claim 4: THE TORAH FORMED IN THE PERSIAN PERIOD — driven by Persian imperial authorization of local laws

**Schmid's claim:** "It seems somewhat concrete that [the formation of the Torah] took place in the Persian period. A number of scholars link its compilation to the so-called Persian imperial authorization of local laws." The conflicting content between Deuteronomistic and Priestly sections "bears clear signs of a theological and legal compromise, so its emergence is more easily conceived with the assumption of external pressure than without it."

**Evidence presented:**

- Chronicles and Ezra-Nehemiah refer to the Torah as a fixed collection (Neh 8:1-8)
- The Septuagint translation of the Torah (~250 BCE) shows it was considered authoritative AND complete by that date
- The Persian Empire "did not have a central system of laws. Instead, the peoples within the empire could live according to their own laws, as long as these laws received Persian authorization"
- "Was the Torah the corpus of laws produced by the Jewish community in the province of Yehud for the Persian administration, according to which they wanted to live? It is conceivable, and the portrayal in Ezra 7 may contain a memory of such a process"
- The Torah contains "quite heterogeneous material" — the combination of conflicting D and P material makes sense as a "theological and legal COMPROMISE" under external (Persian) pressure
- "The Torah has no interest in disclosing its historical origins as a normative text; it receives its authority from the depiction of its connection to the revelation at Sinai"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is the most contested claim in the chapter. The Persian imperial authorization theory is plausible but not proven. The evidence is circumstantial: (1) the Torah's heterogeneous character suggests compromise, (2) Persian imperial policy allowed local law codes, (3) Ezra 7 MAY preserve a memory of this. But the theory has been critiqued for lacking direct evidence.

**What's at stake:** THIS IS THE CLAIM THAT CHANGES EVERYTHING for the Truth project. If the Torah was compiled in the Persian period as a compromise document under imperial pressure, then:
1. The narratives in Genesis-Deuteronomy are NOT pre-exilic sources for Israelite religion
2. The "polytheistic" elements in Genesis (God walking in Eden, divine council language) are NOT fossilized survivals of early polytheism — they're literary creations by Persian-period scribes
3. The Torah is a THEOLOGICAL CONSTRUCT, not a historical record
4. Smith's use of Genesis and Exodus as evidence for pre-exilic polytheistic belief becomes methodologically indefensible
5. Dever's archaeological evidence becomes the PRIMARY source for pre-exilic religion

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date substantial portions of the Pentateuch to the monarchy. The American consensus (Cross, Freedman, Smith) generally dates J and E to the monarchy, with only the final redaction being exilic/post-exilic. The Documentary Hypothesis traditionally dated J to the 10th century BCE. Schmid's position represents the European critical school's maximal skepticism.

**Alternative reading:** The Persian imperial authorization theory explains the Torah's PROMULGATION, not its COMPOSITION. The texts could have been composed earlier and only compiled/authorized under the Persians. The "compromise" character could reflect internal Judean theological debates rather than external imperial pressure. Schmid's reading of the D/P tension as evidence for Persian-period compromise is one interpretation among several.

**My assessment:** This is Schmid's most important claim for the Truth project, and it's also his most vulnerable. The evidence is plausible but circumstantial. However, even if one rejects the imperial authorization theory, the broader claim that the Torah's FINAL FORM is Persian-period is harder to dispute (the Septuagint translation in 250 BCE provides a terminus ad quem). The key question is: how much pre-Persian material survives in the Torah? Schmid tends toward minimalism; other scholars find more. The Truth project needs to weigh Schmid's maximalism against the more moderate positions of Römer and Smith.

## Claim 5: The Nevi'im was closed pre-Maccabean (~200 BCE) — Daniel missed the cut

**Schmid's claim:** "The closing of the Nevi'im section of the canon (Joshua-Malachi) around 200 BCE led to the development of a commentary literature of its own, available especially in the pesharim from Qumran. The fact that the Nevi'im was constructed in the pre-Maccabean period is revealed especially in the book of Daniel's inclusion in the Ketuvim. Daniel is a prophetic book, but it apparently could no longer make it into the Nevi'im because its composition took place in the Maccabean period."

**Evidence presented:**

- Daniel was composed in the Maccabean period (167-164 BCE) — too late for the already-closed Nevi'im
- Daniel had to be included in Ketuvim as a prophetic book that missed the prophets' deadline
- Joshua 1:7-8 and Malachi 3:22-24 (ET 4:4-6) form an inclusio around the Nevi'im — "Remember the teaching of my servant Moses" in Malachi mirrors "Be careful to act in accordance with all the law that my servant Moses commanded you" in Joshua 1
- References to the "Torah of Moses" throughout Joshua-2 Kings are anachronistic but show that the Torah was understood as the standard for history
- Malachi was likely separated from Zechariah to create the number TWELVE (the number of completion) — "Malachi" may be an artificial name from Mal 3:1 ("my messenger")

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Daniel argument is strong: a Maccabean-period prophetic book that ended up in Ketuvim is best explained by the Nevi'im already being closed. The Joshua-Malachi inclusio is a well-recognized literary feature.

**What's at stake:** The closure of Nevi'im around 200 BCE means the prophetic books (including the Deuteronomistic History in the Former Prophets) were considered authoritative by that date. This provides a TERMINUS AD QUEM for the composition of these books — they must have been substantially complete by ~200 BCE. This doesn't rule out earlier composition but limits how late they can be.

**My assessment:** The Daniel argument is persuasive. The Joshua-Malachi inclusio is a genuine literary feature — Schmid's observation about the Torah-of-Moses references being anachronistic is important. These references were ADDED to make the prophetic books serve as Torah interpretation.

## Claim 6: The Ketuvim served as a "melting pot" — and its theological contribution is the "mundane everyday application" of Torah and Prophets

**Schmid's claim:** The Ketuvim formed after the closure of both Torah and Nevi'im. Three hypotheses exist for its emergence: (1) a melting pot for authoritative literature after Nevi'im's closure, (2) an instrument to safeguard tradition in the Maccabean period, (3) an anthology of exemplary Jewish literary genres resisting Hellenistic cultural influence. Schmid adds: "the nature of the theological content of the Ketuvim should be stressed more strongly. This concern...appears in the relationship of these newly formed writings with the Law and the Prophets, in their mundane everyday application."

**Evidence presented:**

- The order of Ketuvim books varies considerably across manuscripts — "at least seventy different orders"
- The Aleppo Codex and Codex Leningradensis place Chronicles at the BEGINNING of Ketuvim (as historical introduction to Psalms); the standard order places it at the END (closing with 2 Chr 36:23b: "Let him go up!")
- Ruth before Psalms creates a Davidic genealogy → Psalms trajectory
- Qoheleth and Song of Songs were controversial — debated at Jamnia

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The "melting pot" description is apt but Schmid's "mundane everyday application" thesis about the Ketuvim's theological contribution is underdeveloped here.

**My assessment:** The Ketuvim section is the least developed part of Schmid's canon discussion. He gestures at a theological rationale (everyday application) but doesn't develop it. This matters less for the Truth project since the Ketuvim are less directly relevant to the pre-exilic religion question.

## Chapter 11 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Fixed canon is post-biblical | HIGH | Qumran evidence is standard |
| Jamnia synod is myth | VERY HIGH | Now scholarly consensus |
| Original two-part canon | HIGH | Convergent witnesses across sources |
| Torah formed in Persian period | MEDIUM | Circumstantial evidence; no direct proof of imperial authorization |
| Nevi'im closed ~200 BCE | MEDIUM-HIGH | Daniel's exclusion is strong evidence |
| Ketuvim as melting pot | MEDIUM | Underdeveloped theological rationale |

**Strongest section:** The evidence for an original two-part canon (Ben Sira, Qumran, NT). Multiple independent witnesses converge.

**Weakest section:** The Persian imperial authorization theory. It's plausible but the evidence chain is: Persian policy of local laws + heterogeneous Torah = compromise = external pressure. Each link is debatable.

**Overall significance for the Truth project:** This is where Schmid delivers his most direct challenge to the American consensus. If the Torah formed in the Persian period (not earlier), then Genesis through Deuteronomy are NOT windows into pre-exilic Israelite polytheism. They are Persian-period theological constructions. The conflicting D and P material — which traditional source criticism interpreted as evidence of earlier, independent sources — Schmid interprets as evidence of a Persian-period COMPROMISE document. This is a radically different reading of the same textual evidence. The dating question is the single most important unresolved issue for the entire Truth project, and Schmid's position represents the maximal skeptical end of the spectrum.
