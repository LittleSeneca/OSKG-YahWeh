## Chapter 6: The Origins and Development of Israelite Monotheism

This is the capstone. Smith synthesizes everything into a historical narrative.

### Implicit Claim: The Four-Stage Model of Monotheism Development
→ [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]] | **smith-ehg-6.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Monotheism developed through four stages (Judges → Early Monarchy → Late Monarchy → Exile), driven by monarchy, writing, covenant theology, international crisis, and social change. It was both "evolution" (from polytheism) and "revolution" (rejection of all other deities).
  Depends on: [[claim-convergence-differentiation-framework-israelite-religion]], [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]], [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]], [[claim-yahweh-solar-language-royal-development]]. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-canaanite-practices-originally-yahwistic]].

### The Four-Stage Model

| Stage | Period | Key Development |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **1. Judges** (1200-1000) | Convergence begins. Yahweh is the central deity of Israel. El, Baal, Asherah are present. Early poetry mixes their imagery. "It is difficult to add more." |
| **2. Early Monarchy** (1000-800) | Monarchy drives convergence. Davidic covenant binds Yahweh to dynasty. Centralization begins. Writing emerges. Yahweh becomes international in scope. Baal tolerated. |
| **3. Late Monarchy** (800-587) | Differentiation intensifies. Baal rejected (9th c. trauma retrojected). Prophetic critiques expand to asherah, high places, dead practices, solar worship. Covenant theology deepens. Writing becomes dominant mode. Hezekiah and Josiah centralize. |
| **4. Exile** (587-538) | Monotheism crystallizes. Second Isaiah: "I am Yahweh, there is no other." Radical redefinition: Yahweh is not just Israel's god but the ONLY god. Old family/state model collapses; new universal model emerges. Ezekiel and Second Isaiah composed as written works. |

### Key Causal Factors Smith Identifies

1. **The monarchy** — both conservative (preserved old practices) and innovative (drove centralization). NOT just the villain.
2. **Writing** — made legal and prophetic norms authoritative for wider audiences. Enabled sustained theological reflection (Ezekiel, Second Isaiah).
3. **Covenant theology** — the Davidic covenant created an exclusive bond between Yahweh and the dynasty; this exclusivity eventually extended to the whole people.
4. **International crisis** — Assyrian and Babylonian pressure made local gods seem impotent. Universal empire required a universal god.
5. **Social change** — the collapse of the family patrimony model enabled the rise of the individual as the primary religious unit, which corresponded to a single god for the cosmos.
6. **Competition with prophecy** — necromancy competed with prophetic inquiry; condemning the dead strengthened the prophets.

### Smith's Critique of Other Models

- **Albright/Bright (early Mosaic monotheism):** Wrong. "Monotheism was hardly a feature of Israel's earliest history." The features they call "secondary idolatry" were indigenous Israelite heritage. The "purest form of Yahwism" belonged to the LATE monarchy, not the early period.
- **Mendenhall:** The monarchy was not the villain. It made "several religious contributions crucial to the development of monolatry."
- **Lang ("prophetic minority"):** Partially right, but the Yahweh-alone position "may not have developed as a purely prophetic position." Other sectors of society contributed.
- **The Exile as sole cause:** The exile was crucial, but "the literary expression of monotheism at a relatively late point... 'overwrites' and obscures the long development involving the earlier phenomenon of monolatry."

### The Unresolved Problem

Smith is honest: "The development lying behind Israelite monotheism becomes impossible to trace back to the point of ancient Israel's historical appearance ca. 1200." The reasons FOR convergence remain mysterious. We can trace the path FROM convergence to monotheism, but we can't explain why convergence happened in the first place.

**Final framing:** Monotheism was both "evolution" (grew out of limited Israelite polytheism continuous with neighbors) AND "revolution" (the eventual rejection of all other deities distinguished Israel from its neighbors).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the broad outline. The four-stage model has considerable explanatory power. But the causal claims (writing drove monotheism, social change enabled it) are more speculative — correlation is clearer than causation.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the core question. If Smith is right, monotheism is a human achievement — a product of political centralization, scribal culture, international crisis, and social change. It's not a revelation FROM God; it's a human construction ABOUT God. Whether God was INVOLVED in this process is a theological question Smith doesn't address. But the historical process doesn't require God as an explanation.

**My assessment:** This is the chapter that most directly engages the "so what?" of the entire book. Smith's four-stage model is elegant and evidence-based. His honesty about what can't be explained (why convergence happened at all) is commendable. The chapter reads as a mature scholar's synthetic reflection, not a polemic.

## Chapter 7: Postscript — Portraits of Yahweh

### Claim 2: Yahweh became a composite deity
→ [[claim-yahweh-composite-deity-product-of-convergence]] | **smith-ehg-7.1** | Confidence: medium
  The biblical Yahweh is a composite built from El's wisdom, Baal's storm, Asherah's nurturing, the sun's radiance, and Anat's ferocity — the product of the same convergence processes the Bible condemns. Priesthood shaped the final form by removing divine sex and death.
  Depends on: [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]], [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]], [[claim-yahweh-solar-language-royal-development]], [[claim-female-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]], [[claim-anat-martial-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]], [[claim-three-stage-convergence-el-yahweh]]. Supports: [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]].

**Smith's argument:** The Yahweh of the Bible is not a simple, consistent character. He's a composite — built from El's wisdom, Baal's storm, Asherah's nurturing, the sun's radiance, and Anat's ferocity. This wasn't an accident. It was the product of deliberate theological work over centuries.

**The processes that produced the biblical God:**

| Process | Example |
|---------|---------|
| **Addition** | Yahweh receives El's titles, Baal's storm language, Asherah's maternal imagery |
| **Paradox** | Yahweh is manifest IN storm and sun but transcends both; is male but described with female imagery |
| **Polemic as absorption** | Condemning Baal's storm-god status while claiming storm power for Yahweh |
| **Recontextualization** | Solar language moved from "Yahweh IS the sun" to "Yahweh CREATED the sun" (Gen 1:14) |
| **Reduced anthropomorphism** | Divine "name," "glory," and "face" replace direct bodily presence |

### What Was Lost

Some Canaanite divine roles were deliberately excluded from Yahweh:

- **Sex:** "Yahweh does not have a consort according to any biblical source; nor does he engage in divine sex."
- **Death:** "Yahweh does not die, even figuratively."
- **Drunkenness and carousing:** El's party behavior at Ugarit has no biblical parallel.

Smith's explanation: the priesthood, for whom holiness meant separation from sex and death, shaped the final form of Yahweh. "For the priesthood there were several levels of cultic purity, and the deity represented the epitome of this hierarchy." The God who emerged was a priestly God — sexless, deathless, and transcendent.

### The Poetic Close

Smith ends with something approaching theology:

> "Yahweh exercised a variety of roles, even sometimes conflicting ones, to the detriment of the cults of other deities. Yahweh sometimes embodied apparently contradictory capacities. Yahweh was seen as manifest in nature and beyond nature... imaged in the human person yet only partially imaginable... sufficiently powerful both to protect and punish Israel... Yahweh was equally a personal deity, whose pain matched Israel's pain... Yahweh's qualities were often expressed in terms largely shaped by the characteristics of other deities belonging to ancient Israel's heritage that Israel rejected in the course of time."

This is Smith's version of "the LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious" — but he gets there through history, not revelation. The composite God is BOTH a historical construction AND a genuine theological achievement. Smith doesn't resolve this tension. He doesn't need to.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the specific claims about what was lost and why. The priestly influence explanation is plausible but speculative.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is where Smith's project lands. The God you worship is a composite of at least four earlier deities, shaped by priestly editors who systematically removed divine sex and death from his profile. Whether this makes him MORE true (a genuine theological synthesis) or LESS true (a historical accident) depends entirely on your theological priors. Smith provides the history. You provide the theology.

## Final Assessment: The Book as a Whole

### What Smith Achieved

1. **Established the convergence/differentiation framework** as the best available model for understanding Israelite religious development
2. **Documented the specific evidence** for Yahweh's absorption of El, Baal, Asherah, and solar imagery
3. **Demonstrated** that practices later condemned as "Canaanite idolatry" were originally legitimate Israelite religion
4. **Provided a plausible historical narrative** from the Judges period through the Exile
5. **Maintained intellectual honesty** about the limitations of the evidence

### What Smith Didn't Achieve

1. **Explain WHY convergence happened.** The most fundamental question remains unanswered.
2. **Resolve the Asherah-as-goddess debate.** His minority position is probably wrong.
3. **Fully address the dating problem.** If Schmid is right that the texts are Persian-period, much of Smith's reconstruction is built on sand.
4. **Engage the theological question.** Smith brackets "what does this mean for belief?" — which is methodologically appropriate for a historian but leaves the reader hanging.

### The Book's Value for Faith Deconstruction

Smith doesn't set out to destroy faith. He's a Catholic scholar doing history. But his conclusions are devastating for traditional theology:

- God has a history. He changed over time.
- The God of the Bible is a composite of earlier deities.
- Monotheism is a human achievement, not a divine revelation.
- The Bible's own narrative (pure origins, later corruption) is exactly backward.
- The practices the Bible most vehemently condemns were once legitimate Israelite religion.

Whether these conclusions destroy faith or refine it depends on what you do with them. Smith's own Catholicism suggests he found a way to hold both. But he doesn't tell you how.
