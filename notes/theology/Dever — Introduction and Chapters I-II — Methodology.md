# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Introduction + Chapters I-II

## Why Dever Matters

Dever is the archaeologist. Smith built his case from texts. Römer built his from texts and historical reconstruction. Dever builds his from the ground — figurines, cult stands, altars, inscriptions, tomb deposits, the material remains of actual religious practice. He repeatedly criticizes text-based scholars (including Smith, explicitly) for ignoring the archaeological evidence that would change their conclusions.

He's also the only author in our Tier 1 list who was raised fundamentalist, became a minister, lost his faith, and converted to Reform Judaism as a secular humanist. His personal journey shapes his voice. He knows what's at stake for believers because he was one.

## Introduction: The Archaeologist's Manifesto

### Dever's Stated Biases (Honest and Useful)

Dever lays out his position upfront:

> "I was reared in a deeply religious family... My father was a fire-breathing fundamentalist preacher... I went to Harvard to study Old Testament theology... only to discover that while I had the necessary dogmatic temperament, I really had no talent for that discipline... theology by now seemed to me a dead end."

He became a nominal Jew, then "not a theist... essentially a secular humanist." He's honest about where he stands: sympathetic to religion as a human phenomenon, skeptical of theology, convinced that archaeology tells us what texts can't.

### His Critique of Smith (Direct and Pointed)

This is the most important methodological passage in the book. Dever says of Smith:

> "Despite his critique of the inadequacies of many other scholars and his acknowledgment that 'synthetic archaeological research has reached a new level of sophistication,' **I regret to say that I find Smith's work is not informed by any archaeological data that would change his almost exclusively text-based approach in any essential way.** Thus he says of the crucial question of understanding the term 'A/asherah' that despite the new evidence since 1990 he is still not inclined to accept the likelihood that it names a goddess."

Dever attributes Smith's resistance to the goddess interpretation to psychology: Smith "rejects the observation of some critics that his interpretation is 'psychologically unprepared to deal with the opposite outlook.'"

> **This is the core disagreement between the two most important scholars in the field.** Smith says the evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess. Dever says Smith is ignoring the archaeological evidence that proves exactly that.

### Key Methodological Claims

| Claim | Implication |
|-------|------------|
| "This is a book about ordinary people... not about the extraordinary few who wrote and edited the Hebrew Bible" | The Bible represents elite, late theology — not what most Israelites believed |
| "I shall not regard it here as Revelation" | Theological commitments distort historical analysis |
| "Archaeologists are poorly equipped to be paleo-psychologists" | We can reconstruct practice, not belief |
| "Folk religion" = what people actually DID, not what scribes said they should do | The real religion of Israel is archaeologically recoverable |
| "Book religion" = the canonical theology of the Hebrew Bible | This was a late, elite construction that never reached most Israelites |

**The two-religions thesis:** Dever argues Israel had TWO religions running in parallel:
- **"Book religion"** — the theology of the Deuteronomistic reformers, centralized, aniconic, monotheistic — what the Bible PRESCRIBES
- **"Folk religion"** — what ordinary people actually practiced at home, at local shrines, with figurines and standing stones and asherah poles — what archaeology REVEALS

These overlapped, competed, and eventually the "Book religion" won — but only at the very end of the monarchy, and only among the elite who produced the Bible.

## Chapter I: Defining and Contextualizing Religion

### Dever's Definition

Dever adopts Hans Penner's definition: "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." Simple, functional, focused on practice. He rejects theological definitions as prescriptive rather than descriptive.

### The Three Fundamental Religious Concerns

Dever argues ancient Israelites had three "ultimate concerns" that religion addressed:

1. **Survival** — "Existing under extraordinarily difficult conditions, in a marginal economy, they knew existentially that they lived in a mysterious, unpredictable, perilous world." No afterlife. No immortality of the soul. When you died, that was it. Religion had to deal with the threat of extinction NOW.

2. **Aligning with the universe** — "It was essential to 'personalize' the numinous powers that ultimately ruled the universe and to 'get on their side.'" The gods could kill you for no reason. You needed them on your side.

3. **Placating the deities** — "The 'care and feeding of the gods'" — sacrifices, offerings, rituals. "Ancient religion was... rather messy. It was in fact 'savage' — a brutal, often bloody, life-and-death struggle, the outcome of which was by no means certain."

> **Dever's religion is not Smith's religion.** Smith traces literary themes. Dever describes a world where religion is about not dying, about getting the gods to make it rain, about your children surviving infancy. This is a much more visceral picture than anything in Smith or Römer.

### The Harsh Realities of Daily Life

Dever devotes significant space to describing what life was actually like — because he argues you can't understand religion without understanding the conditions it addressed:

- Israel was "the size of New Jersey," with a total population never exceeding 150,000
- Jerusalem at its height had maybe 5,000 people
- "Israel was an obscure cultural and historical backwater of the ancient Near East. It would have been long forgotten except for... the Hebrew Bible"
- 80-90% of the population lived in hamlets and villages of a few hundred
- Life expectancy was about 30 years; lower for women
- Malnutrition was common; arthritis in teenagers from stoop labor
- "There were no sanitary facilities at all, not even 'outhouses.' And there was, of course, no running water"
- Literacy was below 5% — "ordinary people could not have read the Bible if they had had it"

**The implication:** The Hebrew Bible was written BY a tiny urban elite FOR a tiny urban elite. Most Israelites never heard it, couldn't have read it, and wouldn't have recognized its theology as their religion. Their religion was practiced at home — figurines, standing stones, asherah poles, offerings to ancestors — not at the Jerusalem temple.

### The Family as the Center of Religion

Dever emphasizes that religion was kin-based and domestic, not institutional:
- The "house of the father" (*bet-'av*) was the basic social and religious unit
- Women were the primary practitioners of household religion
- Family shrines, not temples, were where most religious activity happened
- Ancestor veneration was central (the *rephaim*, feeding the dead)
- "Book religion" was a late male clerical construction imposed on a much older family-based religion that was largely women's domain

## Chapter II: The History of the History

Dever surveys the major approaches to Israelite religion from the 19th century to the present:

| School | Key Figures | Dever's Critique |
|--------|------------|-----------------|
| History of Religions | Wellhausen, Gunkel | Too broad, too diachronic, ignores material evidence |
| Myth and Ritual | Hooke, Engnell | Too theoretical, imposes patterns |
| Old Testament Theology | Eichrodt, von Rad, Wright | Apologetics, not history; "prescriptive rather than descriptive" |
| Sociological approaches | Gottwald, Mendenhall | Better, but still too dependent on texts |
| Recent revival | Smith, Zevit, Keel/Uehlinger, Albertz | Much better, but still under-utilizing archaeology |

Dever's strongest praise goes to Zevit (*The Religions of Ancient Israel*, 2001) for his openness to archaeological evidence, and to Keel/Uehlinger (*Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God*, 1998) for their iconographic approach.

His sharpest criticism is reserved for theologians: "Theology... remains essentially an enterprise of apologetics, and moreover seems to conceive of religion merely in terms of ideas rather than of practice."

## Cross-Reference: Dever vs. Smith vs. Römer on Method

| Scholar | Primary Data | Definition of Religion | What's Recoverable? |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Smith** | Ugaritic texts, biblical texts, comparative ANE | Literary/theological traditions | Beliefs and imagery (textually mediated) |
| **Römer** | Biblical texts, inscriptions, historical context | Historical development of a tradition | "Traces of memory" embedded in late texts |
| **Dever** | Archaeological artifacts, iconography, ethnography | Practice — "what people actually DID" | Behavior, ritual, material culture |

Dever would say Smith and Römer are reconstructing the religion of the 5% who could write. He's reconstructing the religion of the 95% who couldn't.
