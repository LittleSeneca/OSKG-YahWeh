# Kaufmann — Chapter IX: Some Aspects of the Popular Religion

This is Kaufmann's most comprehensive and systematic chapter — the one where he must address the actual content of popular religion and square it with his thesis that Israelite religion was monotheistic and distinct from paganism from the beginning. He covers sin and evil, the national element, priesthood and temple, the popular cult, the dead, Torah morality vs. wisdom morality, the doctrine of retribution, and the Book of Job. The chapter is in many ways the heart of his argument: if he can show that popular Israelite religion was non-pagan at every level, his entire edifice holds. If not, it collapses.

## Claim 1: Israelite religion solved the problem of evil monotheistically — by replacing myth with history
→ [[claim-evil-explained-human-rebellion-history-not-myth]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Genesis 1-11 depicts all evils as consequences of human rebellion, not primordial divine conflict — theodicy through history rather than theomachy. A genuinely illuminating reading, but Kaufmann overstates uniqueness by ignoring Mesopotamian parallels.

## Claim 2: The national element was particularist in form but universalist in content
→ [[claim-national-element-particularist-form-universalist-content]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.2** | Confidence: medium-high
  Israel combined national particularism with cosmic universalism distinctively — YHWH's dominion extended everywhere even though his special grace was restricted to Israel. But the "theoretical universalism" may be evidence of monolatry, not monotheism.

## Claim 3: The Israelite priesthood and temple cult were radically non-pagan — silent, non-magical, defined by awe of holiness
→ [[claim-priestly-cult-non-pagan-silent-non-magical-awe-of-holiness]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.3** | Confidence: medium
  P's temple is "the kingdom of silence" — no incantation, spell, or do ut des. Perceptive about what P OMITS, but P is prescriptive ideal, not descriptive — the silent temple may reflect exilic theology, not pre-exilic reality.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-biblical-cult-transformed-no-mythological-magical-basis]], [[claim-biblical-ban-magic-divination-blanket-monotheistic]] | **Challenged by:** [[claim-cult-places-objects-considered-divine]]

## Claim 4: The popular cult was an independent, non-pagan sphere flourishing OUTSIDE the silent temple
→ [[claim-popular-cult-independent-non-pagan-outside-silent-temple]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.4** | Confidence: medium
  Prayer and psalmody were popular, not priestly — the entire Psalter is pre-exilic. The popular/priestly distinction is analytically useful, but the dating of the Psalter as entirely pre-exilic is tendentious and rejected by most scholars.

## Claim 5: Israel had no ancestor cult — the realm of death was de-religionized
→ [[claim-no-ancestor-cult-death-de-religionized-israel]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  The Hebrew Bible lacks an elaborated afterlife theology — a genuinely striking contrast with Egypt and Mesopotamia. But the claim that this characterized POPULAR religion is undermined by evidence of necromancy and grave offerings; the biblical silence may reflect elite suppression, not popular indifference.

## Claim 6: Torah morality and wisdom morality are two independent, parallel traditions — wisdom preserves the earlier, universal form
→ [[claim-wisdom-morality-earlier-universal-torah-transformed-nationally]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.6** | Confidence: medium
  Wisdom lacks Torah's national-covenantal framework — a genuine literary observation. But the chronological inference (wisdom = earlier) is unprovable and probably wrong; the absence of national particularism may be a genre convention, not evidence of primitivity.

## Claim 7: National covenant morality represents a HIGHER, not more primitive, stage
→ [[claim-national-covenant-morality-higher-stage-not-primitive]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.7** | Confidence: low-medium
  The "sublime prophetic passion" transcends the individual — corporate responsibility is the mature form. But punishing children for parents' sins (Exod 20:5) is arguably more primitive, not "higher." The value judgment is arbitrary and driven by chronological need.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-wisdom-morality-earlier-universal-torah-transformed-nationally]], [[claim-torah-pre-prophetic-earliest-stage-israelite-religion]] | **Contradicted by:** [[claim-evolution-monotheism-to-monolatry-not-reverse]]

## Claim 8: The doctrine of individual retribution is NOT late — it's found in the earliest sources
→ [[claim-individual-retribution-present-from-beginning-not-late]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.8** | Confidence: high
  Individual retribution appears from Adam and Eve onward alongside collective punishment. Kaufmann is simply right — the critical claim that Ezekiel "discovered" individual responsibility is nonsense. But the real question is what happens to the doctrine under the pressure of theodicy, not its date.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-national-covenant-morality-higher-stage-not-primitive]] | **Supports:** [[claim-evolution-monotheism-to-monolatry-not-reverse]]

## Claim 9: The Book of Job represents the triumph of faith over wisdom
→ [[claim-job-triumph-faith-over-wisdom-israelite-theodicy]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.9** | Confidence: medium-high (reading), medium (conclusion)
  Kaufmann's structural reading (the two Jobs, Elihu as transition) is genuinely illuminating. But his conclusion that Job demonstrates "conquest of pagan wisdom" domesticates a more disturbing book — God never actually answers the justice question.
  **Depends on:** [[claim-individual-retribution-present-from-beginning-not-late]], [[claim-wisdom-morality-earlier-universal-torah-transformed-nationally]] | **Supports:** [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]]

## Claim 10: The rift between ideal and reality was social, not religious — classical prophecy was born from social cleavage
→ [[claim-rift-ideal-reality-social-not-religious-prophecy-birth]] | **kaufmann-ri-9.10** | Confidence: medium
  Social exploitation was the prophets' primary target, not combatting polytheism. Kaufmann is right that social legislation was aspirational, but he drives an artificial wedge between "religious" and "social" that ancient Israelites wouldn't have recognized.

## Chapter IX Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Evil explained through human rebellion, not myth | MEDIUM-HIGH | Mesopotamian theodicy parallels (Atrahasis, Erra); overstates uniqueness |
| 2: National element was particularist form, universalist content | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Theoretical universalism" may be evidence of monolatry, not monotheism |
| 3: Priestly cult was radically non-pagan — silent, non-magical | MEDIUM | P is prescriptive ideal, not descriptive; may reflect exilic theology |
| 4: Popular cult was independent, non-pagan sphere outside temple | MEDIUM | Sharp popular/priestly separation is Kaufmann's construct; Psalter dating is tendentious |
| 5: No ancestor cult; death was de-religionized | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaeological evidence of ancestor cult; biblical silence may be suppression |
| 6: Wisdom morality is earlier, universal — Torah transformed it | MEDIUM (observation), LOW (chronology) | Genre convention, not chronology; wisdom literature may be late |
| 7: National covenant morality is the HIGHER stage | LOW-MEDIUM | Values corporate over individual — driven by chronological need, not evidence |
| 8: Individual retribution is present from the beginning | HIGH | Straw man — critics don't claim it was "invented" by Ezekiel |
| 9: Job demonstrates triumph of faith over wisdom | MEDIUM-HIGH (reading), MEDIUM (conclusion) | Whirlwind speeches are nature wisdom, not revelation; book subverts theodicy |
| 10: Rift was social, not religious — prophecy born from social cleavage | MEDIUM | Separates social from religious artificially; ignores idolatry evidence |

**Strongest sections:**
1. **The de-religionization of death (Claim 5):** Even if Kaufmann overstates the popular dimension, his observation that the Hebrew Bible lacks an elaborated afterlife theology is genuinely important and distinctive. The contrast with Egypt is stark and demands explanation.
2. **The reading of Job's structure (Claim 9):** Kaufmann's two-level reading (Job the righteous man vs. Job the sage) is genuinely illuminating and helps resolve the apparent contradiction between the prose frame and the poetic dialogue.
3. **The contrast between Torah morality and wisdom morality (Claim 6):** The literary observation is sound and important, even if the chronological inference is dubious.

**Weakest sections:**
1. **The "silent temple" (Claim 3):** Kaufmann's most methodologically problematic argument. He reads P as transparent historical description, ignoring the possibility that P is idealizing and late. The silence of the priestly cult may be theological aspiration, not cultic reality.
2. **The dating of the Psalter (Claim 4):** Kaufmann's claim that the entire Psalter is pre-exilic is rejected by virtually all scholars and requires special pleading. It's driven by his need to locate psalmody in the "early religion."
3. **The national > individual moral hierarchy (Claim 7):** Kaufmann's most transparently agenda-driven argument. He needs national covenant morality to be the mature form, so he argues for it — but the argument doesn't survive scrutiny.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals both the power and the peril of Kaufmann's method. His close reading of biblical texts often yields genuine insights — the absence of ancestor cult, the two streams of morality, the structure of Job. But his historical conclusions almost always outrun his evidence. He reads theological ideals as historical realities, dates texts to fit his system, and dismisses contrary evidence as "popular deviance" or "vestigial paganism." The chapter is brilliant as biblical theology and deeply problematic as history.

**Key unresolved tension:** Kaufmann's treatment of the archaeology problem is entirely absent. He never addresses the figurines, the standing stones, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, the high places. For a chapter on "popular religion," this silence is deafening. He constructs popular religion entirely from texts — and from texts that he dates to suit his needs. The archaeological evidence that might challenge his thesis is simply not engaged. This is the chapter's most fundamental vulnerability, and it's one that Kaufmann never addresses anywhere in the book.

**What Kaufmann would need to show (and doesn't):** That the popular practices attested archaeologically (female figurines, Asherah worship, high-place sacrifice, standing stones) were not "genuine polytheism" but vestigial, contentless imitation of pagan forms. He makes this argument about the biblical texts' depiction of idolatry, but never tests it against the material evidence. The figurines are not vestigial — they're ubiquitous. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions pair Yahweh with Asherah in blessing formulas — that's not contentless imitation. Kaufmann's entire popular religion thesis rests on ignoring the archaeology.

## Key Tensions with Other Scholars

| Scholar | Kaufmann's Position | Opposing View | Evidence |
|---------|--------------------|---------------|----------|
| **Smith (Chapter 1)** | No genuine polytheism in popular Israelite religion | Deities from Canaanite pantheon were actively worshipped in Israel | Onomastics, narrative evidence, archaeology |
| **Smith (Chapter 3)** | Idolatry was vestigial, not genuine worship of other gods | Asherah was worshipped as Yahweh's consort | Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions, pillar figurines |
| **Day (Ch 5, 8)** | Israelite religion was monotheistic from the beginning | Absolute monotheism emerged only in the exile; earlier was monolatry | Prophetic polemic, archaeological evidence, Ugaritic parallels |
| **Lewis (Ch 10)** | Priestly cult was silent, non-magical, radically non-pagan | Holiness system was ideological tool for priestly power, not theological revolution | P's gradations of holiness map onto social hierarchy |
| **Fleming (Ch 7)** | Yahweh was always conceived as sole God | Yahweh's early character shows features of a local warrior deity | Early poetry, divine warrior imagery, theophany traditions |
| **Römer (Ch 10-12)** | Monotheism was original and popular | Monotheism emerged gradually; exile was the catalyst | Deutero-Isaiah, Persian period context, epigraphic evidence |
