# Kaufmann — Chapter IX: Some Aspects of the Popular Religion

This is Kaufmann's most comprehensive and systematic chapter — the one where he must address the actual content of popular religion and square it with his thesis that Israelite religion was monotheistic and distinct from paganism from the beginning. He covers sin and evil, the national element, priesthood and temple, the popular cult, the dead, Torah morality vs. wisdom morality, the doctrine of retribution, and the Book of Job. The chapter is in many ways the heart of his argument: if he can show that popular Israelite religion was non-pagan at every level, his entire edifice holds. If not, it collapses.

---

## Claim 1: Israelite religion solved the problem of evil monotheistically — by replacing myth with history

**Author's claim:** Paganism, with its plurality of divine principles, had no problem of evil. Israelite religion, positing One God, HAD to explain the origin of evil. Genesis 1-11 is the answer: "In place of myth Israelite religion conceived the historical drama of human rebellion and sin." The legends explain all evils — natural (death, pain), moral (murder, violence), and religious (idolatry) — as consequences of human freedom misused. No primordial evil principle, no theomachy, no metadivine realm. "The Bible does not regard the idolatry of the nations as a sin for which they must be punished... idolatry is the fruit of sin, a punishment, rather than a primary sin itself."

**Evidence presented:**
- Genesis 1 (P) pronounces each act of creation good; man created in God's image
- JE's Eden story: evil is veiled from man but accessible; the serpent seduces but does not compel; the tree is not the source of knowledge generally but of the knowledge of and desire for evil
- Sexual desire appears as the archetypal sin; procreation is not a blessing but the outcome of sin
- Cain brings murder; the Flood generation brings universal depravity; Babel brings the confusion of tongues and the origin of nations
- No story dedicated to the origin of idolatry — it appears after Babel as the consequence of God hiding himself from man
- Paganism is depicted as presumptuous worship of no-gods, rooted in man's boundless arrogance — the Bible goes to "the very heart of paganism, to its magical basis"
- The absence of theomachy (YHWH vs. Rahab/dragon) in Genesis 1-11 is deliberate: biblical legend "did not root sin and evil in Rahab or the dragon — conceived of as a Satanic figure"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive observation that Genesis 1-11 represents evil as the consequence of human rebellion. But LOW-MEDIUM on Kaufmann's claim that this is a uniquely Israelite achievement. Mesopotamian literature (Atrahasis, Erra and Ishum) also grapples with the origin of suffering and human-divine tension, though within a polytheistic framework. Kaufmann overstates the uniqueness.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite monotheism was truly a new metaphysical insight or a variation on Near Eastern theodicy themes. If Genesis 1-11 can be shown to share structural features with Mesopotamian primeval traditions, Kaufmann's claim of radical originality weakens.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who trace Genesis 1-11 to Mesopotamian antecedents (Atrahasis → Flood; Enuma Elish → creation; Sumerian paradise myths → Eden). The uniqueness is real but may be more a matter of theological inflection than absolute novelty.

**Alternative reading:** Genesis 1-11 IS distinctive in its monotheistic framing and its insistence that evil originates in human will, not divine conflict. But it's not de novo — it's a monotheistic recasting of shared Near Eastern narrative material. Kaufmann's "absolutely different" rhetoric overstates what is better described as a profound transformation.

**My assessment:** This is one of Kaufmann's strongest sections. His reading of Genesis 1-11 as theodicy — not primitive myth but sophisticated answer to the problem evil poses for monotheism — is genuinely illuminating. The observation that no story explains the ORIGIN of idolatry (it's a consequence, not a primordial reality) is sharp. But he ignores the Mesopotamian parallels that would contextualize his claim of uniqueness. He reads the biblical text as if it emerged in a vacuum.

## Claim 2: The national element in Israelite religion was particularist in form but universalist in content — and didn't compromise monotheism

**Author's claim:** "Israelite religion was national in its forms and in the scope of its activity from its beginnings to the end of the Second Temple period. Ideally, however, YHWH was not a national, but a cosmic God; the area of his special grace was restricted to Israel, but his dominion extended everywhere." The covenant concept is not the matrix of monotheism; rather, "it was the monotheistic idea that precluded every conception but this." Israel's election is not ethnic superiority — Israel is "the fewest of all peoples" (Deut. 7:7) and the youngest nation. The relation is one of free choice, not natural kinship. Israel's history is "the historical dimension of YHWH's revelation, over against the mythological-magical dimension of paganism."

**Evidence presented:**
- Genesis makes Israel the youngest of peoples; the world existed long before Israel
- YHWH is not Israel's kinsman; Palestine is not his natural habitat; the cult is not the source of his vitality
- "The covenant has no pagan meaning; it does not unite the two parties in any natural or mysterious way"
- Israel is "the fewest of all peoples" — the disparity between exalted God and lowly nation is emphasized, not grudgingly admitted
- The greatest moments of glory are the Exodus and Wandering (signs and wonders), not the conquest
- Intermarriage prohibitions are religious/cultural, not racial — Egypt is favored for historical reasons, Ammon and Moab excluded for the same
- The third generation of settlers could join "the community of YHWH" (Deut. 23:8 f.)
- Gerim (resident aliens) are made equal to native-born in all Torah laws
- The Torah was given to non-Israelites too — they participated in the covenant (Deut. 29:10 ff.)
- The idea of purely religious conversion is foreshadowed in Naaman, who returns to his land but becomes a YHWH worshipper

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive point that Israelite religion contains genuine universalist elements. MEDIUM on Kaufmann's claim that this demonstrates the national element was never a compromise of monotheism. The universalism he identifies is largely theoretical ("the nations SEE YHWH's greatness but remain idolaters") — it doesn't translate into actual mission or inclusion until much later.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite particularism (YHWH as the God of Israel specifically) is evidence that early Israelite religion was henotheism/monolatry rather than genuine monotheism. Kaufmann wants to say: Israel always believed YHWH was the ONLY God; the national focus was just the arena of his special self-revelation.

**Who disagrees:** Römer and Smith would argue that the particularist framework IS the original religion — YHWH was Israel's god among other gods of other nations — and universal monotheism developed later. The evidence for this is the very "theoretical universalism" Kaufmann describes: Israel acknowledges YHWH rules the nations but doesn't expect them to convert. That's monolatry, not monotheism.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann has genuinely identified something distinctive — Israelite religion combined national particularism with cosmic universalism in a way that paganism didn't. But this combination may have developed over time rather than being original. The earliest material (Song of the Sea, Song of Deborah) is purely national; cosmic universalism grows over the monarchic period.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is at his most persuasive here. The observation that Israel's literature consistently emphasizes Israel's SMALLNESS and weakness — making YHWH's favor entirely gracious rather than merited by national greatness — IS distinctive and supports his claim that Israelite particularism was qualitatively different from pagan national religion. But his evidence cuts both ways: the very fact that this "theoretical universalism" is so abstract and non-missionary suggests it's a relatively late theological refinement, not the original popular conception.

## Claim 3: The Israelite priesthood and temple cult were radically non-pagan — defined by silence, absence of magic, and the awe of holiness

**Author's claim:** The priestly temple is "the kingdom of silence." P makes no reference to the spoken word in describing temple rites. All acts are performed without incantation, spell, or prayer. The aim of the cult is not to secure blessing (do ut des) but to "hallow the sanctuary, to guard it from all impurity, to surround it with a cordon of awe, so that it should be a fit place for God's revelation." There is "no magical or mythological rite designed to heighten the power of the deity" — no fertility rites, no resurrection drama, no sexual elements, no funerary cult, no ecstasy or enthusiasm. The priestly cult excluded everything that in paganism reflected the life-and-death cycle of the gods.

**Evidence presented:**
- P's temple is the "tent of meeting" — the place appointed for God to meet Israel, not to hear prayer
- The festivals are marked only by additional sacrifices for atonement and "pleasing odor" — the first sheaf is waved for acceptance, not interpreted as a fertility rite
- The Day of Atonement in P involves only purification and atonement — no activity aimed at seeking a good decision
- Blessing is "on the periphery of priestly ritual, mere adjuncts to the main rites"
- No psalm is attributed to priests; song is no part of the priestly cult
- No priestesses, no musical or orgiastic element, no Dionysiac ecstasy
- Priests are severely enjoined from contact with death — high barriers between priest and the realm of the dead
- The priest's torah is law and statutes, cult and morality — "not an esoteric lore of cosmic magic"
- The spoken word in pagan cult articulated magical-mythological sense; its elimination "made the details of ritual lose their intrinsic meaning and become a vehicle expressive of human submission to the command of God"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on Kaufmann's reading of P. He's genuinely perceptive about what P OMITS compared to pagan ritual texts. But MEDIUM-LOW on his claim that this reflects actual Israelite practice rather than P's idealizing program. P is prescriptive, not descriptive — the silence may be theological aspiration, not cultic reality. Moreover, he doesn't address whether the popular cult (which he separates sharply from the priestly) supplied what the priesthood excluded.

**What's at stake:** Whether the evidence for Israelite cult (standing stones, figurines, high places) represents pagan practice that Kaufmann must dismiss as deviant, or whether even the "official" cult was more continuous with Canaanite practice than he allows.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who find traces of Canaanite cultic practice THROUGHOUT the biblical record — not just in condemned "popular deviance" but in the very institutions Kaufmann describes. The cherubim throne, the ark as footstool, the temple as divine dwelling — these have clear Near Eastern parallels. Lewis (Ch 10) argues that even the priestly holiness system was an ideological tool for constructing priestly hierarchy, not a theological revolution.

**Alternative reading:** The Israelite cult WAS distinctive — Kaufmann is right about that. But it was a gradual differentiation from Canaanite norms, not an absolute break. The silence and anti-magic emphasis may be exilic/post-exilic theologizing, not pre-exilic reality. P is our latest source, and its anti-mythological rigor may reflect Second Temple piety, not early Israelite practice.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most rhetorically powerful but methodologically problematic section. He reads P as transparent historical description of actual pre-exilic temple practice. But if P IS late (as virtually all scholars hold), the "silent temple" may be a theological ideal projected backward. The striking absence of incantation, spell, and myth from P tells us about P's theology — not necessarily about what happened in Solomon's temple. Kaufmann is right that P's vision is remarkably non-pagan, but wrong that this proves early popular religion was non-pagan.

## Claim 4: The popular cult was an independent, non-pagan sphere — prayer and psalmody flourished OUTSIDE the temple, safely separated from the priestly silent sanctuary

**Author's claim:** "The priestly cult in the temple of silence could not contain the abundance of popular religious sentiment. Around the silent sanctuary throbbed the joyous popular cult, all tumult and passion." Because the folk remained outside the temple, their activity "was not linked with any specific symbol of sanctity" and "what might otherwise have savored of magic became innocuous." Prayer belonged "almost exclusively to the popular religion" — it is not mentioned in P. Prayer was spontaneous, unformulated, separate from sacrifice, and valid anywhere (even outside the land). Psalms were popular, not priestly; the Psalter is pre-exilic.

**Evidence presented:**
- The great festivals (Passover, Weeks, Tabernacles) got their distinctive character from popular celebration, not priestly rite
- Passover was a home sacrifice; mazzoth was unrelated to temple; first fruits were brought in festive procession
- Sabbath and new moon had social-moral rationales reflected in popular observance, not temple ritual
- The autumn new year was the popular festival; the spring new year was priestly
- Psalms mentioning YHWH's kingship and cosmic judgment (Ps 47, 81, 89, 95-100) are popular new year psalms
- Prayer: no fixed forms in early religion; the only formulated prayer in the Torah is the non-priestly tithe-confession
- Psalmody: incongruities between psalms and their narrative settings (Hannah's hymn, Jonah's psalm) prove psalms were fixed stereotypes already in pre-exilic times — "this phenomenon testifies to the fixation of psalm stereotypes... in early times"
- The Psalter collections are all pre-exilic — "there is no psalm whose plain sense requires a dating later than the exilic Psalm 137"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on Kaufmann's separation of popular and priestly religion. It's a genuinely useful analytical distinction. LOW on his dating of the entire Psalter to pre-exilic times. Most scholars date significant portions of the Psalter to the post-exilic period. LOW-MEDIUM on his claim that psalms were "popular" rather than connected to temple liturgy — the Psalms are full of temple imagery and many scholars see them AS temple liturgy.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann needs the popular cult to be non-pagan to maintain his thesis. By separating it physically from the temple (outside, not inside), he can argue that any magical or pagan elements were "innocuous" — not linked to the symbols of sanctity. This is a spatial argument doing theological work.

**Who disagrees:** Form critics (Gunkel, Mowinckel) who see the Psalms as integral to temple liturgy, not separate from it. Scholars of Israelite festival calendars who find Canaanite agricultural festival patterns underlying the biblical festivals. The sharp popular/priestly distinction may be Kaufmann's construct, not ancient reality.

**Alternative reading:** The popular cult WAS closely connected to the temple — processions entered it, psalms were sung in it, prayer was offered toward it. The distinction between "silent priestly temple" and "tumultuous popular outside" is Kaufmann's idealization. In reality, temple and people were integrated, and the cult included elements Kaufmann wants to attribute to an innocuous popular sphere.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is onto something real — Israelite psalmody IS distinctive in its monotheistic and non-magical character, and prayer WAS relatively unformulated compared to pagan incantation. But his dating of the entire Psalter as pre-exilic is tendentious, and his spatial theology (outside = innocuous, inside = silent holiness) is a theoretical construct that lets him quarantine evidence of popular religious exuberance from the official cult. It's clever but not falsifiable.

## Claim 5: Israel had no ancestor cult, no cult of the dead — the realm of death was de-religionized

**Author's claim:** "The realm of the dead, the rites connected with death and burial, as well as the destiny of the soul in the other world, play no part in the religion of YHWH. This is one of the most astonishing features of Israelite religion." Although the dead were believed to exist (Sheol, shades), "biblical faith draws no religious or moral inferences from this notion." Funeral rites "lost their pagan meaning in Israel without striking fresh roots in the new faith." Caring for the dead is not a cult act but "steadfast loyalty" — ethical behavior, not religious devotion. The dead have no power to help or harm; they are tranquil, asleep. "Nowhere is ancestor-worship or a cult of the dead referred to."

**Evidence presented:**
- Deuteronomy 26:14 forbids only consecrated food to the dead — profane food gifts are not offensive, proving such gifts were not regarded as offerings
- The dead is 'elohim only in the narrow mantic sense (invoked by necromancers) — "no other divine quality is referred to"
- No cultic rites at graves of national heroes or ancestors are mentioned
- The dead have no demonic power — the concept of an autonomous demonic realm is "altogether lacking in the Bible"
- The rite of the heifer (Deut. 21:1 ff.) no longer aims at appeasing the ghost but is directed "wholly toward YHWH"
- The blood of a murdered man "cries out to YHWH" — it doesn't seek vengeance on its own; YHWH is the avenger (Gen. 4:10)
- The famine in David's time comes from YHWH's wrath, not the revenge of murdered Gibeonites (2 Sam 21)
- Self-mutilation, shaving, and tattooing were banned — but not rending clothing or fasting, because their original meaning was forgotten
- The Bible considers treatment of the dead "a matter of universal, human culture... altogether secular"
- "The dead do not praise him; those who go down to the pit do not call upon him" (Isa. 38:18; Pss. 6:6; 30:10)
- "Biblical religion knows nothing of a judgment of souls in an afterlife" — a central motif of Egyptian faith
- Why did biblical faith not create a monotheistic afterlife? Because "the especial intransigence of these pagan materials" made them impossible to assimilate without importing pagan concepts (infernal gods, dying gods, apotheosis)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive claim that the Hebrew Bible lacks an elaborated afterlife theology and ancestor cult. This is genuinely striking compared to Egypt and Mesopotamia. LOW-MEDIUM on his claim that this absence characterized POPULAR religion. The biblical texts themselves attest to necromancy (1 Sam 28), food offerings to the dead (Deut. 26:14), and anxiety about proper burial — all of which suggest the dead WERE a religious concern at the popular level, even if official theology suppressed it.

**What's at stake:** One of Kaufmann's strongest arguments for Israelite distinctiveness. If Israel truly de-religionized death — while Egypt built pyramids and Mesopotamia had elaborate netherworld theology — this IS a radical departure requiring explanation. But was it popular or elite? The evidence of popular practice (necromancy, grave goods, standing stones as memorials) suggests the elite suppression of death-cult was never complete at the popular level.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who find evidence of an Israelite ancestor cult in the archaeological record. Elizabeth Bloch-Smith argues that Judahite burial practices (family tombs, grave goods, food offerings) represent a functioning ancestor cult. Karel van der Toorn finds ancestor cult as the background to teraphim and the reverence for family tombs. Kaufmann's claim that "nowhere is ancestor-worship referred to" is true only if we accept the biblical texts' own framing — which is precisely what's in question.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical SILENCE about ancestor cult is itself evidence of its existence — the texts suppress and polemicize against practices that were real and widespread. The very prohibitions (Deut. 18:11; Lev. 19:31) prove people were doing these things. Kaufmann reads the silence as evidence of absence; critical scholarship reads it as evidence of suppression.

**My assessment:** This is where Kaufmann's most original insight (the de-religionization of death) and his most glaring blind spot coexist. He IS right that the Hebrew Bible is remarkable for its lack of developed afterlife theology — compare Egypt's Book of the Dead or the Mesopotamian netherworld visions. This absence requires explanation. But his claim that this reflects POPULAR religion rather than elite theological exclusion is undermined by the very evidence he cites: necromancy was practiced, food was given to the dead, people cared desperately about proper burial and family tombs. The popular religion MAY have been more concerned with the dead than Kaufmann allows, and the biblical silence may reflect a theological program of suppression, not popular indifference.

## Claim 6: Torah morality and wisdom morality are two independent, parallel traditions — wisdom preserves the earlier, universal form; Torah transforms it into national covenant morality

**Author's claim:** "The moral conceptions of the Bible have been crystallized in two parallel but distinct traditions: Torah and prophecy on the one hand, and the wisdom literature on the other." Torah morality is prophetic, national, grounded in covenant — "the source of morality is the will of God." Wisdom morality is sapiential, individual, universal — "its source is not prophetic revelation, but understanding and discernment." Wisdom literature "exhibits hardly a trace of the influence of either [Torah or prophecy]; it runs as a parallel and independent stream of thought." Far from being late, "individual and universal morality was the earlier, and the societal-national morality the offspring of a later, inner Israelite development."

**Evidence presented:**
- Proverbs contains all of the Decalogue except the ban on idolatry and Sabbath — but frames them as prudent counsel, not divine command
- Proverbs has no covenant concept, no historical/national motivation for morality
- "In striking contrast with the Torah, the moral rules of Proverbs are never provided with a historical or national motive"
- The cultic and social-moral aspects of Sabbath are equally ignored in Proverbs
- "Despite its solicitude for depressed classes, Proverbs lacks the great command of loving the stranger — in fact, there is no word at all about gerim"
- Proverbs' morality is utilitarian: "Morality is the way to success"
- Wisdom speaks of torah and commandment — but they are "the instruction and commands of father, mother, sage, or of wisdom personified. There is nothing about a revealed Torah, concerning which a covenant has been made."
- Torah and wisdom were only identified later (Ben-Sira onward)
- Biblical wisdom belongs to the common sapiential legacy of the ancient Near East — "Hardly anything in biblical wisdom (apart from its monotheistic viewpoint) cannot be paralleled in the literatures of Egypt and Mesopotamia"
- The Torah materials have been "fundamentally recast" — state law transformed into national-religious law, ancient universal morality given national framework
- "The absence of a distinctively Israelite element in wisdom morality indicates the antiquity of the style of that literature"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive observation that wisdom literature lacks Torah's national-covenantal framework. This is a genuine literary phenomenon that demands explanation. LOW on his dating conclusion — that wisdom therefore preserves the EARLIER form. It could equally be that wisdom literature deliberately adopts an international, universal style as a literary convention, while the actual development was the reverse: national covenant morality came first, and wisdom represented a later, cosmopolitan intellectual movement.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann's chronological inversion. If national covenant morality is the LATER development (as he claims), then the prophets who emphasize it are not innovators but culminators of a long process — and the earlier "international" wisdom morality proves Israel was always ethically sophisticated in a universalist way. But if national covenant morality is EARLIER, then wisdom literature's universalism is a late achievement — which supports the critical narrative of progressive universalization.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus dates Proverbs' final form to the Persian or Hellenistic period. The identification of Wisdom with Torah (Ben-Sira) is seen as the culmination of a process, not a late innovation. Kaufmann's dating of Proverbs as pre-exilic is possible for individual sayings, but the collection as a whole shows signs of late composition. His claim that the absence of Israelite particularism proves ANTIQUITY rather than cosmopolitan literary style is circular.

**Alternative reading:** Wisdom literature's universalism is not evidence of primitivity but of a deliberate international literary persona. The sages adopted the voice of "everyman" wisdom, suppressing national particularities as a genre convention. This doesn't tell us about chronological priority — it tells us about genre. Torah and wisdom are parallel streams in Israelite thought, but we can't date their relative priority from their literary form alone.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's literary observation is genuinely valuable — the contrast between Torah's national-covenantal morality and wisdom's individual-universal morality is real and important. But his chronological inference is unprovable and probably wrong. Both streams likely existed concurrently throughout Israel's history, with Torah representing the distinctively Israelite contribution and wisdom representing Israel's participation in the international sapiential tradition. Neither is "earlier" — they're different genres with different social locations.

## Claim 7: The national covenant morality represents a HIGHER, not more primitive, stage — the "sublime prophetic passion" is the culmination of a morality that transcends the individual

**Author's claim:** National covenant morality is "not primitive, but rather the highest stage of a morality transcending the individual." The new idea is that morality is an expression of the will of God — his absolute command. "God created not only the realm of the is, but the realm of the ought as well." Human freedom is the root of sin, but also the basis of genuine moral responsibility. "The intense passion of the national morality is the final demonstration that is not primitive... It is no longer satisfied with individual responsibility; morality is not 'a private matter.'" Communal responsibility springs from "the ardent desire to realize the demands of morality — a societal task which lies beyond the power of any individual to accomplish."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Sinaitic covenant makes the moral-legal element the very core — "the moral imperatives are the end and purpose of the initial declaration of God's sovereignty"
- "The community to whom God manifested himself becomes corporately responsible, becomes, as it were, a new moral person"
- The Torah addresses not the family, tribe, or state, but "the people of the covenant"
- "The 'you' of the laws shifts imperceptibly from the individual to the people"
- Pagan morality and wisdom counsel in "deliberate, reasonable accents"; Torah and prophets "speak impassionedly of guilt"
- Punishment "is not merely a danger or threat, but a necessary part of the moral order"
- The prophets' moral passion is grounded in the Torah's covenant framework — not their own innovation

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann is right that biblical morality has a distinctive intensity and that the covenant framework creates a unique kind of corporate accountability. But MEDIUM-LOW on his claim that this is a "higher" stage. The moral progressivism here is a value judgment, not a historical argument. Moreover, corporate punishment (children for parents, nation for king) is arguably MORE primitive, not less, than individual accountability.

**What's at stake:** Whether the prophets were innovators or traditionalists. If the national covenant morality is the mature form, the prophets are its preachers, not its creators. This supports Kaufmann's larger thesis that the Torah precedes prophecy.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who sees individual moral accountability as a moral advance over collective punishment. Ezekiel 18 is usually read as a breakthrough — the individual is responsible for his own sin. Kaufmann would have to see this as a regression to primitive individualistic wisdom morality, which seems perverse.

**Alternative reading:** Individual and corporate responsibility coexisted throughout Israelite history. Neither is "higher" — they address different moral questions. The passion of prophetic morality comes from its eschatological urgency, not from its corporate framework.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's valorization of the corporate over the individual feels forced — he needs national covenant morality to be the mature form to maintain his Torah-before-prophecy chronology. But the argument collapses if we ask: is punishing children for parents' sins (Exod. 20:5) morally "higher" than holding each individual accountable? Kaufmann's "corporate > individual" moral hierarchy is arbitrary and driven by his chronological needs.

## Claim 8: The doctrine of individual retribution is NOT late — it's found in the earliest sources alongside collective retribution

**Author's claim:** "It is a widespread opinion that the doctrine of individual retribution is a late development of Israelite religion... This opinion is a compound of errors. The notion of individual retribution is to be reckoned among the ideas that Israel inherited from the ancient cultures. It is found, alongside the idea of collective punishment, in Egypt and Mesopotamia as well."

**Evidence presented:**
- The first mention of retribution (Adam and Eve) is individual
- Noah and his generation receive strictly individual requital
- Lot's family members are requited variously according to their deserts
- The Egyptian midwives, the wilderness generation, Caleb, Joshua, Zelophehad, David are all rewarded or punished individually
- Various Torah laws (sin and guilt offerings, vows, moral injunctions) reflect individual requital
- Collective retribution exists alongside — primarily in family, tribal, and national frameworks
- The punishment of children for fathers was eventually abolished (Deut. 24:16; 2 Kings 14:6) — but both customs coexisted
- "The people of Israel constitute a special sphere of collective responsibility" based on covenant — not natural, but artificial
- Multiple auxiliary assumptions explain apparent injustices: God tests loyalty, God is patient, God waits until the measure of sin is full

**Confidence:** HIGH on the factual claim that individual retribution appears in early biblical material. Kaufmann is simply right about this — the critical claim that individual retribution was "discovered" by Ezekiel is nonsense. MEDIUM on his broader point: the coexistence of individual and collective retribution doesn't prove the doctrine of retribution was PROBLEM-FREE in early Israelite thought. The existence of Job, Psalm 73, and prophetic complaints about the wicked prospering shows it WAS a problem.

**What's at stake:** A subordinate but revealing point. The critical claim that Ezekiel "discovered" individual responsibility is part of a larger narrative about the evolution of moral consciousness. If Kaufmann can show that individual retribution was present from the beginning, he undermines one pillar of the evolutionary model.

**Who disagrees:** The "Ezekiel as discoverer of individual responsibility" claim is largely a straw man at this point — most scholars acknowledge that individual and collective responsibility coexisted throughout. Kaufmann is fighting a 19th-century battle here.

**Alternative reading:** The issue isn't whether individual retribution existed in early Israel — it's that the EXILE created a crisis where collective punishment (the nation destroyed for accumulated sin) no longer made sense, and individual accountability became theologically dominant. Ezekiel 18 isn't inventing individual responsibility; it's making it the PRIMARY framework in a new situation.

**My assessment:** A solid corrective to a simplistic evolutionary scheme, but Kaufmann overstates his case. The coexistence of individual and collective retribution is well established. The more interesting question is what happens to this doctrine under the pressure of theodicy — which Kaufmann addresses in his treatment of Job.

## Claim 9: The Book of Job represents the triumph of faith over wisdom — Israelite theodicy's distinctive contribution

**Author's claim:** Job is "religious to its very core. It comes from the realm of revelation, not wisdom; this is the distinctive Israelite feature of the book." The legend's Job is a righteous man who withstands trial; the dialogue's Job is a sage whose mind leads him to deny the moral order. "With wonderful subtlety the poet lets Job the sage finish the work of Satan and deal Job the righteous man the final blow: to deprive him of his dearest treasure, faith in divine justice." Yet even in despair, Job clings to his righteousness. "His final word is this great oath. Though his world has collapsed, he clings to the one value that is left him, his righteousness." God's answer is not a rational solution but a theophany — the very fact of revelation restores faith. "God's answer is the beginning of Job's restoration. In itself, it restored his last and severest loss, his faith in God's providence."

**Evidence presented:**
- Two elements combine: prose framework (ancient legend) and poetic dialogue (wisdom)
- The Satan of the legend tests whether a righteous man can withstand trials — he does
- The dialogue's Job is a sage driven by logic to deny moral providence: "the wicked man prospers and goes unpunished"
- Yet Job never curses God, never draws impious conclusions — he remains "profoundly moral and religious" throughout
- Job's companions argue the conventional wisdom dogma: "Job has suffered, ergo he is wicked"
- Elihu (a transition figure, possibly the poet's reflex) argues from providence in nature and human moral consciousness
- YHWH's speech from the whirlwind is the decisive answer — not its content, but the fact of revelation itself
- "I had heard of thee only a report, but now my eye beholds thee" (42:5 f.) — this is Job's last word
- The book is pre-exilic — nothing stands in the way of this dating
- "Pagan wisdom asks and answers; it presumes itself capable of solving the problem and proceeds to pass a negative verdict concerning the gods. Israelite wisdom asks but does not answer; it knows that man is dust and ashes"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on Kaufmann's reading of Job's structure (the two levels of Job the righteous man and Job the sage). This is genuinely insightful. MEDIUM on his claim that the book is pre-exilic — the language and theology are compatible with multiple datings. LOW-MEDIUM on his claim that the answer is "religious" rather than "sapiential" — the whirlwind speeches ARE wisdom literature (nature wisdom), and the content matters as much as the theophany.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite theodicy represents a genuine advance over pagan wrestling with suffering. Kaufmann's answer: yes, because it refuses to blame God or posit an evil principle, and because the final resolution is faith in God's presence, not a rational solution.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the whirlwind speeches as non-answers — God browbeats Job with displays of power rather than addressing the justice question. The book may be subverting theodicy rather than resolving it. Kaufmann's reading of the theophany as "the answer" is one interpretive tradition among several.

**Alternative reading:** The book of Job may be DEEPLY subversive of the retribution doctrine. God never says Job was wrong about the lack of moral order — he just says Job doesn't understand creation. The epilogue restores Job's fortunes but the theological problem remains unresolved. The book may be wisdom literature's critique OF wisdom literature, not a religious resolution.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's structural reading (the two Jobs, Elihu as transition) is elegant and illuminating — this is genuinely helpful exegesis. But his conclusion that the book demonstrates the "conquest of pagan wisdom" by Israelite faith is too neat. The book is more disturbing than Kaufmann allows — God never actually answers the question, and the happy ending doesn't retroactively justify the suffering. Kaufmann domesticates Job into his system rather than letting it trouble his system. That said, his observation that Israelite theodicy is distinctive in REFUSING to blame God or posit an evil principle IS important — even if Job pushes that refusal to its breaking point.

## Claim 10: The rift between ideal and reality was social, not religious — classical prophecy was born from social cleavage, not from combatting paganism

**Author's claim:** "The ancient narrative sources count one religious-cultic sin against the people: straying after foreign gods. Violations of other religious laws are not mentioned there, nor in the psalms, and only incidentally in the latter prophets." The real gap between ideal and reality was in "the relationships between man and man" — social exploitation, class division, oppression of the poor. "In the Torah, prophets, wisdom and psalm literature the distinction [between 'righteous' and 'wicked'] is a socio-moral, not a religious one." The Torah's social legislation was largely "utopian" and "edifying literature" — not enforceable law. As the monarchy developed, exploitation grew, the rift widened, and "out of this rift, classical prophecy was born."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Torah prescribes no penalties for violation of its social laws — they were "moral sanctions and exhortations"
- "The Torah provided no real legal recourse for the oppressed"
- The Torah's social background was tribal/patriarchal — it became obsolete under the monarchy, saying nothing of royal officialdom
- The Torah forbids usury, yet creditors took children as slaves (2 Kings 4:1)
- Sabbath emancipation of slaves was enacted as a NOVELTY in Zedekiah's time and immediately reversed (Jer. 34:8 ff.)
- Even the Restoration community took debtors' children into slavery (Neh. 5:1 ff.)
- "Sabbath and festival, sacrifice and sacred dues, matters of purity and impurity were all part of popular life and popular piety"
- The one sin consistently condemned is "straying after foreign gods" — which Kaufmann reads as limited cultic deviance, not polytheism

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann is right to identify social exploitation as a major concern of the prophets. But his claim that this is the ONLY rift — that there was no religious problem — depends on his prior claim that popular idolatry was not genuine polytheism. If popular religion WAS more syncretistic than Kaufmann allows, the prophets were addressing BOTH social and religious deviance.

**What's at stake:** Whether classical prophecy was primarily a social movement (Kaufmann's view) or a religious reformation (the critical view). This determines how we read the prophetic corpus.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the prophets as combatting genuine polytheistic practice. The Deuteronomistic History presents the fall of both kingdoms as punishment for idolatry — not social injustice. Kaufmann has to argue that this framing is retrospective theodicy, not historical reality.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets addressed BOTH social exploitation and religious syncretism. These were connected: worship of Baal and Asherah involved agricultural fertility rites that had social dimensions (land tenure, economic exploitation). You can't separate the religious from the social in ancient Israel — they were the same thing.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann makes an important point that has been underappreciated: the social legislation of the Torah was indeed aspirational rather than enforceable, and social exploitation WAS a major prophetic concern. But he drives an artificial wedge between "religious" and "social" that ancient Israelites wouldn't have recognized. The prophets' condemnation of idolatry and their condemnation of social injustice are two sides of the same coin — covenant violation. Kaufmann's attempt to make social injustice the primary rift while minimizing the religious rift is a consequence of his need to maintain that popular religion was essentially monotheistic.

## Chapter IX Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Evil explained through human rebellion, not myth | MEDIUM-HIGH | Mesopotamian theodicy parallels (Atrahasis, Erra); overstates uniqueness |
| 2: National element was particularist form, universalist content | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Theoretical universalism" may be evidence of monolatry, not monotheism |
| 3: Priestly cult was radically non-pagan — silent, non-magical | MEDIUM | P is prescriptive ideal, not descriptive; may reflect exilic theology |
| 4: Popular cult was independent, non-pagan sphere outside temple | MEDIUM | Sharp popular/priestly separation is Kaufmann's construct; Psalter dating is tendentious |
| 5: No ancestor cult; death was de-religionized | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaeological evidence of ancestor cult; biblical silence may be suppression |
| 6: Wisdom morality is earlier, universal — Torah transformed it | MEDIUM (observation), LOW (chronology) | Genre convention, not chronology; wisdom literature may be late |
| 7: National covenant morality is the HIGHER stage | LOW-MEDIUM | Values corporate over individual — driven by chronological need, not evidence |
| 8: Individual retribution is present from the beginning | HIGH | Straw man — critics don't claim it was "invented" by Ezekiel |
| 9: Job demonstrates triumph of faith over wisdom | MEDIUM-HIGH (reading), MEDIUM (conclusion) | Whirlwind speeches are nature wisdom, not revelation; book subverts theodicy |
| 10: Rift was social, not religious — prophecy born from social cleavage | MEDIUM | Separates social from religious artificially; ignores idolatry evidence |

**Strongest sections:**
1. **The de-religionization of death (Claim 5):** Even if Kaufmann overstates the popular dimension, his observation that the Hebrew Bible lacks an elaborated afterlife theology is genuinely important and distinctive. The contrast with Egypt is stark and demands explanation.
2. **The reading of Job's structure (Claim 9):** Kaufmann's two-level reading (Job the righteous man vs. Job the sage) is genuinely illuminating and helps resolve the apparent contradiction between the prose frame and the poetic dialogue.
3. **The contrast between Torah morality and wisdom morality (Claim 6):** The literary observation is sound and important, even if the chronological inference is dubious.

**Weakest sections:**
1. **The "silent temple" (Claim 3):** Kaufmann's most methodologically problematic argument. He reads P as transparent historical description, ignoring the possibility that P is idealizing and late. The silence of the priestly cult may be theological aspiration, not cultic reality.
2. **The dating of the Psalter (Claim 4):** Kaufmann's claim that the entire Psalter is pre-exilic is rejected by virtually all scholars and requires special pleading. It's driven by his need to locate psalmody in the "early religion."
3. **The national > individual moral hierarchy (Claim 7):** Kaufmann's most transparently agenda-driven argument. He needs national covenant morality to be the mature form, so he argues for it — but the argument doesn't survive scrutiny.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals both the power and the peril of Kaufmann's method. His close reading of biblical texts often yields genuine insights — the absence of ancestor cult, the two streams of morality, the structure of Job. But his historical conclusions almost always outrun his evidence. He reads theological ideals as historical realities, dates texts to fit his system, and dismisses contrary evidence as "popular deviance" or "vestigial paganism." The chapter is brilliant as biblical theology and deeply problematic as history.

**Key unresolved tension:** Kaufmann's treatment of the archaeology problem is entirely absent. He never addresses the figurines, the standing stones, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, the high places. For a chapter on "popular religion," this silence is deafening. He constructs popular religion entirely from texts — and from texts that he dates to suit his needs. The archaeological evidence that might challenge his thesis is simply not engaged. This is the chapter's most fundamental vulnerability, and it's one that Kaufmann never addresses anywhere in the book.

**What Kaufmann would need to show (and doesn't):** That the popular practices attested archaeologically (female figurines, Asherah worship, high-place sacrifice, standing stones) were not "genuine polytheism" but vestigial, contentless imitation of pagan forms. He makes this argument about the biblical texts' depiction of idolatry, but never tests it against the material evidence. The figurines are not vestigial — they're ubiquitous. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions pair Yahweh with Asherah in blessing formulas — that's not contentless imitation. Kaufmann's entire popular religion thesis rests on ignoring the archaeology.
