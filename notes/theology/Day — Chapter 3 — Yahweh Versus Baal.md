# Day — Chapter 3: Yahweh Versus Baal

Day covers Baal's manifestations in Israel, the identity of Jezebel's Baal, Hebrew scribal distortions of Baal's name (Baal-zebub, bosheth, abomination of desolation), and the nature of the god Dagon. The chapter is more philological and text-critical than theological — it's about how the OT polemically handles the name of its greatest rival deity.

---

## Claim 1: "The Baals" are local manifestations of ONE cosmic deity (Hadad/Baal), not distinct gods

**Author's claim:** Prior to Ugarit, scholars (like W. Robertson Smith) thought "the Baals" referred to "quite distinct Canaanite deities, each Baal having its separate local identity." The Ugaritic texts revealed that Baal "was the epithet (though becoming a personal name) of one great cosmic deity, Hadad, so that the local Baals were, in fact, simply local manifestations of this particular deity" — "analogous to the local manifestations of the Virgin Mary in the Roman Catholic Church" (p. 69).

**Evidence presented:**
1. Ugaritic pantheon list (KTU² 1.47.5-11) repeats the name Baal seven times, suggesting "for the Ugaritians he could appear in various manifestations" (p. 69)
2. OT place names reflect local Baal cults: Baal-Gad, Baal-Hamon, Baal-Hazor, Baal-Hermon, Baal-Meon, Baal-Peor, etc. — at least 20+ such places (pp. 69-70)
3. Extra-biblical attestations: Baal-Zaphon at Ugarit, Baal of Lebanon (KAI 31.1, 2), Baal-Meon on the Moabite stone (lines 9, 30) (p. 70)
4. Baal epithets independent of specific sanctuaries: Baal-Shamem ("Baal of the heavens"), Baal-zebul ("Prince Baal") (pp. 70-71)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Ugaritic evidence is decisive, and Day's Catholic analogy is both precise and accessible. The distinction between the god and his local manifestations is now standard in the field.

**What's at stake:** The OT wasn't fighting a diffuse polytheism with dozens of unrelated Baals — it was fighting ONE god who had successfully established himself at many sanctuaries across Palestine. This makes the Yahweh-vs-Baal conflict more coherent: it's a battle between two cosmic deities, not Yahweh against an undifferentiated swarm.

**Who disagrees:** Pre-Ugaritic scholars (W. Robertson Smith); the view is now essentially uncontested.

**My assessment:** This is Day establishing interpretive groundwork, not making a controversial claim. The Ugaritic data settled this question definitively.

## Claim 2: Jezebel's Baal was Baal-Shamem (the universal storm god), NOT Melqart (city god of Tyre)

**Author's claim:** "A much better case can be made that Jezebel's Baal was Baal-Shamem, another important god worshipped at Tyre, whose role as a storm god suggests that he was essentially the same as the Ugaritic Baal and the Baal known elsewhere in the Old Testament" (p. 75). The majority view identifying him as Melqart is wrong.

**Evidence presented (against Melqart):**
1. **Late sources:** The Melqart parallels (Herakles as philosopher, journey to Libya, awakening from winter sleep) come from "late sources" with "no certainty that they reflect traditions going back to the ninth century BCE" (p. 75)
2. **Wrong season for a storm god:** Melqart's awakening was celebrated in Peritios (Feb/March) — but Baal's death in summer and resurrection in autumn coheres perfectly with the rain cycle, which was "the point at issue between Elijah and the prophets of Baal in 1 Kings 18" (p. 75)
3. **Esar-haddon treaty** (7th c. BCE, only two centuries after 1 Kings 18): Lists Tyrian deities — Baal-sameme, Baal-malage, and Baal-saphon are invoked for storm curses (raising evil winds, sinking ships). Melqart is listed SEPARATELY with Eshmun for land destruction. This "clearly distinguishes Melqart from various Baal deities" and shows Melqart was NOT yet a Baal deity at this period (p. 76)
4. **"Baal of Tyre" is late:** The title is only attested for Melqart in a 2nd c. BCE inscription from Malta (KAI 47.1) — centuries after Elijah (p. 76)
5. **Classical equation:** Melqart was equated with Herakles, not Zeus. Baal-Shamem was identified with Zeus (p. 76)
6. **Royal names:** No Tyrian king names contain "Melqart," but many contain "Baal" (e.g., Ittobaal/Ethbaal, Jezebel's father) — most naturally referring to Baal-Shamem (p. 76)

**Evidence presented (for Baal-Shamem):**
1. **Carmel = Zeus = Baal-Shamem:** The god of Mt Carmel was always equated with Zeus. A marble foot inscription from Carmel dedicates "to Heliopolitan Zeus [god of] Carmel." Pseudo-Scylax (4th c. BCE) attests Carmel as "holy mountain of Zeus." Zeus = Baal-Shamem, not Melqart (Herakles) (pp. 76-77)
2. **Shalmaneser III's annals** (841 BCE): Refer to "Mount Ba'li-ra'-si [Baal of the Headland]" — a local Baal of Carmel who was "simply a local form of the universal Baal" (p. 77)
3. **Political plausibility:** "Ahab would have committed political suicide had he attempted to promote" Melqart, a foreign city god. But Baal-Shamem was already worshipped by Canaanite populations in Israel — he "gained such a large following" because he was familiar (p. 77, citing Mulder)

**Polemical significance:** In 1 Kings 18, Yahweh defeats Baal by sending fire (lightning) and then rain — the very powers Baal was supposed to control. "Accordingly, the polemic is especially marked, as Yahweh is shown as the God who can bring lightning and the rain, which were regarded as Baal's particular sphere of influence" (pp. 77-78)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Day's case is better argued than the Melqart consensus. The Esar-haddon treaty is the strongest single piece of evidence — it's contemporary (7th c.) and clearly distinguishes Melqart from Baal deities associated with storms. But the Melqart view remains the majority, and Day is fighting uphill.

**What's at stake:** If Jezebel's Baal was Baal-Shamem (not Melqart), then the Elijah conflict was not about a foreign import — it was about the SAME Baal the Canaanites already worshipped. The threat was internal syncretism, not external imposition. This changes the political and religious dynamics of the narrative.

**Who disagrees:** Majority view (R. de Vaux, C. Bonnet, and most standard commentaries) identifies Jezebel's Baal as Melqart. Day is in the minority with O. Eissfeldt, M.J. Mulder, Mark S. Smith, and B. Mazar.

**Alternative reading:** De Vaux's Melqart identification has the advantage of explaining why this Baal was specifically TYRIAN and promoted by a Tyrian princess. Day's Baal-Shamem theory has to explain why Jezebel would promote a god already worshipped in Israel.

**My assessment:** Day's Esar-haddon treaty argument is genuinely strong. The treaty clearly distinguishes Melqart from storm-associated Baal deities. The Carmel = Zeus = Baal-Shamem chain is also solid. But Day's argument that Melqart was "foreign" while Baal-Shamem was "familiar" cuts both ways — why would Jezebel need to PROMOTE a familiar god? The Melqart identification explains the Tyrian connection more naturally. I'd say the evidence tilts toward Day but not decisively.

## Claim 3: Baal-zebub is a deliberate polemical distortion of Baal-zebul ("Prince Baal")

**Author's claim:** "The most probable explanation of the name Baal-zebub is that which sees the name as a deliberate distortion of Baal-zebul, 'Baal the Prince'" (p. 80). All attempts to justify the MT reading Baal-zebub as authentic are unconvincing.

**Evidence presented (rejecting "Baal of flies" readings):**
1. Fly oracles unattested in Semitic world (Macalister)
2. Philistine origin theories lack Semitic parallels for fly-as-death-symbol (Riemschneider/Gaster)
3. Ugaritic *dbbm* more likely means "enemies" or "words," not "flies" (Saracino's appeal rejected by other translators)
4. "Baal with fly ornaments" (Tangberg) — lacks parallels and doesn't sound like a divine title
5. Fensham's "Baal of the flame" (connecting to Ugaritic *dbb* "flame") — the vocalization *zebub* is wrong for this etymology

**Evidence presented (for Baal-zebul):**
1. **Ugaritic:** Baal's frequent epithet is *zbl bʻl* "Prince Baal" (KTU²). The reverse order *bʻl zbl* is attested in a 2nd c. BCE Punic name from Sardinia (KAI 67.1-2) (pp. 80-81)
2. **Symmachus** presupposes Baal-zebul in 2 Kgs 1:2 (mss j and z) (p. 80)
3. **NT Beelzebul:** Almost all Greek mss read Beelzebul (not Beelzebub). Only Vaticanus and Sinaiticus have Beelzeboul. Vulgate/Peshitta read Beelzebub — clearly secondary harmonization with the OT (p. 81)
4. **Pattern of distortion:** This fits other OT distortions: *bošet* "shame" for Baal, *Ashtoreth* (vowels of *bošet*) for Ashtart, and the vowels of Molech (p. 81)
5. **Demonization trajectory:** The name of the leading god, when abominated, became the name of the leading demon (Satan). "The idea that pagan gods are demons is found in Deut 32:17; Ps 105:37; Bar 4:7; Ps 95:5 LXX; also 1 Cor 10:20 and Rev 9:20" (p. 81)
6. **NT wordplay:** Mt 10:25 plays on *zebul* meaning "dwelling" — "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much less will they malign those of his household" (pp. 81-82)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is one of Day's strongest arguments in the chapter. The convergence of Ugaritic attestation, Symmachus, NT manuscript evidence, and the broader pattern of scribal polemical distortion makes this close to certain.

**What's at stake:** This reveals a deliberate, sophisticated scribal strategy of theologically motivated name-distortion. "Baal the Prince" → "Lord of the Flies" is not a translation error — it's an insult. The same pattern holds for *bošet* "shame" replacing Baal in personal names.

**Who disagrees:** Those defending the MT reading (Saracino, Tangberg, Fensham with "Baal of the flame"). None are convincing.

**My assessment:** Day's case is essentially unanswerable. The NT Beelzebul evidence is the clincher — if early Christians knew the name as Beelzebul (not Beelzebub), and Beelzebul matches Ugaritic *zbl bʻl*, the MT reading is a secondary corruption. The broader pattern of scribal theologically-motivated distortions seals it.

## Claim 4: *Bošet* ("shame") is a deliberate scribal substitution for Baal in personal names

**Author's claim:** "*Bošet*, 'shame', has been substituted for *baʻal*, 'Baal', by later scribes in the personal names Ish-bosheth (for Eshbaal), Mephibosheth (for Meribaal), and Jerubbesheth (for Jerubbaal)" (p. 83).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Explicit usage:** Hos 9:10 and Jer 11:13 use *bošet* "shame" to denote Baal explicitly in condemnatory contexts (p. 82-83)
2. **Jer 3:24:** "The shameful thing (*bošet*) has devoured all..." — another clear Baal reference
3. **Pattern:** Individuals with *baʻal* in their names get the *bošet* substitution — and ONLY those individuals. This targeted pattern can't be coincidence (p. 83)
4. **Against Tsevat:** Tsevat's alternative (Akkadian *baštu* "dignity, pride") fails because: (a) it doesn't explain why ONLY individuals with *baʻal* names get this second name; (b) Tsevat ignores the passages where *bošet* clearly means "shame" for Baal, where "dignity" would be contextually absurd (p. 83)
5. **Against Hamilton:** Hamilton's "protective spirit" reading is "forced" — *baštu* means "dignity, pride, vigour," not "protective spirit" (p. 84)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is settled scholarship. The explicit usage in Hos 9:10 and Jer 11:13 proves the word was used as a Baal-substitute. The targeted pattern in personal names (only Baal-names get *bošet* variants) is a pattern that demands explanation, and scribal substitution is the only one that works.

**What's at stake:** Every time you read "Ish-bosheth" or "Mephibosheth" in Samuel, you're reading a theological edit. The original names honored Baal. The scribes couldn't change history but they could change the names.

**Who disagrees:** M. Tsevat (Akkadian *baštu* "dignity") and G.J. Hamilton ("protective spirit"). Day's rebuttals are thorough and convincing.

**My assessment:** Day destroys Tsevat's argument with two simple points: the selective pattern and the ignored passages. This claim has been standard in scholarship for over a century and Tsevat/Hamilton haven't succeeded in overturning it.

## Claim 5: "Abomination of desolation" in Daniel is a wordplay on Baal-Shamem

**Author's claim:** Nestle's 1884 recognition that *šiqqûṣ šōmēm* ("abomination of desolation") is a wordplay on Baal-Shamem ("Lord of the heavens") is correct. *Šiqqûṣ* "abomination" replaces Baal, and *šōmēm* "desolating" replaces *šāmēm* "heavens" (pp. 84-85).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Phonetic wordplay:** The substitution of *šōmēm* for *šāmēm* is a near-homophone — classic Hebrew wordplay
2. **Pattern consistency:** Substituting *šiqqûṣ* for Baal's name fits other OT usages where *šiqqûṣ* denotes detested pagan gods (1 Kgs 11:5, 7) (p. 85)
3. **Historical fit:** Antiochus IV dedicated the Jerusalem temple to Zeus Olympios. Zeus Olympios = Baal-Shamem. The Peshitta of 2 Macc 6:2 refers to the deity as *bʻl šmyn* (p. 85)
4. **Zeus-Baal equation:** "Baal and Zeus were commonly equated in the ancient world" and "Mt Olympus was frequently equated with heaven" — so Zeus Olympios → Baal-Shamem is natural (p. 85)

**Against Keel's objection:** Keel claims there's no evidence Zeus Olympios = Baal-Shamem and that "abomination of desolation" refers to swine offered on the altar, not the altar itself. Day: 1 Macc 1:54, 59 "clearly indicates that the term refers to the altar" (p. 85). And Zeus Olympios for a Hellenized Syrian "must have constituted some form of the Syrian god Baal."

**Against Bickerman(n)/Hengel:** They argue this was "extreme syncretistic Yahwism" (Yahweh equated with Zeus Olympios/Baal-Shamem by the Hellenizing party). Day: "It is more likely... that Antiochus' cult should rather be envisaged as pure paganism" — both earliest Jewish and gentile sources say this, and 2 Macc 6:7 mentions enforced Dionysus worship too, which doesn't fit monotheistic syncretism (p. 85-86)

**Confidence:** HIGH. Nestle's wordplay theory is widely accepted and phonetically elegant. The historical equation (Zeus Olympios = Baal-Shamem) is well-supported.

**What's at stake:** Daniel isn't just using apocalyptic code — he's continuing the OT tradition of polemical name-distortion. The "abomination of desolation" is not a mysterious symbol but a specific insult directed at Baal-Shamem, the god Antiochus installed in the Temple.

**Who disagrees:** O. Keel (claims no Zeus-Baal Shamem equation; argues "abomination" refers to swine, not altar). E. Bickerman(n) and M. Hengel (see it as syncretistic Yahwism, not pure paganism).

**My assessment:** Day's case is strong. The phonetic play is elegant and consistent with OT patterns. The Keel objection about swine is refuted by 1 Macc. The Bickerman/Hengel "syncretistic Yahwism" theory is interesting but requires reading against the earliest sources.

## Claim 6: Dagon was a storm/fertility god (not a fish god), and Baal was his literal son

**Author's claim:** Dagon was "a storm god... implicitly a fertility god" whose name likely derives from Arabic *dagana/dagga/dagâ* "to be cloudy, rainy" (p. 89). Baal is called *bn dgn* "son of Dagon" — this is literal parentage, not metaphorical.

**Evidence presented (against fish god):**
1. No evidence of sufficient antiquity for Dagon as fish god. The idea originates with Jerome and medieval Jewish commentators (p. 87)
2. Wellhausen's emendation of 1 Sam 5:4 to read *dāgô* "his fishy part" is "widely rejected" — LXX reads "the spine of Dagon" suggesting the original was *gēwô* "his back/trunk" or *gēw dāgôn* "the trunk of Dagon" (pp. 87-88)

**Evidence presented (for storm/fertility god):**
1. **Grain association:** Philo of Byblos (c. 100 CE): "Dagon, who is Grain." Hebrew *dāgān* means "grain, corn." The Philistine plain where Dagon was worshipped was "particularly corn-rich" (p. 88)
2. **Mesopotamian evidence:** Dagan equated with Enlil (CT 24, 6.22), who was a high god associated with storm. Dagan's wife at Mari is Šalaš, whose name resembles Šala, wife of the Assyrian storm god Adad (= Hadad) (p. 89)
3. **Etymology:** Albright's derivation from "to be cloudy, rainy" — plausible but "complete certainty is not possible" as the root is only attested in Arabic (p. 89)

**Evidence presented (Baal as literal son of Dagon):**
1. Baal repeatedly called *bn dgn* "son of Dagon" at Ugarit (KTU² 1.2.I.18-19; 1.5.VI.23-24) and *ḥtk dgn* "scion of Dagon" (KTU² 1.10.III.34)
2. Against Fontenrose (Dagon = El): The names never appear in poetic parallelism (as co-referential deities normally do) and are distinguished in offering lists
3. Against Wyatt (*bn dgn* = "son of rain" metaphorically): (a) no evidence for *dgn* meaning "rain" in Ugaritic — it means "grain"; (b) Wyatt cites no instances of metaphorical *bn* in Ugaritic; (c) *ḥtk* means "parent/scion" and is used parallel with *ʼab* "father" — a literal reading is far more natural
4. **Dual paternity solution:** Baal was literally son of Dagon but ALSO son of El in the sense that "all the Ugaritic gods were... members of the pantheon which had its origin in El." Analogous to NT "son of David" — one is literal descent, the other is membership in the lineage (p. 90)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for storm god; HIGH for literal son of Dagon; LOW-MEDIUM for the Arabic etymology (Day hedges appropriately).

**What's at stake:** Dagon's nature matters for understanding the Philistine religion the Israelites encountered. If he was a storm/fertility god like Baal, the Philistine pantheon was Canaanite-derived, not Aegean.

**Who disagrees:** Fish-god proponents (older view, now largely abandoned). Fontenrose (Dagon = El). Wyatt (metaphorical *bn dgn*).

**My assessment:** The fish-god theory is dead and Day buries it efficiently. The metaphorical *bn dgn* reading is clever but requires too many unsupported linguistic leaps. Day's dual-paternity solution is elegant and the NT analogy is apt.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| "The Baals" = local manifestations of one god | HIGH | Essentially uncontested since Ugarit |
| Jezebel's Baal = Baal-Shamem (not Melqart) | MEDIUM-HIGH | Majority view still favors Melqart |
| Baal-zebub = distortion of Baal-zebul | HIGH | Ugaritic + NT evidence converging |
| *Bošet* = scribal substitution for Baal | VERY HIGH | Tsevat/Hamilton rebuttals fail |
| "Abomination of desolation" = wordplay on Baal-Shamem | HIGH | Nestle's theory widely accepted; Keel's objections addressed |
| Dagon = storm/fertility god, literal father of Baal | MEDIUM-HIGH | Etymology uncertain; dual paternity is speculative but reasonable |

**Strongest section:** The Baal-zebub/Baal-zebul argument. This is Day at his philological best — Ugaritic, Symmachus, NT manuscripts, and the broader pattern of scribal distortion all converge on the same conclusion.

**Weakest section:** The Jezebel's-Baal-as-Baal-Shamem argument. Day makes a strong case but is fighting the majority, and the Melqart identification has the advantage of explaining the specifically Tyrian character of Jezebel's cult.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals Day's method: he's a text scholar working through philology, not an archaeologist. When he needs to decide between competing identifications, he reaches for inscriptions and manuscripts, not material culture. This is both a strength (precision with texts) and a limitation (Dever would say he's missing the ground-level picture). The chapter also demonstrates that OT hostility to Baal operated at the level of *names* — the scribes didn't just argue against Baal, they linguistically deformed his name into insults.
