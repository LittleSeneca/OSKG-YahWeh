# Lewis — Chapter 2: The History of Scholarship on Ancient Israelite Religion — A Brief Sketch

Lewis traces the intellectual history of the field from the Enlightenment through the early 21st century. The chapter is organized around major scholarly movements rather than a strict timeline, and Lewis is careful to flag where his own preferences lie (he's clearly a Cross student, favors integrating text + archaeology + iconography, and is skeptical of both maximalist and minimalist extremes).

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Schematizing is necessary but imperfect | HIGH | Alternative schemes could be more inclusive |
| Wellhausen was philosophically loaded with anti-Judaism | VERY HIGH | Morgan and Barton's defense of Wellhausen as "presuppositionless" (but this is a minority view) |
| Ugaritic discoveries most impactful of 20th C | HIGH | Dead Sea Scrolls for later periods |
| Albright/Kaufmann distinctiveness untenable, Cross's synthesis best | VERY HIGH | Confessional scholars who reject comparative method |
| Fribourg school transformed the field | VERY HIGH | Over-interpretation of ambiguous iconography |
| 1970s shift to personal piety/family religion | VERY HIGH | Convergence may be partly retrospective construction |
| Feminist criticism reshaped the field | VERY HIGH | Integration into mainstream scholarship is still ongoing |

**Strongest section:** The treatment of Wellhausen. Lewis doesn't just describe the documentary hypothesis — he exposes its philosophical and moral underpinnings (anti-Judaism, social Darwinism, Romantic hero-worship of the prophets). This is essential context for understanding why later scholars (Cross, Smith, Dever) reacted against it.

**Weakest section:** The revisionist/minimalist historians get surprisingly thin treatment (about two paragraphs) given how much they've dominated historiographical debates since the 1990s. Lewis clearly disagrees with them but doesn't engage their arguments in depth here. Compare Dever, who devotes entire chapters to refuting minimalism.

**What Lewis signals by his coverage choices:**
- He's a Cross student through and through — continuity with novelty, typological method, value of epigraphy and iconography
- He's sympathetic to the social-scientific turn (Albertz, Gottwald, Meyers) without being a doctrinaire materialist
- He's methodologically pluralist — he thinks text, archaeology, iconography, and social science all have roles
- He's more generous to feminist scholarship than many of his male peers
- He's impatient with minimalist historiography but doesn't want to spend pages refuting it

**Cross-references:** This chapter pairs naturally with Dever Ch. II (which covers similar ground from an archaeologist's perspective) and with Cross's own introduction (which Lewis cites extensively). The Wellhausen critique echoes Römer's brief treatment in *Invention of God* and Smith's more extended engagement in *Origins*. Lewis's discussion of the myth-and-ritual school is more detailed than most comparable surveys and worth comparing with Heiser's use of divine council patterns (which has its own myth-and-ritual echoes).
