# Lewis — Chapter 2: The History of Scholarship on Ancient Israelite Religion — A Brief Sketch

Lewis traces the intellectual history of the field from the Enlightenment through the early 21st century. The chapter is organized around major scholarly movements rather than a strict timeline, and Lewis is careful to flag where his own preferences lie (he's clearly a Cross student, favors integrating text + archaeology + iconography, and is skeptical of both maximalist and minimalist extremes).

---

## Claim 1: Schematizing intellectual history is necessary but always falsifies to some degree — the best categories are the ones that "falsify it least"

**Lewis's claim:** Quoting C.S. Lewis: "All divisions will falsify our material to some extent; the best one can hope is to choose those which will falsify it least." He also invokes Kuhn's "paradigm shift" model as an alternative to gradualist narratives, acknowledging that both continuity and novelty coexist.

**Evidence:** Lewis doesn't argue this — he asserts it as a methodological premise. The entire chapter structure (Enlightenment → German criticism → Wellhausen → Robertson Smith → Gunkel → Biblical Theology → Myth-and-Ritual → Ugarit → Albright/Kaufmann → Cross → Iconography → Revisionists → Archaeology → Social Sciences → Family Religion → Feminism) is his chosen scheme.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the claim is true. MEDIUM that Lewis's specific scheme is optimal. He notably leaves out several traditions (Catholic biblical scholarship, Israeli archaeology's particular development, the impact of the Dead Sea Scrolls on the field — he mentions the Scrolls only to say Ugarit was more impactful).

**What's at stake:** The categories structure what students of the field will see as important. If Lewis's scheme overweights German Protestant scholarship and underweights other traditions, it perpetuates a distorted picture of the field.

**Who disagrees:** Dever covers similar ground in Ch. II of Did God Have a Wife? but organizes around archaeology vs. text rather than intellectual movements. Römer barely covers historiography at all (his book begins with geographic origins).

**My assessment:** Lewis's scheme is conventional and defensible for an English-language audience. The chapter is stronger on the 19th-20th century than on contemporary developments, which get compressed into the final few pages.

---

## Claim 2: Wellhausen's synthesis dominated the field not because it was original but because it was brilliantly written — and it was philosophically loaded with anti-Judaism and social Darwinism

**Lewis's claim:** Wellhausen's reconstruction was "unilinear and evolutionary in nature, progressing in positive fashion from primitive, animistic origins to the ethical monotheism of the prophets" — but then "once it reached such heights, it would lose its ability to grow and became a static, mechanical abstraction in post-exilic Judaism." Lewis emphasizes that "it is no mere coincidence that 1859 saw the publication of Charles Darwin's The Origin of Species" and quotes Geller: Wellhausen refracted his view through "the optimistic evolutionary lenses of social Darwinism."

**Evidence:** Lewis cites de Wette's caricature of "Judaism" as "the unfortunate reinterpretation of Hebraism," Vatke's Hegelian dialectic (thesis → antithesis → synthesis mapped onto pre-prophetic → prophetic → post-prophetic), and Wellhausen's famous line: "One God one sanctuary, that is the idea." He also quotes Wellhausen's description of post-exilic Judaism as lifeless: "the warm pulse of life no longer throbbed in it... the soul was fled, the shell remained."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that Wellhausen's work was philosophically shaped. HIGH that anti-Judaism was a significant factor. The anti-Jewish element has been documented extensively (Levenson, Heschel, Geller). The Darwinian influence is more debated — Morgan and Barton argue Wellhausen explicitly avoided philosophical commitments.

**What's at stake:** If Wellhausen's framework is contaminated by anti-Judaism, the entire documentary hypothesis needs reexamination — not necessarily rejection, but cleansing of its value judgments. Many scholars accept the literary sources (J, E, D, P) while rejecting the evolutionary schema.

**Who disagrees:** Morgan and Barton (1988) argue Wellhausen worked in the spirit of "research free of presuppositions." More conservative scholars might dispute the anti-Judaism charge as anachronistic. Heiser doesn't engage Wellhausen directly but implicitly rejects the evolutionary scheme.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right. Wellhausen's anti-Judaism isn't subtle. The question is whether it invalidates his literary analysis or just his historical reconstruction. Most critical scholars today accept the four-source model while rejecting his valuation of post-exilic Judaism as "lifeless."

## Claim 3: The Ugaritic discoveries had a more profound impact on the study of Israelite religion than any other manuscript discovery of the 20th century — including the Dead Sea Scrolls

**Lewis's claim:** "Over time the Ugaritic texts would come to have the most profound impact on the study of ancient Israelite religion of any manuscript discovery of the twentieth century (far more than even the Dead Sea Scrolls)."

**Evidence:** Ugaritic provided: a Northwest Semitic language remarkably close to Hebrew; a full pantheon with deities directly comparable to biblical names (ʾIlu/ʾEl, Baʿlu/Baʿal, ʾAthiratu/ʾAsherah, ʿAnatu/ʿAnat, Rashpu/Reshep); cosmic monsters (Yammu/yām, Litanu/Leviathan, Tunnanu/tannin, Motu/māwet); cultic terminology for sacrifices; and — crucially — full mythological narratives (the Baal Cycle, Kirta, Aqhat) that show how a West Semitic pantheon actually functioned. The Dead Sea Scrolls, by contrast, illuminate Second Temple Judaism, not the origins of Israelite religion.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Smith makes the same claim in Origins. Cross's entire career was built on this premise. The Ugaritic texts are the single most important comparative corpus for understanding pre-exilic Israelite religion.

**What's at stake:** This claim justifies the heavy use of Ugaritic material throughout Lewis's book (especially Chapters 4-5 on El). If Ugarit is less relevant than Lewis claims, his comparative method is weakened.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars focused on Persian/Hellenistic period religion (the minimalist school) would say the Dead Sea Scrolls are more important for understanding the religion that actually shaped Judaism and Christianity. But for reconstructing *Iron Age* Israelite religion, Ugarit is unquestionably primary.

**Alternative reading:** The claim is about "most profound impact" — not about which is more important overall. It's a statement about the history of the discipline, and it's correct.

**My assessment:** Uncontroversial among specialists. The Baal Cycle alone — with its divine council, combat myth, temple-building, and theomachy — illuminates dozens of biblical passages that were previously opaque.

## Claim 4: Albright's and Kaufmann's insistence on Israel's radical distinctiveness was undermined by archaeological evidence — and Cross offered a more nuanced middle ground

**Lewis's claim:** Kaufmann asserted that "Israelite religion was an original creation of the people of Israel. It was absolutely different from anything the pagan world ever knew" and that "the Bible is utterly unaware of the nature and meaning of pagan religion." Albright shared this passion for uniqueness but, because of his command of Levantine material culture, was forced to acknowledge that "the ignorance presupposed by Kaufmann's view is simply incredible." Cross then offered the synthesis: Israel's religion "stood in a clear line of continuity with the mythopoeic patterns of West Semitic, especially Canaanite myth" while simultaneously exhibiting "radical differentiation" and "the appearance of novelty."

**Evidence:** Lewis quotes Albright directly: "there was so much exchange of cultural influences between Israel and its neighbors on all sides of its tiny territory... that the ignorance presupposed by Kaufmann's view is simply incredible." He quotes Cross's methodological principle: "I believe it is as illegitimate methodologically to resort to the category of the sui generis in explaining historical sequences, as it is contrary to scientific method to resort to the category of miracles." He also cites Levenson's critique of Kaufmann: "We cannot conclude from the inaccuracy of the Israelite poet's satire that he had no understanding of the spiritual life of his Canaanite neighbor."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that Kaufmann's position is untenable. HIGH that Cross's synthesis has held up. The archaeological evidence from Kuntillet ʿAjrud alone — which Lewis discusses later in the chapter — demonstrates that Israelites worshipped Canaanite deities alongside Yahweh.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite religion is a "miracle" or a historical development. Kaufmann's position supports confessional claims about unique revelation. Cross's position allows for genuine religious innovation within a cultural continuum — which is how most historians understand cultural change.

**Who disagrees:** Heiser would push back on the "polytheism" framing — he'd say Israelites acknowledged other elohim (divine beings) but only ever worshipped Yahweh. Conservative evangelicals (Provan, Long, Longman) would defend a modified form of Israel's distinctiveness.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's position is now held only by the most conservative scholars. Albright's modified distinctiveness is closer to the evidence but still overstates the case. Cross's continuity-with-novelty framework is where the field has landed — and where Lewis himself stands.

## Claim 5: The Fribourg school of iconographic study transformed the field by forcing text-centric scholars to engage with visual evidence

**Lewis's claim:** Othmar Keel's 1972 work "offered a massively detailed study of how visual symbols could supplement the written text" and that "systematic studies were rare in a discipline that preferred philology and theology to the worlds of archaeology and art history." The Fribourg school has turned out "numerous volumes" and "exhaustive studies of previously overlooked seals and amulets." Keel and Uehlinger's *Gods, Goddesses and Images of God* (1998) and Schroer's four-volume *Die Ikonographie Palästinas/Israels* are cited as landmarks.

**Evidence:** Lewis notes that female divine iconography became especially relevant after the Kuntillet ʿAjrud and Khirbet el-Qom discoveries mentioning Asherah. Cornelius's 2004 comprehensive iconographic study of the goddess is cited. Lewis also provides a footnote listing databases: "Extensive new databases such as Schroer's four-volume Die Ikonographie Palästinas/Israels und der Alte Orient (2005–2018) provide researchers with unparalleled resources."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The integration of iconography is one of the major methodological advances of the last 50 years. Lewis's own Chapters 5 and 7 are built on Fribourg-school foundations.

**What's at stake:** If iconography is treated as supplementary rather than primary, a huge swath of evidence is missed — especially for popular religion (seals, amulets, figurines) that the textual record largely ignores.

**Who disagrees:** Text-centric philologists who see iconography as inherently ambiguous and subjective. The Fribourg school has been criticized for over-interpreting images — Keel himself acknowledged the risk: "We constantly run the risk of reading these pictures too concretely, or having avoided that risk, of treating them too abstractly."

**My assessment:** Lewis's embrace of iconography is one of the book's strengths and a direct inheritance from his teacher Cross (who applied typological method to artifacts) and his Johns Hopkins environment (with its Near Eastern archaeology focus). Chapters 5 and 7 will be the test of whether he uses iconography well.

## Claim 6: The 1970s witnessed a paradigm shift toward personal piety and family religion — driven independently by Meshel (Kuntillet ʿAjrud), Barkay (Ketef Hinnom), and Albertz (Persönliche Frömmigkeit)

**Lewis's claim:** Three scholars working independently in the mid-to-late 1970s "would serve as harbingers of the next decades." Meshel's excavation of Kuntillet ʿAjrud revealed "Yahweh and his A/asherah" and "revolutionized the field." Barkay's Ketef Hinnom amulets showed the priestly benediction used apotropaically in a family tomb context. And Albertz's work distinguished "personal piety" from "official religion," arguing that family religion had "very low" institutionalization.

**Evidence:** Lewis quotes Barkay et al.: "the use of the confessional statement in Ketef Hinnom I... introduces a context associated with personal piety and family life — that of family tomb and burial of an individual." He cites Albertz's definition of family religion as centered on the household: "that face-to-face community that lived together in the house of the family head, the center of its everyday life."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that these three developments shifted the field. HIGH that they happened largely independently (Meshel and Barkay are archaeologists who wouldn't have been reading Albertz's German monograph). The convergence is genuine.

**What's at stake:** Before the 1970s, Israelite religion was studied primarily through the lens of official, state-sponsored cult. The shift to family/personal religion opened up the study of women's religion, household cult, ancestor veneration, and non-elite practice — topics the Hebrew Bible's elite authors had little interest in documenting.

**Who disagrees:** Dever has argued that archaeology had already been pointing toward domestic religion for decades but biblical scholars weren't listening. Albertz himself would say his work built on earlier German scholarship about personal piety.

**Alternative reading:** The convergence is partly an artifact of Lewis's periodization. Other developments (processual archaeology, the Annales school's longue durée, the rise of social history) were creating conditions where these discoveries would inevitably be read through the lens of non-elite religion.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right to highlight this as a turning point. His own Chapter 8 (Yahweh as family god) grows directly from this tradition. The Kuntillet ʿAjrud material is arguably the single most destabilizing archaeological discovery for traditional understandings of Israelite religion.

## Claim 7: Feminist criticism has fundamentally reshaped the field — and this is not a niche concern but central to understanding Israelite religion

**Lewis's claim:** Lewis traces feminist contributions from Elizabeth Cady Stanton's *The Woman's Bible* (1895) through the second wave (Trible, 1978) to the current integration of feminist criticism with archaeology, iconography, and social-scientific approaches. He singles out Carol Meyers as the scholar whose work "stands out among all others" for blending archaeological and textual analysis of women's roles in household religion.

**Evidence:** Lewis provides an extensive bibliography: Trible, Bird, Ackerman, Brenner, Meyers, Schroer, Bloch-Smith, Stavrakopoulou, Nakhai, Marsman, Ebeling, Chapman, Hackett, Frymer-Kensky, and others. He cites Meyers's argument that "women were *not* much more disadvantaged in their participation of *communal* religious activities than were non-priestly males" — a revisionist claim that challenges the default assumption of patriarchal exclusion.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that feminist criticism has transformed the field. MEDIUM on Meyers's specific claim about women's participation — the evidence is more ambiguous than Lewis's favorable citation suggests.

**What's at stake:** If feminist criticism is right that women were central to household and family religion, then our picture of Israelite religion as male-dominated is an artifact of our sources (written by male elites), not a reflection of actual practice.

**Who disagrees:** More traditional scholars would argue that the biblical text's patriarchal assumptions reflect genuine social realities. The debate isn't about whether women practiced religion (they obviously did) but about their status and autonomy within it.

**My assessment:** Lewis gives feminist scholarship significant space — more than Dever does in his comparable historiography chapter. This is appropriate for a 2020 volume. The challenge is whether the rest of Lewis's book actually integrates feminist insights (e.g., about Asherah, about family religion) or merely nods to them in this chapter and moves on. His focus on El and Yahweh (both male deities) means goddess religion gets less sustained attention than it deserves.

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Schematizing is necessary but imperfect | HIGH | Alternative schemes could be more inclusive |
| Wellhausen was philosophically loaded with anti-Judaism | VERY HIGH | Morgan and Barton's defense of Wellhausen as "presuppositionless" (but this is a minority view) |
| Ugaritic discoveries most impactful of 20th C | HIGH | Dead Sea Scrolls for later periods |
| Albright/Kaufmann distinctiveness untenable, Cross's synthesis best | VERY HIGH | Confessional scholars who reject comparative method |
| Fribourg school transformed the field | VERY HIGH | Over-interpretation of ambiguous iconography |
| 1970s shift to personal piety/family religion | VERY HIGH | Convergence may be partly retrospective construction |
| Feminist criticism reshaped the field | VERY HIGH | Integration into mainstream scholarship is still ongoing |

**Strongest section:** The treatment of Wellhausen. Lewis doesn't just describe the documentary hypothesis — he exposes its philosophical and moral underpinnings (anti-Judaism, social Darwinism, Romantic hero-worship of the prophets). This is essential context for understanding why later scholars (Cross, Smith, Dever) reacted against it.

**Weakest section:** The revisionist/minimalist historians get surprisingly thin treatment (about two paragraphs) given how much they've dominated historiographical debates since the 1990s. Lewis clearly disagrees with them but doesn't engage their arguments in depth here. Compare Dever, who devotes entire chapters to refuting minimalism.

**What Lewis signals by his coverage choices:**
- He's a Cross student through and through — continuity with novelty, typological method, value of epigraphy and iconography
- He's sympathetic to the social-scientific turn (Albertz, Gottwald, Meyers) without being a doctrinaire materialist
- He's methodologically pluralist — he thinks text, archaeology, iconography, and social science all have roles
- He's more generous to feminist scholarship than many of his male peers
- He's impatient with minimalist historiography but doesn't want to spend pages refuting it

**Cross-references:** This chapter pairs naturally with Dever Ch. II (which covers similar ground from an archaeologist's perspective) and with Cross's own introduction (which Lewis cites extensively). The Wellhausen critique echoes Römer's brief treatment in *Invention of God* and Smith's more extended engagement in *Origins*. Lewis's discussion of the myth-and-ritual school is more detailed than most comparable surveys and worth comparing with Heiser's use of divine council patterns (which has its own myth-and-ritual echoes).
