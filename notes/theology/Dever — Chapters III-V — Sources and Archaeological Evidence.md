# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Chapters III-V

## Sources, Methods, and Archaeological Evidence

---

## Chapter III: Sources and Methods

### Claim: Archaeology is a primary source — equal or superior to the biblical texts for reconstructing Israelite religion

**Dever's claim:** The biblical texts are late, elitist, and theologically biased. Archaeology provides data that is contemporary, populist, and unedited. For the study of folk religion (what ordinary people actually practiced), archaeology is not just supplementary — it's often superior.

**Evidence presented — the six arguments for archaeology:**

1. **Quantity:** "Archaeological data are already more extensive than all the biblical texts put together, and they will be much more so in future." The Bible is static; archaeology is dynamic.

2. **Variety:** "Artifacts come not only from the sacred ideas and institutions that the Hebrew Bible focuses on... but from the whole range of 'secular' activities of ordinary people."

3. **Chronology:** Biblical texts in their present form cover "only about one-third of Israel's history." Archaeological artifacts "are by nature contemporary with all the periods." Carbon 14 dating delivers ±50 years; biblical scholars disagree by centuries.

4. **Representation:** "The biblical texts, produced by a small circle of literati, are elitist. Yet the archaeological data... are 'populist' by nature." They represent every class, every profession, and especially women.

5. **Objectivity:** "What we call 'the archaeological record' has not been edited by anyone." Biblical texts were edited from the beginning and reinterpreted for 2,000 years.

6. **Secular vs. theocratic history:** The Bible is "theocratic history." Archaeology produces "a secular history... often more realistic, more comprehensive, better balanced, and ultimately more satisfying."

### The Biblical Texts and Their Limitations

Dever accepts mainstream critical scholarship on the sources:
- **J** (Yahwist): 9th-8th century, southern. If post-exilic (as revisionists claim), "this source would tell us nothing whatsoever about supposed 'ancestral religion.'"
- **E** (Elohist): 8th century, northern. Prophetic interests.
- **P** (Priestly): Late monarchy or post-exilic. "Irrelevant for folk religion during the monarchy."
- **Dtr** (Deuteronomistic): The most important source. 7th-century core, edited through exile. "A call to repentance... a demand for the wayward people of Israel to restore the pure, monotheistic ideals of its original 'pure' religion."

Dever's "middle ground" on historicity: "I reject absolutely the assertion of some 'revisionists' that the Bible is not about history at all. On the other hand, the Hebrew Bible is obviously not history in the modern sense... I shall often regard the Hebrew Bible as 'historicized fiction' — stories that are based on some genuine historical events, but always told in such a way as to advance the ideological agenda of the writers."

**Key methodological insight:** "The prophets... it would not be misleading to say that the real religions of ancient Israel consisted precisely of all the things that the prophets condemned, all of them incidentally illustrated by archaeological discoveries."

### Dever's Critique of Text-Only Scholars

Dever names names. He says Smith's major work "does not even mention archaeology (only some archaeologists) in surveying trends since 1990." He quotes Smith's dismissal — archaeology "can alleviate only some of the difficulty" — and treats it as revealing. For Dever, this is the blind spot of the entire text-based approach.

**Confidence:** HIGH that archaeology provides data texts don't (self-evident). MEDIUM that it's "superior" — depends on the question being asked. For folk practice, Dever is right. For belief and theology, texts are indispensable.

## Chapter IV: The Hebrew Bible — Religious Reality or Theological Ideal?

### Claim: The Hebrew Bible describes an ideal religion that never existed — "Book religion" vs. the reality of practice

**Dever's claim:** The religion prescribed by the biblical writers was a late, idealistic construction. The "real" religion of ancient Israel was the folk religion that the prophets and Deuteronomists condemned. By studying what they condemned, we can reconstruct what people actually practiced.

**Evidence presented — the biblical ideal vs. archaeological reality:**

**High places (*bāmôt*):**
- Condemned by Deuteronomists as Canaanite pagan practice
- BUT: Saul visited a high place looking for a "seer" with no hint of disapproval (1 Sam 9:5-14)
- David's priest Zadok visited the high place at Gibeon "according to all that is written in the law of the Lord" (1 Chron 16:37-42)
- Solomon went to the high place at Gibeon with "the whole assembly" (1 Kings 3:3-15)
- **The biblical writers themselves admit** that before the Temple was built, high places were legitimate: "the people were sacrificing at the high places, however, because no house had yet been built for the name of the Lord" (1 Kings 3:2)
- "Curiously, polemics against the high places among the idealistic reformers of the late monarchy are conspicuously absent in descriptions of religious life in the United Monarchy"

**Family and household shrines:**
- Judges 17: Micah's house had "graven and molten images," *teraphim*, an ephod, and a family priest — "a house of gods" (*bet 'elohim*)
- Judges 6: Gideon's father had "an altar of Baal" and "an asherah" — at the family compound
- "In ancient Israel there must also have existed household or private shrines, indeed in far greater numbers"
- These were not aberrations — they were the NORM

**The Temple was not the center of national religion:**
- The Jerusalem Temple was "a royal chapel" — ordinary people couldn't enter
- "For most people in ancient Israel, the Temple liturgy and the official Temple theology... were unknown, and they would have been irrelevant in any case"
- "Many people in ancient Israel had probably never been to Jerusalem in their whole lives"
- "Despite the fact that many today still resonate with 'Book religion,' it was not the real religion of ancient Israel, at least for the majority"

**Confidence:** HIGH that high places were originally legitimate (the biblical texts themselves show this). HIGH that family religion was the norm not the exception. HIGH that the Temple was an elite institution.

## Chapter V: Archaeological Evidence for Folk Religions

This is the core of Dever's book — the artifacts. He presents eight categories of archaeological evidence for folk religion.

### 1. Cultic Sites and Sanctuaries

- The **Arad temple** (8th century BCE): A full Yahwistic temple in southern Judah with a holy of holies, two standing stones (*massebot*), and two altars. Deliberately decommissioned (possibly to save it from Assyrians, or during Hezekiah's reform).
- The **"Bull Site"** in northern Samaria (12th century): Open-air hilltop shrine with a bronze bull figurine — "probably representing either El or Baal, but eventually associated with Yahweh." Evidence that bull iconography for Yahweh was older than the biblical polemic against Jeroboam's calves.
- **Dan** high place: A large platform with altar, associated with Jeroboam's bull cult.
- Dozens of smaller open-air shrines and cult corners throughout Israel and Judah.

### 2. Standing Stones (*Massebot*)

- Found at nearly every excavated Israelite site
- At Arad: two stones in the holy of holies — probably "Yahweh and his Asherah"
- At Hazor: a row of standing stones at the city gate
- At Gezer: ten massive standing stones, possibly representing a treaty or covenant
- These were everywhere. They were normal. They only became "idolatrous" when the Deuteronomists redefined Yahwism.

### 3. Altars

- Multiple types: large four-horned stone altars (Beer-Sheba, Arad), smaller portable incense altars, and simple earth-and-stone altars at domestic sites
- The four-horned stone altar at Beer-Sheba was deliberately dismantled — probably during Hezekiah's reform
- Small limestone incense altars found at many domestic sites — evidence of household worship

### 4. Figurines — The Key Evidence

This is Dever's strongest evidence:

- **Judean Pillar Figurines (JPFs):** Over 3,000 found, overwhelmingly in domestic contexts (houses, not temples). Usually a nude female with emphasized breasts, holding them or with hands supporting them. "Found in almost every excavated 8th-6th century house in Judah."
- These figurines were mass-produced from molds — they were cheap, accessible, and everywhere
- Most scholars identify them as representations of **Asherah** — the Mother Goddess
- They were used in domestic rituals for fertility, protection in childbirth, and general well-being
- "They were part of the everyday religious paraphernalia of the typical Judean household"
- Also: horse-and-rider figurines (possibly solar), animal figurines, model furniture, miniature vessels

> **Dever's key argument:** You can't explain away 3,000 nude female figurines in domestic contexts as anything other than popular goddess worship. Smith's text-based approach misses this entirely.

### 5. Inscriptions

- Kuntillet Ajrud: "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" (8th century)
- Khirbet el-Qom: "Yahweh and his Asherah" — a burial inscription invoking blessing "by Yahweh and his Asherah" (8th century)
- Dever reads these as clear evidence that Asherah was Yahweh's consort — contra Smith's "cult symbol" reading

### 6. Cult Stands

- The **Taanach cult stand** (10th century BCE): Four tiers showing a nude female (Asherah?), lions, cherubim, a tree of life, and a young bull. "An encyclopedia of Canaanite-Israelite religion."
- Similar stands found at other sites — used for burning incense, making offerings
- Often decorated with palms, trees, animals — trees represent the goddess

### 7. Votive Offerings

- Small objects deposited at shrines: miniature vessels, beads, amulets, jewelry
- Evidence of personal piety — ordinary people making offerings for health, fertility, protection

### 8. Tombs and Mortuary Practices

- Bench tombs with multiple burials, food and drink offerings, lamps, personal items
- Evidence of feeding the dead and ancestor cults — practices condemned in the Bible but archaeologically ubiquitous

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the figurines (the data is overwhelming — thousands of examples). HIGH on the Arad temple and standing stones. HIGH on Kuntillet Ajrud as evidence for Asherah.

## The Central Disagreement: Dever vs. Smith on Asherah

This is where Dever makes his most direct challenge to Smith:

| Issue | Smith | Dever |
|-------|-------|-------|
| Kuntillet Ajrud | "His asherah" = Yahweh's cult symbol | "His Asherah" = Yahweh's consort goddess |
| 3,000+ figurines | Minimally discussed | Central evidence for goddess worship |
| Method | Texts first, archaeology supplementary | Archaeology primary, texts secondary |
| The goddess question | "Insufficient evidence" | "The evidence is overwhelming" |

Dever's implicit argument: Smith is a text scholar who doesn't understand archaeological evidence. If he did, he'd see that 3,000 goddess figurines in domestic contexts, combined with inscriptions invoking "Yahweh and his Asherah" and biblical polemic against the asherah — the cumulative case for Asherah as goddess and consort is decisive.

**My assessment:** Dever's archaeological evidence significantly strengthens the case against Smith's minority position. The figurines alone are a problem Smith never adequately addresses. Dever + Römer together make a compelling case that Smith is wrong on Asherah.
