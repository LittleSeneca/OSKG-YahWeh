# Lewis — Chapter 9: Yahweh as King and Yahweh as Judge

Lewis examines Yahweh as sovereign monarch and cosmic magistrate. The king metaphor draws on ancient Near Eastern royal ideology — kings as chief cultic officiants and mediators between heaven and earth — but Israelite kings were notably NOT called priests and did not receive cult. The judge metaphor is multi-layered: royal judicial ideology, non-royal judiciaries (family, elders, priests), prophetic counter-discourses prosecuting injustice, and theodicy (Job, Qoheleth). The chapter culminates in Psalm 82, where Yahweh prosecutes the gods themselves for judicial failure.

**Strongest sections:** Psalm 82 analysis (the mythopoeic/mythopoetic framework is the right way to handle the ambiguity). The royal cult survey (comprehensive comparative context). The treatment of the absence of royal legislation in Israel (Moses, not the king, is the lawgiver — a genuinely distinctive feature).

**Weakest section:** The apocalyptic justice section feels rushed — it's an important topic but gets compressed.

**What this chapter establishes:**
- Yahweh as king is the central metaphor of the Hebrew Bible's understanding of God
- Israelite kingship is both embedded in ancient Near Eastern patterns and distinctive in crucial ways (no deification, no priestly identity, no legislation)
- Yahweh as judge operates at every level: royal, local, personal, cosmic
- The wisdom tradition represents the most intellectually honest engagement with the limits of divine justice

**Cross-references:** Ch9's treatment of Psalm 82 is essential reading alongside Heiser's Unseen Realm (which reads the psalm very differently), Sommer's Bodies of God (for the fluidity interpretation), and Tigay's onomastic evidence (for the prevalence of Yahweh names). The royal cult discussion complements Cross's treatment of Davidic ideology.
