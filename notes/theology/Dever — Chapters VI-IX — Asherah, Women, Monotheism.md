# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Chapters VI-IX

## Asherah, Women's Cults, Monotheism, and Conclusions

---

## Chapter VI: The Goddess Asherah and Her Cult

### Claim: Asherah was a real goddess, Yahweh's consort, worshipped throughout Israel and Judah — and archaeology proves it

**Dever's claim:** The biblical writers' ambivalence about the asherah reflects their embarrassment about a "Hebrew Goddess" they couldn't deny but wanted to forget. "Originally the biblical writers were embarrassed by the notion of a 'Hebrew Goddess,' but they could not condemn her and her cult without tacitly, but covertly, acknowledging her existence." By the time the canonical texts were finalized, "the old Mother Goddess had been driven underground and all but forgotten." But archaeology has brought her back.

**Evidence presented — the biblical language itself:**

- The Hebrew *'asherah* occurs over 40 times in the Bible
- The consonantal text can be read EITHER as "the asherah" (symbol) OR "Asherah" (goddess) — the ambiguity is deliberate
- In at least several passages, the term MUST refer to a goddess, not just a symbol:
  - 1 Kings 15:13: "an abominable image made for Asherah" — you can't make an image FOR an image
  - 1 Kings 18:19: "the 400 prophets of Baal and the 400 prophets of Asherah" — if Baal is a deity, so is Asherah
  - 2 Kings 23:4: listed alongside Baal and "the host of heaven" — both deities
- The ancient versions (Greek Septuagint, Latin Vulgate) understood *'asherim* as connected with trees and sacred groves, but also occasionally with the goddess Astarte — preserving a memory of a female deity

> **Dever's key hermeneutical point:** "The final redactors of the Hebrew Bible did not know who 'Asherah' had been, or whether she had existed at all. But the consonantal text — now Scripture — could not be altered. So the text we have implies that we are confronting in 'asherah only a 'symbol.' But again, a symbol of what? of whom? Functioning in what religious circles? And how did a 'mere symbol' actually work? I believe that only archaeology... can answer these questions."

### The Archaeological Evidence for Asherah

Dever presents the full cumulative case:

1. **The Ugaritic background:** 14th-13th century texts portray Asherah (*Aṯiratu*) as El's consort, "Lady Asherah of the Sea," "Mother of the Gods," "Creatress of the Gods." She had seventy divine sons. When El and Yahweh merged, Asherah came with the package.

2. **The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions** (c. 800 BCE): "Yahweh of Samaria/Teman and his Asherah"

3. **The Khirbet el-Qom inscription** (c. 700 BCE): Burial inscription invoking blessing "by Yahweh and his Asherah"

4. **The 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines:** Mass-produced nude female figurines found in domestic contexts throughout Judah. "Found in almost every excavated 8th-6th century house in Judah." These are almost certainly Asherah representations.

5. **Cult stands and iconography:** The Taanach stand shows a nude female, lions, cherubim, a tree of life, and a young bull — "an encyclopedia of Canaanite-Israelite religion."

6. **Standing stones at Arad:** Two *massebot* in the holy of holies — probably representing Yahweh and Asherah as a divine couple.

7. **The persistence of the symbol:** Even after Josiah's reform removed the asherah from the Jerusalem temple (2 Kings 23:6-7), the figurines continued in domestic contexts. Folk religion outlasted official religion.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that Asherah was worshipped in Israel and Judah (the cumulative evidence is overwhelming). HIGH that she was understood as Yahweh's consort (the inscriptions are unambiguous about the pairing). HIGH that Dever's archaeological case is stronger than Smith's text-based skepticism.

## Chapter VII: Asherah, Women's Cults, and "Official Yahwism"

### Claim: The suppression of Asherah was the suppression of women's religion

**Dever's claim:** Folk religion — centered on Asherah, fertility, healing, and the family — was primarily women's domain. "Official" Yahwism — centered on the Temple, the priesthood, written law, and exclusive monotheism — was men's domain. The Deuteronomistic reform was, in effect, a male clerical coup against women's traditional religious authority.

**Evidence presented:**

- The household was the primary locus of religious practice for most Israelites — and the household was women's domain
- "Women were the primary practitioners of household religion" — tending family shrines, making offerings, using figurines for fertility and protection
- Asherah was associated with fertility, childbirth, nursing, and the life cycle — all women's concerns
- The figurines were used by women: "prayers in clay" for conception, safe delivery, milk production, children's health
- "Women's work was mainly although not exclusively at home: harvesting some things; preserving, preparing, and cooking food (a constant task); spinning, weaving, and making all the family's clothes, bedding, and floor coverings; making baskets and pottery; caring for both the elders and a house full of children; and, as I shall show, tending to the family shrine and all of the religious rituals"
- The Deuteronomistic reformers were ALL men — priests, scribes, kings. They centralized worship in Jerusalem, which most women could never visit, and eliminated the domestic cults that women had practiced for centuries.
- "Official religion was a largely male preserve: priests, Temple functionaries, scribes, royal officials — all men. Women were excluded from the priesthood, from writing, from public office."
- "It is women's cults, centering on the veneration of Asherah, that the reformers set out to destroy."

**The feminist dimension:** Dever is careful to distinguish between "mainstream feminism" and "doctrinaire feminism," but his argument is inherently feminist: the biblical religion we've inherited is a male construction that systematically suppressed women's religious experience. Recovering Asherah is recovering women's history.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on women as primary domestic religious practitioners (ethnographic parallels + archaeological context support this). MEDIUM that the reform was specifically anti-women (rather than anti-polytheism generally). The gendered dimension is real but hard to prove was the PRIMARY motivation.

## Chapter VIII: From Polytheism to Monotheism

### Claim: Monotheism was a late, elite construction that never fully displaced folk religion

**Dever's claim:** The biblical narrative of Israel's journey from pure monotheism to syncretistic corruption and back is exactly backward. "Israelite religion was everywhere and always polytheistic in practice until the very end of the monarchy — and for most people, even beyond." Monotheism was the invention of a small circle of reformers who wrote the Bible. It only became the "religion of Israel" because they won the battle of the books.

**Evidence presented:**

- The archaeological evidence of polytheistic practice (figurines, multiple temples, inscriptions to other deities) continues UNABATED through the entire monarchy. Josiah's reform didn't stop it.
- Even Josiah's reform was limited: "we have no archaeological evidence whatsoever that Josiah's reform had any lasting effect on folk religion"
- The prophets were a tiny minority — "the 'Yahweh-alone' movement was a minority party until the very end"
- The exile created monotheism not because everyone became monotheistic but because the elite who went into exile wrote the Bible: "Monotheism was a 'book religion' — the religion of a book, written by the few for the few"
- "The real religions of ancient Israel persisted long after the canon was closed, and they have continued even into modern times in various guises"

**The irony Dever highlights:** "The very fact that the biblical writers condemn folk religion so vehemently is the best proof that it was widespread. You don't rail against things that aren't happening."

**Confidence:** HIGH that monotheism was a late elite construction (consensus across Smith, Römer, and Dever). HIGH that folk polytheistic practice continued despite reforms. MEDIUM that the exile was the sole catalyst (Dever, like Römer, may overstate the exile's role vs. longer developments).

## Chapter IX: What Does the Goddess Do to Help?

### Dever's Conclusion — Why This Matters

Dever ends with a personal, reflective chapter:

**For understanding the Bible:** "The Hebrew Bible is not a history of Israelite religion. It is a 'minority report' — the polemic of a small group of zealots who eventually won the day. But they won by writing the history, not by converting the masses."

**For understanding God:** "The God of the Hebrew Bible is a composite — he absorbed the characteristics of many earlier deities. And one of those deities was female. Traces of the Goddess survive even in the canonical text: in Wisdom literature, in the Shekhinah of later Judaism, in the feminine imagery for God in Isaiah."

**For women today:** "Recovering the Goddess is not about reviving ancient Israelite religion — that's impossible and undesirable. It's about recognizing that women's religious experience has been systematically suppressed in the Western monotheistic traditions. The Bible is not the whole story. It's not even most of the story."

**For faith:** Dever doesn't tell the reader what to believe. He tells them what he found in the ground. "I have tried to give the forgotten people of ancient Israel — especially the women — their voice back. What you do with that voice is up to you."

## Final Assessment: The Three Books Together

| Question | Smith | Römer | Dever |
|----------|-------|-------|-------|
| **Yahweh's origin** | Canaanite pantheon → absorption of El | Southern desert → Midian → Israel | Same as Römer; adds archaeological context |
| **Asherah: goddess or symbol?** | Symbol (minority) | Goddess — Yhwh's consort (majority) | Goddess — Yhwh's consort (overwhelming archaeological evidence) |
| **Monotheism's emergence** | Exile; multiple factors | Exile; primarily political | Exile; elite "Book religion" imposed on polytheistic masses |
| **Primary evidence** | Ugaritic texts, biblical texts | Biblical texts, inscriptions, history | Archaeological artifacts, figurines, cult sites |
| **The Bible's religion** | Literary/theological tradition | Elite construction with "traces of memory" | "Minority report" — never the religion of most Israelites |

**Where they converge:** All three agree that Israelite religion was polytheistic in practice until the late monarchy, that monotheism emerged in the exile, and that the biblical narrative of pure origins is theological retrojection.

**Where they diverge:** Smith is more cautious (especially on Asherah), more text-focused, and more methodologically anxious. Römer tells a cleaner narrative story. Dever builds his case from the ground up with artifacts — and he's the most confident that Asherah was Yahweh's consort.

**My current assessment after three books:** The cumulative case for Asherah as Yahweh's consort is now very strong — Smith's caution looks like excessive skepticism in light of Römer's textual argument and Dever's archaeological data. The case for monotheism as a late, elite construction is essentially settled. The case for Yhwh's southern origin is strong but not definitive. The "so what?" for faith is the same question Smith raised and none of them fully answer: if God has a history, what does that mean for belief in God?
