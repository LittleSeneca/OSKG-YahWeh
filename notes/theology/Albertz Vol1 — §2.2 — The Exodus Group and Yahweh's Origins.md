# Albertz — §2.2: The Religion of the Liberated Larger Group (the Exodus Group)

This is the section where Albertz lays out his account of Yahweh's origins. It's the single most important section in Volume I for the debate about where Yahweh came from and what kind of god he originally was. Albertz accepts the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis, argues Yahweh was a southern storm/mountain god older than Israel, and explains how an "alien god" could become a symbol of political liberation for an oppressed group of foreign conscript laborers in Egypt.

---

## Claim 1: The Exodus group was not "all Israel" but a small, declassed, ethnically mixed group of conscripted laborers (of 'Apiru/Hebrew type) in Ramesside Egypt — not nomads

**Albertz's claim:** "There was only one group of what was later to become Israel in Egypt." It was "an economically assimilated but socially declassed group of foreign conscripts to forced labour... whose solidarity had been undermined by state measures." The group was not nomadic: "from the start Yahweh religion is not a nomadic religion, as is constantly asserted."

**Evidence presented:**
- The biblical tradition itself notes a "clear jump" in group size from patriarchal families to the Exodus group — the social form shifts
- The Egyptians set the group to build "store cities Pithom and Ramses" (Ex 1:11), consistent with Ramesside construction in the eastern Delta (13th c.)
- The group is described as settled in the Delta (Ex 9:26) and unfamiliar with nomadic survival strategies in the wilderness — they need help from actual nomads (Num 10:29-32)
- Moses has an Egyptian name (msy, "give birth," like Thut-mose, Ramses) and is mistaken for an Egyptian by Midianites (Ex 2:19)
- The group is frequently called "Hebrews" ('ibrî) in Ex 1-12, and Yahweh is called "God of the Hebrews" (Ex 3:18; 5:3; 7:16; 9:1, 13; 10:3) — a recollection that the Exodus group belonged to the 'Apiru/Habiru class
- Helck's theory: the group was a detachment of prisoners-of-war of ethnically differing origin, conscripted for state building work
- An Egyptian letter of Ramses II mentions 'pr.w (Apiru) "bringing stones for the great pylon" — direct evidence of Apiru in building projects

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The social description (declassed forced laborers) is well-supported by Egyptian parallels. But the size ("one group, not all Israel") and the identification with 'Apiru are debated. The "Hebrew/Apiru" connection has a long and contested history in scholarship.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh religion began with a small, marginal outsider group rather than "all Israel," then: (a) Yahweh was adopted by other Israelite tribes later — Yahweh religion spread, not descended; (b) the Exodus was a minor event that later became the founding myth of a much larger entity; (c) the anti-domination, liberationist character of Yahweh religion is rooted in its actual sociological origin, not later theology.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a larger Exodus (though most critical scholars accept a small-group model). Minimalists (Thompson, Lemche) would question whether ANY Exodus group existed. Evangelical scholars would dispute the small-group reconstruction.

**Alternative reading:** The "Hebrew/Apiru" identification could be later editorial glossing rather than authentic memory. And the small-group hypothesis, while common, makes the Exodus tradition's centrality in later Israelite identity genuinely puzzling — why would a minor escape become THE founding story?

**My assessment:** Albertz's reconstruction is standard critical scholarship, well-argued and well-sourced. The strongest single point is the Egyptian name of Moses — if Moses were an Israelite invention, why give him an Egyptian name? The weakest point is the size of the group — Albertz assumes a small group but provides no mechanism for how its traditions became normative for all Israel. That mechanism is presumably what §2.3 (the tribal alliance) will explain.

## Claim 2: Yahweh was a southern Palestinian mountain/storm god (Hadad type) worshipped by Midianites/Kenites before Moses — Yahweh is older than Israel

**Albertz's claim:** "The god Yahweh is older than Israel; he was a southern Palestinian mountain god before he became the god of liberation for the Moses group." Moses encountered Yahweh through his Midianite father-in-law Jethro, a priest. Yahweh's original character was a storm god of the Hadad/Baal type, whose "dynamic properties... are twisted round into the political and historical sphere."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Egyptian lists** from Amenophis III (14th c.) and Ramses II (13th c.) attest "Y-h-w3" as a geographical/ethnic designation in "Š3św land" — the Shasu (nomads) of Yhw, in the region of southern Palestine/Edom
- **Old poetic texts** locate Yahweh's origin in the south: "Yahweh came from Sinai, dawned from Seir, shone forth from Mount Paran" (Deut 33:2; Judg 5:4-5; Ps 68:8-9; Hab 3:3). Yahweh is called "the one from Sinai" (Judg 5:5)
- **The Moses-Midian connection**: Moses fled to Midian, married the daughter of a Midianite priest (Ex 2:15ff.; 3:1; 18:1), and Jethro "invites the Israelites to a sacrificial meal for Yahweh on the mountain of God" (Ex 18:12). "This account cannot have been invented" because later relations with Midianites were hostile
- **Kenites** were regarded as notable Yahweh-worshippers later (Gen 4:15; Judg 4:17ff.) and settled within Judah (Judg 1:16)
- **Elijah's pilgrimage** to Horeb (I Kings 19), "forty days' journey south of Beersheba," suggests Yahweh was connected with a mountain in that southern desert region
- The mountain of God tradition was deliberately obscured later — Deuteronomists relocated Sinai to the remote peninsula and used "Horeb" as a cryptic cipher ("desolate land") to avoid Edomite associations after 587

**Confidence:** HIGH on the general southern origin (Egyptian Shasu-of-Yhw lists + old poetry + Moses-Midian tradition form a strong triangulation). MEDIUM on the storm-god/Hadad typology (the evidence is epiphanic imagery which could be generic theophanic language rather than distinctive storm-god identity).

**What's at stake:** This is the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis — Yahweh was NOT originally an Israelite god. He was borrowed/imported from southern nomadic tribes. If true, Israelite religion began with adoption of an alien deity, not with the revelation of a hitherto-unknown god. This directly contradicts any "early monotheism" model (Kaufmann) and supports the evolutionary consensus.

**Who disagrees:** Fleming (2021) argues Yahweh was NOT taken from outsiders but the name reflects Israel's own diverse origins. Others argue the Shasu-Yhw connection is a coincidence of similar-sounding names. Evangelical scholars would reject the Midianite origin.

**Alternative reading:** The Shasu-of-Yhw could be an early Israelite group already worshipping Yahweh, not a pre-Israelite group. The direction of borrowing could be Israel → Midian rather than Midian → Israel. Or the Moses-Midian connection could be a literary device explaining later Kenite integration into Judah.

**My assessment:** This is one of the strongest sections in the book. The triangulation of Egyptian inscriptions, old poetry, and the Moses-Midian tradition (with its criterion of embarrassment — Israel wouldn't invent a Midianite origin for its god) is compelling. Albertz's explanation of WHY an alien god could become a liberation symbol (not yet incorporated into any state pantheon, worshipped by freedom-loving nomads) is elegant. The deliberate obscuring of Sinai's Edomite location by later tradition (Horeb as "cipher") is a clever observation.

## Claim 3: Yahweh religion originated in a political liberation process, giving it three structural differences from family piety: historical/political orientation, mediated revelation, and conditional relationship (demands loyalty and "faith" as active political option)

**Albertz's claim:** "Israel's Yahweh religion arises in the liberation process of an oppressed outsider group... its world of religious symbols is therefore directly related to the process of historical and political liberation." This gives Yahweh religion a fundamentally different character from family piety in three ways: (1) historical and political orientation vs. family life-cycle; (2) mediated through representatives (Moses) vs. direct encounter; (3) conditional — requires active human decision and loyalty — vs. unconditional divine care.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Political orientation**: Yahweh's action is related to "the central situation of the group in an emergency" that is political (oppression by the Egyptian state), not familial. The "promise" is of liberation and new land — a long-term historical process spanning a generation (forty years), not a one-year pregnancy cycle
- **Mediated relationship**: "It is Moses who is given the oracle of Yahweh" — communication to the larger group requires a representative because "it is impossible to arrive at a decision unless the individual can formulate a view over which the group can find a consensus"
- **Conditional character**: Unlike the unconditional promise of a son in family piety, Yahweh's liberation requires Moses to "persuade the group, bring it solidarity and mobilize it," and the group must "decide whether they will commit themselves to the uncertain word of God and take the risky way to their liberation, thus abandoning all the social security that they still have"
- This decision is called "faith" ('mn in the hiphil) in Ex 4:1ff.; 14:11ff., 31 — "it does not just mean a religious but also an active political option"
- **Bias against domination**: Because Yahweh religion began with a social outsider group, it has "a bias against domination, transcending present social circumstances" that distinguishes it from ancient Near Eastern state religions which "derive themselves from earliest mythical times" and function to "legitimate rule and stabilize the existing social order"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The structural contrasts Albertz draws between family piety and official religion are analytically sharp and illuminating. But they may be too neat. The evidence that early Yahweh religion was *conditional* (requiring human response) while family piety was *unconditional* could reflect Albertz's Lutheran categories more than ancient reality.

**What's at stake:** If this structural analysis is correct, the tensions that run through Israelite religious history (prophetic critique vs. royal ideology, ethical demands vs. cultic reassurance, liberation vs. stabilization) are built into Yahweh religion from its origin in a liberation movement of social outsiders. The conflicts aren't accidents — they're the working-out of a fundamental structural tension.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more continuity between El worship and Yahweh worship, or who see the liberationist character as a later prophetic/Deuteronomistic retrojection onto an originally more conventional ANE deity.

**Alternative reading:** The "liberation" and "faith as political option" language in Exodus might be exilic or post-exilic retrojection, not authentic memory. The earliest recoverable stratum might show a more conventional patron deity who helps his clients escape, without the elaborate "political liberation" and "bias against domination" framing.

**My assessment:** The structural analysis is Albertz at his most brilliant and most vulnerable. Brilliant because it produces genuine analytical leverage — it explains why certain tensions recur throughout Israelite history. Vulnerable because the contrasts may be overdetermined by his sociological model. The claim that family piety is "unconditional" while official religion is "conditional" feels like a Lutheran law/gospel scheme mapped onto ancient Israel. That said, the observation that Yahweh religion's historical/political orientation IS distinctive in the ANE context is well-supported by comparative evidence (Cross made a similar point about myth becoming epic).

## Claim 4: Sinai was likely a mountain sanctuary in the Edom/Midian border region; the Exodus group experienced theophany there, combining historical and cultic experience — with history taking priority

**Albertz's claim:** After liberation from Egypt, Moses led the group to the mountain sanctuary of Yahweh where he had received his original oracle. The Midianites introduced the Exodus group to Yahweh's cult there (Ex 18:12). Sinai was "once a mountain sanctuary in the frontier area between Edom and Midian which was visited by a number of nomadic tribes." The tradition was later deliberately obscured to distance Yahweh from Edomite/Midianite associations.

**Evidence presented:**
- The mountain of God where Moses received his call (Ex 3:1ff.) is explicitly identified as the mountain where the liberated group would worship (Ex 3:12) — later tradition obscured this connection
- Old poetic traditions locate Yahweh's abode in Edom/Seir/Paran, east and west of the Arabah — right next to Midianite territory
- "Sinai" in the old poetry does not denote a specific mountain but a *region* — the name was later applied to a specific mountain
- Deliberate obscuring: Deuteronomists distance Sinai from Edom/Seir (Deut 1:2: "eleven days' journey from Horeb in the direction of Mount Seir to Kadesh Barnea") and use "Horeb" (= "desolate land") as a cipher
- Archaeology: almost no Late Bronze Age settlements on the Sinai peninsula; settlement gap at Kadesh from end of 3rd millennium to 10th century — "today there is more to suggest that the Old Testament Sinai is not to be sought on the present Sinai peninsula but east of the Arabah or the Gulf of Akaba"
- The theophany experience at Sinai added "the experience of the cultic nearness of God" to the "historical experience" — this combination is another "specific feature of Israelite religion." Unlike other ANE religions where cult dominates, "Israelite religion... maintained the priority of the historical experience of God. The Israelite relationship to God was not grounded in the Sinai theophany but in the Exodus."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the Edom/Midian location (the old poetry, the archaeological gap, and the deliberate obscuring converge). MEDIUM on the "priority of historical over cultic" as distinctive (comparative claims about ANE religion are hard to verify).

**What's at stake:** If Sinai was in Midian/Edom, the Kenite hypothesis is further strengthened — Israel first encountered Yahweh AT a Midianite sanctuary. The entire "Mosaic revelation" narrative is reinterpreted as the Exodus group being initiated into an existing cult.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain a traditional Sinai peninsula location. Those who argue the "Edomite" poetic references are anachronistic. Those who see the Exodus-Sinai connection as a literary construct, not historical memory.

**Alternative reading:** The archaeological argument from silence (no LB settlements on Sinai peninsula) has been challenged by Anati's claims about Har Karkom, though these are not widely accepted. The Edomite poetic references (Judg 5; Deut 33) may reflect later geographical knowledge retrojected, not original location.

**My assessment:** This is well-argued and aligns with Römer's similar conclusions in *The Invention of God* (Chapters 2-3). The archaeological gap on the Sinai peninsula is a genuine puzzle for the traditional location. Albertz's reconstruction of deliberate obscuring by later tradents is consistent with the textual evidence. The "historical priority over cultic" claim is more theological than historical — it's Albertz's confessional Protestant preference (word over sacrament) showing through.

## Claim 5: The "covenant" at Sinai is a late (Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic) theological retrojection; the Decalogue and image prohibition developed gradually from the 8th century onward, not from Moses

**Albertz's claim:** "It is quite certain that we cannot yet speak of a covenant in the full theological and legal sense in the early period." The covenant conception "only goes back to the Deuteronomic/Deuteronomistic theologians." Similarly, the image prohibition developed late: "the battle against divine images only began with the prophet Hosea" (8th c.), not with Moses. Pre-exilic Yahweh worship freely used cultic images — a bull statuette was found in northern Samaria, massebas stood in the Arad temple, a possible Yahweh figurine at Hazor.

**Evidence presented:**
- The pre-Deuteronomic Sinai narrative "probably first spoke of an obligation of Israel to the law" (Ex 24:3ff.*), expanded to a formal covenant "only in the early post-exilic period" (KD)
- Hosea was the FIRST to polemicize against divine images (Hos 4:17; 8:4f., 6; 10:5; 11:2; 13:2; 14:4, 9) — before him, Yahweh worship with images was uncontroversial
- Archaeological evidence: massebas in the Arad temple's holy of holies; Yahweh worship at Dan with images (Judg 17f.); the Nehushtan serpent at Jerusalem (II Kings 18:4); the Bethel bull
- The image prohibition developed gradually: late 8th c. = ban on precious metal statues (Ex 20:23b); 7th c. = ban on any divine images (Ex 20:4a); later = ban on traditional cult symbols like massebas and asheras (Deut 16:21f.); exilic period = extension to house cults
- "Yahweh is not originally a god who could be drawn into the world through an image as a guarantor of the existing social order, but a god who with his promise of liberation transcends the existing world"

**Confidence:** HIGH on the archaeological evidence for image-use in pre-exilic Yahwism (multiple sites, multiple artifact types). HIGH on the gradual development of the image prohibition (stages can be traced textually). MEDIUM on the covenant being entirely late (some form of formalized relationship before Deuteronomy is plausible).

**What's at stake:** If the covenant and commandments are late theological constructs, the entire "Mosaic law" tradition is a retrojection. The distinctive elements of Israelite religion (aniconism, covenant, law) are products of the monarchy and exile, not the founding period. Moses shrinks from lawgiver to liberator/organizer.

**Who disagrees:** Evangelical and Jewish traditional scholars who maintain Mosaic origins for the covenant and commandments. Also scholars who see pre-Deuteronomic covenant traditions (e.g., the Shechem covenant in Joshua 24).

**Alternative reading:** Even if the full-blown "covenant" concept is Deuteronomic, a less formalized "obligation" or "relationship" that later developed into covenant theology is entirely plausible and arguably presupposed by the pre-Deuteronomic material.

**My assessment:** This is solid critical scholarship. The archaeological evidence for images in Israelite cult is decisive — Albertz is right that pre-exilic Yahwism was aniconic only in aspiration, not in practice. The gradual development of the image prohibition is well-documented. I'm slightly less convinced about the covenant — the total absence of any pre-Deuteronomic covenant concept seems overstated. But the basic point (the fully developed Sinai covenant is a later projection) is standard critical consensus.

## §2.2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Exodus group = small declassed mixed group of forced laborers | MEDIUM | Minimalist denial of any historical Exodus; size of group unknown |
| 2. Yahweh = southern mountain god worshipped by Midianites before Israel | HIGH (southern origin); MEDIUM (Midianite specifically) | Fleming's alternative; Shasu-Yhw as coincidence |
| 3. Political liberation origin → structural differences from family piety | MEDIUM | Overly neat contrasts may reflect Albertz's Lutheran categories |
| 4. Sinai in Edom/Midian; historical priority over cultic | MEDIUM-HIGH | Limited archaeological data; comparative "cult dominance" claim hard to verify |
| 5. Covenant and image prohibition are late (8th-6th c., not Mosaic) | HIGH (image prohibition); MEDIUM (covenant) | Possible pre-Deuteronomic covenant traditions |

**Strongest section:** The argument for Yahweh's southern origin (Claim 2). Albertz's triangulation of Egyptian inscriptions, old poetry, and the Moses-Midian tradition (with criterion of embarrassment) is tight and convincing. The explanation of WHY an alien southern god could work as a liberation symbol is excellent.

**Weakest section:** The structural contrasts between family piety and official religion (Claim 3), which sometimes feel like a sociological grid applied too neatly. The "unconditional vs. conditional" and "pre-moral vs. ethical" dichotomies are suspiciously Lutheran.

**Connection to broader debate:** Albertz's account of Yahweh's origins is essentially the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis with a social-historical twist. He agrees with the consensus (Cross, Smith, Römer) on the southern origin but emphasizes the sociological mechanism: Yahweh's *alien status* was the key — an outsider god not yet captured by any state apparatus could serve as the symbolic focus for a liberation movement. This is a more materialist explanation than Smith's "convergence and differentiation" model or Römer's emphasis on political accidents. For Albertz, *class* (the Exodus group as declassed laborers) is as important as *geography*.

**What to watch:** The centrality of "liberation" and "bias against domination" as Yahweh's core character will drive the rest of Albertz's narrative. If he can show that Israelite religious history is the story of how this liberation impulse was alternately suppressed (by monarchy), revived (by prophets), and institutionalized (by Deuteronomists), the model earns its keep. If the liberationist framing feels forced onto later material, it collapses.
