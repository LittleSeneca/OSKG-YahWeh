# Smith — Chapter 5: The Traits of Deities

Smith asks what a deity WAS considered to be in Ugaritic and Israelite thought — moving from structural organization (Part I) to characteristic traits. Four traits are examined across both corpora: strength/size, body/gender, holiness, and immortality. The chapter's central finding: Israelite monotheism did not invent new divine traits — it REDUCED the number while concentrating valued traits in one deity. Yahweh has no peers, no sex, no death, no family — but maintains all the expected roles of divine protection and blessing. Monotheism produces a deity "more concealed in character, more revealed in function."

## Claim 1: Superhuman strength and size
→ [[claim-divine-superhuman-strength-size-universal]] | **smith-obm-5.1** | Confidence: very-high
  Superhuman strength and size are universal divine traits across Ugarit and Israel, archaeologically confirmed by temple architecture (Ain Dara, Solomon's Temple). The shared trait establishes Israel's continuity with ANE neighbors.
  Depends on: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]]. Supports: [[claim-divine-powers-map-onto-spatial-zones]].

## Claim 2: Anthropomorphism persists
→ [[claim-anthropomorphism-persists-israel-transcendent]] | **smith-obm-5.2** | Confidence: high
  Anthropomorphism is the norm across Ugarit, Mesopotamia, and Israel — Israel never abandoned it but developed a "transcendent" form (imago Dei reverses the analogy). Selective suppression retained personal traits while eliminating those implying peer deities (divine sexuality, death).
  Supports: [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]], [[claim-israel-broadly-west-semitic-religion-not-pristine-monotheism]]. Contradicts: De Moor's crisis-of-polytheism thesis.

## Claim 3: Yahweh gendered male, not sexual
→ [[claim-yahweh-male-gendered-not-sexual]] | **smith-obm-5.3** | Confidence: high
  Yahweh's maleness expresses authority and personal relationship, not biology. Female imagery applied to Yahweh is metaphorical, consistent with ANE practice (male gods with female epithets).
  Contradicts: Trible's androgyny and Eilberg-Schwartz's homoeroticism readings. Supports: [[claim-female-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]], [[claim-asherah-question-genuinely-unresolved]].

## Claim 4: Holiness as brilliance and separation
→ [[claim-holiness-brilliance-separation-suppressed-myth]] | **smith-obm-5.4** | Confidence: medium-high
  Holiness is rooted in "this-worldly" brilliance and cleanliness; Israel's priesthood developed holiness as separation from sex and death, which likely censored older mythologies involving divine sexuality and death. The silence about divine sex/death may be evidence of successful suppression, not original absence.
  Supports: [[claim-convergence-differentiation-framework-israelite-religion]], [[claim-cult-places-objects-considered-divine]].

## Claim 5: Immortality is the norm; divine death is narrative device
→ [[claim-immortality-norm-divine-death-narrative-polemic]] | **smith-obm-5.5** | Confidence: high
  Deities are free from death across the ANE. When gods die in narratives, it serves cosmological functions (Tiamat's carcass = cosmos) and dead gods lack cult. Israel's Psalm 82 uses divine death POLEMICALLY, not narratively — another instance of monotheistic rhetoric.
  Supports: [[claim-divine-council-seventy-sons-of-el]]. Contradicts: Frazer's "dying and rising gods" paradigm. Edges to smith-obm-6.1-6.3.

---

## Claim 6: Convergence of valued divine traits
→ [[claim-monotheistic-convergence-valued-divine-traits]] | **smith-obm-5.6** | Confidence: high
  The monotheistic Yahweh represents convergence of valued traits, not invention of new ones. All four traits (strength, body/gender, holiness, immortality) are shared with Ugarit and Mesopotamia. Israel's distinctiveness is in the reduction and combination — a deity "more concealed in character, more revealed in function."
  Supports: [[claim-yahweh-composite-deity-product-of-convergence]], [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]]. Pivot from Part II (traits) to Part III (monotheism's emergence).

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

**Strongest section:** Section 2 (Body and Gender). The treatment of anthropomorphism across three cultures (Ugarit, Mesopotamia, Israel) with attention to both persistence and transformation is the best synthetic writing in Part II. The rejection of De Moor's crisis thesis and of androgyny/homoeroticism readings is crisp and well-argued.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 (Immortality). Too much space on the Ecclesiastes 3:11 digression, which is philologically interesting but not load-bearing for the chapter's argument.

**Most striking detail:** The four-tiered correspondence between human problems and divine traits (powerlessness → strength, absence → sexuality, guilt → holiness, mortality → eternity). This is Smith's most humanistic moment — a reminder that he's doing history of religions, not just philology.

**Cross-reference:** The chapter's conclusion that monotheism eliminated divine sex and death while retaining blessing/protection functions directly anticipates Ch 6 (Baal's death) and Ch 8-10 (monotheistic rhetoric). The suppression of divine sexuality by priestly holiness categories connects to Ch 4's Asherah discussion — if Asherah WAS a goddess, her elimination may have been driven by priestly holiness concerns as much as by monotheistic theology. Sommer's *The Bodies of God* provides a complementary argument about divine embodiment across ANE and Israelite texts; Smith's approach is more comparative and less theological.
