# Smith — Ch4: Pluralities, Pairings, and Other Divine Relations

Smith explores the finer-grained relationships within the divine household: military retinues, dead ancestors, divine pairings, the Asherah question, hypostases (Athtart as Name of Baal), divinized qualities, and sacred objects. The chapter closes Part I by arguing that Ugaritic polytheism achieved conceptual unity through the family metaphor — a unity Israel would later reconfigure around a single individual deity after the erosion of traditional lineage structures.

---

## Claim 1: Divine military retinues (Baal's helpers, the Reshephs) fit the patrimonial household model

**Author's claim:** Baal has a meteorological retinue (CAT 1.5 V 6-9) and "divine helpers" ('il t'dr b'l in 1.47.26). Resheph appears as both singular god and plural "Reshephs" (ršpm). "As members of the second tier of the divine assembly and sons of the divine patriarch, these two gods were in a position to have retainers work for them. Accordingly, these retinues are well within the paradigm of the patrimonial household."

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal's meteorological retinue in CAT 1.5 V 6-9.
- 'il t'dr b'l, "Baal's divine helpers" in pantheon lists (1.47.26, 1.118.25).
- mhr b'l wmhr 'nt, "Baal's and Anat's warriors" in 1.22 I 8-9.
- The plural ršpm in CAT 1.91.11 (entering the royal palace/sanctuary).
- Egyptian comparison: Ramses III's army "as mighty as Rashaps."
- Sidonian inscriptions: 'rṣ ršpm, "land of Reshephs" (KAI 15:2).
- BH rešep as part of Yahweh's theophanic vanguard in Habakkuk 3:5 and Deuteronomy 32:23-24.
- Philo of Byblos (PE 1.10.20): allies of Elos called "Eloim" — the pattern of naming a group after its patron deity.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Ugaritic evidence for divine retinues is fragmentary but consistent. The Philo of Byblos parallel strengthens the model.

**What's at stake:** If divine retinues are structurally similar to human household retainers, the family model extends even to military organization — the pantheon is a household, not a bureaucracy. This reinforces Smith's core argument about the family as root metaphor.

**Who disagrees:** Few directly dispute this; the evidence is simply sparse. Alternative models might emphasize bureaucratic/administrative hierarchy (as in Mesopotamia) over household retainers.

**Alternative reading:** The plural forms (ršpm, 'eloim) could be honorific plurals or abstract collectives rather than literal "retinues named after the patron god."

**My assessment:** The data is suggestive but thin. The strongest parallel is Philo of Byblos, and his reliability is debated. The Israelite reflex (Resheph in Yahweh's retinue in Hab 3:5) is a good data point — it shows the concept traveled.

## Claim 2: The Rephaim and mlkm represent dead royal ancestors, functioning as cultural identity markers

**Author's claim:** The Rephaim (rp'um) and mlkm ("kings") in Ugaritic ritual texts represent deceased royal ancestors — the Rephaim as older tribal predecessors, the mlkm as more recent monarchs. At Ugarit they mark cultural IDENTIFICATION with the monarchy. In deuteronomic texts, the Rephaim mark cultural DISTANCE — they become the ancient "Canaanites" whom Israel is to disidentify from.

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.161 distinguishes rp'um (older predecessors) from mlkm (deceased monarchs).
- rp'u bears the same address of 'ttrt as mlk (1.108.2; 1.107.42; 4.790.17), suggesting identification or conflation.
- El's blessing in 1.15 III 2-4, 13-15 evokes rp'um alongside high deities — royal cult integrated both.
- Israelite parallel: Bloch-Smith's 1992 Judahite Burials shows popular Israelite devotion to the dead. Deuteronomists react AGAINST this.
- "Both the Ugaritic and biblical views of the Rephaim are the products of their societies... Rephaim signal cultural distance or 'disidentification'" in deuteronomic texts.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Ugaritic evidence is genuinely tricky — Smith admits "the evidence supports little more than conjectures." The Israelite inversion theory is plausible but speculative.

**What's at stake:** If the Rephaim function as identity markers, the transition from polytheism to monotheism involves not just theology but CULTURAL SELF-DEFINITION. Monotheism becomes partly an exercise in saying "we are NOT them."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read rp'um as a distinct category of chthonic deities rather than ancestors. The reconstruction of 1.161 is debated.

**Alternative reading:** The Rephaim could be chthonic deities proper, not dead kings — the ancestor interpretation over-reads fragmentary ritual texts. The deuteronomic "Rephaim" might reflect independent traditions rather than a polemical inversion.

**My assessment:** Smith's caution is warranted — he calls the evidence "tricky" and admits it supports "little more than conjectures." The cultural-identity argument is elegant but needs more data than we have. The Bloch-Smith connection to Israelite practice is good.

## Claim 3: Ugaritic binomial divine names reflect priestly theological reflection on divine interrelations — not fusion of originally separate deities

**Author's claim:** The prevalence of paired divine names (Kothar wa-Hasis, Shahar and Shalim, El and Athirat, etc.) reflects "theological reflection" on relationships within divinity. Against de Moor's "fusion" theory: "In many cases, we do not possess enough information to confirm or preclude such a conclusion."

**Evidence presented:**
- Extensive catalogue of paired names from ritual and mythological texts: 'nt w'ttrt, ktr wḫss, šḥr wšlm, 'il w'atrt, 'arṣ wšmm, mt wšr, etc.
- Distribution is strongest in ritual texts (esp. CAT 1.100) — "one might interpret many of them largely as associated pairings for offering sacrificial provisions."
- Some binomials lack coordinating w- (Kothar Hasis), analogous to biblical compounds: Yahweh Elohim (Gen 2), El Elyon (Gen 14:18-20), Adonay Yahweh (Ezekiel).
- De Moor's fusion theory: Anat and Athtart as "two 'Anatu-goddesses who were regarded as aspects of one divine being." Smith: "Is there really enough evidence?"
- Del Olmo Lete's syncretism theory: double names reflect merging of Amorite and Canaanite pantheons.

**Confidence:** HIGH that binomials are pervasive and significant. MEDIUM that they represent "theological reflection" specifically. The priestly-scribal context is plausible but under-argued.

**What's at stake:** If binomials are theological reflection, Ugaritic scribes were doing something conceptually sophisticated — not just listing gods but exploring their interrelations. This supports Smith's larger claim that polytheism had conceptual coherence.

**Who disagrees:** De Moor (fusion from separate deities), del Olmo Lete (syncretism of two pantheons).

**Alternative reading:** Binomials could be simple scribal convention — a way to organize offering lists efficiently. The "theological reflection" might be modern over-reading.

**My assessment:** Smith's resistance to de Moor's fusion theory is well-taken — we simply don't know the prehistory of these pairings. But his alternative ("theological reflection") is also speculative. The biblical parallel (Yahweh Elohim as redactional combination) is interesting but may not generalize to Ugarit.

## Claim 4: The Asherah question is genuinely unresolved — Smith backs away from his earlier skepticism but remains cautious

**Author's claim:** "Currently...most scholars believe that Asherah was a goddess in ancient Israel, possibly even Yahweh's consort. In short, the jury seems still to be out on the issues, which are undeniably complex." This represents a shift from his earlier view that the asherah was merely a cult symbol.

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.43.13: l'nth — interpreted by Dietrich/Xella as "for his Anat" (possessive suffix on divine name, implying consort relationship), but Pardee reads -h as adverbial/locative. Smith: "There is no entirely satisfactory answer."
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscriptions (ca. 800): lyhwh...wl'šrth, "Yahweh and his asherah." The suffixed form parallels the Ugaritic l'nth question.
- Tel Miqne/Ekron inscriptions (7th c.): l'šrt and lqdš on shards; silver medallion with goddess on lion; "for Baal and for Padi" inscription. Gitin: "the inhabitants worshiped the goddess Asherah."
- 2 Kings 23:4: cult of "Baal, Asherah and all the host of heaven" in Jerusalem Temple.
- 2 Kings 21:7: pesel hā'ăšērâ — an "image" of the asherah, which "normally an image would point to a deity."
- Smith's own shift: "In the past I adopted this view [asherah as symbol only]. Other scholars have also since expressed doubt about Asherah as a goddess."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The evidence for Asherah as goddess is strong (Tel Miqne, 2 Kgs 21:7, Kuntillet Ajrud). But the consort question specifically remains genuinely ambiguous — does the suffix on 'šrth denote possession ("his" Asherah) or something else?

**What's at stake:** The single most explosive question in the field: did Yahweh have a wife? If yes, Israelite religion was far more "Canaanite" than the biblical narrative admits. If no (the asherah was a cult object, not a goddess), the polytheistic elements are more about objects than persons.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (confident: Asherah was Yahweh's consort, based on archaeology and inscriptions), Römer (agrees she was a goddess), Olyan (Asherah as goddess but not necessarily Baal's consort). The minority: Smith's earlier self, Tigay (onomastic evidence shows no Asherah names), and others who read asherah as cult object.

**Alternative reading:** The asherah could have been a hypostasis or symbol that SOME Israelites treated as a goddess and others did not — the disagreement may have been internal to ancient Israel, not just modern scholars.

**My assessment:** Smith's shift from skepticism to agnosticism is honest and tracks the field. After Dever's archaeological evidence (3000+ pillar figurines) and Römer's epigraphic analysis, the weight favors Asherah as goddess. But Smith is right that the Kuntillet 'Ajrud grammar (the suffix on 'šrth) is not a slam dunk — Pardee's caution about Ugaritic -h on proper names applies here too.

## Claim 5: Athtart as "Name of Baal" (šm b'l) represents a divine hypostasis — a cultically available presence, with biblical parallels in the "Name" theology

**Author's claim:** Athtart's title šm b'l, "name of Baal," marks her as the hypostasis or cultic presence of Baal. The biblical divine "Name" (šēm) operates similarly: it is the theophanic manifestation that accompanies Yahweh, especially in martial contexts.

**Evidence presented:**
- Athtart's title šm b'l in CAT 1.16 VI 56 and KAI 14:18 (Phoenician).
- Philo of Byblos (PE 1.10.31): Astarte and Baal rule together as king and queen under El's consent.
- Cross, Mullen, McCarter, Olyan: Athtart as name-hypostasis of Baal.
- Biblical parallels: Exodus 23:20-21 — Yahweh's messenger has "my name within him." The name acts as warrior in Isaiah 30:27.
- Psalm 29:1-2: the "glory of his name" (kěbôd šěmô) in a storm theophany context — the name manifests radiance comparable to Akkadian melammu.
- Personal names from Ugarit and Amarna: šm b'l, šm 'nt, šum-adda — "name of [deity]" as a personal name pattern.
- McCarter: hypostases as "cultically available presence in the temple."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The "name" as hypostasis has strong support in biblical theology and reasonable Ugaritic backing. But whether Athtart was ORIGINALLY understood as a hypostasis or acquired this title secondarily is unclear.

**What's at stake:** If the biblical "Name theology" (Deuteronomic emphasis on God's name dwelling in the temple) has a polytheistic background in divine hypostases, it transforms how we read a central biblical concept. The Name wasn't an abstraction — it was a mode of divine presence inherited from polytheism.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read šm b'l as simply "the renown/fame of Baal" rather than a hypostasis. Those who see Name theology as a deuteronomic innovation, not an inheritance.

**Alternative reading:** The title šm b'l could reflect Athtart's role as Baal's herald or representative rather than an actual hypostatic manifestation. The name "dwelling" in the temple could be a metaphor rather than a theophanic reality.

**My assessment:** This is one of Smith's most interesting arguments — and it has implications for Parts II-III. If the divine Name in Israel inherits the function of a goddess-hypostasis, then monotheism absorbed and transformed a feminine divine presence. Smith doesn't quite say this, but it's the implication.

## Claim 6: Divinized qualities (ḥnn, ṣdq mšr, kbd, nr) represent semi-independent divine entities, especially in liturgical contexts

**Author's claim:** Ugaritic texts list qualities alongside deities (CAT 1.65: ḥnn 'il, nṣbt 'il, šlm 'il; CAT 1.123: ṣdq mšr, kbd w nr). These are "divinized qualities" that function as divine attendants, especially in theophanic/liturgical settings.

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.65: "The Mercy of El, The Constancy of El, The Well-being of El" invoked after El and Athirat.
- CAT 1.123.12-16: nġh, ṣdq mšr, ḥn bn 'il, kbd w nr alongside standard deities.
- ṣdq mšr parallels Philo of Byblos' Misor and Sydyk.
- Biblical parallels: nōgāh, kābôd, nûr appear in theophanic contexts (2 Sam 22:13; Ezek 10:4; Hab 3:4).
- Psalm 43:3: "'Light' and 'Truth' are personified as two of Yahweh's attendants, sent from the divine council as 'guardian angels' to guide the psalmist to the Temple."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The biblical parallels make the pattern convincing. Ugaritic evidence by itself could be read differently but the Israelite reflexes confirm the conceptual category.

**What's at stake:** If qualities could be divinized, the line between "deity" and "attribute of deity" was fluid in West Semitic thought. This matters for monotheism: when Israel attributes Mercy, Truth, Glory to Yahweh alone, it may be absorbing what were once separate divine entities into the single god's personality.

**Who disagrees:** Those who read the lists as abstract nouns (liturgical invocations of qualities) rather than hypostatized entities. The boundary between "invoking a quality" and "invoking a semi-divine personification of that quality" is blurry.

**Alternative reading:** These could be scribal shorthand — ways to list what the deity provides to worshippers (mercy, constancy, well-being) rather than independent entities.

**My assessment:** The biblical reflexes are the strongest evidence here. When Psalm 43:3 treats Light and Truth as divine guides (not just abstractions), it confirms that this pattern of thought was alive in Israel. Smith's continuum from quality → personified quality → attendant deity is plausible.

## Claim 7: Cult places and objects were considered divine — divinity adhered to cult

**Author's claim:** "By virtue of association with particular deities or their cult sites, particular locales could be regarded as divine." Also cult objects: the lyre (knr) appears in deity lists. "Based on the extension of the rubric of divinity to cult places and objects, one may suggest that divinity was considered to adhere to cult."

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal's mountain Sapan is labeled "divine" (1.3 III 29, IV 19) and appears in deity lists (1.47.32).
- The sacred garden and spring appear in ritual contexts.
- 'arṣ w šmm ("Earth and Heaven") and ġrm w['mqt] ("Mountains and Valleys") in deity lists — Cross reads them as "olden gods" from ancient theogony.
- The lyre (knr) in deity list 1.47.32; Jacobsen on Mesopotamian harps: "even the harps were considered deities."
- Consumables of cult (dqt, trt) appear in divine terms.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The specific examples are solid (Sapan, lyre), but the general claim that "divinity adhered to cult" goes beyond the evidence. The mechanism (association? consecration? representation?) is not specified.

**What's at stake:** If cult objects were divine, the "aniconic" strain in Israelite religion (no images of Yahweh) was a radical departure, not just from other ANE religions but from the very logic of how divinity works — divinity inhabits objects.

**Who disagrees:** This is relatively uncontroversial for Ugarit but the degree of divinization is debated. Were these "divine" in the same sense as deities, or merely "sacred/set apart"?

**Alternative reading:** Objects in deity lists might represent the deity's paraphernalia listed for ritual completeness, not because they were thought to BE divine. The lyre might represent the god's instrument, not a god itself.

**My assessment:** The evidence for Sapan and the lyre is concrete. The extension to "divinity adheres to cult" is Smith drawing out implications — reasonable but stated more confidently than the evidence strictly warrants. The connection to Israelite aniconism (developed later) is important.

## Claim 8: The conceptual unity of Ugaritic polytheism was achieved through the divine family — Israel moved toward individual divine accountability after lineage erosion

**Author's claim:** "For ancient Ugarit, conceptual religious unity was expressed most strongly in the identification of the divine council as a divine family." Israel's later monotheism, by contrast, "was denuded of the divine family, a development perhaps intelligible in light of Israel's weakening family lineages and patrimonies."

**Evidence presented:**
- At Ugarit, the patronym was "the most important element...when identifying a person" (Pardee). Land transfers were from "sons of X" to "sons of Y." The family was the strongest form of social identity.
- "The polytheistic family may have provided what would have been in Ugaritic culture the most 'natural' means to express singleness and coherence in 'divine society.'"
- In Israel, by the 7th-6th centuries: prophetic and deuteronomic texts proclaim individual accountability (Jer 31:29-30; Ezek 18; Deut 24:16) over inherited family guilt.
- The post-exilic bêt 'ābôt ("fathers' house") replaces the older bêt 'āb — a shift possibly reflecting decline of traditional family structure.
- "A culture with a diminished lineage system...might be more predisposed both to hold to individual human accountability for behavior and to see an individual deity accountable for the cosmos."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is Smith's most ambitious sociological claim in Part I, and the evidence is correlational, not causal. The erosion of lineage and the rise of monotheism CO-occur but proving causation is another matter.

**What's at stake:** If Smith is right, monotheism is not just a theological development but a SOCIAL one — it emerges when the old family-based social structures that made the divine family intelligible break down. This is a materialist explanation for what is usually treated as an intellectual/spiritual development.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see monotheism driven by theological factors (the prophets' ethical vision), political factors (Assyrian/Babylonian imperial pressure), or literary factors (scribal redaction). Anyone who thinks the timeline of lineage erosion doesn't match the timeline of monotheistic emergence.

**Alternative reading:** The correlation could be reversed: monotheism's emphasis on individual accountability might have DRIVEN changes in family structure, not the other way around. Or both could be effects of a third cause (urbanization, imperial disruption).

**My assessment:** This is Smith's boldest claim in Part I and the least well-supported. It's a fascinating hypothesis that ties the theological and the social together, but as Smith admits, it's a "working hypothesis." The timeline needs more precision: when exactly did lineage systems erode in Judah, and can we date the earliest monotheistic expressions with enough confidence to establish sequence? I'm skeptical but intrigued.

## Ch4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Military retinues fit household model | MEDIUM-HIGH | Sparse Ugaritic evidence |
| 2: Rephaim as cultural identity markers | MEDIUM | Alternative readings of rp'um |
| 3: Binomials as theological reflection | MEDIUM | Scribal convention explanation |
| 4: Asherah question unresolved | MEDIUM | Weight of evidence favors goddess |
| 5: Athtart as Name-hypostasis | MEDIUM-HIGH | Alternative reading of šm as "renown" |
| 6: Divinized qualities as semi-independent entities | MEDIUM-HIGH | Boundary between quality and personification |
| 7: Cult places/objects as divine | MEDIUM | Degree of divinization unclear |
| 8: Lineage erosion drove monotheism | LOW-MEDIUM | Correlation ≠ causation; timeline questions |

**Strongest section:** Section 5 on Athtart as Name of Baal. The connection to biblical Name theology is genuinely illuminating and well-supported by textual parallels. This is Smith at his best — drawing a line from Ugaritic concept to Israelite transformation.

**Weakest section:** Section 8 (Afterword). The sociological argument about lineage erosion is provocative but the evidence is thin and the causal mechanism underspecified. Smith himself calls it a "working hypothesis" — it reads like a sketch of what would need a full monograph to defend.

**The Asherah pivot:** This chapter is notable for Smith's shift on Asherah. In his earlier book (The Early History of God), he was a skeptic. Here he concedes the majority now disagrees with his old position and the "jury is still out." This intellectual honesty is admirable, but the section on Asherah (section 4) is noticeably shorter and more tentative than its importance warrants.

**Cross-reference:** Smith's argument about the divine family as conceptual unity (Claim 8) extends the framework from Ch3. Cross's work on El's divine family (the 70 sons) is foundational here. Römer's *Invention of God* makes parallel arguments about the social context of monotheism but grounds them more in political history (imperial pressure) than family structures. Tigay's onomastic data — showing predominantly Yahwistic personal names even in early periods — creates tension with Smith's model: if most Israelites named their children after Yahweh, how "polytheistic" was the family structure really?
