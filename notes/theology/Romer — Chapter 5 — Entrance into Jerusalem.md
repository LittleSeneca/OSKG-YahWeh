# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapter 5

## The Entrance of Yhwh into Jerusalem

---

## Claim 1: Shiloh was Yhwh's first major sanctuary in Israel — archaeology confirms a major cult site destroyed c. 1050 BCE

**Römer's claim:** Before Jerusalem, before the monarchy, the primary center of Yhwh worship was Shiloh, located in the territory of Ephraim. Archaeology confirms an important cult site there in the 12th-11th centuries, destroyed by fire around 1050 — probably by the Philistines. The Bible preserves "strong historical memories" of Shiloh as Yhwh's home, memories that were still alive when Jeremiah wrote in the 6th century.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Biblical memory:** "The authors of the Bible seem to have been concerned to emphasize the importance of this site and of its destruction; they were also haunted by the memory that Shiloh had been once the home of a sanctuary of Yhwh." Jeremiah 7 and 26 recall its destruction centuries later.
- **Archaeological confirmation:** Shiloh was occupied in the 2nd millennium, became "very important again in the mid-twelfth to mid-eleventh centuries," and was then destroyed by fire around 1050 — "possibly by the Philistines."
- **The Samuel connection:** "Samuel had been offered by his mother to the sanctuary at Shiloh, which is described not as a tent but as a temple built with proper walls." Samuel is the transitional figure — a prophet of the old El-worshipping tradition (his name means "El is exalted" or "His name is El") who anoints Saul as king.
- **Shiloh as the bridge:** "It is perhaps because of this holy place (or because of the prophet Samuel) that Yhwh then became the god of Saul."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Shiloh was a major cult site (archaeology confirms significant occupation and destruction). MEDIUM-HIGH that it was specifically Yahwistic (the biblical tradition is consistent, but the archaeological evidence for the DEITY worshipped is indirect). MEDIUM that Samuel is the historical bridge figure (the Samuel narratives are late and idealized).

**What's at stake for faith:** Yhwh's first home in Israel was NOT Jerusalem. It was Shiloh — a modest hill-country sanctuary in Ephraim. The great temple of Solomon was a late development. Before David, Yhwh was a regional deity of the central highlands, worshipped at a site that was eventually destroyed and abandoned. The "city of God" was not originally God's city.

---

## Claim 2: David brought Yhwh to Jerusalem — this was a political masterstroke, not a divine revelation

**Römer's claim:** Jerusalem was originally a Canaanite city with its own deity (probably El Elyon, "God Most High" — see Genesis 14's Melchizedek, "priest of El Elyon" at Salem/Jerusalem). David conquered Jerusalem and made it his capital — a political masterstroke because it belonged to no tribe, making it neutral ground. David then brought Yhwh's ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) — a THEOLOGICAL masterstroke. He unified northern Yhwh worship (from Shiloh) with Jerusalem's El traditions under a single royal cult.

**Evidence presented — the political calculus:**

- **Jerusalem was not originally Israelite.** It was a Canaanite/Jebusite city that had never been conquered by any Israelite tribe. "David conquered Jerusalem" — the Bible is explicit about this (2 Samuel 5:6-9).
- **Jerusalem belonged to no tribe.** This was its political advantage: "David's city" rather than "Judah's city" or "Benjamin's city." It could serve as a neutral capital for a united kingdom.
- **The original deity of Jerusalem was El Elyon.** Genesis 14:18-20 has Melchizedek, king of Salem, as "priest of El Elyon" — a tradition that predates Yhwh's arrival. The name "Jerusalem" (*Yĕrûšālayim*) may be related to the god Shalem, a West Semitic deity.

**Evidence — David's theological synthesis:**

- **The ark transfer (2 Samuel 6):** David brings the ark of Yhwh — the symbol of the northern/Ephraimite Yhwh tradition — to Jerusalem. This is the narrative expression of a political-theological merger.
- **The royal psalms:** Psalm 110 (a royal psalm): "Yhwh said to my lord [the king]: 'Sit at my right hand'" — the Davidic king is enthroned alongside Yhwh in the old El city. Psalm 2 describes Yhwh and "his anointed" (the king) ruling the nations from Zion.
- **The double legitimation:** Yhwh gets a new capital and a royal temple. David gets divine sanction for his new dynasty. Both benefit from the arrangement.

**The Tel Dan stele (8th century BCE):**
- The only extra-biblical reference to David. Three Aramaic fragments celebrate Hazael of Damascus's victory over an Israelite-Judean coalition.
- The phrase *btdwd* is interpreted by most scholars as "house of David" — confirming a Davidic dynasty existed by the 8th century.
- BUT: "This tells us nothing about the historical figure of David but does show that the Aramaeans in the eighth century called the kingdom of Judah 'house of David,' just as the Assyrians called the kingdom of Israel 'house of Omri.'"

**The historical uncertainty about David:**
- "It is very difficult to discern the concrete historical facts behind the biblical narratives of the origins of the monarchy."
- "The three kings Saul, David, and Solomon have been constructed by the editors of the Bible as paradigmatic types": Saul (rejected king → northern kingdom), David (warrior/chosen → dynasty), Solomon (builder/sage).
- BUT: "There are several traits in the narrative... that cannot be pure invention." Kingdoms DID emerge in the Levant around 1000 BCE. The Philistine threat was real. David was originally a Philistine vassal — the biblical texts "try to give an apologetic interpretation of this fact."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Jerusalem was originally non-Israelite and that David conquered it (the biblical account is consistent and the archaeology supports a Canaanite city). MEDIUM that David brought Yhwh specifically (the ark narrative is plausible but late). MEDIUM that the El-Yhwh merger was David's deliberate policy (the evidence is circumstantial — we have the RESULT of the merger but not the process).

**What's at stake for faith:** The "city of God," the "place where God's name dwells," was someone else's city first. Yhwh moved in as a political arrangement — the patron deity of a new dynasty, installed in a conquered capital. The Psalms that celebrate God's enthronement in Zion are royal propaganda. Jerusalem is holy because David made it his capital, not because God chose it from eternity. The theology followed the politics.

## Claim 3: Saul, David, and Solomon are paradigmatic constructs — but behind them lie genuine historical developments

**Römer's claim:** The biblical portrayal of the first three kings is heavily shaped by the Deuteronomistic editors' theological program. But "the passage from Iron Age I to Iron Age II (about 1000) coincides with the origin of kingdoms in the Levant (Moab, Ammon, the Aramaean kingdoms). The fact that an Israelite 'kingdom' arose in an area under strong influence from the Philistines is certainly an element of the stories that has some historical basis."

**Evidence presented:**
- No extra-biblical attestation of Saul, David, or Solomon as individuals (the Tel Dan stele mentions the "house of David," not David himself)
- Saul's territory was in the Philistine sphere of influence; David was originally a Philistine vassal — "even though the biblical texts try to give an apologetic interpretation of this fact"
- Samuel (whose name is El-based) is the transitional figure who anoints Saul (whose name contains no theophoric element)
- The biblical accounts of Saul's rise are "very divergent" (1 Samuel 8-12) — evidence of multiple, conflicting traditions being preserved

**Confidence:** HIGH that the biblical portraits are theologically shaped. MEDIUM that a historical David existed (the Tel Dan stele confirms the dynasty; a dynasty normally has a founder). LOW on the specific details of David's reign.

**What's at stake for faith:** The founder of the Messianic line, the ancestor of Jesus, the man after God's own heart — may have been a Philistine vassal who seized a Canaanite city and installed his patron deity there for political reasons. The biblical portrayal of David as the ideal king is theology, not biography.

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |

| Shiloh was Yhwh's first major sanctuary | HIGH | Archaeological evidence for WHICH deity is indirect |
| David conquered Jerusalem | HIGH | Consistent biblical + archaeological evidence |
| Jerusalem's original deity was El Elyon | MEDIUM-HIGH | Genesis 14 may retroject later theology |
| David brought Yhwh to Jerusalem | MEDIUM | Ark narrative is plausible but late |
| The El-Yhwh merger was David's policy | MEDIUM | Evidence is circumstantial |
| Saul/David/Solomon as paradigmatic constructs | HIGH | The theological shaping is obvious |

**Strongest section:** The analysis of Jerusalem's political geography — David's choice of a neutral, non-tribal capital makes excellent political sense.

**Weakest section:** The claim that David deliberately engineered the El-Yhwh merger — plausible but we have no direct evidence of David's religious policies.

**The narrative arc through Chapter 5:** Southern desert god → Midianite mediation → adopted by El's people → installed in El's city by a political warlord. Yhwh now sits where El sat. The identification of the two gods is accomplished politically, not theologically.
