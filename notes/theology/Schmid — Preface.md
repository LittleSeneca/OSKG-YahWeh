# Schmid — Preface

Schmid situates his project as a "historical theology" — a descriptive, reconstructive enterprise that identifies where the Hebrew Bible itself engages in "reflexive and synthesizing efforts" that can be called theology. He explicitly renounces kerygmatic or normative ambitions. The German original carried no qualifier because German "Theologie" is less loaded than English "theology."

---

## Claim 1: The theology of the Hebrew Bible as a discipline is in crisis
→ [[claim-ot-theology-crisis-disconnected-from-broader-field]] | **schmid-ht-preface.1** | Confidence: high
  The subdiscipline of OT theology maintains an "almost extra-territorial existence" — not integrated into the communal discourse of Hebrew Bible studies. While other subdisciplines have undergone "enormous upheavals in the past forty years," theology has "remained comparatively diffuse." The aging curve of authors (Eichrodt at 43 → Jeremias at 76) is empirical evidence of a field whose practitioners delay synthesis until late career.
  **Edges:** Supports: [[claim-historical-theology-must-clarify-theology-and-hebrew-bible-definitions]], [[claim-historical-theology-descriptive-reconstructive-not-normative]]. Disagreed by: Spieckermann, Hartenstein (theology has "blossomed").

---

## Claim 2: The project must first clarify what "theology" means
→ [[claim-historical-theology-must-clarify-theology-and-hebrew-bible-definitions]] | **schmid-ht-preface.2** | Confidence: high
  "The project of a 'theology of the Hebrew Bible' is in need of clarification both from the side of theology as well as from the side of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament." Köhler's classic definition is "little more than a tautology." The "almost inflationary" plural use of "theology" has supplanted the classical conception. And "there is no such thing as THE Hebrew Bible" — multiple canonical forms require different theologies.
  **Edges:** Depends on: [[claim-ot-theology-crisis-disconnected-from-broader-field]]. Supports: [[claim-historical-theology-descriptive-reconstructive-not-normative]]. Disagreed by: Gerstenberger, Fischer (plural "theologies" IS the correct approach).

---

## Claim 3: "Theology" here is a descriptive, reconstructive enterprise
→ [[claim-historical-theology-descriptive-reconstructive-not-normative]] | **schmid-ht-preface.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  "'Theology of the Hebrew Bible' should essentially be carried out as a reconstructive (and not a constructive) undertaking." Theology as "a descriptive project" — identifying where the Hebrew Bible itself engages in "reflexive and synthesizing efforts." The German original lacked "historical" because German "Theologie" can denote a historical approach without kerygmatic overtones.
  **Edges:** Depends on: [[claim-ot-theology-crisis-disconnected-from-broader-field]], [[claim-historical-theology-must-clarify-theology-and-hebrew-bible-definitions]]. Disagreed by: Janowski (theology concerns application, not just genesis); Barthians (theology MUST be normative).

---

## Claim 4: The book is a "Hebrew Bible undertaking" — neither Christian nor Jewish
→ [[claim-schmid-hebrew-bible-neither-christian-nor-jewish]] | **schmid-ht-preface.4** | Confidence: low-medium
  "In this respect it is neither a Christian nor a Jewish, but rather a Hebrew Bible undertaking. It is not a project immediately related to present-day application; it instead has an initially historical focus." This framing lets Schmid work with the Jewish Tanakh without claiming to do Jewish theology, and discuss Christian reception without being bound by Christian dogma.
  **Edges:** Depends on: [[claim-historical-theology-descriptive-reconstructive-not-normative]]. Disagreed by: Levenson, Sommer (the category "Hebrew Bible" is a Christian construct pretending to neutrality); Christian theologians (OT theology IS Christian theology by definition).

## Chapter-Level Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Theology of HB is in crisis/disconnected | HIGH | Empirical — if theologies engaging new literary history proliferate, this ages quickly |
| Need to clarify "theology" and "Hebrew Bible" | HIGH | Definitional — clarification is always needed; the real test is whether his definitions work |
| Theology as descriptive/reconstructive | MEDIUM-HIGH | Philosophical — can description ever be genuinely non-normative? |
| "Neither Christian nor Jewish" framing | LOW-MEDIUM | Ideological critique — the claim to neutrality is itself a theological stance |

**Strongest section:** The diagnosis of theology's disconnection from the broader field, and Schmid's commitment to letting literary-historical findings drive theological conclusions.

**Weakest section:** The claim to be "neither Christian nor Jewish." It's an aspiration dressed as a methodological achievement.

**Overall:** The Preface establishes Schmid's project clearly: a descriptive theology that takes the late-dating revolution in Hebrew Bible scholarship seriously. The ambition is admirable. Whether he can deliver a "descriptive" theology that still counts as "theology" is the question the rest of the book must answer. The claim to confessional neutrality is the most vulnerable part — and ironically, the most similar to the American "history of religions" approach (Smith, Dever) that Schmid partly agrees with and partly transcends.
