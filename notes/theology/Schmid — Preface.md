# Schmid — Preface

Schmid situates his project as a "historical theology" — a descriptive, reconstructive enterprise that identifies where the Hebrew Bible itself engages in "reflexive and synthesizing efforts" that can be called theology. He explicitly renounces kerygmatic or normative ambitions. The German original carried no qualifier because German "Theologie" is less loaded than English "theology."

---

## Claim 1: The theology of the Hebrew Bible as a discipline is in crisis because it has lost integration with the broader field

**Schmid's claim:** The subdiscipline of OT theology maintains an "almost extra-territorial existence" — it is "not really integrated into the communal discourse of Hebrew Bible studies." While introductory studies, the history of Israel, and the history of Israelite religion have undergone "enormous upheavals in the past forty years," theology has "remained comparatively diffuse."

**Evidence presented:**

- The major theologies have been published by scholars at progressively older ages (Eichrodt at 43 in 1933 → Jeremias at 76 in 2015)
- Von Rad's 1957 work set such a high standard that it produced a "ten years' pause in the stream of OT theologies"
- Important drafts have been published in recent years, but unlike the close interaction in non-theological subdisciplines, theology has not integrated new conclusions

**Confidence:** HIGH. The aging curve of authors is an empirical observation. The disconnect between theology and other subdisciplines is widely acknowledged in the field — Schmid's diagnosis of a "mechanization of biblical studies" when theological questions are neglected is a pointed but fair critique of German-language scholarship.

**What's at stake:** If Schmid is right that theology has become disconnected from critical scholarship, then theologies written without engagement with recent literary-historical findings (especially late dating) are methodologically obsolete. This sets up his entire project: a theology that takes the new literary history seriously.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue that OT theology has recently "blossomed" — Hermann Spieckermann, Friedhelm Hartenstein. Schmid acknowledges their work but says a "blossoming" requires "rootedness in the topsoil of Hebrew Bible studies."

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that theology's separateness is a feature, not a bug — it pursues different questions than history or literary criticism. The "lack of integration" is appropriate to a discipline with different aims.

**My assessment:** Schmid's diagnosis rings true. The late-dating revolution in European scholarship has massive implications for theology that most theologies have not yet absorbed. Schmid's project — letting the new literary history drive theological conclusions — is the right response.

## Claim 2: The project must first clarify what "theology" means for the Hebrew Bible and what "Hebrew Bible/Old Testament" designates

**Schmid's claim:** "The project of a 'theology of the Hebrew Bible' is in need of clarification both from the side of theology as well as from the side of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament." He notes a "lack of orientation" about what "theology" means, coupled with the problem that "there is no such thing as the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament" — we have Jewish and Christian versions requiring different theologies.

**Evidence presented:**

- Ludwig Köhler's classic definition ("opinions, thoughts, and concepts of the OT brought into the correct contextual configuration") is "little more than a tautology"
- The plural and "almost inflationary" use of "theology" has "virtually supplanted the classical conception"
- Multiple canonical forms exist: the Jewish Tanakh and various Christian Old Testaments

**Confidence:** HIGH. These are definitional problems, not empirical claims. The canonical diversity is indisputable. The "inflationary" use of "theology" is a fair observation — if everything is "theology," nothing is.

**What's at stake:** Schmid's definition of theology as "reflexive and synthesizing efforts" will determine what counts as theological data. If theology is too broadly defined, the project collapses into general description. If too narrow, it misses the phenomena Schmid wants to capture.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who favor the plural "theologies of the Hebrew Bible" approach (Gerstenberger, Fischer). They would say the "inflationary" use IS the correct approach — the Hebrew Bible contains multiple theologies, not one.

**My assessment:** Schmid's call for clarification is well-taken. The key move will be in how he defines "theology" in §§2-9. The canonical diversity point is crucial — he'll need to specify which canon he's working with.

---

## Claim 3: "Theology" here is a descriptive, reconstructive enterprise — not normative or kerygmatic

**Schmid's claim:** "'Theology of the Hebrew Bible' should essentially be carried out as a reconstructive (and not a constructive) undertaking." He understands theology as "a descriptive project" — "Where does the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament allow for the recognition of reflexive and synthesizing efforts that could be described at least rudimentarily as theology?"

**Evidence presented:**

- The German original lacked the qualifier "historical" because German "Theologie" can denote a historical approach without kerygmatic overtones
- He cites parallels: de Pury and Knauf (1995) on "kerygmatic or descriptive?", Carl Steuernagel (1925), Dalit Rom-Shiloni (2016) on a "Jewish descriptive approach"
- He acknowledges the alternative: "under certain presuppositions and by means of certain definitions it is quite possible to pursue 'theology' of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament as a 'normative' undertaking"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The claim itself is a methodological choice, not an empirical claim. It's coherent and well-defended. The question is whether a merely "descriptive" project can still count as "theology" in any meaningful sense — Schmid anticipates this objection.

**What's at stake:** This is the central methodological commitment of the entire book. If Schmid is wrong that descriptive theology is a legitimate form of theology, then his project is misnamed — it's history of Israelite religion, not theology. If he's right, he's carved out a space for theology that takes critical scholarship seriously without being captured by dogmatic commitments.

**Who disagrees:** Bernd Janowski argues that theology "concerns not only the genesis of the Hebrew Bible but also its application." Traditional systematic theologians would say theology MUST be normative. Barthians would reject a merely historical approach entirely.

**Alternative reading:** The descriptive/normative distinction may be false. All description involves selection and interpretation. Schmid's "descriptive" theology will inevitably reflect normative judgments about what counts as "theological." The alternative is to make those normative commitments explicit rather than hiding behind "description."

**My assessment:** Schmid's approach is intellectually honest and methodologically appropriate for a critical scholar. But he's right that some will call this "not real theology." The key question is whether his "descriptive" theology can generate insights that matter beyond the historical — he'll need to show that understanding what the texts MEANT theologically has implications for what they might MEAN theologically. If his project is purely antiquarian, the critics have a point.

## Claim 4: The book is a "Hebrew Bible undertaking" — neither Christian nor Jewish

**Schmid's claim:** "In this respect it is neither a Christian nor a Jewish, but rather a Hebrew Bible undertaking. It is not a project immediately related to present-day application; it instead has an initially historical focus."

**Evidence presented:**

- He acknowledges that Jewish and Christian canons differ and each "strictly speaking requires its own theology"
- He frames his approach as "elevating the textually-indicated theological profile of the canon as a whole, divisions of canon, books, texts, and themes"
- From a Protestant perspective: "the authority of Scripture does not come in the form of imperatives, but that imperatives come through the mediation of encultured propositions of thought that have proven increasingly normative on the basis of the weight of their content"

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The claim to be "neither Christian nor Jewish" is ambitious but question-begging. The selection of the Hebrew Bible as an object of study is itself a theological choice shaped by Christian and Jewish tradition. Can a scholar in the Christian theological tradition (Zurich is a Protestant faculty) truly produce a "non-Christian" reading?

**What's at stake:** This framing lets Schmid work with the Jewish Tanakh without claiming to do Jewish theology, and discuss Christian reception without being bound by Christian dogma. It's a strategic move that creates intellectual space. But it may be impossible to actually achieve.

**Who disagrees:** Jewish scholars (Levenson, Sommer) have argued that the very category "Hebrew Bible" is a Christian construct that pretends to neutrality while masking Christian assumptions. Christian theologians would say theology of the OT IS Christian theology by definition.

**Alternative reading:** The claim to neutrality is a rhetorical stance, not a genuine methodological achievement. Schmid is doing Protestant historical-critical theology with a secular veneer. That's fine — but it's better to own it than to claim a neutrality that doesn't exist.

**My assessment:** This is the weakest claim in the Preface. Schmid wants to have it both ways: the academic credibility of "neutral" historical study and the relevance of theological inquiry. The "neither Christian nor Jewish" framing is a noble aspiration but probably unachievable. His actual work will inevitably reflect his location in a Protestant theological faculty. That doesn't invalidate his conclusions, but the framing overstates the neutrality.

## Chapter-Level Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Theology of HB is in crisis/disconnected | HIGH | Empirical — if theologies engaging new literary history proliferate, this ages quickly |
| Need to clarify "theology" and "Hebrew Bible" | HIGH | Definitional — clarification is always needed; the real test is whether his definitions work |
| Theology as descriptive/reconstructive | MEDIUM-HIGH | Philosophical — can description ever be genuinely non-normative? |
| "Neither Christian nor Jewish" framing | LOW-MEDIUM | Ideological critique — the claim to neutrality is itself a theological stance |

**Strongest section:** The diagnosis of theology's disconnection from the broader field, and Schmid's commitment to letting literary-historical findings drive theological conclusions.

**Weakest section:** The claim to be "neither Christian nor Jewish." It's an aspiration dressed as a methodological achievement.

**Overall:** The Preface establishes Schmid's project clearly: a descriptive theology that takes the late-dating revolution in Hebrew Bible scholarship seriously. The ambition is admirable. Whether he can deliver a "descriptive" theology that still counts as "theology" is the question the rest of the book must answer. The claim to confessional neutrality is the most vulnerable part — and ironically, the most similar to the American "history of religions" approach (Smith, Dever) that Schmid partly agrees with and partly transcends.
