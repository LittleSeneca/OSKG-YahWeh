# Lewis — Conclusion

The conclusion is brief — essentially a recap of the seven "tastings" and an honest acknowledgment of what was omitted. Lewis uses the conclusion to flag what a truly comprehensive volume would require: full treatment of goddesses (using Astarte as an illustrative case study), polytheism, angelic/demonic beings, and the many divine attributes he couldn't cover (Yahweh as creator, storm god, healer, shepherd, teacher, etc.).

## Conclusion Overall Assessment

The conclusion serves its purpose: recapping the seven tastings, acknowledging limitations, and gesturing toward future work. The Astarte excursus is the most valuable part — it functions as a kind of penitential offering for the book's neglect of goddesses. The bibliography and indices that follow (not included in this text file excerpt) are presumably massive and would be essential for any researcher.

**The book as a whole:**
Lewis has produced a magisterial reference work that synthesizes textual, epigraphic, iconographic, and archaeological evidence for El and Yahweh in ancient Israelite religion. Its greatest strengths: the integration of iconography (the Fribourg school's legacy), the sophisticated use of onomastics (Albertz's revolution), the careful distinction between El and Yahweh as originally separate deities, the balanced treatment of the Midianite hypothesis, and the compelling argument for aniconism as genuine Israelite practice rather than Deuteronomistic propaganda. Its greatest weakness: the near-total neglect of goddess religion, which makes the book less "ancient Israelite religion" than "the masculine divine in ancient Israel." The book is essential reading for anyone working on Yahweh's origins or Israelite aniconism. It should be read alongside Dever (for archaeology of popular religion), Stavrakopoulou (for divine embodiment), and Smith (for the emergence of monotheism).
