# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 3

## Yahweh and Asherah

---

## Claim 1: The asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol, not evidence of a separate goddess cult in monarchic Israel
→ [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] | **smith-ehg-3.1** | Confidence: high
  Smith's strongest claim. The asherah was a Yahwistic symbol throughout the monarchy — widespread, long-lasting, and associated with Yahweh not Baal.
  Contradicts: [[claim-asherah-el-consort-transferred-to-yahweh]] (Römer), [[claim-asherah-goddess-in-ot-passages]] (Day)
  Supports: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]], [[claim-female-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]]
  Challenged by: [[claim-asherah-goddess-in-ot-passages]] (Day's five-passage rebuttal), [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (Römer)


## Claim 2: The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to the asherah symbol, not the goddess
→ [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]] | **smith-ehg-3.2** | Confidence: medium
  Grammar favors Smith (possessive suffix on divine name unusual in Hebrew) but Ugaritic counter-examples exist. Majority reads same inscription and reaches opposite conclusion.
  Contradicts: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (Römer reads same inscription as proof of divine couple)
  Partially supported by: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-cult-object-symbolized-goddess]] (Day agrees on grammar, disagrees on conclusion)
  Challenged by: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (Römer), [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-cult-object-symbolized-goddess]] (Day's hybrid)


## Claim 3: The biblical evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess in monarchic Israel
→ [[claim-biblical-evidence-insufficient-for-goddess]] | **smith-ehg-3.3** | Confidence: low
  Smith at his most skeptical — and most vulnerable. Each biblical passage is ambiguous but the cumulative case is stronger than Smith allows. Majority disagrees.
  Contradicts: [[claim-asherah-goddess-in-ot-passages]] (Day directly examines same passages, concludes goddess)
  Challenged by: [[claim-bible-deliberately-obscures-yhwh-asherah-link]] (Römer — Bible's silence is evidence FOR the goddess)
  Partially supported by: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]] (consistent with Smith's broader skepticism)


## Claim 4: Female imagery from the asherah was eventually absorbed into descriptions of Yahweh
→ [[claim-female-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]] | **smith-ehg-3.4** | Confidence: medium
  The Wisdom-asherah connection is speculative but the broader absorption pattern is well-supported. Female imagery applied to Yahweh may trace back to Asherah's maternal/nurturing profile.
  Depends on: [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] (absorption requires asherah within Yahwism)
  Supported by: [[claim-asherah-was-stylized-tree]] (Day on tree imagery providing iconographic bridge)
  Challenged by: general ANE convention argument (female imagery was normal divine rhetoric, not goddess-derived)


## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 3

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Asherah was a Yahwistic symbol throughout monarchy | HIGH | Very solid — biblical and inscriptional evidence converge |
| Kuntillet Ajrud refers to symbol, not goddess | MEDIUM | Ugaritic counter-examples for divine names with suffixes |
| Biblical evidence insufficient for goddess | LOW | Cumulative case is stronger than Smith allows; majority disagrees |
| Female imagery absorbed into Yahweh | MEDIUM | Could be general ANE convention, not Asherah-specific |

**Strongest section:** §1 (Distribution in the Biblical Record). The data is clear and compelling.

**Weakest section:** §4 (Asherah — An Israelite Goddess?). Smith's skepticism may be excessive. I'd bet against him here.

**Most interesting section:** §5 (Assimilation of Imagery). Even if Smith is wrong about the goddess question, the broader pattern of absorption is well-established. The Wisdom-asherah connection is speculative but generative.

**The unresolved tension:** Smith argues extensively that the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol, not a goddess. But if it was just a Yahwistic symbol that never represented a goddess, WHY did the Deuteronomists condemn it so violently? Smith's answer (secondary confusion with Astarte, offense at divinatory/healing functions) is weak. The simpler explanation is that the symbol DID represent a goddess, and the polemicists knew it. Smith needs a better explanation for the intensity of the opposition.
