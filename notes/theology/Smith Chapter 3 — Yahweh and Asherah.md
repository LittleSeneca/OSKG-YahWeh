# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 3

## Yahweh and Asherah

---

## Claim 1: The asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol, not evidence of a separate goddess cult in monarchic Israel

**Smith's claim:** The asherah (a wooden pole or stylized tree) was present in both northern and southern royal cults and in popular religion. It was associated with Yahweh, not Baal. But it was a SYMBOL within Yahwism, not evidence that a goddess named Asherah was actively worshipped as Yahweh's consort during the monarchy. The goddess may have existed earlier (Genesis 49:25 possibly preserves her epithets), but by the monarchic period, her cult had been absorbed into Yahweh's.

**Evidence presented — distribution of the asherah:**

- **Geographic spread:** Attested at Ophrah (Judges 6), Samaria (1 Kings 16:33, 2 Kings 13:6), Jerusalem (2 Kings 21:7, 23:6), Bethel (2 Kings 23:15), and generally "on every high hill and under every green tree" (1 Kings 14:23, 2 Kings 17:10)
- **Chronological spread:** From Judges period through Josiah's reform
- **Cultic association:** "Olyan has argued that the asherah was associated historically with Yahweh and not with Baal." In 1 Kings 18:19 the prophets of Asherah appear only once vs. Baal's prophets five times — "some critics view the single reference as a secondary addition"
- **No prophetic opposition before the 8th century.** Jehu opposed Baal but not the asherah. Hosea opposed Baal but not the asherah. "Olyan observes that no prophet opposed the asherah until the eighth century, and the prophetic passages that criticize the asherah appear to be Deuteronomistic or derivative."
- **Function:** Next to altars (Deut 16:21, Judges 6:25-26), possibly used for divination (Hosea 4:12, Habakkuk 2:19, Micah 5:11-13) and healing (Talmud Pesahim 25a prohibits using asherah wood for remedies — implying it WAS so used)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the distribution and Yahwistic association (the data is clear). MEDIUM on the divination/healing functions (evidence is sparse and Talmudic source is late).

**What's at stake for faith:** If the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol (not a foreign goddess), then the Deuteronomistic reformers weren't purging foreign contamination — they were destroying elements of their own Yahwistic tradition that they had come to view as unacceptable. The "idolatry" they condemned was, in many cases, traditional Yahwism. This reframes the entire biblical narrative of reform.

**Who disagrees:** Almost no one disputes the asherah's presence and Yahwistic association. The debate is about what it MEANT. Majority: it represented the goddess Asherah as Yahweh's consort. Smith: it was a symbol that had lost its goddess connection.

**My assessment:** This is Smith's strongest claim in the chapter. The evidence for the asherah's widespread presence in Yahwistic contexts is overwhelming. Whether it represented a goddess or not is a separate question (see Claim 3 below), but the basic point — that Yahwism included this symbol for centuries — is solid.

## Claim 2: The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to the asherah symbol, not the goddess

**Smith's claim:** The 8th-century inscriptions reading "Yahweh of Samaria/Teman and his asherah" (*lyhwh... wl'šrth*) most naturally refer to a cult symbol belonging to Yahweh, not to a goddess named Asherah who was his consort. The grammar supports "his asherah" (the symbol) over "his Asherah" (the goddess).

**Evidence presented — the grammar debate:**

- The pronominal suffix on *'šrth* indicates it's a common noun, not a personal name. "Divine names do not appear in Hebrew with a pronominal suffix."
- **Counter acknowledged:** Ugaritic HAS examples of divine names with suffixes ('aṯrty, 'nth) — so the grammar argument isn't airtight. Smith concedes this.
- **Semantic argument:** "If l'šrth refers to the goddess... then it is unclear what 'his Asherah' means. Only by assuming an ellipsis of 'his consort, Asherah'... does this interpretation make reasonable sense." If it means "his asherah" (the symbol), then it straightforwardly means something that belongs to Yahweh — which makes better sense.
- **McCarter's hypostasis theory:** The asherah could be a hypostasis of Yahweh (like "the Name of Baal" or "the Face of Baal" in Phoenician) — a personified divine attribute, not a separate goddess.
- **Etymological critiques:** Proposals to translate *'šrth* as "sanctuary," "consort," "goddess," or "trace" lack etymological basis in Hebrew. The word means what it looks like: "his asherah."
- **The texts are Israelite, not foreign:** They use Hebrew vowel letters (*matres lectionis*), not Phoenician conventions. The pottery is Judean. Kuntillet Ajrud was likely a royal Judean way-station.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The grammatical argument is strong but not decisive (Ugaritic counter-examples exist). The semantic argument ("his X" makes more sense for a possession than a consort) is genuinely compelling but not a proof.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the crux of the "did God have a wife?" question. If Smith is right, the answer is no — the asherah was Yahweh's symbol, not his consort. If the majority is right, Yahweh had a wife, and Israelite religion included a divine couple. The difference is enormous.

**Who disagrees:** The majority (Dever, Day, Olyan, Zevit, Ackerman, Hadley, Keel/Uehlinger). See the Foreword/Preface note for the full list and the [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins|Kuntillet Ajrud grammar debate]].

**Alternative reading:** The majority: the suffix on *'šrth* is a genuine possessive ("his Asherah"), and the goddess was Yahweh's consort by virtue of the Yahweh-El identification (El's consort became Yahweh's consort). The Ugaritic parallels for divine names with suffixes show this is grammatically possible, even if unusual.

**My assessment:** Smith has the better grammatical argument (possessive suffix on a personal name IS unusual in Hebrew), but the majority has the better cumulative case (the biblical polemic, the iconography, the Ugaritic background all point toward a goddess). I'd flag this as: grammar favors Smith, everything else favors the majority. Reasonable scholars can disagree. The evidence is genuinely ambiguous.

## Claim 3: The biblical evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess in monarchic Israel

**Smith's claim:** The biblical passages usually cited as evidence for Asherah as a goddess are either (a) late polemical constructions, (b) references to the cult symbol rather than a deity, or (c) references to Astarte misidentified as Asherah. "There is no clear reference to the goddess in the Bible, apart from 1 Kings 18:19, possibly a polemic against Astarte."

**Evidence presented — Smith evaluates each passage:**

| Passage | Traditional Reading | Smith's Counter |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **1 Kings 18:19** ("prophets of Asherah") | Asherah was a goddess with prophets in Israel | Asherah is not attested in ANY Tyrian/Phoenician text. This is probably a polemic against Astarte, mislabeled as Asherah. Possibly a secondary gloss. |
| **2 Kings 21:7** ("image of the asherah") | *Pesel* (image/idol) implies a goddess statue | *Pesel* could refer to an elaborate form of the asherah pole, not necessarily an anthropomorphic statue. |
| **2 Kings 23:4** (asherah between "the baal" and "host of heaven") | Listed between two deities, therefore a deity | Cultic objects can be made FOR a symbol just as for a deity. Verse 6 describes the SAME asherah being dragged out and burned — it's a wooden pole, not a statue. |
| **Judges 3:7** ("the asherahs") | Plural goddesses | Generic term for foreign goddesses, paired with "the baals." Interchangeable with "the astartes" in Judges 2:13 — this shows generic usage, not a specific goddess. |
| **Jeremiah 2:27** (tree = father, stone = mother) | Asherah as consort of Yahweh (Olyan) or Baal (others) | The maternal language could apply to Yahweh himself (Deut 32:18 uses birth imagery for Yahweh). Not necessarily a goddess reference. |
| **Genesis 49:25** (*šādayim wārāḥam*) | Goddess epithets | Possibly early goddess language, but refers to El's consort, not Yahweh's — and was NOT applied to Yahweh in later tradition. |

**Additional negative evidence:**
- No personal names with Asherah as theophoric element (contrast El, Baal, Yahweh, even Anat)
- Prophetic and legal condemnations never refer to the goddess — only the symbol
- "Asherah was not a Phoenician or Punic goddess during the Iron Age" — so 1 Kings 18:19's Tyrian context doesn't fit

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Smith at his most skeptical — and his most vulnerable. The majority of scholars he otherwise agrees with reject his conclusion here.

**What's at stake for faith:** See above. This is the "did God have a wife?" question.

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone. But Smith also lists scholars who agree with him (at least partially): Lang, Miller, Tigay, Winter, Frevel, Korpel, Cross, McCarter (hypostasis theory), Emerton (cautious).

**Alternative reading:** Smith is right that each individual passage is ambiguous. But the CUMULATIVE case is stronger than any single passage. When you have an asherah pole next to Yahweh's altar, inscriptions saying "Yahweh and his asherah," biblical polemic treating the asherah as a deity, and Ugaritic background with Asherah as El's consort — the simplest explanation is that Israel inherited the goddess along with the god El. Smith's alternative (the symbol lost its goddess connection early) requires more steps and more speculation.

**My assessment:** Smith is probably wrong here, but his caution is methodologically instructive. He's applying the same skepticism to the goddess claim that conservatives apply to the polytheism claim: "the evidence isn't sufficient to prove it." The difference is that Smith applies skepticism EVEN WHEN the conclusion would help his larger argument. That's intellectual honesty. But intellectual honesty doesn't make him right — and the cumulative case for Asherah as a goddess is stronger than Smith allows.

## Claim 4: Female imagery from the asherah was eventually absorbed into descriptions of Yahweh

**Smith's claim:** While the asherah as a distinct cult symbol was eliminated, its imagery — particularly maternal, nurturing, life-giving language — was absorbed into Yahweh's profile. The figure of Wisdom in Proverbs 1-9 is the clearest example. Yahweh was described in female imagery without being considered female.

**Evidence presented:**

- **Wisdom as transformed asherah:** Proverbs 3:13-18 describes Wisdom as a "tree of life" — the same imagery associated with the asherah. The passage forms a chiasm using the root *'šr* ("happy"), echoing the root of Asherah's name.
- **Deuteronomy 32:18:** "The Rock who begot you... the God who gave you birth" (*mĕhōlĕlekā*) uses birth imagery for Yahweh. This is the Yahwistic transformation of the maternal stone/tree symbolism condemned in Jeremiah 2:27.
- **Hosea 14:9:** "I am like an evergreen cypress, from me comes your fruit" — possibly transforms asherah tree imagery into a Yahwistic symbol of life.
- **Female imagery for Yahweh:** *Rḥm* (compassion/womb) language (Isa 49:13, Jer 31:20, Hos 2:21), mother imagery (Isa 42:14, 46:3, 49:15, 66:9, 13). "It is precisely these features that belong to Asherah in Canaanite literature."
- **ANE parallels:** Mesopotamian and Hittite prayers address gods with both male and female parental imagery. "Female metaphors do not imply a female status for a god." Yahweh could receive female imagery without being considered female.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on direct Asherah-to-Wisdom connection (interesting but speculative). HIGH that female imagery was applied to Yahweh. MEDIUM that this imagery specifically derives from Asherah rather than general ANE conventions.

**What's at stake for faith:** Moderate. The female dimension of biblical God-language has a history — and that history may trace back to a goddess whose cult was suppressed. The nurturing, life-giving aspects of God that many Christians find most meaningful may be inherited from Asherah.

**Who disagrees:** J.W. Miller argues female imagery for Yahweh is metaphorical and rare; Yahweh was understood as male. The female language doesn't derive from a goddess — it's normal ANE divine rhetoric.

**Alternative reading:** The female imagery for Yahweh is genuinely innovative (especially in Second Isaiah) and represents theological expansion, not absorption of a suppressed goddess. The Wisdom figure draws on ANE wisdom traditions, not Asherah specifically.

**My assessment:** The Wisdom-asherah connection is one of Smith's more speculative arguments, but it's intriguing. The broader point — that Yahwism absorbed and transformed imagery from suppressed cults — is well-supported elsewhere (Baal's storm imagery, El's titles) and applying it to Asherah is reasonable even if the specific examples are debatable.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 3

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Asherah was a Yahwistic symbol throughout monarchy | HIGH | Very solid — biblical and inscriptional evidence converge |
| Kuntillet Ajrud refers to symbol, not goddess | MEDIUM | Ugaritic counter-examples for divine names with suffixes |
| Biblical evidence insufficient for goddess | LOW | Cumulative case is stronger than Smith allows; majority disagrees |
| Female imagery absorbed into Yahweh | MEDIUM | Could be general ANE convention, not Asherah-specific |

**Strongest section:** §1 (Distribution in the Biblical Record). The data is clear and compelling.

**Weakest section:** §4 (Asherah — An Israelite Goddess?). Smith's skepticism may be excessive. I'd bet against him here.

**Most interesting section:** §5 (Assimilation of Imagery). Even if Smith is wrong about the goddess question, the broader pattern of absorption is well-established. The Wisdom-asherah connection is speculative but generative.

**The unresolved tension:** Smith argues extensively that the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol, not a goddess. But if it was just a Yahwistic symbol that never represented a goddess, WHY did the Deuteronomists condemn it so violently? Smith's answer (secondary confusion with Astarte, offense at divinatory/healing functions) is weak. The simpler explanation is that the symbol DID represent a goddess, and the polemicists knew it. Smith needs a better explanation for the intensity of the opposition.
