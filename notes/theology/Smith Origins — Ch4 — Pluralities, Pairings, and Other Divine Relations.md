# Smith — Chapter 4: Pluralities, Pairings, and Other Divine Relations

The culminating chapter of Part I. Smith surveys the smaller-scale divine relations that fill out the family household model: military retinues, dead ancestors, divine pairings, the Asherah problem, hypostatic names, divinized qualities, and the extension of divinity to cult objects. The Afterword ties Part I together with the book's most important sociological argument: Ugaritic society's family-centered identity made the divine family a "natural" expression of cosmic unity; Israel's declining lineage system (8th-6th centuries) made the individual deity more intelligible. Monotheism has a SOCIAL precondition.

---

## Claim 1: Military retinues of Baal and Resheph — and later Yahweh — reflect the patrimonial household structure

**Author's claim:** "As members of the second tier of the divine assembly and sons of the divine patriarch, these two gods were in a position to have retainers work for them. Accordingly, these retinues are well within the paradigm of the patrimonial household."

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal's meteorological retinue (1.5 V 6-9) and "Baal's divine helpers" ('il t'dr b'l, 1.47.26 = 1.118.25).
- Resheph appears in plural form (ršpm) in Ugaritic (1.91.11 — cult statues of Resheph entering the royal palace), Egyptian (Ramses III's army "as mighty as Rashaps"), and Phoenician ('rṣ ršpm, "land of Reshephs" — Sidonian inscriptions KAI 15:2).
- Habakkuk 3:5: Resheph as member of Yahweh's theophanic retinue alongside Deber. "Habakkuk 3:5 mentions Resheph as a member of Yahweh's theophanic retinue. Given the warrior character of both Baal and Resheph, these pluralities would seem to be military retinues of the gods after whom they are named."
- Philo of Byblos (PE 1.10.20): "the allies of Elos, i.e. Kronos, were called 'eloim', as the ones named after Kronos would be 'Kronians'" — a divine military retinue named after the patron deity. Suggests that biblical 'elōhîm may carry the same semantic range — not just "gods" but "allies/warriors of El/Yahweh."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The evidence for Resheph's plural retinue across Egypt, Ugarit, Phoenicia, and Israel is independently attested and convergent. The extension to the meaning of 'elōhîm via Philo of Byblos is more speculative.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh's "divine assembly" was originally a warrior retinue on the model of Baal's and Resheph's, then the "host of heaven" is not a late import but an ancient West Semitic structure inherited by Yahwism. The biblical 'elōhîm may have once meant something closer to "divine warriors" than abstract "gods."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read 'elōhîm in the Bible strictly as "God" or "gods" without the martial retinue connotation. The Philo of Byblos passage is late and may reflect Hellenistic reinterpretation.

**Alternative reading:** The plural forms (ršpm, 'elōhîm) could be plurals of majesty/amplification rather than evidence of retinue structure. The Philo of Byblos passage could be euhemeristic rationalization, not reliable tradition.

**My assessment:** The cross-cultural attestation of Resheph-plurals (Egypt, Ugarit, Phoenicia, Israel) is strong. The Philo of Byblos link to 'elōhîm is intriguing but thin — one late text. The Habakkuk 3:5 connection between Resheph and Yahweh's retinue is the best biblical data point.

## Claim 2: The Rephaim are cultural markers of identity — insiders for Ugarit, outsiders for deuteronomic Israel

**Author's claim:** "The Rephaim then are cultural markers of identity, insiders for the Ugaritic monarchy and society as well as Israelite popular religion, but outsiders for deuteronomic authors. Both the Ugaritic monarchy and authors of deuteronomic works use the putatively ancient cultural tradition of the Rephaim to claim political identity and authority."

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit: CAT 1.161 shows the Rephaim (rp'um) as older tribal ancestors, distinguished from mlkm (more recent deceased monarchs). Both belong to a single line of royal ancestors. El's blessing evokes the rp'um (1.15 III 2-4, 13-15).
- The cult of the dead was not separate from the cult of high deities — 'il'ib ("ancestral god," literally "the father who has become god") appears in Ugaritic pantheon lists (1.47.2).
- Older scholarship contrasted "Canaanite embrace" of death with Israelite rejection. More recent scholarship (Bloch-Smith's 1992 Judahite Burials) shows Israelite popular devotion to the dead was substantial.
- Deuteronomistic texts recode the Rephaim as the ancient "Canaanite" inhabitants of the land — cultural "disidentification" rather than identification.
- "Death and the realm of death play an integral role in the Ugaritic presentation of the cosmos: death is a part of life and life a part of death." Baal is cast in the role of dead king and hero in the final two tablets of the Baal Cycle.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The distinction between rp'um (ancestral heroes) and mlkm (monarchs) is textually supported but the details are uncertain. The identity-marker argument is interpretive but plausible.

**What's at stake:** If the Rephaim shift from insiders (Ugarit, popular Israel) to outsiders (Deuteronomistic Israel), this is a microcosm of monotheism's larger pattern: what was once indigenous divine reality gets recoded as foreign "idolatry."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the Rephaim as consistently foreign/Canaanite in all biblical texts (the traditional reading). Those who question whether popular Israelite devotion to the dead was as widespread as Bloch-Smith argues.

**Alternative reading:** The deuteronomistic coding of Rephaim as ancient inhabitants might preserve a genuinely old tradition about pre-Israelite peoples — not a recoding at all. The Ugaritic Rephaim might be a different concept entirely sharing only a name.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument is plausible as a pattern (insider → outsider recoding is what monotheism does repeatedly), but the Rephaim case specifically is underdetermined by the evidence. The Ugaritic Rephaim are ancestor figures; the biblical Rephaim are legendary giants/peoples. Are these the same concept? Possibly, but the connection is looser than Smith implies. The stronger part of this section is the observation that death was integral to Ugaritic cosmology, not opposed to life — this sets up Chapter 6.

## Claim 3: Ugaritic literature is characterized by extensive divine pairings that provide a model for understanding biblical compound divine names

**Author's claim:** "Ugaritic literature is conspicuous for its many pairings across genres." These binomials "suggest an exaltation of the dynastic and national god" when they appear in biblical contexts.

**Evidence presented:**
- Comprehensive catalogue of pairings (section 3): Anat + Athtart, Shahar + Shalim, El + Athirat, Kothar wa-Hasis, Dagan + Baal, Heaven + Earth, Mountains + Valleys, etc. — attested across ritual texts (1.100 especially) and myths.
- Types of pairings: family relations (El + Athirat), "natural" pairs (Dawn + Dusk), fused identities (Kothar wa-Hasis = "Skill and Wisdom"), father-son (Dagan + Baal).
- Some binomials lack the coordinating conjunction w- ("and"): Kothar wa-Hasis → Kothar Hasis. This pattern appears in biblical texts: Yahweh Elohim (Gen 2:7-9), El Elyon (Gen 14:18-20), Adonay Yahweh (Ezekiel 2:4).
- "Much of the Ugaritic and biblical material containing such binomials belongs to priestly scribal traditions." The pairing of Yahweh + Elohim in Gen 2 may reflect a priestly redactional hand "which added the divine title God ('elōhîm) used in Genesis 1 to the divine name Yahweh attested in Genesis 2."
- De Moor's theory of gradual fusion from two deities into one is questioned: "it is unclear that such an evolution applies generally to double names."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the descriptive catalogue of Ugaritic pairings. MEDIUM for the claim that biblical binomials derive from the same priestly-scribal impulse.

**What's at stake:** If biblical compound divine names follow Ugaritic patterns, then the scribal culture that produced the Bible was working within inherited West Semitic literary conventions — not inventing new theological forms. The combination Yahweh + Elohim in Genesis is not a theological innovation but a conventional scribal practice applied to the national god.

**Who disagrees:** De Moor's fusion model (two deities → one). Del Olmo Lete's syncretism model (two pantheons merging). Conservative scholars who see biblical binomials as unique theological expressions rather than conventional scribal practice.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical binomials could reflect independent development — compound names are a near-universal feature of religious language. The Ugaritic parallels might be typological, not genetic.

**My assessment:** The catalogue itself is the strongest contribution — it shows how pervasive the pairing pattern is at Ugarit. The connection to biblical binomials is plausible but not forced. Smith's skepticism about de Moor's fusion model is well-placed — we simply don't have enough data to track evolutionary trajectories for most of these pairs.

## Claim 4: The Asherah-as-goddess question remains unresolved — and Smith unusually hedges

**Author's claim:** "In short, the jury seems still to be out on the issues, which are undeniably complex." This is notable because Smith was previously in the skeptical camp (asherah = cult symbol, not goddess). Here he acknowledges the majority favors the goddess reading but doesn't fully commit.

**Evidence presented:**
- Kuntillet 'Ajrud (ca. 800): "Yahweh and his asherah" (lyhwh... wl'šrth). The -h suffix on a proper name is problematic — divine names generally don't take pronominal suffixes. But Pardee's alternative (adverbial -h) is also problematic.
- 2 Kings 23:4: cult of "Baal, Asherah and all the host of heaven" in the Jerusalem temple (late 7th century). 2 Kings 21:7: "image of the asherah" (pesel hā'ăšērâ) — an image normally points to a deity.
- Khirbet el-Qom: additional inscription mentioning Yahweh and asherah.
- Tel Miqne/Ekron inscriptions (7th century, destruction 603 BCE): l'šrt and lqdš on shards. Ambiguous — Phoenician cognates favor "sanctuary" reading; Hebrew cognates favor goddess. "The inscriptions from Tel Miqne are ambiguous."
- Silver medallion from Tel Miqne: figure praying to goddess standing on lion — possibly Asherah.
- "Complicating matters, the deuteronomistic detractors of the symbol may have engaged in guilt by association, with the god Baal who, as S. M. Olyan has argued, had no primary relationship to Asherah."
- "Currently, however, most scholars believe that Asherah was a goddess in ancient Israel, possibly even Yahweh's consort."

**Confidence:** DEBATABLE — Smith himself assigns no confidence, and that's the point. The evidence is genuinely ambiguous.

**What's at stake:** Whether Yahweh had a consort is the single most explosive question in Israelite religion. If yes, Israelite monotheism eliminated a goddess who was once Yahweh's partner. If no, the asherah was always a cult object, not a deity. Smith's hedging is significant because he wrote *The Early History of God* largely without committing to Asherah as goddess — here he opens the door wider.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (*Did God Have a Wife?*): strongly affirms Asherah as goddess and consort. Olyan: Asherah as goddess. Day, Zevit, Hadley: goddess camp. Older Smith, Tigay: cautious/skeptical camp. Keel and Uehlinger: iconographic evidence supports goddess.

**Alternative reading:** The asherah was a symbol of the goddess but not the goddess herself — a distinction ancient worshippers might not have made, but modern scholars might insist on. The -h suffix on l'šrth could be a double feminine marker rather than a pronominal suffix.

**My assessment:** This section is fascinating because you can see Smith wrestling with his own prior position. He hasn't fully flipped, but he's clearly moved from "probably not a goddess" to "the jury is out." The strongest evidence for the goddess reading is (a) the pesel in 2 Kings 21:7 (images represent deities), (b) the cumulative weight of multiple inscriptions across multiple sites, and (c) the wider polytheistic context that makes a divine couple expected. The strongest counter-evidence is the grammatical problem with the pronominal suffix on a proper name. I lean toward the goddess reading, but Smith's caution is intellectually honest.

## Claim 5: Athtart as "name of Baal" represents divine hypostasis — and the biblical "name" (šēm) may preserve this concept

**Author's claim:** Athtart's title šm b'l ("name of Baal") represents her as a hypostasis — "the cultically available presence in the temple" of the god. The biblical divine "name" (šēm) as theophanic presence (Exodus 23:20-21, Psalm 29) may preserve the same concept, possibly once associated with a goddess.

**Evidence presented:**
- Athtart's title šm b'l (1.16 VI 56; KAI 14:18). Cross, Mullen, McCarter, Olyan: interpret her as name-hypostasis of Baal.
- Philo of Byblos: Astarte and Baal "ruled the land together under the consent of El" (PE 1.10.31). Olyan: "she is queen alongside Baal who is king."
- Personal names at Ugarit and Amarna incorporate šm + divine name: šmb'l, šm'nt, šu-um-daddu, šum-ad-da — suggesting the name was understood as a personal entity related to the deity.
- Biblical parallels: Exodus 23:20-21 — "my name is within him" (šĕmî bĕqirbô) referring to the divine messenger. The name acts as warrior (Isaiah 30:27). Psalm 29:1-2 — the "glory of his name" (kĕbôd šĕmô) manifests in storm theophany.
- "The extra-biblical evidence for the name may suggest the background for the name in ancient Israel, otherwise lacking in textual support: the name may have been associated at one time with a goddess in early Israelite religion."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM for the hypostasis reading of Athtart. LOW-MEDIUM for the claim that the biblical šēm was once a goddess — this is speculative and Smith presents it as suggestion, not assertion.

**What's at stake:** If the biblical "name of Yahweh" theology has roots in a goddess hypostasis, then deuteronomic "name theology" (God's name dwells in the temple, not God himself) may be a demythologized survival of an older goddess concept. The abstraction of divine presence was once a person.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read "name theology" as a deuteronomic innovation to solve the problem of divine transcendence, not as a survival of polytheism. Those who see šm b'l as a title meaning "Baal's fame/reputation" rather than a hypostasis.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical "name" could be an independent Israelite development — many cultures have "name" as a concept of presence and reputation. The Ugaritic parallels are suggestive but not demonstrative.

**My assessment:** The hypostasis argument is creative and textually grounded, but the leap from "Athtart is Baal's name-hypostasis" to "Yahweh's name was once a goddess" is a leap. Smith hedges appropriately: "may have been associated," "possible insight," "perhaps." The connection needs more evidence than he provides. But the observation that Ugaritic šm b'l and biblical šēm share warrior-theophanic contexts is genuinely interesting.

## Claim 6: Divinized qualities in Ugaritic texts become theophanic attributes in biblical texts

**Author's claim:** The qualities listed after deities in CAT 1.65 and 1.123 represent divinized attributes that "could attach to any number of benevolent deities to express various qualities of those deities to worshippers, especially in liturgical contexts."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.65: after El and Athirat, the text lists "the Mercy of El" (ḥnn 'il), "the Constancy of El" (nṣbt 'il), "the Well-being of El" (šlm 'il). These appear to be divinized qualities invoked alongside deities.
- CAT 1.123: lists Light (ngh), Firmness (srr), Eternity ('d), Rule (šr), Right (ṣdq), Justice (mšr), Compassion (ḥn), Glory (kbd), Light (nr). Ṣdq mšr parallels Philo of Byblos's Misor and Sydyk/Sedek.
- Biblical theophanic contexts use the same terms: nōgah, kābôd, and nûr in 2 Samuel 22:13 = Psalm 18:13; Ezekiel 10:4; Habakkuk 3:4. These are not abstractions but belong to the divine appearance.
- Kābôd is comparable to Akkadian melammu — the theophanous radiance accompanying the deity's procession.
- Psalm 43:3: "Light" ('ôr) and "Truth" ('ĕmet) are personified as Yahweh's attendants — "two of Yahweh's attendants, sent from the divine council as 'guardian angels.'"

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Ugaritic divinized qualities. MEDIUM-HIGH for the continuity into biblical theophanic language.

**What's at stake:** If divine qualities were once independent divine beings (members of the retinue), their absorption into Yahweh's theophany is another instance of convergence — multiple divine beings collapse into attributes of the one God. What was a pantheon of qualities becomes one God's radiance.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the Ugaritic quality-lists as abstract nouns in apposition, not independent divine beings. The line between "divinized quality" and "poetic attribute" is blurry.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical theophanic language could draw on common ANE storm-god imagery without depending specifically on Ugaritic divinized-quality traditions. The vocabulary overlaps because both describe storm theophanies.

**My assessment:** The CAT 1.123 list is the strongest evidence — ṣdq mšr clearly parallels known divine figures (Sydyk and Misor in Philo of Byblos). The others are harder to call. Smith's argument that these qualities became Yahweh's theophanic attributes is a nice example of convergence at the micro level: what were independent entities in Ugarit became aspects of one God in Israel.

## Claim 7: Polytheism's "conceptual unity" was grounded in the family — the social structure of Ugarit made the divine family "natural"

**Author's claim:** "Given the prominent familial terminology in the Ugaritic administrative texts, the polytheistic family may have provided what would have been in Ugaritic culture the most 'natural' means to express singleness and coherence in 'divine society.'" This is Part I's capstone argument.

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugaritic personal names: the patronymic (bn/bt + PN) was the most important identifier. D. Pardee: "the Ugaritians seem to have considered the patronym the most important element to be stated when identifying a person."
- Administrative texts (4.7, 4.356, 4.357): land transfers from "the sons of X" to "the sons of Y." Family is the primary social unit.
- Contrast with Mesopotamia: "the language of council and hierarchical relations within this grouping predominates there." The divine family is "more prevalent in the Ugaritic texts."
- Contrast with Israel: the divine family has been "eclipsed" in the Bible. What remains is "a streamlined bureaucracy headed by an absolute monarch." "Yahweh not only lacks peers within the pantheon; with his genealogy largely erased from the biblical record, he becomes a god not only without peer but also without precedent."
- Social explanation: Israel's lineage system eroded in the 8th-6th centuries (royal power over patriarchal authority, land consolidation, warfare). The post-exilic bêt 'ābôt ("fathers' house") differs from the older bêt 'āb — signaling decline of traditional family structure. Prophetic texts (Jeremiah 31:29-30; Ezekiel 18) proclaim individual accountability over inherited guilt.
- "A culture with a diminished lineage system, one less embedded in traditional family patrimonies due to societal changes in the eighth through sixth centuries, might be more predisposed both to hold to individual human accountability for behavior and to see an individual deity accountable for the cosmos."
- Against de Moor's "crisis of polytheism" thesis: "Ugaritic polytheism probably offered its adherents a far more integrated vision of reality than its modern students have ever imagined."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the descriptive contrast (Ugarit = familial; Israel = monarchical/bureaucratic). MEDIUM for the causal argument (weakened lineages → individual deity → monotheism). This is a sociological hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.

**What's at stake:** This is the most ambitious argument in the book. Smith is offering a SOCIAL explanation for monotheism: changes in family structure made the divine family unintelligible, making the single deity the "natural" way to conceive of cosmic unity. If correct, monotheism is not a theological discovery but a sociological adaptation. If wrong, the entire Part I buildup leads to a speculative conclusion.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who sees monotheism as primarily a THEOLOGICAL development (revelation, prophetic insight, philosophical refinement). De Moor's "crisis of polytheism" school. Scholars who see the family model as one metaphor among many, not THE organizing principle.

**Alternative reading:** The decline of the divine family in Israelite texts might reflect theological CHOICE (Yahweh-alone movement) rather than social DETERMINISM (lineage erosion). The prophets who proclaimed individual accountability (Ezekiel 18) were making theological arguments, not reporting sociological change. The arrow of causation could run the other direction: monotheistic theology eroded family-based religious structures.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument is elegant and the Ugaritic-Israelite contrast is genuine. But the causal mechanism is underdetermined. Did weakened lineages cause monotheism, or did monotheistic theology (with its emphasis on individual accountability before one God) contribute to weakening lineages? The correlation is real; the causation is debatable. This is Smith's most ambitious theoretical move and also his most vulnerable. The Afterword section is beautifully written — the Baal quotation at the end is genuinely moving — but as argument it's more suggestive than demonstrative.

## Chapter 4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Military retinues reflect patrimonial household | MEDIUM-HIGH | Plural terminology as literary convention |
| 2: Rephaim as identity markers | MEDIUM-HIGH | Loose Ugarit-Israel connection |
| 3: Divine pairings provide model for biblical binomials | HIGH (catalogue) / MEDIUM (biblical link) | Typological vs. genetic relationship |
| 4: Asherah-as-goddess unresolved | DEBATABLE | Smith's own hedging reflects the evidence |
| 5: Athtart/šēm as hypostasis | MEDIUM (Athtart) / LOW-MEDIUM (biblical) | Speculative leap from Ugarit to Israel |
| 6: Divinized qualities → theophanic attributes | HIGH (Ugarit) / MEDIUM-HIGH (biblical) | Boundary between entity and attribute |
| 7: Family as polytheism's unity; lineage decline enabled monotheism | HIGH (descriptive) / MEDIUM (causal) | Causation underdetermined |

**Strongest section:** The Afterword (Section 8). The synthesis of Part I's argument — divine family as polytheism's unity, Israel's lineage decline as monotheism's social precondition — is the intellectual payoff of the entire first half of the book. The Pardee citation about patronyms is a perfect piece of evidence: the culture that identified people primarily by their fathers produced a pantheon identified primarily by its patriarch.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 (Yahweh and his consort). Smith's hedging is intellectually honest but the section doesn't advance his argument — it's a survey of an unresolved debate. The connection to the chapter's theme (divine relations) is clear, but the section reads as obligatory rather than integral.

**Most striking detail:** The observation that Yahweh's genealogy has been "largely erased from the biblical record" — so he becomes "a god not only without peer but also without precedent." This is not just about eliminating other gods; it's about eliminating Yahweh's own PAST. Monotheism requires divine amnesia.
