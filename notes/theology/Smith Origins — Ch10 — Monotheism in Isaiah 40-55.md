# Smith — Chapter 10: Monotheism in Isaiah 40-55

The book's culminating chapter. Smith reads "Second Isaiah" as the parade example of monotheistic rhetoric — not because it invents monotheism but because it deploys it most intensively. The monotheistic declarations (Isaiah 43:10-11; 44:6, 8; 45:5-7, 14, 18, 21; 46:9) are embedded in a polemic against Babylonian cult images, specifically the mis pi ("mouth-washing") ritual that animated statues into living gods. But the polemic has a double target: outsiders (Babylonian idol-makers) AND insiders (Judeans tempted to participate). The chapter closes with the book's most important hermeneutical claim: later readers "read for the monotheistic God" across the Bible, erasing vestiges of polytheism — just as the biblical tradents themselves did.

## Claim 1: Second Isaiah's monotheistic statements are polemic embedded in three thematic contexts
→ [[claim-isaiah-monotheistic-statements-embedded-in-polemic]] | **smith-obm-10.1** | Confidence: high
  All monotheistic statements confined to Isaiah 40-48, embedded in: (1) Yahweh as cosmic creator, (2) Yahweh chooses Israel, (3) idols are empty. None can be read as abstract philosophical theology. ## Claim 2: The polemic targets the mis pi ritual — and the real target is Judeans, not Babylonians
→ [[claim-isaiah-44-mis-pi-double-audience-judeans]] | **smith-obm-10.2** | Confidence: high
  Second Isaiah inverts every element of the mouth-washing ritual: stresses human manufacture, denies senses to images, mocks processions. The double audience — Judeans saying "My idol caused them" (Isa 48:5) — shows monotheism as INTRA-faith consolidation, not interfaith crusade. Best section of the chapter. Depends on: [[claim-isaiah-monotheistic-statements-embedded-in-polemic]], [[claim-monotheism-rhetoric-inner-community-discourse]].

## Claim 3: Isaiah 44 reuses older tradition (Deut 32, First Isaiah) as insider referentiality
→ [[claim-isaiah-44-reuses-deuteronomy-32-first-isaiah]] | **smith-obm-10.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  Deut 32's "no other god" polemic provides the template for Isa 44's monotheistic rhetoric; Isaiah 40 reverses Isaiah 13-14's oracles against Babylon. Monotheism emerges from scribal reuse of older texts — scripture creates monotheism. Depends on: [[claim-isaiah-monotheistic-statements-embedded-in-polemic]], [[claim-isaiah-44-mis-pi-double-audience-judeans]].

## Claim 4: Monotheism is "not a new stage of religion but a new stage of rhetoric" — later readers construct the myth of original monotheism
→ [[claim-monotheism-as-exilic-rhetoric-not-religious-revolution]] | **smith-obm-10.4** | Confidence: high
  The book's thesis: when political boundaries collapsed in exile, Israel elevated Yahweh's mastery to cosmic scale. Believers today "read for the monotheistic God" and erase polytheistic vestiges, constructing a "historical myth" of original monotheism — just as the biblical tradents did. This is the capstone claim depending on: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-monotheism-rhetoric-inner-community-discourse]], [[claim-monotheistic-statements-exilic-late-monarchy]], [[claim-monotheism-as-transformed-canaanite-myth]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]], and all three Ch10 claims.

**Strongest section:** Section 2 (Outsider Context — the mis pi comparison). The detailed knowledge of Mesopotamian ritual practices and the point-by-point contrast with Second Isaiah's polemic is Smith at his absolute best. This is philology in service of interpretation.

**Weakest section:** Section 3 (Isaiah 44 close reading). The intertextual connections are interesting but the argument sometimes gets lost in the detailed enumeration of semantic contrasts. The Deut 32 link is strong; the Isa 13-14 reversal is less certain.

**Book's final argument:** The last two paragraphs are among the most important in the entire book. Smith isn't just describing monotheism's origins — he's describing how religious traditions construct their own histories. The "historical myth" of original monotheism is not a lie; it's how traditions work. Modern believers who "read for the monotheistic God" are participating in the same hermeneutical process as the ancient tradents who selected, edited, and transmitted the biblical texts. Smith's final move is to make his READERS aware of this process without demanding they abandon it.

**Cross-reference:** The claim that monotheism is "not a new stage of religion but a new stage of rhetoric" is the book's thesis, first stated in the Introduction (Claim 9) and now demonstrated through the full arc of evidence. The mis pi comparison exemplifies the method: read biblical polemic against its ANE background, identify what's being inverted, and recognize that the target is internal (Judeans) not external (Babylonians). This final chapter ties together every thread in the book: the Ugaritic background (Parts I-II), the historical emergence (Ch 7-8), the theological transformations (Ch 9), and the rhetorical deployment (Ch 10).
