# Stavrakopoulou — Prologue + Chapter 1: Dissecting the Divine

---

## Prologue

### Claim: The Bible's God has been systematically sanitized — this book recovers the real, embodied deity

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** "This is the book I'd have liked to read when I was at university. It tells the story of the real God of the Bible, in all his corporeal, uncensored, scandalous forms. Stripping away the theological veneer of centuries of Jewish and Christian piety, this book disentangles the biblical God from his scriptural and doctrinal fetters to reveal a deity wholly unlike the God worshipped by Jews and Christians today."

**Who she's writing against:** Her theology professor and peers at university who were "censoring the Bible, sanitizing its deity of any mythological, earthy or unsettling characteristics." The sanitized God is a product of centuries of theological interpretation. The real God — the one in the texts — is something else entirely.

**Her voice:** Stavrakopoulou is an atheist writing for a general audience. She doesn't claim neutrality. She's explicitly interested in tearing down the sanitized version and showing the scandal underneath. This is more polemical than Smith or Römer, closer to Dever in tone.

**Confidence:** HIGH that she genuinely intends to recover the embodied biblical God. The question is whether her reconstruction is accurate or whether she overcorrects.

**What's at stake:** Stavrakopoulou is the most radical voice in the debate. Where Sommer argues God had MULTIPLE bodies (fluidity), Stavrakopoulou argues God had ONE literal physical body — a supersized humanoid form — that later theology spiritualized away.

## Chapter 1: Dissecting the Divine

### Claim 1: The biblical God had a literal, physical body — and this is the CONSENSUS reading, not a fringe position

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** "In academic circles, there is nothing particularly contentious about this paraphrase of the opening chapter of the book of Genesis. Most biblical scholars would agree that when God says, 'Let us make humankind in our image, in our likeness,' he is addressing the other members of the 'divine assembly.'" And humans bearing God's image means they share his physical form — just as Seth shares Adam's "image and likeness," and divine statues share the "image and likeness" of their gods.

**Evidence — what the embodied God looked like:**
> "He had a head, hair and a face; eyes, ears, a nose and a mouth. He had arms, hands, legs and feet, and a chest and a back. He was equipped with a heart, a tongue, teeth and genitals. He was a god who breathed, in and out. This was a deity who not only looked like a human — albeit on a far more impressive, glamorous scale — but who very often behaved like a human."

**The behavioral catalogue:**
> "He enjoyed evening strolls and hearty meals; he listened to music, wrote books and made lists. He was a god who not only spoke, but whistled, laughed, shouted, wept and talked to himself. He was a god who fell in love and into fights; a god who squabbled with his worshippers and grappled with his enemies; a god who made friends, raised children, took wives and had sex."

**Stavrakopoulou on the source of this portrait:** "This portrait of God has not been lifted from obscure myths inscribed on long-abandoned clay tablets. It is drawn from the Bible itself."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that the biblical texts describe an embodied God (this aligns with Sommer's much more careful Introduction — the data is overwhelming). HIGH on the specific catalogue of body parts (each is textually attested). MEDIUM on "took wives and had sex" — this is more inferential and depends on reading Asherah as a literal consort/wife.

**What's at stake:** Stavrakopoulou is making the same claim as Sommer (God has a body), but with a crucial difference: Sommer argues God had MULTIPLE bodies (fluid embodiment model). Stavrakopoulou argues for ONE physical body. Where Sommer's reading preserves monotheism (one God, many manifestations), Stavrakopoulou's reading is more radical: God is literally a physical being with a body located in space.

### Claim 2: The incorporeal God is a Greek philosophical invention — not a biblical idea

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** "It is the broadly Platonic notion of the otherness and unlikeness of the divine to anything in or beyond the universe that has shaped the more formal theological constructions of God in the Western religious imagination. And yet these constructions are built on a conceptual framework very much at odds with the Bible itself."

**Evidence — the historical development:**
1. Xenophanes (6th-5th c. BCE): Argued the gods shouldn't be imagined with human bodies or behaviors — "Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods everything that leads to blame and abuse"
2. Plato (c. 429-347 BCE): God is incorporeal, invisible, abstract — an intellect, not a body
3. Aristotle (c. 384-322 BCE): The divine as "unmoved mover" — pure thought thinking itself
4. These ideas "gradually came to shape the thinking of certain Jewish and Christian intellectuals" toward the close of the first millennium BCE
5. By the early centuries CE, Jewish and Christian elites were "drawing ever-sharper distinctions between the heavenly and the earthly, the divine and the human, and the spiritual and the bodily"

**The irony:** The God of the philosophers (incorporeal, immaterial, abstract) replaced the God of the Bible (embodied, physical, present). Jews and Christians now worship a Greek philosophical construct, not the deity described in their own scriptures.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the historical development (this is standard intellectual history). Stavrakopoulou's framing is deliberately provocative but substantively accurate.

**What's at stake:** The "God" of Western theism — the invisible, transcendent, immaterial being of creed and catechism — is a Greek import. The biblical God walked, ate, fought, and had a body you could see. The two are radically different beings with the same name.

---

### Claim 3: The Genesis creation account reflects a polytheistic divine council — God addresses other deities

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** "In academic circles, there is nothing particularly contentious" about the reading that God addresses the divine council in Genesis 1:26 ("Let US make humankind in OUR image"). This God "had yet to be imagined as the only divine being in the universe."

**The comparison:** "Like the Babylonian deity Marduk, or the Greek god Zeus, this ancient deity had long been cast as the king of the cosmos, but like them, he was far from alone in the heavens."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the divine council reading of Genesis 1:26 (this is the scholarly consensus, shared by Heiser, Sommer, Smith, and essentially everyone except fundamentalists).

**What's at stake:** The first chapter of the Bible — the foundational text of Jewish and Christian theology — describes a polytheistic divine council. "God" is one deity among others, the chief of a pantheon. The Bible's opening act is not "In the beginning, God created alone" but "In the beginning, the high god addressed his council."

## Prologue + Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| Biblical God has a literal, physical body | VERY HIGH |
| Incorporeal God = Greek philosophical import | HIGH |
| Genesis 1:26 = divine council | HIGH |
| God "took wives and had sex" | MEDIUM (more inferential) |
| Stavrakopoulou's portrait is drawn "from the Bible itself" | HIGH on body parts; MEDIUM on behaviors/social life |

**Stavrakopoulou's rhetorical strategy:** She positions herself as the honest reader cutting through centuries of theological censorship. The facts are on her side — the texts DO describe an embodied God. But her tone (scandalous, provocative) is designed to unsettle, not just inform. She's writing for a popular audience that will be shocked by what she reports, not for scholars who already know it.

**Relationship to Sommer:** Same data, different framework. Sommer: fluid embodiment — God has MULTIPLE bodies, overlapping selves. Stavrakopoulou: one literal body — God is a supersized humanoid. The difference matters: Sommer's reading allows for monotheism with complexity; Stavrakopoulou's reading pushes toward a more radically physical God that is harder to reconcile with later theology.
