# Smith — Introduction

Smith sets up a book-length argument about the conceptual structures of West Semitic divinity and how Israelite monotheism emerged from within Israelite polytheism. The Introduction has four sections: scope of the study, problems with "monotheism/polytheism" terminology, problems with "Canaanite," and methodological cautions about literature, history, theology, and myth.

---

## Claim 1: The conceptual unity of polytheism has been neglected
→ [[claim-conceptual-unity-west-semitic-polytheism-neglected]] | **smith-obm-intro.1** | Confidence: high
  No overall investigation of the indigenous conceptual structures of West Semitic polytheism has been undertaken — this book fills that gap. Egyptology and Assyriology have broached conceptual coherence of their polytheisms; West Semitic studies have not.
  Supports: [[claim-center-periphery-spatial-scheme-ugaritic-divinity]], [[claim-divine-family-polytheism-oneness]]. Challenged by: Keel and Uehlinger's argument that Ugarit is not primary evidence for Canaan/Israel.

---

## Claim 2: "Canaanite" is a modern scholarly construct — "West Semitic" is preferable
→ [[claim-canaanite-modern-scholarly-construct-west-semitic-preferable]] | **smith-obm-intro.2** | Confidence: high
  "'Canaanite' is a misleading term that often clouds analysis." Ugaritic is linguistically not Canaanite (Rainey, Zadok), and the term is always external. Yet Smith defends Ugaritic relevance via specific textual parallels (Sapan, Leviathan, divine council).
  Depends on: [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]]. Challenged by: Keel/Uehlinger (Ugaritic texts are not primary sources for Canaan/Israel), Lemche (minimalist position).

---

## Claim 3: Monotheism and polytheism are modern categories
→ [[claim-monotheism-polytheism-modern-categories-ancients-different]] | **smith-obm-intro.3** | Confidence: very-high
  "Monotheism and polytheism in themselves hold little meaning for the ancients." No polytheist thought of his system as "polytheism"; no ancient Israelite would understand "are you a monotheist?" Scholarly treatments that elevate monotheism at polytheism's expense are "apologetics, not history."
  Supports: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]]. Challenged by: the possibility that conceptual distinctions existed even without theoretical vocabulary.

---

## Claim 4: Israel was broadly West Semitic in its religion
→ [[claim-israel-broadly-west-semitic-religion-not-pristine-monotheism]] | **smith-obm-intro.4** | Confidence: high
  Israel's perception of itself as a separate culture and religion "grew over a long period of time. It did not leap into being on Mount Sinai." The Sinai narratives are largely products of the monarchy and later. Israelite religion developed FROM West Semitic polytheism, not against it.
  Depends on: [[claim-israelite-culture-was-canaanite-no-separation]]. Supports: [[claim-conceptual-unity-west-semitic-polytheism-neglected]], [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]]. Challenged by: Tigay's onomastic data (potential early monolatry).

---

## Claim 5: Etymology of 'ēl is unresolved — "strength" etymology unproven
→ [[claim-el-etymology-unresolved-strength-unproven]] | **smith-obm-intro.5** | Confidence: high
  "We should exercise great caution before accepting and using this etymology." M.H. Pope: "None of [the etymological proposals] carries conviction." The root was not productive in Ugaritic or Hebrew, so "strength" was probably not a live connotation.
  Low stakes for the main argument. Contradicts: scholars who confidently derive 'ēl from "strength" (older reference works, Cross).

---

## Claim 6: The divine family provided polytheism's own form of "oneness"
→ [[claim-divine-family-polytheism-oneness]] | **smith-obm-intro.6** | Confidence: medium
  "The divine family was for polytheism a sort of 'mono-theism.'" In a society where the family was the highest level of social identification, the polytheism of a divine family was more intelligible than monotheism. Schloen's four-tier pantheon corresponds to four levels of family household.
  Supports: [[claim-conceptual-unity-west-semitic-polytheism-neglected]]. Needs testing against data in chapters 1-4.

---

## Claim 7: Biblical myth exists — the Baal Cycle is the standard
→ [[claim-biblical-myth-exists-baal-cycle-standard]] | **smith-obm-intro.7** | Confidence: high
  "If the Baal Cycle is a myth, then biblical narratives about the storm-god Yahweh are mythic." By Smith's narrower definition (narratives about divine beings comparable to the Baal Cycle), passages including Genesis 1-2, Exodus 15, Deut 32:8-27, Psalm 74, and Isaiah 14 are "myth." Ugaritic myths offer "continuous narrative, like a modern film" while biblical sources provide "a series of glimpses, a handful of snapshots or frames."
  Depends on: [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]]. Supports: [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]], [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]].

---

## Claim 8: Ugaritic texts remain the best available background for Israelite religion
→ [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]] | **smith-obm-intro.8** | Confidence: very-high
  "Of all the ancient texts found in the Levant containing this kind of information, the Ugaritic texts are also most proximate in time and place to ancient Israel." The diversity of later reflexes (Phoenician, biblical, Aramaic, classical) demonstrates Iron Age transmission. Specific parallels: Mount Sapan, Leviathan, Tannin, divine council terminology.
  Supports: [[claim-canaanite-modern-scholarly-construct-west-semitic-preferable]], [[claim-israel-inherited-cosmic-enemy-names]], [[claim-biblical-myth-exists-baal-cycle-standard]]. Challenged by: Keel/Uehlinger (Ugaritic texts are not primary sources for Canaan/Israel).

---

## Claim 9: Monotheism was a rhetoric, not a "religious stage"
→ [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]] | **smith-obm-intro.9** | Confidence: medium-high
  "Monotheism has apparently achieved a status in modern discourse that it never held in ancient Israel, where it functioned as a rhetoric expressing and advancing the cause of Israelite monolatrous practice." Modern discussions have reified the idea and disconnected it from context.
  Depends on: [[claim-monotheism-polytheism-modern-categories-ancients-different]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]]. Supported by: [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]]. Challenged by: the rhetoric/ontology false dichotomy — "there is no god besides me" is both. Needs testing against chapters 8-10.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Conceptual unity of polytheism neglected | HIGH | Smith imposing coherence where it didn't exist |
| 2: "Canaanite" is problematic; "West Semitic" better | HIGH | Ugarit-Israel distance overstated by critics |
| 3: Mono-/polytheism are modern categories | VERY HIGH | Ancient distinction between gods still real |
| 4: Israel was broadly West Semitic religiously | HIGH | Early distinctiveness data from Tigay/Cross |
| 5: Etymology of 'ēl is unresolved | HIGH | New epigraphic discoveries |
| 6: Divine family = polytheism's "oneness" | MEDIUM | Needs data from Part I |
| 7: Biblical myth exists (by Baal Cycle standard) | HIGH | Boundary between myth and mythic imagery |
| 8: Ugaritic texts are best available background | VERY HIGH | Minimalist challenges to Ugarit-Israel link |
| 9: Monotheism was rhetoric, not a stage | MEDIUM-HIGH | Rhetoric/ontology false dichotomy |

**Strongest section:** Section 2 — the critique of how scholars have used "monotheism" and "polytheism" as apologetic categories. Smith at his most incisive.

**Weakest section:** Section 3 — the "Canaanite" discussion is important but gets bogged in classification debates. The payoff ("therefore Ugaritic texts remain relevant") could be stated with less throat-clearing.

**Smith's intellectual stance:** He positions himself as a believing scholar who uses critical tools without letting them become weapons. He quotes Ricoeur on "smashing idols to let symbols speak" and explicitly refuses to "choose sides" between polytheism and monotheism. This is more than methodological — it's a bid for intellectual legitimacy with both critical scholars and faith communities.

**Cross-reference note:** Smith's claim that Israel was broadly West Semitic (Claim 4) dovetails with Cross's argument that El was Israel's original god — both see continuity rather than rupture. But Cross emphasizes the distinctiveness of YHWH as Divine Warrior more than Smith does here. Römer's *The Invention of God* makes similar arguments about gradual emergence. Tigay's onomastic data (predominantly Yahwistic names from early periods) provides a potential counterweight — if most Israelites were already monolatrous by the 8th century, Smith's "long polytheistic background" may be overstated.
