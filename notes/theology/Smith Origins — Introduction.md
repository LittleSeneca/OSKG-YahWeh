# Smith — Introduction

Smith sets up a book-length argument about the conceptual structures of West Semitic divinity and how Israelite monotheism emerged from within Israelite polytheism. The Introduction has four sections: scope of the study, problems with "monotheism/polytheism" terminology, problems with "Canaanite," and methodological cautions about literature, history, theology, and myth.

---

## Claim 1: The conceptual unity of polytheism has been neglected — this book fills that gap

**Author's claim:** "No overall investigation has been undertaken for the conceptual unity of West Semitic polytheisms; this book aims to redress this lack." Previous scholarship either catalogued individual deities (atomistic) or imposed external typologies (cosmic/social). "This book attempts to combine the advantages of these approaches in examining the major indigenous conceptual structures that ancient Ugaritic and Israelite societies used to construct their religious reality."

**Evidence presented:** 
- The scholarly gap is real: a 1989 volume on polytheistic systems covers Hinduism and Greek religion, but "the essay on ancient Israel treats only the issue of monotheism in the Bible, and the author of the essay, a well-known expert on Ugaritic, ignores polytheism."
- Egyptology and Assyriology have broached conceptual coherence of their polytheisms; West Semitic studies have not.
- Smith's four-pronged approach: (1) divine groupings from largest to smallest (Part I), (2) shared characteristics of deities (Part II), (3) monotheism as emergent rhetoric (Part III).

**Confidence:** HIGH. The gap Smith identifies is genuine and the approach is well-motivated.

**What's at stake:** If Smith succeeds, West Semitic polytheism gets treated as a coherent thought-system rather than a grab-bag of deities serving as negative foil for Israelite monotheism. This changes how we evaluate the transition to monotheism — it becomes an intellectual shift within a coherent system, not a leap from chaos to order.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat polytheism as inherently inferior to monotheism (the "apologetics, not history" Smith criticizes). Also those who argue Ugarit is too distant from Israel to be relevant (Keel and Uehlinger).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that the lack of West Semitic theological treatises means the "conceptual unity" Smith seeks is being imposed retrospectively — that polytheism's coherence was practical/ritual, not conceptual/theological.

**My assessment:** Smith's framing is intellectually honest and fills a real gap. The danger is over-systematizing — imposing coherence where the ancients themselves might not have felt it. Smith acknowledges this risk.

## Claim 2: "Canaanite" is a modern scholarly construct that flattens diversity — "West Semitic" is preferable

**Author's claim:** "'Canaanite' is a misleading term that often clouds analysis." Smith largely avoids it, preferring "West Semitic."

**Evidence presented:**
- Rainey (1960s): Ugaritic is not Canaanite — CAT 4.967 lists a Canaanite alongside an Egyptian and an Ashdodite; an Akkadian text from Ugarit distinguishes "sons of Ugarit" from "sons of Canaan."
- Lemche goes further: "Canaanite" never appears as self-designation; it's always an external label. But Smith pushes back: Lemche relies on an argument from silence. "It seems odd to suppose that foreign courts from Egypt to Hatti to Mesopotamia used the term 'Canaanite,' but the people whom others designated with this term never knew themselves by it."
- Zadok: Ugaritic may be the "westernmost dialect of 'Amorite' type" — not straightforwardly Canaanite.
- The "Canaanite shift" (ā > ō) may be a SOUTH Canaanite feature, not present in Ugaritic or even north Canaanite, per Albright.
- Yet Smith defends Ugaritic relevance: specific names and terms (Mount Sapan in Psalm 48:3, Leviathan, Tannin) are common to Ugaritic and biblical texts. "These specific points of contact between Ugaritic and Israelite religion need not be understood as pointing to a single or 'same' religion, but they do point to a larger religious tradition shared broadly by West Semitic peoples."
- Keel and Uehlinger correctly note Ugaritic texts "are not primary sources for the religious history of Canaan and Israel," but Smith argues they remain essential as background.

**Confidence:** HIGH that "Canaanite" is problematic. MEDIUM-HIGH that "West Semitic" is a better category. The evidence for Ugarit-Israel literary continuity is strong.

**What's at stake:** If Ugaritic texts are irrelevant to Israelite religion (as Hillers, Keel/Uehlinger, Lemche argue to varying degrees), a major comparative corpus is lost and Smith's entire project is undermined. If they ARE relevant, Smith has the best available data for reconstructing Israel's polytheistic background.

**Who disagrees:** Lemche (minimalist: "Canaanite" is a Persian-period construct), Hillers (Ugaritic ≠ Canaanite, therefore irrelevant to Israel), Keel and Uehlinger (Ugaritic texts are not primary sources for Canaan/Israel).

**Alternative reading:** Even if "Canaanite" is problematic as a category, Ugaritic texts might map to a NORTHERN coastal culture with limited relevance to highland Israel. Smith's counter is the specific textual parallels — but those could reflect shared ANE stock rather than direct cultural continuity.

**My assessment:** Smith wins this argument. The specific parallels (divine council, Baal's enemies, Sapan/Zaphon, Leviathan) are too precise to dismiss. But he's right to hedge: Ugarit provides BACKGROUND, not a direct ancestor.

## Claim 3: Monotheism and polytheism are modern categories — ancients didn't think in those terms

**Author's claim:** "Monotheism and polytheism in themselves hold little meaning for the ancients apart from the identity of the deities whom they revered and served. No polytheist thought of his belief-system as polytheistic per se. If you asked ancient Mesopotamians if they were polytheists, the question would make no sense." Same for monotheism: "If you asked ancient Israelites around the Exilic period (587-538) if they were monotheists, they would not have understood the question."

**Evidence presented:**
- The terms are a product of modern Western encounter with non-Western cultures.
- Monotheism has become a "civil religion" for secularized Westerners — a "sublime idea" substituting for actual religious practice.
- Biblical scholars have traditionally elevated monotheism at polytheism's expense: monotheism as "historical" vs. polytheism as "nature/fertility religion"; monotheism as "ethical" vs. polytheism as "deification of power." Smith: "This is apologetics, not history."
- No evidence for "fate" as an independent order in West Semitic mythologies: "Ugaritic lacks a word even approximating this notion, and Akkadian šimtu...refers to a 'determined course' that can be changed."
- Recent treatments still make "polytheism...immoral and impotent, reflecting a 'pessimism'...'a pantheon of disillusion.'"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is one of Smith's strongest points and is broadly accepted in critical scholarship.

**What's at stake:** If the modern categories distort the ancient data, then scholarly narratives about "the rise of monotheism" may be imposing a framework the ancients wouldn't recognize. The question becomes not "when did Israel become monotheistic?" but "what was the function of monotheistic rhetoric in its ancient context?"

**Who disagrees:** Albright and his school treated monotheism as an objective religious stage. Some conservative scholars still treat the terms as transparent. Heiser would say the Bible's "monotheism" always acknowledged other elohim — the categories don't map cleanly.

**Alternative reading:** While the ancients lacked OUR terms, they clearly distinguished between their god and others' gods. The absence of "-ism" vocabulary doesn't mean the conceptual distinction didn't exist.

**My assessment:** Smith is right about the historical contingency of our terminology, and his critique of scholarly apologetics is bracing. But he may overstate the case: the distinction between "Yahweh alone exists" and "many gods exist" is a real cognitive distinction even if the vocabulary wasn't theoretical.

## Claim 4: Israel was broadly West Semitic in its religion — not a separate, pristine monotheistic culture from the start

**Author's claim:** "The prophets seem to have been quite right in observing that Israel maintained religious practices quite like the ones conducted by its neighbors. But while the prophets thought this trend to be of secondary influence, it would seem that in fact these practices were old ones in Israel."

**Evidence presented:**
- "Israel's perception of itself as a separate culture and religion destined to a covenantal life only with Yahweh grew over a long period of time. It did not leap into being on Mount Sinai."
- "The Sinai narratives, largely a product of the monarchy and later periods, reflect Israel's long historical struggle to understand itself as a priestly people."
- Smith's prior book (The Early History of God) already demonstrated convergence and differentiation — Israelite religion developed FROM West Semitic polytheism, not against it.
- This book works "with a model of Israelite religion that developed in many diverse ways from a larger West Semitic background."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the consensus position in critical biblical scholarship, though debated by conservatives.

**What's at stake:** The entire traditional narrative of Israel's unique, revealed monotheism from the beginning is inverted. Israel's distinctiveness is a LATE development, not an original condition. For theology, this raises questions about revelation vs. cultural evolution.

**Who disagrees:** Conservative/traditional scholars who maintain Mosaic monotheism. Albright's school. Those who see Israel as fundamentally distinct from "Canaanite" culture.

**Alternative reading:** Even if practices were shared, Israel's THEOLOGY (covenant, exclusive worship) may have been genuinely distinctive from early on. Shared practice doesn't equal shared theology. Tigay's onomastic evidence suggests widespread popular monolatry, which supports some early distinctiveness.

**My assessment:** Smith's model is persuasive for practice and conceptual structures. But the timing question matters enormously: if exclusive Yahwism was earlier than Smith thinks (as Tigay's onomastic data might suggest), the "long evolution" model gets compressed. Cross's work on El traditions also complicates the picture — if El was Israel's original god, the break with West Semitic religion may have happened differently than Smith describes.

## Claim 5: Etymology of 'ēl/'il/ilu is unresolved — "strength" etymology is unproven but not impossible

**Author's claim:** "We should exercise great caution before accepting and using this etymology" (the derivation from *'y/wl, "to be strong"). But he doesn't dismiss it outright.

**Evidence presented:**
- Common view: 'ēl from *'y/wl, "to be pre-eminent, strong." Related to 'ayil ("leader/chief/ram") and 'eyāl ("strength") in Psalms 22:20, 88:5.
- But the idiom yeš/'ên lě'ēl ("it is/is not in the power of X") has been reanalyzed by Watson: he reads *'yn l' lyd- ("there is no power to the hand of"), removing 'ēl entirely. Smith doubts this: "l'y is hardly a productive root with this meaning in BH."
- Ugaritic 'ul and 'awl may mean "army" not "strength."
- M.H. Pope: "None of [the etymological proposals] carries conviction."
- D. Pardee: counterindications to the *'y/wl theory.
- The root was not productive in Ugaritic or Hebrew, so "strength" was probably not a live connotation.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the etymology is unsettled. Smith's agnosticism is well-supported.

**What's at stake:** Low stakes for the book's main argument, but important for anyone claiming to know what "El" originally "meant." If El = "the Strong One," that shapes how we understand his character.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who confidently derive 'ēl from "strength" (common in older reference works). Cross uses the etymology to connect El with power imagery.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the etymology is uncertain, the FUNCTION of El in the texts (patriarch, creator, wise judge) may be more informative than any putative original meaning.

**My assessment:** Smith's caution is admirable. Etymology is a weak reed for this kind of work, and he's right to note it and move on. Nothing in his argument depends on the etymology.

## Claim 6: The divine family provided polytheism with its own form of "oneness" — comparable to what monotheists associate with monotheism

**Author's claim:** "The notion of the family household perhaps provided polytheism with the sort of 'oneness' that monotheists associate with monotheism. Indeed, in a society where the highest level of social association and identification was the family and not the individual, the polytheism of a divine family would have been far more intelligible than any notion of monotheism. Stated differently, the divine family was for polytheism a sort of 'mono-theism.'"

**Evidence presented:** (Developed in Part I, previewed here):
- J.D. Schloen's work on Ugaritic society: four tiers of the pantheon correspond to four levels of the family household.
- Familial terminology is widespread for the pantheon.
- The patrimonial household provided conceptual unity.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is an intriguing hypothesis but the evidence is previewed, not demonstrated — the Introduction only sketches the argument.

**What's at stake:** If Smith is right, polytheism wasn't chaotic multiplicity but an ordered system with its own coherence. This radically reframes the monotheism-vs-polytheism contrast. It also suggests monotheism wasn't the invention of "oneness" but a REINTERPRETATION of an existing unity concept.

**Who disagrees:** This is Smith's own synthetic claim, not a consensus position. Scholars who see polytheism as inherently fragmented would disagree. The claim needs testing against the data in chapters 1-4.

**Alternative reading:** The family metaphor may have been one of several organizing principles for the pantheon, not THE unifying concept. Royal/administrative metaphors might compete with familial ones.

**My assessment:** Intriguing but needs the evidence from Part I to evaluate. The move from "the family was the highest social unit" to "the divine family was a form of oneness" is a leap. I'll watch for how well this holds up in chapters 1-4.

## Claim 7: Biblical myth exists — the Baal Cycle is the standard, and many biblical passages meet the same criteria

**Author's claim:** "If the Baal Cycle is a myth, then biblical narratives about the storm-god Yahweh are mythic. As a working definition, biblical narrative considered to relate events of remote antiquity and centering on the action of at least one divine figure comparable to narrative in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle is 'myth.'"

**Evidence presented:**
- Oden's four-part definition: myths are (1) narratives, (2) traditional/orally transmitted, (3) with more-than-human characters, (4) set in remote antiquity. By this definition, "virtually any biblical narrative with a long history of transmission placing Yahweh or other divine beings in the distant past is a myth."
- Smith's preferred narrower definition: "narratives about divine beings," using Baal Cycle as the standard.
- Biblical texts qualifying as myth: Genesis 1-2, Genesis 6:1-4, Exodus 15, Deuteronomy 32:8-27, 33:2-5, Job 1-2, Psalm 74:12-17, 82:1-7, Isaiah 14:12-15, Ezekiel 28:11-19, Habakkuk 3:3-15, Daniel 7-12.
- "Israel generated relatively few new myths, and the majority of them drew on older mythic material."
- Contrast: Ugaritic myths offer "continuous narrative, like a modern film" while biblical sources provide "a series of glimpses, a handful of snapshots or frames."

**Confidence:** HIGH that biblical texts contain mythic material by any reasonable definition. The specific list is debatable at the margins but the principle is solid.

**What's at stake:** If the Bible contains myth (not just "mythic imagery"), the traditional contrast between biblical "history" and pagan "myth" collapses. This has been settled in critical scholarship for decades but remains contested in confessional contexts.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who reserve "myth" for polytheistic narratives and "history" or "theology" for biblical texts. The older German tradition (Ewald, Gunkel) defined myth as stories about deities BUT NOT the Deity of Israel.

**Alternative reading:** Smith's narrower definition (divine-centered narratives) is defensible but the line between "myth" and "mythic imagery" is blurry. The Psalms are prayers, not narratives — are they "myths" or just "containing mythic material"?

**My assessment:** This is careful methodological ground-clearing. Smith's distinction between full myth (narrative) and mythic imagery (allusion) is useful and largely tracks actual scholarly practice. The list of biblical myths is reasonable. The "film vs. snapshots" metaphor is memorable and apt.

## Claim 8: Ugaritic texts remain the best available background for Israelite religion despite temporal and cultural distance

**Author's claim:** "Of all the ancient texts found in the Levant containing this kind of information, the Ugaritic texts are also most proximate in time and place to ancient Israel." Ebla is too distant geographically and temporally. Mari and Emar have less information about divinity specifically.

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit flourished ca. 1350-1150 BCE — within a few centuries of earliest Israel (ca. 1200).
- Ugarit is ~100 miles north of Beirut on the Mediterranean coast.
- "The diversity of later reflexes of material in the Ugaritic texts shows that mythic narratives were transmitted in the areas known in the Bible as Canaan and Israel during the Iron Age." These include Phoenician inscriptions, biblical and rabbinic texts, Aramaic inscriptions, and classical sources.
- Specific parallels: Mount Sapan/Zaphon (Psalm 48:3), Leviathan, Tannin, Ashtaroth and Edrei (CAT 1.108.2-3), vocabulary found in Israel's oldest poems.
- The Baal Cycle and other Ugaritic texts preserve full mythological narratives — unlike later Phoenician/Punic texts.
- Cultural differences exist: Ugarit was urban/cosmopolitan/centralized; early Israel was rural/diverse/decentralized. But "these distinctions do not diminish the significance of the parallels; rather, they more precisely define them."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The relevance of Ugaritic texts to biblical studies is one of the most firmly established facts in the field. Even scholars who dispute "Canaanite" as a label don't deny the specific textual parallels.

**What's at stake:** The entire comparative enterprise. Without Ugarit, we have no West Semitic mythological narratives to compare with biblical texts. The divine council, divine family, and Baal-Yahweh parallels all depend on this corpus.

**Who disagrees:** Minimalists like Lemche who question the entire comparative project. But few scholars dispute the utility of Ugaritic texts — they dispute the interpretive frameworks.

**Alternative reading:** Ugaritic texts might illuminate a SPECIFIC northern coastal culture that influenced Israel indirectly through "Canaanite" intermediaries — relevant but not as direct background.

**My assessment:** Smith's defense is strong. The specific parallels (Sapan, Leviathan, divine council terminology) make the case. The temporal/cultural gap is real but manageable — Smith acknowledges it openly.

## Claim 9: Scholarly discussions of monotheism have become detached from ancient Israelite reality — monotheism was a rhetoric, not a "religious stage"

**Author's claim:** "Monotheism has apparently achieved a status in modern discourse that it never held in ancient Israel, where it functioned as a rhetoric expressing and advancing the cause of Israelite monolatrous practice." Modern discussions "have reified the idea of monotheism and disconnected it from its larger religious context."

**Evidence presented:**
- Monotheism appears clearly in biblical texts dating to the 6th century BCE (possibly 7th).
- It was "an inner-Israelite development over hundreds of years, not a feature known from Israel's inception."
- Modern Western culture uses monotheism as a "civil religion" binding different religious groups; also blames monotheism for violence and exclusivity.
- Both the celebration and the critique assume monotheism is a standalone phenomenon, which in ancient Israel it was not.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The distinction between monotheism-as-rhetoric and monotheism-as-stage is Smith's key contribution. It needs testing against the texts in chapters 8-10.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism is rhetoric rather than an ontological position, the entire modern debate about "monotheism's virtues/vices" is asking the wrong question. The ancient texts weren't making philosophical claims about the number of gods — they were making political-theological claims about Yahweh's supremacy.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat monotheism as a clear evolutionary stage (Axial Age theorists following Jaspers). Traditional theologians who see monotheism as a revealed truth rather than a rhetorical strategy.

**Alternative reading:** Even if monotheistic language had rhetorical function, it ALSO made ontological claims. "There is no god besides me" (Isaiah 45:5) is a truth claim as well as rhetoric. The two aren't mutually exclusive.

**My assessment:** This is Smith's central thesis and it's provocative. The distinction is genuinely illuminating — it explains why monotheistic statements in the Bible are relatively rare and clustered in specific contexts (polemic, exile). But Smith may overcorrect: rhetoric and ontology aren't exclusive categories. I'll watch how he handles this in chapters 8-10.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Conceptual unity of polytheism neglected | HIGH | Smith imposing coherence where it didn't exist |
| 2: "Canaanite" is problematic; "West Semitic" better | HIGH | Ugarit-Israel distance overstated by critics |
| 3: Mono-/polytheism are modern categories | VERY HIGH | Ancient distinction between gods still real |
| 4: Israel was broadly West Semitic religiously | HIGH | Early distinctiveness data from Tigay/Cross |
| 5: Etymology of 'ēl is unresolved | HIGH | New epigraphic discoveries |
| 6: Divine family = polytheism's "oneness" | MEDIUM | Needs data from Part I |
| 7: Biblical myth exists (by Baal Cycle standard) | HIGH | Boundary between myth and mythic imagery |
| 8: Ugaritic texts are best available background | VERY HIGH | Minimalist challenges to Ugarit-Israel link |
| 9: Monotheism was rhetoric, not a stage | MEDIUM-HIGH | Rhetoric/ontology false dichotomy |

**Strongest section:** Section 2 — the critique of how scholars have used "monotheism" and "polytheism" as apologetic categories. Smith at his most incisive.

**Weakest section:** Section 3 — the "Canaanite" discussion is important but gets bogged in classification debates. The payoff ("therefore Ugaritic texts remain relevant") could be stated with less throat-clearing.

**Smith's intellectual stance:** He positions himself as a believing scholar who uses critical tools without letting them become weapons. He quotes Ricoeur on "smashing idols to let symbols speak" and explicitly refuses to "choose sides" between polytheism and monotheism. This is more than methodological — it's a bid for intellectual legitimacy with both critical scholars and faith communities.

**Cross-reference note:** Smith's claim that Israel was broadly West Semitic (Claim 4) dovetails with Cross's argument that El was Israel's original god — both see continuity rather than rupture. But Cross emphasizes the distinctiveness of YHWH as Divine Warrior more than Smith does here. Römer's *The Invention of God* makes similar arguments about gradual emergence. Tigay's onomastic data (predominantly Yahwistic names from early periods) provides a potential counterweight — if most Israelites were already monolatrous by the 8th century, Smith's "long polytheistic background" may be overstated.
