# Albertz — §2.3–2.4: The Pre-State Tribal Alliance and Family Piety

These sections complete Part 2 (Before the State). §2.3 describes the social and religious organization of the Israelite tribal alliance — Albertz's answer to how a decentralized, anti-monarchical society could function. His key claim: Israel was originally El-worshipping; Yahweh was adopted secondarily by the alliance when the Exodus group arrived, and Yahweh's alien/outsider status made him a BETTER symbol of opposition to domination than El. §2.4 documents how family piety continued largely untouched by the rise of official Yahweh religion — demonstrating "internal religious pluralism" at work.

---

## §2.3 The Religion of the Pre-State Alliance

### Claim 1: Pre-state Israel was a segmentary, acephalous, deliberately anti-monarchical tribal society — not an amphictyony, not a state

**Albertz's claim:** Israel was a "counter-model to the Canaanite city-states with their monarchical organization." It was deliberately decentralized and anti-domination — an "acephalous" (headless) and "egalitarian" segmentary society. Noth's amphictyony model is wrong (Israel had no central sanctuary); the African segmentary society model (Crüsemann) is better because "it understands the absence of central political institutions not as a lack of power... but as the expression of a deliberate political concern."

**Evidence presented:**
- Political institutions end at the local or tribal level — no permanent bodies above the tribe until the Philistine crisis forced development
- Two bodies existed: elders (heads of families/clans) handled minor business; the assembly of arms-bearing free men made major decisions — "a primitive form of democracy"
- Almost no permanent political offices — commanders reverted to ordinary status after crises (Gideon in Judg 8:22f.)
- Minimal social stratification: Saul ploughed his own field (I Sam 11:5); families were largely self-sufficient; the "ideal of the small farmer" was reality
- Peaceful relations existed with non-monarchical Canaanite cities (Gibeon, Shechem); monarchical cities were conquered
- Tribal structure was partly fictive — tribes were regional-political coalitions, not biological descent groups

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The segmentary society model is well-established in anthropology and fits the biblical data better than the amphictyony model. The deliberate anti-domination character may be slightly idealized.

**What's at stake:** If Israel's social origins are anti-monarchical by design, the later monarchy represents not evolution but regression — a betrayal of Israel's founding social vision. This sets up the prophetic critique as recovery of original values, not innovation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more institutional complexity in pre-state Israel, or who argue the anti-monarchical tradition is a later retrojection (the monarchy was more continuous with tribal leadership than the biblical texts admit).

**Alternative reading:** The segmentary model from African ethnography may be a problematic analogue for Iron Age Palestine. The "deliberate" character of Israel's anti-domination stance may be Albertz reading later prophetic ideology back into the pre-state period.

### Claim 2: The name "Israel" ("El rules") and the altar inscription "El, the god of Israel" (Gen 33:20) prove the tribal alliance originally worshipped El, not Yahweh — Yahweh was adopted secondarily

**Albertz's claim:** "The conclusion is unavoidable that the element 'el in Israel originally points to a stage in its religious history in which El and not yet Yahweh was the god of the tribal alliance of Israel." The Merenptah stele (1219 BCE) mentions "Israel" before the Exodus group could have arrived — "the Egyptian mention is probably to be related to a still pre-Yahwistic Israel." Yahweh was brought by the Exodus group in a "second phase" and was adopted because he was a BETTER symbol of opposition to domination than El.

**Evidence presented:**
- The name "Israel" (yiśrā'ēl) = "El rules" or "may God show himself as ruler" — formed with the divine name El, not Yahweh
- Gen 33:20: Jacob names an altar "El, the god of Israel" ('ēl 'ĕlōhê yiśrā'ēl) — 'ēl is clearly a divine name, not a generic term
- The Merenptah stele (1219 BCE) attests "Israel" as a people in Canaan before the Exodus group's likely arrival
- The Rachel tribes (Manasseh, Ephraim, Benjamin) — those associated with the Exodus group — represent a later layer of the alliance compared to the Leah tribes
- Yahweh was "almost predestined" to become the alliance's god: as a solitary southern god not integrated into the Canaanite pantheon, who proved his divinity in liberation from state oppression, "bound unilaterally and exclusively to a group from the lower classes"
- "Nowhere in the Old Testament is there any polemic against the god El" — the fusion was so smooth it left no trace of conflict

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The name "Israel" and Gen 33:20 are intriguing but not definitive. The Merenptah stele's "Israel" could theoretically be a different group. The argument from the absence of anti-El polemic is an argument from silence.

**What's at stake:** This is a major claim: Israel's GOD was originally El, not Yahweh. Yahweh was a latecomer adopted for political-ideological reasons. If true, this is a more radical version of the "Yahweh was not originally Israel's god" thesis than even Smith or Römer present — they tend to see Yahweh present from early on, just as one god among many. Albertz has El as the original deity of the entity called "Israel."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue the name "Israel" need not contain the divine name El specifically (it could be generic "god"). Those who see Yahweh present in the earliest Israel. Mark Smith argues Yahweh absorbed El's characteristics but doesn't claim El was worshipped by "Israel" before Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** The El-worshipping stage could predate the formation of "Israel" as a distinct entity — "Israel" may have formed around Yahweh from the beginning, with the name being older than the entity. Or El and Yahweh may always have been identified in early Israel (as Cross argued: Yahweh was originally an epithet of El).

**My assessment:** This is Albertz's most original and most debatable claim. The evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. The Merenptah stele dating is a real problem for a purely Exodus-based origin of Israel. And the absence of anti-El polemic IS striking — contrast the vehement anti-Baal polemic throughout the OT. But the counter-argument (Cross's position that Yahweh was an El-epithet from the start) explains the same data without requiring a separate El-worshipping phase. I'd rate this as plausible but underdetermined by the evidence.

### Claim 3: Yahweh functioned as both the principle of solidarity (enabling cooperation across tribal lines) AND the guarantee against permanent centralized power — "Yahweh will rule over you" (Gideon, Judg 8:22f.)

**Albertz's claim:** Yahweh religion "made an essential contribution to maintaining the critical balance between the obligation to solidarity and the ideal of freedom." On one hand, Yahweh created solidarity beyond clan and tribe — essential for wars of liberation. On the other, "there was virtually no religious legitimation of political rule." Yahweh's rule blocked human rule: when Gideon was offered the crown, he refused with "Yahweh will rule over you." The temporary charismatic leadership of the Judges (reverting to private life after the crisis) was theologically grounded.

**Evidence presented:**
- Judges 8:22f. — Gideon's refusal of hereditary monarchy
- The pattern of Judges: leaders emerge for crises, then disappear
- Only the relatively weak "spokesman" (nāśî') had religious protection (Ex 22:27) — not elders or the assembly
- The idea that Yahweh was the real owner of the land (Lev 25:23) served to protect family inheritance from being sold off

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Gideon story is a key text, but its dating and whether it reflects pre-state reality or later anti-monarchical ideology is contested.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh's original function was anti-monarchical, then the monarchy (when it comes) is not just a political innovation but a theological crisis — one that the prophets will spend centuries trying to resolve.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Judges as a later, pro-monarchical or Deuteronomistic composition. The Gideon story may be a 7th-century anti-monarchical polemic projected back.

**Alternative reading:** The charismatic leadership model in Judges may reflect how tribal societies actually functioned (temporary war leaders) without requiring a specific anti-monarchical theology. Gideon's refusal may be a literary trope rather than historical memory.

**My assessment:** Consistent with Albertz's overall model but feels slightly idealized. The tension between Yahweh-as-liberator and Yahweh-as-king is real in the texts, but whether it originates in pre-state social organization or in later theological reflection is hard to determine.

## §2.4 Family Piety in the Later Pre-State Period

### Claim 4: Family piety continued largely uninfluenced by official Yahweh religion — a clear case of "internal religious pluralism"

**Albertz's claim:** "The kind of religion practised in the families of this time largely proves to be uninfluenced by the recent rise of Yahweh religion... much though Yahweh as a symbol of religious liberation made his unmistakable stamp especially on the political life of society as a whole, he made little impact on the religious everyday life of the families." This is the clearest demonstration of his "internal religious pluralism" model.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Personal names**: "The predicative elements of theophoric names show virtually no relationship to the events which were so constitutive of Yahweh religion: Exodus, Sinai, 'settlement' or wars of liberation." Specific Exodus verbs (hôṣî', he'ĕlâ) are absent. "Verbs which could have alluded to its immigration are completely lacking." (This directly supports Tigay's onomastic findings — names don't reflect official salvation-history themes.)
- **Individual laments**: The psalms of individual lament use language of personal distress (illness, enemies, death) not national salvation-history
- **Vows and offerings**: Family-level practices like vows (neder), first-fruit offerings, and individual sacrifices continued with their own logic
- **Circumcision**: Probably originated as a family-level rite (apotropaic, associated with fertility/marriage) before being theologized as a "covenant sign" by P
- **Family cult**: Continued at local sanctuaries (bāmôt) and household shrines; the teraphim and other family cult objects persisted

**Confidence:** HIGH. The onomastic evidence is strong and specific. Albertz's observation that personal names don't reference salvation-history is verifiable and important. This is one of his strongest empirical arguments.

**What's at stake:** If family piety was genuinely uninfluenced by official Yahweh religion, then (a) appeals to "what Israelites believed" must specify WHICH Israelites at WHICH social level, (b) the official religion's claim to represent "Israel" is an aspiration, not a description, and (c) the history of Israelite religion is the story of official religion gradually penetrating (and being resisted by) family practice.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more integration between family and official religion. Van der Toorn's work emphasizes how family religion was "Yahwized" rather than remaining separate.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of Exodus/Sinai themes in personal names could reflect naming conventions (names express hopes for the child, not national theology) rather than a genuine religious divide. People with Exodus-theology might still name their children "Yohanan" (YHWH is gracious) rather than "Exodus-deliverance."

**My assessment:** This is solid evidence for Albertz's model, and it converges with Tigay's findings (94% YHWH names but no salvation-history content). The gap between official theology and personal piety is real. The question is whether it indicates "two strata" or just different applications of the same religion in different contexts — like a modern Christian who names their child "Grace" rather than "Calvary" but still believes in the atonement.

## §2.3–2.4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Segmented, acephalous, anti-monarchical tribal society | MEDIUM-HIGH | Idealization of pre-state period; African analogies may not fit |
| 2. Original El-worship; Yahweh adopted secondarily | MEDIUM | Name "Israel" may not prove El-worship; Cross's El-Yahweh identity theory |
| 3. Yahweh as both solidarity principle AND anti-domination guarantee | MEDIUM | Gideon story may be late retrojection |
| 4. Family piety uninfluenced by Yahweh religion (internal pluralism) | HIGH | Personal names may reflect convention, not theological gap |

**Strongest section:** Claim 4 — the evidence from personal names and individual laments for the gap between family piety and official religion. This is empirically grounded and not dependent on Albertz's theoretical framework to be significant.

**Weakest section:** Claim 2 — the "original El-worshipping Israel" thesis. The evidence is limited (one name, one altar inscription, and the Merenptah stele dating) and can be explained by alternative models (Cross's El-Yahweh identity, or El as simply the generic word for "god" in the name). Albertz needs this claim for his narrative (Yahweh as superior liberation symbol replacing El) but the evidence doesn't fully carry it.

**Cross-reference:** Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* argues Yahweh was originally an epithet OF El ("El who creates the hosts"), which would mean El and Yahweh were never separate deities to be fused. Albertz's claim of a two-stage religious history (El → Yahweh) directly contradicts Cross on this point. The absence of anti-El polemic in the OT supports BOTH positions but for different reasons: Cross says it's because El and Yahweh were always the same god; Albertz says it's because the fusion was peaceful and complete. Which is right? The Merenptah stele is the key evidence Albertz adds — if "Israel" existed as an El-worshipping entity before the Exodus group arrived, Cross's model has a chronological problem.

**Connection to Tigay:** Albertz's onomastic observations in §2.4 (personal names don't reference Exodus/Sinai) complement Tigay's broader finding that 94%+ of Hebrew inscriptions contain YHWH theophoric names. The combined picture: names are overwhelmingly Yahwistic (Tigay) but their content reflects family piety concerns, not official salvation-history (Albertz). Names prove popular monolatry but not popular engagement with official theology.

**What to watch:** The "internal religious pluralism" model has now been demonstrated for the pre-state period. The key test in Chapter 3 (the monarchy) will be whether this gap persists, narrows, or becomes contentious — and whether the monarchy represents integration of the two strata or exacerbation of their tension.
