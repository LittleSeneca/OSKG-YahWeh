# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapter 2

## The Geographic Origin of Yhwh

---

## Claim 1: Yhwh originated in the southern deserts (Edom/Seir/Teman), not in Canaan

**Römer's claim:** "The oldest attested occurrences of Yhwh are from the south of Palestine, the territory of Edom and Araba." Yhwh was a southern desert/war god associated with the Shasu nomads. The biblical poetry preserves a genuine memory of his southern origin. This is the scholarly consensus.

**Evidence presented — the four candidate origins Römer evaluates:**

| Site | Proposed Evidence | Römer's Verdict |
|------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Ebla** (Syria, 3rd millennium) | Personal names ending in *-ya* interpreted by Pettinato as short form of Yhwh | **Rejected.** The *-ya* syllable is a hypocoristic (diminutive) ending, not a divine name. "No god Yhwh, moreover, appears in any of the lists of gods to whom sacrifice is offered. So there was no god Yhwh at Ebla." |
| **Ugarit** (Syria, 14th-13th c.) | Fragmentary text (KTU 1.1) with possible YW — "the name of my son, YW" | **Inconclusive.** "We cannot therefore definitively exclude a link between Yw in the Ugaritic text and Yhwh." But the passage is "too fragmentary and unclear." The YW could be Yam (the sea god) or a scribal error for Ym. "Perhaps, however, there is an even simpler solution, which is that what we have here is just a scribal error." |
| **Mari** (Euphrates, 2nd millennium) | Proper names like *Yaḫwi-ilum* | **Rejected.** "These names, derived from a root signifying 'to manifest oneself,' cannot be linked with Yhwh, because they contain no name of a divinity, merely a verbal form signifying 'He manifests himself.'" |
| **Egypt/Soleb** (14th c. BCE) | Inscriptions of Amenophis III listing "the Shasu of Yhw(h)" | **The strongest evidence.** Earliest attestation of Yhwh. "In these texts Yhwʒ seems to be a geographic term (referring to a mountain?) and perhaps also a divine name." |

**The Soleb evidence in detail:**

- **Amenophis III at Soleb, Sudan (c. 1370 BCE):** A list of Shasu nomad territories includes "The country of the Shasu of Yhw(h)"
- **Ramses II at West Amara:** Same designation repeated
- **Papyrus Anastasi VI:** Mentions "the Shasu of Edom" whom Pharaoh Merneptah authorized to sojourn in Egypt with their herds
- **Papyrus Harris I (c. 1150 BCE):** Ramses IV boasts "I destroyed the people of Seir among the Shasu tribes. I razed their tents"
- **Karnak relief (Seti I, c. 1290-1280):** Depicts Shasu with distinctive goatees and hairbands — visual confirmation of the people group

**Archaeological context — the copper connection:**
- The Shasu were involved in copper mining in the Araba valley (Timna, about 30 km north of Eilat)
- Mining activity was at its peak in the 14th-12th centuries BCE
- Punon (Feinan in Jordan), mentioned in Numbers 33:26 and linked with the Shasu in the West Amara list, is also in this area
- This provides an economic context: the Shasu weren't just random nomads — they were connected to a major industry

> **Römer's conclusion:** "The archaeological, epigraphic, and iconographic evidence all place the Shasu in the territory of Edom or Seir, and in Araba at the time of the transition from the Late Bronze to the Iron Age. And among these Shasu there might have been a group whose tutelary god was called 'Yhw.'"

**Confidence:** HIGH that the Soleb inscriptions are the earliest extrabiblical attestation of Yhwh (the reading is widely accepted). MEDIUM-HIGH that this represents Yhwh's place of origin rather than just one place he was known. MEDIUM on the copper connection as the mechanism of cultural transmission (plausible but not proven).

**What's at stake for faith:** The God of Israel is geographically foreign to Israel. He came from Edom — the territory Israel would later consider its enemy. The Bible's own poetry (Judges 5, Deuteronomy 33) celebrates Yhwh "coming from Seir/Edom." The God who "chose Israel" was not originally Israel's god at all. He was adopted.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a Canaanite origin (the Ugaritic fragment is their best but weak evidence). Scholars who argue Yhwh was always indigenous to the central highlands.

**Alternative reading:** The Soleb inscriptions prove Yhwh was KNOWN in the south, not that he ORIGINATED there. He might have been a Canaanite god whose cult spread south and was later re-imported to Israel. The southern origin poems could be late theological retrojections (Persian period scribes creating a mythic origin story).

**My assessment:** Römer's case is well-argued and honest. He discards weak evidence (Ebla, Mari), honestly flags uncertain evidence (Ugarit), and builds around the strongest evidence (Soleb + biblical poetry). His use of the word "might" — "among these Shasu there MIGHT have been a group whose tutelary god was called 'Yhw'" — is proper scholarly caution. The southern origin hypothesis is the best available explanation but not proven beyond doubt.

## Claim 2: Four biblical poems preserve an ancient tradition of Yhwh's southern origin

**Römer's claim:** Four poetic texts — Deuteronomy 33:2, Judges 5:4-5, Psalm 68:8-9, and Habakkuk 3:3 — all describe Yhwh as coming "from the south." These are war poems. They preserve a tradition of Yhwh as a warrior god advancing from Edom/Seir/Teman/Paran to fight for Israel.

**Evidence presented — the four poems:**

| Text | Yhwh's Origin Point | Description |
|------|--------------------|-------------|
| **Deut 33:2** | Sinai, Seir, Mount Paran | "Yhwh came from Sinai, he shone forth from Seir, he was resplendent from Mount Paran" |
| **Judg 5:4-5** | Seir, Edom | "Yhwh, when you came forth out of Seir, when you advanced from the land of Edom, the earth trembled, the sky quaked, the clouds poured down water" |
| **Ps 68:8-9** | "Arid land" (yĕšîmôn) | Nearly identical to Judges 5, but with Yhwh replaced by Elohim (part of the "Elohistic Psalter" — Pss 42-83 systematically replaced Yhwh with Elohim). "It is clear that this originally read: 'Yhwh, the god of Israel.'" |
| **Hab 3:3** | Teman, Mount Paran | "God comes from Teman, the Holy One comes from Mount Paran. His splendor covers the sky, his praise fills the earth." |

**The relationship between Judges 5 and Psalm 68:**
- "The two texts are in large part identical." Both describe Yhwh "coming out" to battle. Both use the same sequence: address in second person → description in third person. Both use the strange phrase *zeh sînay* ("this Sinai").
- Römer argues Judges 5 is the older version, which Psalm 68 reworks and expands.
- Psalm 68:13-14 ("Would you linger in the camp?") alludes to Judges 5:16 ("Why did you remain with the baggage?"). Psalm 68:12's celestial army evokes the combat of the stars in Judges 5:20.
- **"Judges 5, in any case, is at least in its primitive form often considered to be one of the oldest texts in the Hebrew Bible."**

**The dating problem (Römer is honest about this):**
- "Many experts today consider this text to be particularly archaic, with origins in the period even before the institution of the Israelite monarchy." BUT "Others, however, claim that it is a late text, composed on the basis of the narration of this episode in the previous chapter (Judg. 4) in order to give that narration a poetic conclusion."
- "So the current dating of the song in Judges 5 varies from the twelfth century to the Hellenistic era."
- "It is extremely difficult to know whether the text refers to a real historical event or whether, as some have suggested, it is rather a mythological text."
- The Hebrew "is either archaic or consciously archaizing."
- The names are suspicious: Deborah = "bee," Barak = "thunderbolt," Yael = "goat" — parallels with Greek mythology (Melissa the bee nursing Zeus, Amalthea the goat giving him milk).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that the poems preserve a genuine tradition of southern origin (the consistency across four independent poems is hard to explain as coincidence). MEDIUM on the dating (Römer himself acknowledges the range from 12th century to Hellenistic). If the poems are late, they tell us about later theology, not early history.

**What's at stake for faith:** If these are among the oldest texts in the Bible, they preserve Israel's own memory that Yhwh came from outside — from the south, from Edom. The Bible itself testifies to Yhwh's non-Israelite origin. You don't need critical scholarship to reach this conclusion; you just need to read the poetry carefully.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the poems to the Persian or Hellenistic period. The mythological reading (the names are symbolic, not historical).

**Alternative reading:** The "coming from the south" tradition might reflect a later theological construction — scribes in Jerusalem creating a dramatic origin story for their god. The poems would then tell us about 6th-5th century theology, not 12th century history.

**My assessment:** The consistency of the southern origin tradition across FOUR independent poems (Deuteronomy 33, Judges 5, Psalm 68, Habakkuk 3) is impressive. But the dating problem is real. If Schmid is right that the Pentateuch is Persian-period, then these poems — or at least their final form — are much later than the events they seem to describe. Römer's optimism about early dating is the vulnerable point.

## Cross-Cutting: Chapter 2's Architecture

Römer structures this chapter cleverly. He evaluates four candidate origins and eliminates three before arriving at the fourth. This creates rhetorical momentum — the reader feels the case building. But it's important to note: eliminating Ebla, Ugarit, and Mari as origins doesn't PROVE the Soleb/Sinai origin. It just means it's the best remaining candidate, and the evidence for it is still thin.

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Soleb = earliest Yhwh attestation | HIGH | Misreading of the Egyptian (unlikely — widely accepted) |
| Yhwh originated in the south | MEDIUM-HIGH | Could have been known there without originating there |
| Biblical poems preserve southern origin memory | MEDIUM-HIGH | Late dating of the poems (Persian/Hellenistic) |
| Copper trade as transmission mechanism | MEDIUM | Plausible but speculative |

**Strongest section:** The evaluation of candidate origins — methodical, honest, persuasive.

**Weakest section:** The dating of Judges 5 — Römer acknowledges the full range of scholarly opinion but then proceeds as if an early date is correct. His methodological optimism is on display.
