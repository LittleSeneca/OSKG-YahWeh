# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter I: Starting Point and Points of Departure

The methodology chapter. Keel and Uehlinger set out the intellectual program for the entire book: a diachronic, iconography-driven reconstruction of Canaanite/Israelite religion from Middle Bronze IIB (c. 1750) through Iron III (c. 450), using 8,500 stamp seals as the primary dataset. They position themselves against text-only approaches (Smith, de Moor) and against archaeologists who ignore glyptic art (Dever, Holladay). The central argument: seal amulets provide a "seismograph" for religious change — more numerous, more precisely dateable, and more geographically specific than any other source type.

---

## Claim 1: The debate about Yahweh's consort cannot be resolved from texts alone — iconographic evidence is the missing piece
→ [[claim-yahweh-consort-debate-requires-iconographic-evidence-not-texts-alone]] | **keel-ggi-1.1** | Confidence: very-high
  Biblical texts are late, polemical, and represent a minority opinion; the Kuntillet Ajrud debate has been conducted missing an entire category of evidence. Contradicts: [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (text-based consort reading structurally incomplete)

---

## Claim 2: "Polytheistic" tells us almost nothing — must specify WHICH gods, WHERE, WHEN, and in WHAT RELATION to Yahweh
→ [[claim-polytheism-label-insufficient-must-specify-which-gods-where-when]] | **keel-ggi-1.2** | Confidence: high
  The preexilic polytheism consensus is contentless without stratified archaeological controls; biblical dating crisis makes texts unreliable for period-specific reconstruction. Contradicts: [[claim-bible-preserves-polytheistic-traces]] (text-based polytheism claims insufficient without local archaeological correlates)

---

## Claim 3: Seal amulets are the single best source — 8,500 stamp seals function as a "seismograph" for religious change
→ [[claim-seal-amulets-best-source-religious-history-seismograph]] | **keel-ggi-1.3** | Confidence: high
  Seals survive in comparable quantities across all periods, are more-or-less public artifacts, and far outshine all other image-bearing artifacts. Supports: [[claim-iron-age-israel-lacks-male-divine-images-aniconic]] and [[claim-no-iron-age-male-divine-figurines-demonstrates-aniconism]]

---

## Claim 4: Methodology requires analyzing "constellations" of motifs, not isolated images — Assmann's iconicity theory
→ [[claim-constellation-methodology-motif-combinations-not-isolated-images]] | **keel-ggi-1.4** | Confidence: high
  Recurring motif combinations represent stable complexes of ideas; the same constellation (dragon combat) appears with different divine names but the underlying ICON is constant. Supports: [[claim-convergence-differentiation-framework-israelite-religion]] (text-based counterpart)

---

## Claim 5: Standard periodization wrong — Iron IIB/C break at late 8th century (Assyrian conquest), not c. 850
→ [[claim-iron-iib-iic-break-late-eighth-century-assyrian-conquest]] | **keel-ggi-1.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  H. Weippert's architectural periodization (IIB ends c. 850) leaves almost no small artifacts; iconographic evidence shows the real break at Assyrian conquest. Supports: [[claim-fall-samaria-catalyst-monotheism]] and [[claim-neo-assyrian-crisis-drove-theological-shift]]

---

## Claim 6: Survey begins with MB IIB (from 1750 BCE) because continuous cultural tradition begins then — extending to Hebrew Bible
→ [[claim-middle-bronze-iib-starting-point-cultural-continuum-palestine]] | **keel-ggi-1.6** | Confidence: medium-high
  MB IIB marks the start of indigenous iconographic production and a cultural continuum implying Israelite religion developed within a Canaanite matrix. Supports: [[claim-israelite-culture-was-canaanite-no-separation]]. Contradicts: [[claim-israelite-religion-absolutely-different-paganism-from-beginning]] (Kaufmann)

---

## Claim 7: Ugaritic texts cannot be primary evidence for Palestinian/Israelite religion — local Canaanite evidence must come first
→ [[claim-ugaritic-texts-cannot-primary-evidence-palestinian-israelite-religion]] | **keel-ggi-1.7** | Confidence: medium
  Ugarit is geographically distinct from Canaan and temporally distant (Late Bronze vs. Iron Age); Ugaritic parallels should only be used after establishing local Palestinian correlates. Contradicts: [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]] (Smith) and [[claim-ugaritic-texts-most-profound-impact-twentieth-century]] (Lewis)

## Chapter I Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Iconographic evidence is the missing piece | VERY HIGH | Already vindicated by the book's influence |
| 2: "Polytheistic" tells us nothing without specifics | HIGH | Only if the specifics turn out to be uninteresting |
| 3: Seals as "seismograph" | HIGH | Representativeness questions |
| 4: Constellation methodology | HIGH | Over-interpretation risk with conventional motifs |
| 5: Revised Iron IIB/C periodization | MEDIUM-HIGH | Depends on whether other archaeologists accept it |
| 6: MB IIB as starting point | MEDIUM-HIGH | Form continuity ≠ meaning continuity |
| 7: Ugaritic texts should not be primary evidence | MEDIUM | Self-serving; they use Ugaritic too |

**Strongest section:** §5 on sources. The case for seal amulets as the primary dataset is quantitatively compelling and methodologically innovative.

**Weakest section:** §§7-8 on periodization. This is a specialist debate that most readers can't evaluate.

**Most consequential methodological move:** The "constellation" concept (§6). This is what allows them to move from "here are 8,500 seals" to "here is what Israelites believed."
