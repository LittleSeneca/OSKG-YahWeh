# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter I: Starting Point and Points of Departure

The methodology chapter. Keel and Uehlinger set out the intellectual program for the entire book: a diachronic, iconography-driven reconstruction of Canaanite/Israelite religion from Middle Bronze IIB (c. 1750) through Iron III (c. 450), using 8,500 stamp seals as the primary dataset. They position themselves against text-only approaches (Smith, de Moor) and against archaeologists who ignore glyptic art (Dever, Holladay). The central argument is that seal amulets provide a "seismograph" for religious change — more numerous, more precisely dateable, and more geographically specific than any other source type.

---

## Claim 1: The debate about Yahweh's consort cannot be resolved from texts alone — iconographic evidence is the missing piece

**Author's claim:** The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions ("Yahweh... and his Asherah") reignited the question of whether preexilic Israel was polytheistic and whether Yahweh had a divine consort. But "anyone who wants to study actual primary sources must focus on the treasure trove of iconographic and epigraphic documents provided by archaeological research in Palestine/Israel" (line 50-52). Biblical texts are "difficult to interpret historically" and the scholarly debate has been driven by "minority opinion expressed in the religious literature preserved in the Old Testament" rather than material evidence.

**Evidence presented:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions published in 1978 sparked new debate about monotheism's origins (Lang 1981, Haag 1985)
- Feminist theological research intensified the Asherah question — the debate has direct implications for "the status of women in both synagogue and church" (line 20-21)
- Scholars like Lang, Weippert, and Knauf assert preexilic polytheism as consensus, but their evidence is almost entirely textual
- The biblical texts are late, polemical, and represent a "minority opinion" — not recoverable popular religion

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is a statement of the problem, not a factual claim. The gap they identify is real and demonstrable — every existing treatment of the Yahweh/Asherah question relies primarily on texts, and the few that use archaeology (Dever, Holladay) ignore seal amulets.

**What's at stake:** If they're right, then the entire existing debate (Smith vs. Dever, monotheism's dating, Asherah's status as goddess vs. symbol) has been conducted with one hand tied behind its back. The iconographic evidence could shift every major conclusion.

**Who disagrees:** Not exactly "disagreement" — more like neglect. Smith (1990, 2001) acknowledges the "missing puzzle pieces" but doesn't systematically incorporate iconography. Dever uses SOME archaeological evidence (figurines, cult stands) but not seal amulets systematically. Tigay (1986) used onomastic evidence and mentioned iconography in an appendix — they see their work as an expansion of his approach.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that iconography is MORE ambiguous than texts, not less — a seal image doesn't come with a caption explaining what the wearer believed. Keel and Uehlinger address this in §6 with their constellation methodology.

**My assessment:** This is the single strongest framing move in the book. They don't just say "we have new data"; they say "the existing debate is structurally incomplete because it's missing an entire category of evidence." This framing has been vindicated — the book is now cited 71 times in our project notes precisely because it fills this gap.

## Claim 2: Preexilic Israel was polytheistic, but that tells us almost nothing — the real question is WHICH gods, WHERE, WHEN, and in WHAT RELATION to Yahweh

**Author's claim:** "Recent Old Testament research... generally assumes that the religion of preexilic Israel and Judah is to be characterized as thoroughly polytheistic" (lines 68-71). They quote Lang, Knauf, and Weippert establishing the consensus. But they immediately problematize it: "The texts cited by Weippert (Psalms 82, 89; Deut 32:8f.) do in fact assume that other gods existed in addition to Yahweh. But the texts say nothing about whether these deities were venerated in any way in Israel and Judah" (lines 111-116). The dating problem is severe — "widespread uncertainty about how to date the biblical texts" means "such textual material is of little value for providing good evidence when one tries to reconstruct the religion of Israel and Judah for the period of the monarchy" (lines 133-137).

**Evidence presented:**
- Lang (1981, 1983): preexilic Israel had a triad of protector god, national god (Yahweh), and functional deities
- Knauf (1988): different triads at Bethel, Jerusalem, Hebron
- Weippert (1990): even "official" Jerusalem theology was polytheistic; Yahweh had a goddess partner
- Counterpoint: all of these are deductions from texts — none from stratified archaeological evidence
- Tigay (1986) as methodological model: "external controls" from archaeology to balance the "notorious subjectivity" of dating texts

**Confidence:** HIGH that they're right about the consensus. HIGH that they're right about the insufficiency of textual evidence alone. The dating crisis in Hebrew Bible scholarship is real and they don't overstate it.

**What's at stake:** If all we can say is "preexilic Israel was polytheistic" without specifying which gods, in which regions, in which periods, and in what relationship to Yahweh, then the statement is nearly contentless. Their entire chronological survey is designed to answer these specific questions.

**Who disagrees:** Maximalists who trust the biblical chronology would push back on the dating skepticism. Dever has argued that the biblical texts contain recoverable historical memory even if late. Smith explicitly builds his case FROM the texts (Ugaritic and biblical) rather than archaeological evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical texts, even if late, may preserve genuine preexilic traditions through oral transmission. Keel and Uehlinger's skepticism about texts could lead them to underweight genuinely ancient material embedded in late compositions.

**My assessment:** Their critique is measured. They don't reject textual evidence — they integrate epigraphic finds (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom) throughout the book. They're saying texts ALONE can't do the job, not that texts are worthless.

## Claim 3: Seal amulets are the single best source for reconstructing religious history — they function as a "seismograph" for religious change

**Author's claim:** "Not only because of their sheer number but also because of their importance, seal amulets far outshine all other kinds of image-bearing artifacts in value, being even more valuable than inscriptional evidence. Since they are preserved in relatively comparable quantities for all periods, they can virtually serve as the standard by which religious history is documented, particularly because they are more or less public artifacts and can thus serve as a sensitive seismograph to detect subtle shifts in religious history" (lines 531-537).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Fribourg corpus: approximately 8,500 stamp seals from legal excavations in Palestine/Israel, spanning earliest times through the end of the Iron Age
- Seals survive in comparable quantities across all periods (unlike temples, bronze statues, or texts, which are unevenly distributed)
- Seals are "more or less public artifacts" — worn, traded, used by broad populations — not just elite productions
- Different media tell different stories: cylinder seals allow complex "constellations," stamp seals simplify but can still combine motifs, isolated statues lose their iconographic context

**Confidence:** HIGH. The quantitative argument is strong. The corpus size (8,500) dwarfs every other artifact category for religious imagery. The diachronic comparability claim is their key methodological innovation.

**What's at stake:** If seals are as representative as they claim, then shifts in seal iconography ARE shifts in popular religious mentality — not just elite theological fashion. This is the empirical foundation for every chronological argument in chapters II-X.

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the numbers, but one could question representativeness. Seals were owned by the literate/elite class, not the entire population. They may overrepresent official or semi-official religion and underrepresent folk practice. Dever's figurines (3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines) might be MORE representative of popular religion than seals, even if fewer in number.

**Alternative reading:** Seals reflect the iconographic choices of seal-cutters and their elite patrons — they may lag behind or diverge from popular belief. The "public" character they claim could be overstated if seals were primarily administrative tools rather than devotional objects.

**My assessment:** The seismograph metaphor is apt but not proven in this chapter — it depends on whether the diachronic patterns they identify in chapters II-X actually correlate with known historical developments. The proof is in the execution. Their point about different media telling different stories (§6) shows methodological sophistication — they're not naive about the limitations of their own source type.

## Claim 4: The methodology requires analyzing "constellations" of motifs, not isolated images — Assmann's iconicity theory adapted to Levantine material

**Author's claim:** Building on the Heidelberg Egyptologist Jan Assmann, they argue that iconographic interpretation must focus on "constellations" — recurring combinations of motifs that represent stable complexes of ideas — rather than hunting for specific myths behind images. "Mythic texts are nothing other than icons (or else constellations) that have been presented in story form" (lines 657-658). The battle-against-the-dragon constellation appears with different divine names (Baal/Yamm, Marduk/Tiamat, Yahweh/Leviathan, Perseus/sea monster, St. George/dragon) but the underlying ICON is constant.

**Evidence presented:**
- Assmann's distinction between "myth" (narrative) and "constellation" (pre-narrative complex of ideas) — lines 624-640
- The "iconic constancy" principle: "The myth owes its 'iconic constancy' to a few basic constellations" (line 641-642)
- Examples: divine couple = fertility; serpent/dragon combat = order vs. chaos; nursing mother = divine provision
- Different image-bearing media have different capacities: cylinder seals can show complex multi-figure scenes; stamp seals simplify; isolated statues lose context entirely

**Confidence:** HIGH. Assmann's theory is well-established in Egyptology. The adaptation to Levantine material is original but methodologically sound — they're not claiming these constellations originated in Egypt, just that the analytical framework transfers.

**What's at stake:** This is the theoretical heart of the book. Without the constellation method, they're just cataloguing images. With it, they can make claims about religious CONCEPTS from visual patterns. The method allows them to identify the absorption of solar imagery into Yahwism NOT by finding an image labeled "Yahweh as sun god" but by tracking the migration of solar motifs (winged disk, falcon, uraeus) into contexts where they appear alongside Yahwistic symbols.

**Who disagrees:** Text-focused scholars might argue that this method over-interprets visual evidence — that a winged sun disk on a seal doesn't necessarily mean the owner "worshipped" a solar deity, it could be decorative or apotropaic. The "constellation" method assumes intentionality that may not always be present.

**Alternative reading:** Some images may be purely conventional — seal-cutters reproducing standard patterns without theological investment. The constellation method risks treating every pattern as ideologically significant.

**My assessment:** The constellation method is Keel and Uehlinger's most important methodological contribution, and it's been widely adopted in the field. The lingering question is the one they partially acknowledge: how do we distinguish a THEOLOGICAL constellation from an ARTISTIC convention? They answer this pragmatically — by tracking patterns across time, media, and geography. If a motif cluster appears consistently in different media and shifts together, that's evidence of a religious concept, not just an artistic habit.

## Claim 5: The standard archaeological periodization is wrong — Iron IIB/C break must be dated to the late 8th century (Assyrian conquest), not c. 850

**Author's claim:** "If one considers the artistic works, as will be done here, it will seem not only plausible but unavoidable that the break between Iron Age IIB and C must be dated to the second half of the eighth century, when Palestine came increasingly under the direct political influence of the great Assyrian superpower" (lines 824-827). They explicitly critique H. Weippert's periodization, which ends Iron IIB at c. 850 (end of Omri dynasty), because it's based solely on architectural evidence and "prevents her from attaching proper significance to a change of the magnitude of Assyria's conquest" (lines 815-816).

**Evidence presented:**
- H. Weippert's 1988 Handbuch uses ONLY architectural evidence, producing an Iron IIB (c. 925-850) that leaves almost no small artifacts
- Assyrian conquest introduced massive cultural changes: pottery types shift (Zimhoni 1990), settlement patterns change, iconography transforms
- The break at c. 850 leaves the entire 8th century (including the fall of Samaria in 722 and Sennacherib's campaign in 701) lumped into Iron IIC without distinguishing Assyrian from pre-Assyrian material
- Their alternative: Iron IIB = 925-700 (Phoenician/Egyptian orientation), Iron IIC = 700-600 (Assyrian/Aramean influence)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Their argument from iconographic evidence makes intuitive sense, but it depends on the data they'll present in chapters VII-VIII. The dating of archaeological periods is inherently contested and depends on which evidence type you privilege.

**What's at stake:** If they're right, then the religious developments of the 8th century (the "Yahweh alone" movement, the emergence of aniconism, the deuteronomic reform) must be understood against an ASSYRIAN backdrop, not just internal Israelite politics. This directly impacts how we date the emergence of monotheistic rhetoric (cf. Smith Origins Ch8).

**Who disagrees:** H. Weippert explicitly. Also any archaeologist who privileges stratigraphy and architecture over small finds for periodization. The traditional scheme (Iron IIA = United Monarchy, IIB = Divided Monarchy, IIC = Assyrian period) has the advantage of aligning with the biblical narrative — Keel and Uehlinger are partly breaking from that alignment.

**Alternative reading:** The traditional periodization may be less wrong than they claim if architectural change IS a better proxy for political change than small finds. Palaces and fortifications reflect state power more directly than seal motifs, which may change more slowly or for different reasons.

**My assessment:** This is an intramural archaeological debate that I'm not qualified to adjudicate. But their decision has real consequences for the book's narrative: it means Chapter VII (Iron IIB) covers a longer period (925-700) that includes BOTH the Divided Monarchy AND the first Assyrian incursions, while Chapter VIII (Iron IIC) becomes specifically about the Assyrian-dominated 7th century. This framing makes the iconographic shifts they identify in Ch VIII (astralization, goddess revival, orthodox reaction) legible as RESPONSES to Assyrian imperial pressure — which is a more interesting argument than just "things changed."

## Claim 6: The survey begins with Middle Bronze IIB because that's when a continuous cultural tradition begins in Palestine that persists through the Iron Age

**Author's claim:** "One can justify beginning our survey at this point, when the urban culture in Palestine begins to flourish, because from this point on we can deal with a cultural continuum in Palestine that extends all the way to the time of the emergence of the Hebrew Bible" (lines 892-895). The earlier periods (Early Bronze, MB I/Intermediate) are archaeologically sparse for iconography. MB IIA is dominated by Egyptian imports — not local production. MB IIB (c. 1750) is when indigenous Canaanite iconographic traditions first coalesce.

**Evidence presented:**
- Early Bronze IV/MB I (2200-2000): "Iconographic evidence is virtually nonexistent" (line 871)
- MB IIA (2000-1750): "limited generally to Egyptian imports" — scarabs with non-pictorial designs (line 877-881)
- MB IIB (from 1750): Syrian influences arrive, indigenous production begins, Hyksos period creates Canaanite-Egyptian cultural interchange
- "It is also likely that the same ethnic groups continued to maintain their cultural system from the Middle Bronze Age right into the Iron Age" (lines 900-902)
- Finkelstein's (1988) argument: the hill country population didn't disappear during Late Bronze — they shifted to nomadism and resettled in Iron I

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The archaeological rationale is sound. The "cultural continuum" claim about ethnic continuity is more debatable — it depends on whether material culture continuity implies population continuity, which is always contested in archaeology.

**What's at stake:** This justifies the entire chronological scope of the book. If there's no continuity from MB IIB through Iron III, then the diachronic comparisons they make across chapters are invalid. It also implies that Israelite religion didn't emerge from nowhere — it developed within a Canaanite matrix that had been developing for a thousand years.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israel's distinctiveness (the "Israel came from outside" models — conquest, peaceful infiltration, peasant revolt) would resist the implication of deep cultural continuity between Canaanite and Israelite religion. Albright's school, though largely abandoned, would have rejected this framing entirely.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the material culture is continuous, the MEANING of that material culture may have changed radically. A serpent image on a MB IIB scarab may have meant something entirely different from a serpent on an Iron IIB seal, even if the iconography looks similar. Continuity of form ≠ continuity of meaning.

**My assessment:** They're aware of this problem — the constellation method is partly designed to address it by tracking motif COMBINATIONS, not just individual images. The proof is whether they can demonstrate that later Israelites were consciously using and transforming earlier Canaanite iconographic traditions, not just accidentally reproducing them. This connects directly to Smith's convergence thesis (Smith Origins Ch1-3) and the question of how much "Canaanite" religion survived into Israelite Yahwism.

## Claim 7: Using Ugaritic or other northern Syrian texts to interpret Palestinian/Israelite religion is methodologically unsound — the primary context must be local

**Author's claim:** "Conclusions drawn from an interpretation of Bronze Age texts discovered in northern Syria, and the religio-historical hypotheses developed from such evidence, cannot be used uncritically to explain the religious history of Canaan during the second millennium and, though it has happened repeatedly, certainly not to clarify what happened in Israel during the first millennium" (lines 566-571). They note that Ugarit "lies far outside the borders of the province of Canaan, and that the Ugaritic lists... treat a Canaanite trader as a foreigner" (lines 593-595).

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit is geographically and politically distinct from Canaan proper — Ugaritic texts treat Canaanites as foreigners
- The time gap: Ugaritic texts are Late Bronze (14th-13th c.), while the Israelite developments they're used to explain are Iron Age (10th-6th c.)
- The proper method: "compare initially with contemporary evidence from Canaan. When there is a positive correlation... only then can we take the second step and make a hypothetical correlation with Iron Age finds" (lines 572-576)
- Critique of U. Winter's Frau und Göttin (1983): uses predominantly Bronze Age Syrian iconography to interpret Israelite religion

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. They're right about the geographic and chronological problems. But this is also a self-serving argument — it privileges THEIR dataset (Palestinian seals) over the Ugaritic textual corpus that Smith, Cross, and others built their careers on. The Ugaritic texts provide the ONLY detailed mythological narratives from the West Semitic world; discarding them would leave us with almost no textual evidence for the conceptual world that Israelite religion grew out of.

**What's at stake:** If accepted, this methodological principle would fundamentally challenge Smith's entire approach in The Early History of God and The Origins of Biblical Monotheism, both of which rely heavily on Ugaritic parallels. It would also affect Cross's Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic and much of the "myth and ritual" school.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone in the Ugaritic-studies-to-Hebrew-Bible pipeline: Smith, Cross, Day, Clifford, Miller, Coogan. They would argue that Ugaritic religion represents the closest available textual parallel to Israelite religion and that the shared linguistic and cultural heritage justifies the comparison despite geographic distance.

**Alternative reading:** Keel and Uehlinger's position is more nuanced than it first appears. They explicitly say they "do not deny the relevance" of texts from Ebla, Mari, Ugarit, and Emar (lines 561-564). Their objection is to using these texts WITHOUT first establishing local Palestinian correlates. They're calling for methodological rigor, not rejecting comparative work entirely. In practice, they DO use Ugaritic evidence in later chapters — they just insist on diachronic and geographic controls.

**My assessment:** This is the most aggressive methodological claim in the chapter, and the one most likely to generate resistance. It's partly a turf war — iconographers vs. text scholars — but it's also a legitimate point. The Ugaritic-to-Israelite comparison often proceeds as if 500 years and 400 km don't matter. But Keel and Uehlinger's alternative (Palestinian iconography ONLY) has its own limitations: images don't tell us the NAMES of deities, the STORIES told about them, or the THEOLOGY that interpreted them. The ideal approach uses BOTH, as Smith eventually does in his later work, and as Keel and Uehlinger themselves do when epigraphic evidence is available. They know this — they call their approach "complementary" to textual study, not a replacement.

## Chapter I Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Iconographic evidence is the missing piece | VERY HIGH | Already vindicated by the book's influence |
| 2: "Polytheistic" tells us nothing without specifics | HIGH | Only if the specifics turn out to be uninteresting — unlikely given Ch VII |
| 3: Seals as "seismograph" | HIGH | Representativeness questions — were seal owners a narrow elite? |
| 4: Constellation methodology | HIGH | Over-interpretation risk with conventional motifs |
| 5: Revised Iron IIB/C periodization | MEDIUM-HIGH | Depends on whether other archaeologists accept it |
| 6: MB IIB as starting point for cultural continuum | MEDIUM-HIGH | Form continuity ≠ meaning continuity |
| 7: Ugaritic texts should not be primary evidence | MEDIUM | Self-serving; they use Ugaritic too when it suits them |

**Strongest section:** §5 on sources. The case for seal amulets as the primary dataset is quantitatively compelling and methodologically innovative. The critique of earlier scholars (Cook, Ahlström, Dever, Holladay) for ignoring glyptic art is devastating because it's demonstrably true — they DID ignore it.

**Weakest section:** §7-8 on periodization. This is a specialist debate that most readers can't evaluate. The argument may be correct, but it reads as intramural score-settling rather than a contribution to understanding Israelite religion. The periodization matters for the book's internal organization, but the CASE for it should have been in an appendix.

**Most consequential methodological move:** The "constellation" concept (§6). This is what allows them to move from "here are 8,500 seals" to "here is what Israelites believed." Without Assmann's framework, the book would be a catalog, not an argument.

**Tension I'll watch in later chapters:** Their claim that local Palestinian evidence should take priority over Syrian textual evidence (Claim 7) is in tension with their own practice — they WILL use Ugaritic and other non-local evidence when it supports their argument. The question is whether they apply their own methodological strictures consistently or selectively.
