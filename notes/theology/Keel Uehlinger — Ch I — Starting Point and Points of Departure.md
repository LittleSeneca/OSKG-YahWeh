# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter I: Starting Point and Points of Departure

The methodology chapter. Keel and Uehlinger set out the intellectual program for the entire book: a diachronic, iconography-driven reconstruction of Canaanite/Israelite religion from Middle Bronze IIB (c. 1750) through Iron III (c. 450), using 8,500 stamp seals as the primary dataset. They position themselves against text-only approaches (Smith, de Moor) and against archaeologists who ignore glyptic art (Dever, Holladay). The central argument: seal amulets provide a "seismograph" for religious change — more numerous, more precisely dateable, and more geographically specific than any other source type.

---

## Claim 1: The debate about Yahweh's consort cannot be resolved from texts alone — iconographic evidence is the missing piece

**Author's claim:** The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions ("Yahweh... and his Asherah") reignited the question of whether preexilic Israel was polytheistic and whether Yahweh had a divine consort. But "anyone who wants to study actual primary sources must focus on the treasure trove of iconographic and epigraphic documents provided by archaeological research in Palestine/Israel" (line 50-52). Biblical texts are "difficult to interpret historically" and the scholarly debate has been driven by "minority opinion expressed in the religious literature preserved in the Old Testament" rather than material evidence.

**Evidence presented:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions published in 1978 sparked new debate about monotheism's origins (Lang 1981, Haag 1985)
- Feminist theological research intensified the Asherah question — the debate has direct implications for "the status of women in both synagogue and church"
- Scholars like Lang, Weippert, and Knauf assert preexilic polytheism as consensus, but their evidence is almost entirely textual
- The biblical texts are late, polemical, and represent a "minority opinion" — not recoverable popular religion

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is a statement of the problem, not a factual claim. The gap they identify is real and demonstrable — every existing treatment of the Yahweh/Asherah question relies primarily on texts, and the few that use archaeology (Dever, Holladay) ignore seal amulets.

**What's at stake:** If they're right, then the entire existing debate (Smith vs. Dever, monotheism's dating, Asherah's status as goddess vs. symbol) has been conducted with one hand tied behind its back. The iconographic evidence could shift every major conclusion.

**Who disagrees:** Not exactly "disagreement" — more like neglect. Smith (1990, 2001) acknowledges the "missing puzzle pieces" but doesn't systematically incorporate iconography. Dever uses SOME archaeological evidence (figurines, cult stands) but not seal amulets systematically. Tigay (1986) used onomastic evidence and mentioned iconography in an appendix — they see their work as an expansion of his approach.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that iconography is MORE ambiguous than texts, not less — a seal image doesn't come with a caption explaining what the wearer believed. Keel and Uehlinger address this in §6 with their constellation methodology.

**My assessment:** This is the single strongest framing move in the book. They don't just say "we have new data"; they say "the existing debate is structurally incomplete because it's missing an entire category of evidence." This framing has been vindicated — the book is now cited 71 times in our project notes precisely because it fills this gap.

## Claim 2: Preexilic Israel was polytheistic, but that tells us almost nothing — the real question is WHICH gods, WHERE, WHEN, and in WHAT RELATION to Yahweh

**Author's claim:** "Recent Old Testament research... generally assumes that the religion of preexilic Israel and Judah is to be characterized as thoroughly polytheistic." They quote Lang, Knauf, and Weippert establishing the consensus. But they immediately problematize it: "The texts cited by Weippert (Psalms 82, 89; Deut 32:8f.) do in fact assume that other gods existed in addition to Yahweh. But the texts say nothing about whether these deities were venerated in any way in Israel and Judah." The dating problem is severe — "widespread uncertainty about how to date the biblical texts" means "such textual material is of little value for providing good evidence when one tries to reconstruct the religion of Israel and Judah for the period of the monarchy."

**Evidence presented:**
- Lang (1981, 1983): preexilic Israel had a triad of protector god, national god (Yahweh), and functional deities
- Knauf (1988): different triads at Bethel, Jerusalem, Hebron
- Weippert (1990): even "official" Jerusalem theology was polytheistic; Yahweh had a goddess partner
- Counterpoint: all of these are deductions from texts — none from stratified archaeological evidence
- Tigay (1986) as methodological model: "external controls" from archaeology to balance the "notorious subjectivity" of dating texts

**Confidence:** HIGH that they're right about the consensus. HIGH that they're right about the insufficiency of textual evidence alone. The dating crisis in Hebrew Bible scholarship is real and they don't overstate it.

**What's at stake:** If all we can say is "preexilic Israel was polytheistic" without specifying which gods, in which regions, in which periods, and in what relationship to Yahweh, then the statement is nearly contentless. Their entire chronological survey is designed to answer these specific questions.

**Who disagrees:** Maximalists who trust the biblical chronology would push back on the dating skepticism. Dever has argued that the biblical texts contain recoverable historical memory even if late. Smith explicitly builds his case FROM the texts (Ugaritic and biblical) rather than archaeological evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical texts, even if late, may preserve genuine preexilic traditions through oral transmission. Keel and Uehlinger's skepticism about texts could lead them to underweight genuinely ancient material embedded in late compositions.

**My assessment:** Their critique is measured. They don't reject textual evidence — they integrate epigraphic finds (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom) throughout the book. They're saying texts ALONE can't do the job, not that texts are worthless.

## Claim 3: Seal amulets are the single best source for reconstructing religious history — they function as a "seismograph" for religious change

**Author's claim:** "Not only because of their sheer number but also because of their importance, seal amulets far outshine all other kinds of image-bearing artifacts in value, being even more valuable than inscriptional evidence. Since they are preserved in relatively comparable quantities for all periods, they can virtually serve as the standard by which religious history is documented, particularly because they are more or less public artifacts and can thus serve as a sensitive seismograph to detect subtle shifts in religious history."

**Evidence presented:**
- The Fribourg corpus: approximately 8,500 stamp seals from legal excavations in Palestine/Israel
- Seals survive in comparable quantities across all periods (unlike temples, bronze statues, or texts)
- Seals are "more or less public artifacts" — worn, traded, used by broad populations — not just elite productions
- Different media tell different stories: cylinder seals allow complex "constellations," stamp seals simplify but can still combine motifs, isolated statues lose their iconographic context

**Confidence:** HIGH. The quantitative argument is strong. The corpus size (8,500) dwarfs every other artifact category for religious imagery.

**What's at stake:** If seals are as representative as they claim, then shifts in seal iconography ARE shifts in popular religious mentality — not just elite theological fashion.

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the numbers, but one could question representativeness. Seals were owned by the literate/elite class, not the entire population. Dever's figurines (3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines) might be MORE representative of popular religion than seals.

**Alternative reading:** Seals reflect the iconographic choices of seal-cutters and their elite patrons — they may lag behind or diverge from popular belief. The "public" character they claim could be overstated.

**My assessment:** The seismograph metaphor is apt but not proven in this chapter — it depends on whether the diachronic patterns they identify in chapters II-X correlate with known historical developments. The proof is in the execution.

## Claim 4: The methodology requires analyzing "constellations" of motifs, not isolated images — Assmann's iconicity theory adapted to Levantine material

**Author's claim:** Building on Jan Assmann, they argue that iconographic interpretation must focus on "constellations" — recurring combinations of motifs that represent stable complexes of ideas — rather than hunting for specific myths behind images. "Mythic texts are nothing other than icons (or else constellations) that have been presented in story form." The battle-against-the-dragon constellation appears with different divine names (Baal/Yamm, Marduk/Tiamat, Yahweh/Leviathan) but the underlying ICON is constant.

**Evidence presented:**
- Assmann's distinction between "myth" (narrative) and "constellation" (pre-narrative complex of ideas)
- The "iconic constancy" principle: "The myth owes its 'iconic constancy' to a few basic constellations"
- Examples: divine couple = fertility; serpent/dragon combat = order vs. chaos; nursing mother = divine provision
- Different image-bearing media have different capacities

**Confidence:** HIGH. Assmann's theory is well-established in Egyptology. The adaptation to Levantine material is original but methodologically sound.

**What's at stake:** This is the theoretical heart of the book. Without the constellation method, they're just cataloguing images. With it, they can make claims about religious CONCEPTS from visual patterns.

**Who disagrees:** Text-focused scholars might argue that this method over-interprets visual evidence — that a winged sun disk on a seal doesn't necessarily mean the owner "worshipped" a solar deity.

**Alternative reading:** Some images may be purely conventional — seal-cutters reproducing standard patterns without theological investment.

**My assessment:** The constellation method is Keel and Uehlinger's most important methodological contribution. It's been widely adopted in the field.

## Claim 5: The standard archaeological periodization is wrong — Iron IIB/C break must be dated to the late 8th century (Assyrian conquest), not c. 850

**Author's claim:** "If one considers the artistic works, as will be done here, it will seem not only plausible but unavoidable that the break between Iron Age IIB and C must be dated to the second half of the eighth century, when Palestine came increasingly under the direct political influence of the great Assyrian superpower." They critique H. Weippert's periodization, which ends Iron IIB at c. 850 (end of Omri dynasty), based solely on architectural evidence.

**Evidence presented:**
- H. Weippert's 1988 Handbuch uses ONLY architectural evidence, producing an Iron IIB (c. 925-850) that leaves almost no small artifacts
- Assyrian conquest introduced massive cultural changes: pottery types shift, settlement patterns change, iconography transforms
- Their alternative: Iron IIB = 925-700 (Phoenician/Egyptian orientation), Iron IIC = 700-600 (Assyrian/Aramean influence)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The argument from iconographic evidence makes intuitive sense but depends on data presented in chapters VII-VIII.

**What's at stake:** If they're right, then religious developments of the 8th century must be understood against an ASSYRIAN backdrop, not just internal Israelite politics.

**Who disagrees:** H. Weippert explicitly. The traditional scheme (Iron IIA = United Monarchy, IIB = Divided Monarchy, IIC = Assyrian period) has the advantage of aligning with the biblical narrative.

**My assessment:** This is an intramural archaeological debate. Their decision has real consequences for the book's narrative.

---

## Claim 6: The survey begins with Middle Bronze IIB because that's when a continuous cultural tradition begins in Palestine

**Author's claim:** "One can justify beginning our survey at this point, when the urban culture in Palestine begins to flourish, because from this point on we can deal with a cultural continuum in Palestine that extends all the way to the time of the emergence of the Hebrew Bible."

**Evidence presented:**
- Early Bronze IV/MB I (2200-2000): "Iconographic evidence is virtually nonexistent"
- MB IIA (2000-1750): "limited generally to Egyptian imports"
- MB IIB (from 1750): Syrian influences arrive, indigenous production begins, Hyksos period creates Canaanite-Egyptian interchange
- Finkelstein's argument: the hill country population didn't disappear during Late Bronze — they shifted to nomadism and resettled in Iron I

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The archaeological rationale is sound. The "cultural continuum" claim about ethnic continuity is more debatable.

**What's at stake:** This justifies the entire chronological scope of the book and implies Israelite religion didn't emerge from nowhere — it developed within a Canaanite matrix.

**My assessment:** They're aware of the form-continuity vs. meaning-continuity problem. The constellation method is partly designed to address it.

---

## Claim 7: Using Ugaritic or other northern Syrian texts to interpret Palestinian/Israelite religion is methodologically unsound — the primary context must be local

**Author's claim:** "Conclusions drawn from an interpretation of Bronze Age texts discovered in northern Syria... cannot be used uncritically to explain the religious history of Canaan during the second millennium and... certainly not to clarify what happened in Israel during the first millennium." Ugarit "lies far outside the borders of the province of Canaan."

**Evidence presented:**
- Ugarit is geographically and politically distinct from Canaan proper
- The time gap: Ugaritic texts are Late Bronze (14th-13th c.), while Israelite developments are Iron Age (10th-6th c.)
- The proper method: "compare initially with contemporary evidence from Canaan. When there is a positive correlation... only then can we take the second step"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. They're right about the geographic and chronological problems. But this is also self-serving — it privileges THEIR dataset over the Ugaritic textual corpus that Smith, Cross, and others built their careers on.

**What's at stake:** If accepted, this would fundamentally challenge Smith's and Cross's methodological foundations.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone in the Ugaritic-studies-to-Hebrew-Bible pipeline: Smith, Cross, Day, Clifford, Miller.

**My assessment:** Their position is more nuanced than it appears. They explicitly "do not deny the relevance" of texts from Ebla, Mari, Ugarit. Their objection is to using these texts WITHOUT first establishing local Palestinian correlates. In practice, they DO use Ugaritic evidence — they just insist on diachronic and geographic controls.

## Chapter I Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Iconographic evidence is the missing piece | VERY HIGH | Already vindicated by the book's influence |
| 2: "Polytheistic" tells us nothing without specifics | HIGH | Only if the specifics turn out to be uninteresting |
| 3: Seals as "seismograph" | HIGH | Representativeness questions |
| 4: Constellation methodology | HIGH | Over-interpretation risk with conventional motifs |
| 5: Revised Iron IIB/C periodization | MEDIUM-HIGH | Depends on whether other archaeologists accept it |
| 6: MB IIB as starting point | MEDIUM-HIGH | Form continuity ≠ meaning continuity |
| 7: Ugaritic texts should not be primary evidence | MEDIUM | Self-serving; they use Ugaritic too |

**Strongest section:** §5 on sources. The case for seal amulets as the primary dataset is quantitatively compelling and methodologically innovative.

**Weakest section:** §§7-8 on periodization. This is a specialist debate that most readers can't evaluate.

**Most consequential methodological move:** The "constellation" concept (§6). This is what allows them to move from "here are 8,500 seals" to "here is what Israelites believed."
