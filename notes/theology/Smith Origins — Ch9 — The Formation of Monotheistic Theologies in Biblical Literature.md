# Smith — Chapter 9: The Formation of Monotheistic Theologies in Biblical Literature

Smith examines three genres where monotheistic theology reshaped older mythic material: priestly creation (Genesis 1), wisdom literature (Proverbs 1-9), and apocalyptic (Daniel 7). Each represents a different social location replacing the old royal theology: priesthood replaces monarchy, wisdom scribes replace goddess imagery, and apocalyptic replaces the human king with a persecuted community. The chapter closes with a discussion of the "demise of myth" — arguing that myth didn't disappear; it was transformed, muted, and sometimes censored by priestly tradents.

---

## Claim 1: Genesis 1 replaces the royal conflict model of creation with a priestly cosmos-as-sanctuary model

**Author's claim:** "Genesis 1 does not use conflict as the main element in its vision of the cosmos and the place of humanity in it. Instead, the priestly holiness of time and space overshadows the component of conflict."

**Evidence presented:**
- The old model: cosmos as stage for divine conflict. Enuma Elish: Marduk defeats Tiamat, creates cosmos from her carcass, reigns from Babylon. Royal psalms: Yahweh's conflict with cosmic enemies parallels the king's conflict with earthly enemies.
- Genesis 1 removes the conflict: "the audience expects the conflict, as the 'mighty wind'... hovers over the face of the cosmic waters. Rather than conflict, Genesis 1 has God speak or make, and creation happens. With but a word, without conflict, God effects the opening of creation."
- Three transformations of royal theology: (1) conflict eliminated — "the lack of any conflict, or even any personification of the cosmic oceans or waters, is designed to heighten the picture of a powerful God"; (2) royal rule democratized — "the king on earth is not the Israelite king but all humanity" (Gen 1:28). The "image of God" was originally a royal concept (Egyptian: king as image of deity); Genesis 1 applies it to all humanity; (3) priestly imprint — Sabbath (divine rest on day 7), feast-days (lights mark sacred times, Gen 1:14), kashrut foreshadowed (division of realms and animals).
- "Cosmos as sanctuary": the Temple mirrors the cosmos and the cosmos mirrors the Temple. Psalm 78:69: "He built his sanctuary like the high heavens." Psalm 150:1: sanctuary and sky as parallel terms. "Genesis 1's delineation of realms in the universe according to animals may evoke temple practice and in turn a vision of the world as a kind of temple."
- "Monotheistic poetics" (term introduced in Ch 1): tanninim placed within created order (Gen 1:21), not before it. Sun and moon called "greater light" and "lesser light" — traditional titles but now created objects, not deities. "Here ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left."
- Inversion of Ezekiel 1:26: "Ezekiel 1:26 conveys the prophet's vision of the divine with the language of 'image' of the human person... whereas Genesis 1:26-28 presents a vision of the human person in the likeness of the divine." Anthropomorphism is not eliminated but redirected — God is not in human image; humans are in God's image.
- Dating: "Genesis 1 has often been dated to the exilic or post-exilic period." "The politics of creation have changed. There is still a king in this world, but it is the King of Kings... There is no single royal agent on earth whose human foes mirror the cosmic foes of the divine king."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The comparison with Enuma Elish and royal psalms is well-established. The "cosmos as sanctuary" reading builds on Levenson and others.

**What's at stake:** If Genesis 1 is priestly theology replacing royal theology, then creation "out of nothing" or "by word alone" is not an abstract philosophical doctrine — it's a political-theological move that eliminates divine rivals. The cosmos has only one power because Israel has only one legitimate authority (priesthood, not monarchy). Monotheism and theocracy are linked.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Genesis 1 earlier (monarchic period). Those who read Genesis 1 as primarily anti-Babylonian polemic (contra Enuma Elish) rather than internal Israelite priestly reformulation.

**Alternative reading:** Genesis 1's elimination of conflict could be read as affirming God's absolute sovereignty rather than polemic against other gods. The "greater light/lesser light" language might be natural astronomical terminology, not anti-polytheistic.

**My assessment:** The strongest part is the "image of God" democratization — the royal concept applied to all humanity. This is a genuinely important observation. The "cosmos as sanctuary" reading is convincing but depends on the priestly dating of Genesis 1, which is likely but not certain.

## Claim 2: Personified Wisdom in Proverbs 1-9 is a monotheistic replacement for Asherah — absorbing her imagery into a non-divine female figure

**Author's claim:** "The female figure of Wisdom functions as an image of 'the divine patroness of the Israelite school system.'" Wisdom may represent "a move to 'monotheize' the imagery of the Asherah."

**Evidence presented:**
- Wisdom described as "tree of life" (Prov 3:18) — recalls the tree of the asherah. "Like the symbol of the asherah, Wisdom is a female figure, providing life and nurturing."
- The chiasm in Proverbs 3:13-18 uses 'ašrê ("happy") and mĕ'uššār ("made happy") — "perhaps allude to the asherah, the tree symbolizing life and well-being."
- B. Lang: Wisdom as "divine patroness of the Israelite school system." If Proverbs is Levitical (verbal links with Deuteronomy), Wisdom may be patron of Levitical teaching/writing duties.
- "Perhaps both figures [Wisdom and personified Jerusalem] represent substitutions for divine female figures." Von Rad: Wisdom in Prov 9:1-9 as "a contrast and a defence against" goddess customs.
- "The language of Jerusalem and Wisdom personified may have usurped imagery associated generally with goddesses, perhaps specifically with the asherah... and applied it to the figure of Wisdom as a 'counter-advertisement' to the cults of Astarte and the Queen of Heaven."
- This is an example of NEW myth created in a monotheistic context — "new myths may in part represent replacements in service to monotheistic representations of divinity."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Wisdom-Asherah connection is plausible but inferential. The verbal link between 'ašrê and 'ăšērâ is phonetically interesting but not determinative.

**What's at stake:** If Wisdom is a replacement for Asherah, this is convergence at work in literature — not absorbing the goddess into Yahweh (male) but displacing her onto a personified attribute. The goddess doesn't disappear; she becomes Lady Wisdom. This is a more subtle form of monotheization than the priestly elimination of divine enemies.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Wisdom as an independent Israelite development without goddess background. Those who read the 'ašrê/'ăšērâ link as coincidental.

**Alternative reading:** Wisdom could be a hypostasis of Yahweh (like the divine Name or Glory) rather than a replacement for a goddess. She might reflect scribal self-representation (Wisdom as patron of scribes) rather than theological polemic.

**My assessment:** The Wisdom-Asherah connection is suggestive but Smith doesn't push it too hard — he presents it as hypothesis. The most convincing part is the structural parallel: a female figure associated with trees, life, and nurturing appears in exactly the symbolic slot that Asherah once occupied. Whether the authors CONSCIOUSLY intended Wisdom as Asherah's replacement is unknowable, but the functional displacement is real.

## Claim 3: Apocalyptic (Daniel 7) preserves the old conflict mythology but removes the human king — the persecuted community replaces the monarch

**Author's claim:** "Post-exilic apocalyptic preserved the conflictual nature of the forces in the cosmos but with the human king removed from the equation."

**Evidence presented:**
- Daniel 7: four beasts rising from the cosmic sea — "reminiscent of the four empires on earth." The human counterpart to Yahweh is "no human king but a persecuted community that can take solace only in divine help."
- Isaiah 27:1: future defeat of Leviathan as end-times salvation.
- "The political link between these beasts and world empires was no late invention but echoed the mirroring of divine and human kings and the cosmic and human enemies known already in the second millennium."
- Like Genesis 1, Daniel 7 "magnifies Judah's deity over the whole cosmos, despite what takes place in Judah's historical experience."
- "Both Ugaritic and Israelite royal and scribal-priestly groups constituted the primary literary preservers of mythic material, and they were both in a position to use ancient mythic material to suit their own ends."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The apocalyptic reuse of conflict mythology is well-established in scholarship.

**What's at stake:** Apocalyptic is monotheism's most politically radical genre — it preserves the conflict structure of polytheistic myth but removes all divine allies, leaving only Yahweh and a human community with no political power. The old royal theology said: divine king fights cosmic enemies, human king fights earthly enemies. Apocalyptic says: divine king fights ALL enemies; the human community just waits.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see apocalyptic as influenced by Persian dualism rather than as development of West Semitic mythology. Smith rejects this (discussed in Ch 8).

**Alternative reading:** Apocalyptic's four-beast scheme might draw more on Mesopotamian omen traditions than on West Semitic conflict mythology. Daniel 7's "one like a son of man" might represent an angelic figure (Michael) rather than the community.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading of apocalyptic as transformed royal theology is convincing and economical. It explains both the continuity (conflict structure) and the transformation (no human king). The three genres (priestly creation, wisdom, apocalyptic) map neatly onto the three social locations that replaced the monarchy: priesthood, scribal school, and persecuted community.

## Claim 4: Biblical myth didn't "die" — it was transformed, muted, and selectively censored by priestly tradents

**Author's claim:** "Mythic imagery so suffuses Israelite culture that it survived in genres involving discourse about Israel's deity." The absence of full-scale myth (like the Baal Cycle) from the Bible is due to two stages of muting anthropomorphism, priestly censorship, and scribal selection — not to Israel's original aniconic purity.

**Evidence presented:**
- Two stages: (1) "at the outset of known Israelite texts" — Yahweh is less anthropomorphized than Baal even in the oldest poetry. "Baal's conflict with Yamm (CAT 1.2 IV) is discernibly more anthropomorphic than any biblical descriptions of Yahweh's conflict." Divine theriomorphism (gods as animals) is nearly absent in Israel (only vestigial: Num 24:8, Ps 132:2).
- (2) "eighth to sixth centuries" — Deuteronomy 4:12, 15: Yahweh has no "form" (tĕmûnâ). Ezekiel 1 deliberately reduces anthropomorphism. "The language of divine 'form' is found explicitly in Psalm 17:15, and it has been traced to the use of the divine 'form' in the story of Baal's battle against Yamm (CAT 1.2 IV 26)."
- Three reasons for myth's reduction: (1) monolatry concerns — inclusion of other divine images threatened exclusive Yahwism; (2) priestly censorship — "The different priestly lines during this period found their own primary images of Yahweh... sufficiently incompatible with some of the older images and chose not to preserve them, thereby functionally censoring them." Especially divine sex and death — "the presentation of Yahweh generally as sexless and unrelated to the realm of death was apparently produced by a priesthood who viewed this deity as fully removed from realms of impurity, especially sex and death"; (3) scribalism — Yahweh as divine scribe (Exod 31:18) may reflect scribal self-projection.
- But myth survived: "many Second Temple and rabbinic texts, especially apocalyptic literature, indicate the popularity of myth throughout the history of ancient Israel."
- New myths were created: personified Wisdom, personified Jerusalem (Ezekiel 16, 23). "Hence, new myths may in part represent replacements in service to monotheistic representations of divinity."
- Israel's supposedly "anti-Canaanite" and original monotheistic stance is not the explanation. "These changes largely represent internal Israelite developments."
- The chapter's final line: "biblical monotheism constituted part of Israelite's own foundational myth comparable in some respects to Canaan's own myths."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the descriptive claim (myth survives in transformed form). MEDIUM-HIGH for the censorship model. The two-stage model is somewhat schematic.

**What's at stake:** If myth was actively suppressed rather than never existing, the Bible's silence about divine sex, divine death, and divine family is not evidence of original aniconism — it's evidence of successful priestly editing. The absence of evidence IS evidence of suppression.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israel's aniconism and anti-mythological stance as original features. Those who attribute the lack of full-scale myth to genre preferences rather than theological censorship. Talmon: Pentateuch's "normative character" excluded epic.

**Alternative reading:** The lack of full-scale myth might reflect Israel's different literary culture (preference for prose history over poetic myth) rather than theological censorship. Other ANE cultures also didn't produce Baal Cycle equivalents — the Ugaritic corpus might be exceptional, not Israel's lack of it.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument for priestly censorship is the most important claim in this section. It's plausible but impossible to prove — we can't examine what was censored. The two-stage model is useful as a framework but the dating of individual texts is too uncertain for precision. The most convincing point is the specific content that WAS eliminated (divine sex, divine death) — these map precisely onto the priestly holiness concerns identified in Ch 5. That convergence of evidence (what's missing + what the priests cared about) makes the censorship model more than speculation.

## Chapter 9 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Genesis 1 as priestly cosmos-as-sanctuary | HIGH | Dating of Genesis 1 |
| 2: Wisdom as Asherah replacement | MEDIUM | Inferential; 'ašrê/'ăšērâ link thin |
| 3: Apocalyptic as transformed royal theology | HIGH | Persian influence alternative |
| 4: Myth transformed, not eliminated; priestly censorship | HIGH (transformation) / MEDIUM-HIGH (censorship) | Censorship unprovable |

**Strongest section:** Section 1 (Genesis 1). The three transformations of royal theology (no conflict, democratized rule, priestly holiness) are clearly demonstrated and the "image of God" democratization is a genuinely important observation.

**Weakest section:** Section 2 (Wisdom). Too brief; the Wisdom-Asherah connection needs more development than it receives.

**Overall contribution:** This chapter shows that monotheism wasn't just a set of statements ("there is no other god") — it was a constructive theological project that produced NEW myths, NEW genres, and NEW social locations for divine authority. Monotheism didn't just subtract gods; it reorganized the entire symbolic universe.

**Cross-reference:** The "monotheistic poetics" of Genesis 1 was first developed in Ch 1. The Wisdom-Asherah connection ties back to Ch 4's discussion of the Asherah problem. The apocalyptic discussion leads into Ch 10 (Second Isaiah as the most developed monotheistic rhetoric).
