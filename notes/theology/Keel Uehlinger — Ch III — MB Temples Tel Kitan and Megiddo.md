# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter III: The Iconography of the Middle Bronze Age Temples of Tel Kitan and Megiddo

The first temple-iconography chapter. Keel and Uehlinger examine two MB IIB cult sites — the village sanctuary at Tel Kitan (ca. 1750–1650) and the sacral area at Megiddo (Strata XIII–X, ca. 1800–1500) — to establish what the earliest temple iconography in Palestine actually looked like. The chapter covers the weather god and his consort, the falcon-headed figure as a syncretic Canaanite-Egyptian royal deity, the ruler with male and female worshipers, erotic couple imagery, and a summary that challenges the notion of a timeless "Canaanite religion." Key methodological move: the earliest Palestinian temple iconography is not exclusively male — the goddess dominates the figurine corpus at Megiddo (16 female to 6 male), and a female stele appears among the massebahs at Tel Kitan. The chapter also introduces the "jasper group" of seals (1720–1650) as a crucial corpus for tracking the merger of Egyptian and Near Eastern iconographic traditions.

---

## Claim 1: The Tel Kitan female stele proves that massebahs / standing stones are not necessarily male deities — the goddess could be represented in this medium too

**Author's claim:** "What was suggested concerning the row of stelae at Gezer can be confirmed now at this site: stelae made of stone are not necessarily images of male deities. The characteristics depicted on the figure on this stele are clear. The gender is not identified by means of a triangle and a vertical line, like the Middle Bronze Age sheet-gold figures from Gezer, but is represented more realistically by means of sculpted genital lips" (lines 16-21).

**Evidence presented:**
- Tel Kitan (Tell Musa), ca. 15 km south of the Sea of Gennesareth, excavated 1975–1977, dated to first phase of MB IIB (ca. 1750–1650)
- A building (6.90 × 5.50 m) with a row of ~10 small massebahs made of river stone on a stone foundation, 5 m in front of it; two larger massebahs placed before the row
- One stele in the main row was carved — in "very rough fashion" — into the form of a naked woman, ca. 45 cm high (now at Museum of Nir David, Beth-Shean)
- The breasts are "hardly indicated at all" and arms are "little more than two small stumps"; unclear whether she held her breasts
- Eisenberg (1977) published the excavation

**Confidence:** HIGH. The anatomical description is specific and unambiguous — "sculpted genital lips" leaves no room for gender ambiguity. This is an archaeological fact, not an interpretation.

**What's at stake:** This directly challenges the widespread assumption — both scholarly and popular — that massebahs were always male, always representations of the god (often Baal or Yahweh), and that the aniconic standing stone tradition is inherently masculine. If a goddess could be represented as a massebah, then the entire category "standing stone = male deity" collapses. This has downstream implications for interpreting the massebahs condemned in the Hebrew Bible and those found at Israelite sites like Arad.

**Who disagrees:** The text doesn't name specific opponents, but the claim is framed as a correction to a prior assumption. The reference to Gezer suggests that earlier interpreters had treated all stelae as male. Scholars who maintain that massebahs were exclusively male cult objects (e.g., Mettinger's work on aniconism) would need to account for this counterexample.

**Alternative reading:** The stele is rudimentary — "very rough fashion" — and could be an anomaly rather than representative. One female stele among thousands of massebahs doesn't overturn the general pattern. It might represent a local variant rather than a widespread practice.

**My assessment:** The argument is modest — they're not claiming female massebahs were common, only that the category is not inherently male. This is a useful corrective. The real significance is that it shows the goddess had a place even in the most "primitive" cultic installations, not just in the more "sophisticated" metal figurines and seals. The goddess was present across the full spectrum of cultic media — stone, metal, and glyptic.

## Claim 2: At Megiddo, female deity figurines dramatically outnumber male ones (16:6), and the weather god appears primarily in association with fertility, not battle

**Author's claim:** "Unlike at Nahariyah, Gezer, and the settlement of Tel Kitan, male deities are here as well, but they are by far in the minority. Of the 22 figures, 16 are female deities and only six are male (Negbi 1976, 131)" (lines 53-55). On the weather god: "Where he does appear, his relationship with fertility is accentuated, not his aggressive nature; that aspect will become more important during the Late Bronze Age. He does in fact raise his right hand but he holds no weapon, so that the gesture depicts more likely a victory rather than a battle. In his other hand, stretched out in front of himself, the deity holds a branch or a blossom" (lines 142-148).

**Evidence presented:**
- Megiddo sacral area (Area BB): open-air sanctuary in late MB IIA (ca. 1800), restructured in early MB IIB (Strata XII–XI) into a broadroom temple (5.5 × 3 m) with massebahs and altar podium; replaced near end of MB IIB (Stratum X) by a rectangular temple with thick walls, north-facing entrance, and southern niche
- A mold and 21 metal figurines found in MB levels of the sacral area, plus one from Area CC = 22 total
- Three naked female sheet-bronze figures (1800–1700) similar to Nahariyah and Gezer types (Loud 1948, Negbi 1976)
- Later MB IIB (1700–1500): cast female figures wearing high pointed head covering, holding both breasts (illuss. 27a-b)
- Male figures in "Anatolian posture" with weapons appear as early as MB IIA (Loud 1948, Negbi 1976); a limestone casting mold from Stratum XIIIB (1800–1750) shows mass production of weapon-wielding gods
- The cylinder seal from Megiddo (illus. 30): weather god with club, axe, and rope tied around a reclining bull; goddess facing him, pushing clothing aside with one hand; three bull heads emphasizing connection
- On scarabs: weather god holds branch/blossom, not weapon — victory gesture, not battle posture

**Confidence:** HIGH. The figurine count is a direct archaeological datum from Negbi's authoritative 1976 corpus. The observation about fertility emphasis on scarabs is an interpretive claim about iconographic emphasis, but it's well-supported by the visual evidence they reference.

**What's at stake:** This establishes the MB IIB baseline: the goddess is numerically dominant in temple deposits, and even the weather god (who will later become the aggressive warrior Baal of the Ugaritic texts) is depicted primarily as a fertility/vegetation deity. This challenges the assumption — common in text-based reconstructions — that the Canaanite pantheon was always headed by an aggressive male warrior god. The MB IIB evidence suggests a different religious configuration: goddess-centered, with the male deity in a subordinate and fertility-oriented role.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who privilege the Ugaritic textual corpus (Late Bronze, 14th–13th c.) and project its theology backward onto MB IIB Palestine would find this picture unfamiliar. The Ugaritic Baal cycle emphasizes Baal's kingship and warrior aspects; Keel and Uehlinger are suggesting this emphasis is a Late Bronze development, not the original Canaanite pattern.

**Alternative reading:** The figurine count may reflect differential preservation or deposition practices rather than actual cultic emphasis. Female figurines might have been deposited more frequently as votives, while male deity worship took other forms (e.g., massebahs, animal sacrifice) that leave no figurine trace. The scarab evidence about the weather god's peaceful aspect could reflect Egyptian artistic conventions (which avoided depicting foreign gods as threatening) rather than Canaanite theology.

**My assessment:** The 16:6 ratio is striking and hard to explain away. Combined with the evidence from Chapter II (the "Naked Goddess" on seals and the goddess on the Lachish ewer, the Gezer and Nahariyah figurines), it builds a cumulative case that MB IIB religion was goddess-prominent. The observation about the weather god's changing iconography (fertility in MB, warrior in LB) is a classic Keel-Uehlinger move: tracking a single deity's iconographic evolution across periods to show how religious concepts shifted. This directly supports their "seismograph" methodology from Chapter I.

## Claim 3: The falcon-headed figure on MB IIB scarabs represents a deliberate syncretism — the Canaanite royal/fertility god identified with Egyptian Horus

**Author's claim:** "The self-confident Canaanites who lived at the end of the eighteenth century and during the seventeenth and sixteenth centuries associated their royal god, their fertility god, with the chief Egyptian royal god, so they placed a branch or blossom scepter into the hand of their own royal god, their own fertility god" (lines 198-201). "We are encountering a merging of attributes from the Egyptian royal god with those of the Canaanite royal god" (lines 195-197).

**Evidence presented:**
- The falcon-headed figure appears "much more frequently than the weather god on Middle Bronze Age IIB scarabs" (lines 151-152)
- German scholars identified it as Canaanite Horon; English-speaking scholars as Egyptian Re — both sides missed the iconographic tradition (lines 153-159)
- The "jasper group" of seals (1720–1650): green jasper and similar hard stones, produced in Byblos or south of it, characterized by slender elongated figures, males worshiping and being worshiped, rudimentary hieroglyphs (lines 169-177)
- On Old Syrian cylinder seals, the falcon-headed figure wears the Egyptian double crown — clearly the royal god, probably Horus (lines 166-168)
- On scarabs, the figure appears WITHOUT the double crown but WITH a branch or blossom scepter — a non-Egyptian element adapted from the Canaanite weather god (lines 191-196)
- The Hyksos-era scarab from Tell el-Dab‘a shows a pharaoh holding a club AND a branch — "quite unlike what the Egyptians would have included" (lines 206-211)
- The falcon-headed figure victorious over the crocodile (illuss. 34a-b): crocodile is an Egyptian motif, but Egyptians were hesitant to depict it as conquered; in Canaan, Baal's victory over Litanu/Leviathan may have encouraged this portrayal (lines 213-225)
- Horus falcon with outstretched wings on jasper-group scarabs and gold jewelry from Byblos and Tell el-Ajjul — "the kings of Byblos fancied themselves... as if they were in fact little pharaohs" (lines 226-237, quoting M. Chéhab)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The iconographic argument — tracing motifs across cylinder seals and scarabs, noting the addition of the branch to the falcon-headed figure — is methodologically sound. But the identity of the Canaanite deity being syncretized remains somewhat speculative: is it the weather god? A distinct royal god? Horon? The authors don't pin this down definitively.

**What's at stake:** This is the earliest documented case of what will become a recurring pattern in Israelite religion: the selective appropriation of Egyptian royal/divine iconography to express Canaanite (and later Israelite) theological concepts. The falcon-headed figure prefigures the later absorption of solar imagery into Yahwism (Chapters VII–VIII). If Canaanite kings in MB IIB could identify their royal god with Horus, then the later identification of Yahweh with solar attributes (winged disk, falcon, uraeus) is not an innovation but a continuation of a well-established Canaanite practice of iconographic syncretism.

**Who disagrees:** The German vs. English-speaking scholarly divide they mention is real. Scholars who emphasize the Egyptian borrowing (Re identification) would resist the idea that the Canaanite element is primary. Scholars who emphasize the Canaanite distinctiveness (Horon identification) would resist the idea that Egyptian identification was deliberate rather than superficial decoration.

**Alternative reading:** The falcon-headed figure might not represent a "merging" of deities at all. Seal-cutters might have simply combined visually impressive motifs from different traditions without any theological intent. The branch could be a misunderstood Egyptian motif rather than a deliberate Canaanite addition. The Hyksos period was one of intense cultural mixing; what looks like "syncretism" might just be eclectic borrowing.

**My assessment:** This is the most complex and original argument in the chapter. The authors deftly navigate the German/English scholarly divide by moving to a third position: the figure is BOTH Egyptian and Canaanite, deliberately so. Their use of the jasper group as a coherent corpus is methodologically sophisticated — by identifying a specific workshop tradition, they can track how a motif was modified as it moved from cylinder seals (elite, Syrian-influenced) to scarabs (broader, Palestinian). The Tell el-Dab‘a scarab with pharaoh + branch is a compelling piece of evidence for the Hyksos context. My main reservation: the argument assumes that the falcon-headed figure represents a SINGLE deity that was being syncretized, when it could represent different deities in different contexts. The authors acknowledge this ambiguity implicitly by not settling the Horon-vs-Re-vs-weather god question.

## Claim 4: The ruler wearing the garment with thick fringes represents the powerful Canaanite city-state kings who functioned as mediating figures between divine and earthly realms — perhaps deified after death

**Author's claim:** "Schroer has correctly determined that the depiction of the ruler who is wearing the garment with thick fringes and appears all alone provides an indication of the strong position that the Canaanite city-state kings assumed toward the end of the Thirteenth Dynasty (ca. 1700–1650). Politically, this led to the founding of the Hyksos Dynasty in Egypt. Religiously, the sovereigns appear as some sort of mediating figures who bridge the gap between the divine and earthly worlds. Perhaps they were deified after they died" (lines 260-267).

**Evidence presented:**
- The ruler with the "garment with thick fringes" is the dominant figure on jasper-group cylinder seals — centrally positioned (lines 244-247)
- On Old Syrian cylinder seals (18th c.), the garment is worn by both worshipers and the worshiped — they appear side by side on a Hazor piece found in a 13th-century context (lines 248-252)
- In early MB IIB, the Syrian ruler appears often on steatite scarabs. A scarab from Barqai (ca. 1650) is the only piece where the ruler wears BOTH the fringed garment AND the tall Syrian cap (lines 253-260)
- The ruler appears alone — which Schroer interprets as evidence of the strong position of city-state kings (lines 260-263)
- "The use of their images on seal amulets should thus come as no surprise" — implying the ruler-image functioned apotropaically, like a deity-image (line 267-268)
- One unique piece shows an enemy prostrate under the sovereign's throne — "reminds one of the enemies that are under the royal lion" (lines 269-271, cross-referencing illuss. 5a-b from Ch II)
- Fragments of stelae from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem portray the ruler in raised relief — "perhaps these stelae were used in the cultic worship of the person who is depicted" (lines 280-282)
- On jasper-group and steatite scarabs, it is not the ruler but the WORSHIPER who holds the flower and/or raises a hand in greeting — the wearers "seem to have come to the conclusion that depicting their (own) piety and loyalty apparently would possibly guarantee them greater protection than if they wore images of the entities they were worshiping" (lines 290-293)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The iconographic evidence for the ruler's prominence is strong. The claim that he was "deified after death" is explicitly tentative ("perhaps"). The claim about worshipers preferring self-depiction over deity-depiction is speculative and based on inference from the relative frequency of motifs.

**What's at stake:** If MB IIB city-state kings were depicted on seals and stelae in ways normally reserved for deities, and if their images were used apotropaically, then we have evidence for a form of ruler cult in Canaan BEFORE the Israelite monarchy. This complicates the standard narrative that ruler divinization was a foreign (Egyptian/Mesopotamian) import that "corrupted" pure Yahwism. It also provides context for the later debate about whether the Davidic king was considered divine (Ps 2:7, Ps 110, Isa 9:5-6) — this would be a Canaanite inheritance, not an innovation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that Canaanite religion was strictly theocentric (gods only, no ruler cult) would push back. The biblical texts themselves polemicize against any blurring of the divine/human boundary — but that very polemic suggests the practice existed. Minimalists who doubt the historicity of the United Monarchy might see the MB IIB ruler cult as evidence that monarchy and ruler-exaltation were indigenous Canaanite phenomena that Israel inherited, not foreign imports.

**Alternative reading:** The ruler imagery might be political propaganda rather than religious devotion. Wearing a seal with the king's image could signify political loyalty, not worship. The enemies-under-the-throne motif is a standard Near Eastern royal trope (compare Pharaoh smiting enemies) that doesn't necessarily imply divinization. The "garment with thick fringes" might identify an office rather than a specific individual — the images could represent the institution of kingship, not a particular deified king.

**My assessment:** The authors present this claim cautiously, which is appropriate. The evidence is suggestive but not definitive. The most important observation is the shift they identify: on earlier cylinder seals, the fringed-garment ruler is the central figure receiving worship; on later scarabs, the worshiper himself appears, displacing the ruler-image. This suggests a growing emphasis on personal piety in the later MB IIB — the seal-wearer wants to depict HIMSELF as a pious worshiper rather than relying on the ruler's mediating power. This is a genuinely interesting diachronic observation that fits their seismograph methodology: the iconography registers a shift in religious sensibility from mediated (ruler-centered) to direct (worshiper-centered) piety.

## Claim 5: Erotic couple imagery on MB IIB seals depicts the intimacy of god and goddess on a human level — and this entire religious system is specific to MB IIB, not an eternal "Canaanite religion"

**Author's claim:** "Perhaps we should treat a series of representations of couples that convey strongly erotic connotations as analogous to the encounter of the weather god with the 'Naked Goddess.' In any case, such representations are common for the Middle Bronze Age but disappear in the later periods" (lines 315-319). And in the summary: "The religious system of signs that used massebahs, sacred trees, deities and rituals that had a patently sexual character, regarded by many as typical of 'the' Canaanite religion for the entire period from 1500 to 500, may in reality have played a decisive role only during Middle Bronze Age IIB and might have merely undergone radical modifications during the following periods, surviving only in a marginal way as the religion of the pagani, the villagers" (lines 416-422).

**Evidence presented:**
- Old Syrian hematite cylinder seal from Tell el-Ajjul: man and woman drinking together from a vessel through a tube; palms and eight-rayed star (Ishtar/Astarte sphere); a dove replaces the star on a similar seal in Krakow (lines 319-325)
- Scarabs from second half of MB IIB depict a man and woman "being intimate with one another, sometimes showing this more graphically, sometimes less" (lines 336-338)
- Scarab from Megiddo Stratum XII (ca. 1750–1700): ruler in fringed garment with a woman who has a long thick braid; secondary motifs of flower bud and fully opened bloom (lines 339-342)
- These representations disappear in later periods (line 318-319)
- The summary emphasizes: the religious system with patently sexual character was NOT "typical of 'the' Canaanite religion" for the entire 1500–500 period, but was dominant only in MB IIB (lines 416-422)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the descriptive claim (erotic imagery is common in MB IIB and disappears later — this is a falsifiable empirical claim about the seal corpus). MEDIUM on the interpretive claim (that this imagery represents the divine couple on a human level). MEDIUM on the sweeping summary claim about the entire religious system.

**What's at stake:** This is one of the most consequential claims in the chapter. If the "patently sexual" Canaanite religion — with its sacred prostitution, fertility rites, and goddess worship — was primarily an MB IIB phenomenon rather than a permanent feature of Canaanite culture, then the entire biblical polemic against "Canaanite" religion is attacking a system that was already historically obsolete (or at least radically transformed) by the time Israel emerged. The biblical writers may have been fighting a ghost — or at least a much-diminished remnant. This also challenges the scholarly tendency (especially in older works like Albright's) to treat "Canaanite religion" as a static, monolithic entity defined by fertility cults. Keel and Uehlinger are historicizing what others essentialized.

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves are the primary "dissenter" — they treat Canaanite religion as a contemporary threat throughout the monarchy. Scholars who take the biblical polemic at face value as describing actual Iron Age practices would resist the idea that the sexually-charged fertility religion was already a marginal "religion of the pagani" by Iron II. The Ugaritic texts (Late Bronze) DO contain sexually explicit divine narratives (Baal and Anat, El's exploits) — so the system didn't disappear entirely. Dever's work on folk religion emphasizes the continuity of goddess worship (Judean Pillar Figurines, Astarte plaques) well into Iron II.

**Alternative reading:** The disappearance of erotic couple imagery from SEALS may not mean the disappearance of the religious system. It could mean a shift in MEDIA — perhaps such imagery moved to perishable materials (wood, textile) or became too sensitive for public display on seals. The biblical polemic against Canaanite practices is so intense and sustained that it likely had a real target. "Marginal" doesn't mean nonexistent — and marginal folk practices can still generate vigorous orthodox opposition.

**My assessment:** This claim is the chapter's most provocative and potentially its most vulnerable. The empirical observation about seals is solid — the iconographic record does show a decline in sexually explicit imagery after MB IIB. But the leap from "seals changed" to "the entire religious system became marginal" is large. The authors acknowledge this with their hedging: "may in reality have played a decisive role only during MB IIB and MIGHT have merely undergone radical modifications." The reference forward to §4 and backward to Chapter II suggests this is a thesis they'll develop across the book. I'll watch in later chapters whether they can sustain the argument that LB and Iron Age Canaanite/Israelite religion was fundamentally DIFFERENT from the MB IIB pattern, not just a modified version of it. The tension with Römer is worth noting: Römer (The Invention of God) emphasizes CONTINUITY of Canaanite religious patterns (especially the storm god tradition) into Israelite Yahwism, while Keel and Uehlinger are emphasizing DISCONTINUITY and transformation.

## Chapter III Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Tel Kitan female stele disproves all-male massebah assumption | HIGH | Being dismissed as an isolated anomaly; need more examples |
| 2: Megiddo figurine corpus shows goddess dominance (16:6) and weather god's fertility emphasis | HIGH | Differential deposition patterns vs. actual cultic emphasis |
| 3: Falcon-headed figure = deliberate Canaanite-Egyptian royal god syncretism | MEDIUM-HIGH | Motif combination could be decorative eclecticism, not theology |
| 4: Fringed-garment ruler = deified city-state king mediating divine/human | MEDIUM | "Perhaps deified after death" is speculative; could be political, not religious |
| 5: MB IIB "sexual" religious system is period-specific, not eternal Canaanite religion | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptive) / MEDIUM (explanatory) | Shift in seal media ≠ disappearance of religious system; biblical polemic suggests continuity |

**Strongest section:** §25 on the falcon-headed figure. This is the chapter's most original and sophisticated argument. The way they navigate the German Horon vs. English Re debate by shifting to a third position (deliberate syncretism), their use of the jasper group as a coherent corpus, and their integration of the Hyksos political context show the constellation methodology from Chapter I operating at full strength. The Tell el-Dab‘a scarab (pharaoh with club AND branch) is a particularly elegant piece of evidence.

**Weakest section:** §28 on couples. This section is frustratingly brief (barely a page) for a topic that supports the chapter's most sweeping claim (that MB IIB religion was period-specific). The evidence is thin — a few seals and scarabs — and the interpretive leap from "erotic couple imagery exists" to "this represents divine intimacy on a human level" to "this whole system is unique to MB IIB" is made too quickly. More evidence and more careful argumentation would be welcome.

**Most consequential methodological move:** The periodization argument embedded in the summary (§29). By arguing that the "patently sexual" Canaanite religion is an MB IIB phenomenon, not a permanent feature, they are historicizing the entire category "Canaanite religion." This has implications far beyond iconography — it challenges the biblical writers' own framing, much of 20th-century biblical scholarship, and the popular imagination. If they're right, the "Canaanite religion" that Israel defined itself against was already a historical memory by the time Israel emerged.

**Tension I'll watch in later chapters:** The tension between the claim that the sexually-charged fertility system was MB IIB-specific (this chapter) and the goddess's persistence into Iron II (which they'll document in Chapters VII-VIII with Judean Pillar Figurines and Astarte plaques). If the goddess survives, in what sense did the "system" undergo "radical modification"? What exactly changed, and what continued? The answer may be in the distinction between SYSTEM (the integrated complex of massebahs + sacred trees + sexual rituals + divine couples) and individual ELEMENTS (goddess figurines persisting in folk practice). But that distinction needs to be made explicit.

**Cross-reference to Römer:** Römer's *The Invention of God* traces the storm-god tradition from its Bronze Age Canaanite roots through Israelite Yahwism, emphasizing continuity of the warrior-storm deity archetype. Keel and Uehlinger's Chapter III complicates this by showing that even within the Bronze Age, the storm god's iconography shifted — from fertility-associated (MB) to warrior-aggressive (LB). This suggests that "continuity" of a deity type can mask significant transformations in how that deity was conceived and worshiped. The Römer-Keel/Uehlinger synthesis would be: the STORM GOD TRADITION is continuous from MB IIB through Iron II, but its MEANING and ICONOGRAPHIC EXPRESSION changed dramatically — from fertility god paired with a dominant goddess in MB IIB, to warrior god in LB, to national god absorbing solar attributes in Iron II, to aniconic sole deity in Iron III.

**Most important data points for the broader project:**
1. The 16:6 female-to-male figurine ratio at Megiddo — quantitative evidence for goddess prominence in MB IIB temple religion
2. The Tel Kitan female stele — the goddess in the most "primitive" cultic medium, proving massebahs are not inherently male
3. The falcon-headed figure — earliest documented case of Canaanite-Egyptian divine syncretism, prefiguring later solar Yahwism
4. The jasper group (1720–1650) — a coherent, dateable corpus that anchors the entire MB IIB iconographic sequence
5. The disappearance of erotic couple imagery after MB IIB — a concrete, falsifiable claim about religious change that can be tested against the rest of the book's evidence
