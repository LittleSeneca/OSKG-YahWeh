# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter III: The Iconography of the Middle Bronze Age Temples of Tel Kitan and Megiddo

The first temple-iconography chapter. Keel and Uehlinger examine two MB IIB cult sites — the village sanctuary at Tel Kitan (ca. 1750–1650) and the sacral area at Megiddo (Strata XIII–X, ca. 1800–1500) — to establish what the earliest temple iconography in Palestine actually looked like. The chapter covers the weather god and his consort, the falcon-headed figure as a syncretic Canaanite-Egyptian royal deity, the ruler with male and female worshipers, erotic couple imagery, and a summary that challenges the notion of a timeless "Canaanite religion." Key methodological move: the earliest Palestinian temple iconography is not exclusively male — the goddess dominates the figurine corpus at Megiddo (16 female to 6 male), and a female stele appears among the massebahs at Tel Kitan. The chapter also introduces the "jasper group" of seals (1720–1650) as a crucial corpus for tracking the merger of Egyptian and Near Eastern iconographic traditions.

## Chapter III Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Tel Kitan female stele disproves all-male massebah assumption | HIGH | Being dismissed as an isolated anomaly; need more examples |
| 2: Megiddo figurine corpus shows goddess dominance (16:6) and weather god's fertility emphasis | HIGH | Differential deposition patterns vs. actual cultic emphasis |
| 3: Falcon-headed figure = deliberate Canaanite-Egyptian royal god syncretism | MEDIUM-HIGH | Motif combination could be decorative eclecticism, not theology |
| 4: Fringed-garment ruler = deified city-state king mediating divine/human | MEDIUM | "Perhaps deified after death" is speculative; could be political, not religious |
| 5: MB IIB "sexual" religious system is period-specific, not eternal Canaanite religion | MEDIUM-HIGH (descriptive) / MEDIUM (explanatory) | Shift in seal media ≠ disappearance of religious system; biblical polemic suggests continuity |

**Strongest section:** §25 on the falcon-headed figure. This is the chapter's most original and sophisticated argument. The way they navigate the German Horon vs. English Re debate by shifting to a third position (deliberate syncretism), their use of the jasper group as a coherent corpus, and their integration of the Hyksos political context show the constellation methodology from Chapter I operating at full strength. The Tell el-Dab'a scarab (pharaoh with club AND branch) is a particularly elegant piece of evidence.

**Weakest section:** §28 on couples. This section is frustratingly brief (barely a page) for a topic that supports the chapter's most sweeping claim (that MB IIB religion was period-specific). The evidence is thin — a few seals and scarabs — and the interpretive leap from "erotic couple imagery exists" to "this represents divine intimacy on a human level" to "this whole system is unique to MB IIB" is made too quickly. More evidence and more careful argumentation would be welcome.

**Most consequential methodological move:** The periodization argument embedded in the summary (§29). By arguing that the "patently sexual" Canaanite religion is an MB IIB phenomenon, not a permanent feature, they are historicizing the entire category "Canaanite religion." This has implications far beyond iconography — it challenges the biblical writers' own framing, much of 20th-century biblical scholarship, and the popular imagination. If they're right, the "Canaanite religion" that Israel defined itself against was already a historical memory by the time Israel emerged.

**Tension I'll watch in later chapters:** The tension between the claim that the sexually-charged fertility system was MB IIB-specific (this chapter) and the goddess's persistence into Iron II (which they'll document in Chapters VII-VIII with Judean Pillar Figurines and Astarte plaques). If the goddess survives, in what sense did the "system" undergo "radical modification"? What exactly changed, and what continued? The answer may be in the distinction between SYSTEM (the integrated complex of massebahs + sacred trees + sexual rituals + divine couples) and individual ELEMENTS (goddess figurines persisting in folk practice). But that distinction needs to be made explicit.

**Cross-reference to Romer:** Romer's *The Invention of God* traces the storm-god tradition from its Bronze Age Canaanite roots through Israelite Yahwism, emphasizing continuity of the warrior-storm deity archetype. Keel and Uehlinger's Chapter III complicates this by showing that even within the Bronze Age, the storm god's iconography shifted — from fertility-associated (MB) to warrior-aggressive (LB). This suggests that "continuity" of a deity type can mask significant transformations in how that deity was conceived and worshiped. The Romer-Keel/Uehlinger synthesis would be: the STORM GOD TRADITION is continuous from MB IIB through Iron II, but its MEANING and ICONOGRAPHIC EXPRESSION changed dramatically — from fertility god paired with a dominant goddess in MB IIB, to warrior god in LB, to national god absorbing solar attributes in Iron II, to aniconic sole deity in Iron III.

**Most important data points for the broader project:**
1. The 16:6 female-to-male figurine ratio at Megiddo — quantitative evidence for goddess prominence in MB IIB temple religion
2. The Tel Kitan female stele — the goddess in the most "primitive" cultic medium, proving massebahs are not inherently male
3. The falcon-headed figure — earliest documented case of Canaanite-Egyptian divine syncretism, prefiguring later solar Yahwism
4. The jasper group (1720–1650) — a coherent, dateable corpus that anchors the entire MB IIB iconographic sequence
5. The disappearance of erotic couple imagery after MB IIB — a concrete, falsifiable claim about religious change that can be tested against the rest of the book's evidence
