# Schmid — §§6-9: Jewish Reception, Neo-Orthodoxy, Von Rad, and Implicit Theology

These four subsections complete Part B's history of the concept of theology. §6 traces the marginal Jewish reception of "theology." §7 shows how Barth's neo-orthodoxy reversed the Romantic devaluation — now RELIGION was the problem, and theology was rehabilitated. §8 examines von Rad's ambiguous masterpiece and the post-war fragmentation of the discipline. §9 is Schmid's own constructive proposal: the HB contains "implicit theology" — reflective interpretation of religious phenomena accomplished through literary strategies like "stereometric" reading.

---

## §6: The Reception of the Concept of Theology in Judaism

### Claim 1: "Theology" has a short and marginal history in Judaism — medieval Jewish reflection preferred "philosophy of religion"

**Schmid's claim:** "The concept of 'theology' has a comparatively short and controversial history in Judaism, essentially limited to the modern period." Medieval Jewish thinkers expressed systematic reflection as religious philosophy, not theology. Saadia Gaon's *'Emunot we-De'ot* (933 CE) and Maimonides' *Moreh Nevukim* are philosophically grounded presentations of Jewish doctrine that avoid the term "theology."

**Evidence presented:**

- Josephus and Philo used θεολογία but in non-specified, profane Greek senses
- 19th-century *Wissenschaft des Judentums* (Zunz, Geiger) adopted "theology" programmatically — Jewish Theological Seminary of Breslau (1854)
- Works using "theology" for Jewish traditions: Weber (1897), Schechter (1909), Kohler (1918), Marmorstein (1950), Cohon (1971)
- Cohon's definition exactly mirrors Christian usage: "Theology conceptualizes religious experience"
- But the *Encyclopedia Judaica* states: "The Bible contains no systematic treatment of theological problems"
- Medieval alternatives: Saadia Gaon's *Kitab al-Amanat* (933), Maimonides' *Dalālat alḥā'irīn* — both "philosophy," not "theology"
- Modern revival: Yoram Hazony's *The Philosophy of Hebrew Scripture* (2012) — encountered "intense criticism"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The philological and historical evidence is clear. Jewish tradition preferred "philosophy of religion" to "theology."

**What's at stake:** Schmid is showing that "theology" is a Christian category — its application to the HB is not neutral or universal. This supports his decision to avoid calling his work either Christian or Jewish theology. But it also raises the question: if "theology" is a Christian category, can Schmid's project genuinely escape that framing?

**My assessment:** Schmid's honesty about the Christian provenance of "theology" is important. But it creates a tension he hasn't fully resolved: he wants to use "theology" (a Christian term) to describe the HB in a way that's "neither Christian nor Jewish." The Jewish tradition's preference for "philosophy" suggests that alternative framings ARE available and that Schmid's choice of "theology" is itself a confessional decision.

## §7: The Devaluation of the Concept of Religion in Neo-Orthodox Theology

### Claim 2: Barth's "religion is unbelief" reversed the Romantic devaluation — now RELIGION was the problem and THEOLOGY was rehabilitated

**Schmid's claim:** Karl Barth declared "religion is unbelief. It is a concern, indeed, we must say that it is the one great concern, of godless man." Religion, as human work, stands in opposition to revelation. This "stigmatization of the concept of religion" had the effect of rehabilitating "theology" — the term that 19th-century scholarship had treated as pejorative.

**Evidence presented:**

- Barth, *Church Dogmatics* I/2: "Religion is never true in itself and as such"
- The history-of-religions approach had treated theology as dead systematization; Barth reversed this: religion is dead human work, theology is faithful response to revelation
- "Accompanying the gradual success of neo-orthodox theology...skepticism towards 'religion' increased. On the flip side, the concept of theology...bloomed again"
- The result in HB studies: calls for an independent, non-historical theology of the OT
- Emil Brunner and Rafael Gyllenberg went further: "there can be no independent theology of the Hebrew Bible" — because the HB doesn't attest Christian revelation

**Confidence:** HIGH. Barth's "religion is unbelief" is one of the most famous theological declarations of the 20th century. Schmid's analysis of its effect on HB studies is historically accurate.

**What's at stake:** The Barthian rehabilitation of "theology" is what made the 20th-century discipline of OT theology possible — but it saddled it with a fatal ambiguity. Is OT theology describing the HB's own religious thought (descriptive)? Or is it articulating Christian doctrine from OT texts (normative)? The Barthian inheritance tends toward the latter, which is exactly what Schmid resists. Schmid is doing theology in the wake of Barth — but against Barth's normative grain.

**Who disagrees:** George Fohrer critiqued neo-orthodoxy harshly: it "did OT studies great harm through its renunciation of any religio-historical perspective, its disregard for an appropriate understanding of the uniqueness of Israelite religion, and its revival of allegorical and typological interpretation."

**My assessment:** This is the crucial pendulum swing in Schmid's genealogy. Barth saved "theology" as a term but at the cost of disconnecting it from historical scholarship. Schmid wants to keep the term "theology" (Barth's gift) while restoring its connection to historical description (the 19th century's gift). Whether this synthesis is stable is the question.

---

### Claim 3: The "Biblical Theology Movement" insisted on "what it means" in addition to "what it meant" — Schmid resists this

**Schmid's claim:** Krister Stendahl's famous distinction between "what it meant" and "what it means" drove the Biblical Theology Movement. John J. Collins reformulated: "Biblical theology should not...be reduced to the 'historical fact that such and such was thought and believed' but should clarify the meaning and truth-claims."

**Evidence presented:**

- Stendahl, IDB article "Biblical Theology, Contemporary": biblical exegesis must consider "what it means"
- Collins, *Encounters with Biblical Theology* (2005): theology must address "truth-claims from a modern critical perspective"
- John Rogerson, *A Theology of the Old Testament* (2009): theology uses historical criticism "in the service of the interpretation...on the assumption that [OT texts] have something to say to the present"

**Confidence:** HIGH. These are direct citations of programmatic statements.

**What's at stake:** Schmid's project is pointedly REJECTING the "what it means" half of Stendahl's distinction. He's doing "what it meant" — and calling that theology. This is his most controversial move: can theology stop at description?

**My assessment:** Schmid is right to note that the "what it means" demand is historically contingent (its roots are in neo-orthodoxy and the Biblical Theology Movement), not inherent to theology. But he needs to show that his "what it meant" theology actually delivers something worth calling theology. Description alone may not be enough.

## §8: Developments since the Mid-Twentieth Century

### Claim 4: Von Rad's *Old Testament Theology* was epoch-making but genre-confused — it collapsed literary history into theology

**Schmid's claim:** Von Rad's decision to present OT theology as "retelling" was genuinely innovative, grounded in form criticism's discovery of "ancient creedal formulas." But his distinction between "the world of Israel's faith" (history of religion) and "Israel's confession of faith" (theology) was untenable. Carl A. Keller: "Von Rad's book is basically not a theology, but rather an introduction." Schmid agrees: von Rad's work "is many things, but it is not true to its genre."

**Evidence presented:**

- Von Rad's program: theology = "Israel's own explicit assertions about Yahweh" — specifically as "a continuing divine activity in history"
- Friedrich Baumgärtel's critique: the divergence between historical reconstruction and confessional portrayal is "a pseudo problem" — "the 'confessional portrayal of history' that comes from faith is a part of the religious history of Israel and as such can be investigated with the current historical-critical research methodology"
- Keller: von Rad's book is "not a theology, but rather an introduction"
- Von Rad HIMSELF admitted: "the surprising convergence — indeed the mutual intersection — which has come about during the last twenty or thirty years between introductory studies and Biblical theology"
- Von Rad ALSO acknowledged (outside his *Theology*) that the OT "contains not only one, but a number of theologies that diverge greatly"

**Confidence:** HIGH. Von Rad's genre confusion is widely acknowledged. Schmid's analysis is sharp and well-supported by both von Rad's own statements and contemporary critics.

**What's at stake:** Von Rad is the giant whose shoulders (or shadow) all subsequent OT theologies stand in. By showing that von Rad's project was conceptually unstable, Schmid clears space for his own alternative. But von Rad's instinct — that the HB's narrative character demands narrative theological treatment — remains powerful. Schmid's alternative (historical-descriptive theology organized by canon, theme, and literary history) is more analytical but may lose the narrative power that made von Rad's work compelling.

**My assessment:** Schmid's critique of von Rad is fair and well-argued. Von Rad couldn't decide whether he was doing history or theology, so he did something in between and called it both. Schmid's solution — being clear that he's doing historical description — is more honest. But honesty may not be enough. Von Rad's work compelled because it took theological risks. Schmid's work may be more rigorous but less powerful.

### Claim 5: Albertz's *History of Israelite Religion* (1992) revived the religion-over-theology argument — but his own work is "fundamentally just as much a literary history"

**Schmid's claim:** Rainer Albertz argued that "history of religion" is a "more comprehensive Old Testament discipline" than "theology" — it better fits the HB's historical structure, doesn't separate religious statements from their background, and doesn't impose theological abstraction. But Schmid counters: "Albertz's own outline presents a mix of different approaches...his book is fundamentally just as much a literary history as it is a history of the theology of the Hebrew Bible."

**Evidence presented:**

- Albertz's four advantages of history of religion: (1) corresponds better to the historical structure of the OT; (2) takes seriously that religious statements can't be separated from historical background; (3) avoids the compulsion to abstract to a single "theological" level; (4) need not present claims to absoluteness
- Schmid's counter: Albertz's book is organized by biblical themes and follows the biblical structure — it's not independent of the Bible's own framing
- "How differently a history of Israelite religion appears when not based primarily on the biblical witnesses and themes is demonstrated in the contemporary volume by Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Schmid's observation about Albertz's dependence on biblical structure is accurate. But his dismissal of Albertz's argument for history-of-religion is somewhat hasty — Albertz's four advantages are substantive and Schmid doesn't really answer them.

**What's at stake:** Albertz is the most prominent recent advocate for history-of-religion over theology. If Schmid can't convincingly answer Albertz, his project's rationale is weakened. Schmid's best move is to say: history-of-religion is legitimate, but it doesn't exhaust what can be said — the theological dimension (the texts' own reflective, synthesizing character) requires its own treatment.

**My assessment:** Schmid's engagement with Albertz is disappointingly brief. Albertz's four advantages deserve more serious response. Schmid's counter (that Albertz's own work is implicitly theological/literary) is a tu quoque, not a refutation. The deeper question is whether "theology" adds anything to "history of religion" — Schmid needs Parts E-H to demonstrate that it does.

## §9: Implicit Theologies and the Search for a Theology of the Hebrew Bible

### Claim 6: The HB contains "implicit theology" — reflective interpretation of religious phenomena that qualifies as theology even without explicit theological genre

**Schmid's claim:** The concept of theology, as defined by Scholasticism, is "anachronistic" for the HB. But the HB is "neither contains theology nor contains no theology." The HB is "to a large degree...the reflective interpretation of preexisting religious texts. It thereby fulfills — at least for the texts containing reflective interpretations — a basic requirement of theology, if 'theology' is understood as the reflective examination and interpretation of religious phenomena."

**Evidence presented:**

- Köhler: "God is spirit" / "God is light" / "God is love" — these New Testament theological statements have no OT parallel. The HB doesn't make abstract theological assertions
- But the HB DOES contain Fortschreibung (updating interpretation): "the Hebrew Bible should, to a large degree, be viewed as the reflective interpretation of preexisting religious texts"
- Rudolf Smend: the HB has "at least a strong convergence with theology"
- Christoph Levin: "the way of the Old Testament to its theology"
- Norbert Lohfink: theology appears in the HB "only implicitly, indirectly, or reduced"
- Rudolf Bultmann distinguished "explicit" and "implicit" theological thoughts in the NT — Schmid extends this to the HB
- Jan Assmann independently developed "implicit theology" for Egyptian texts: implicit and explicit theology relate "like language and grammar"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The definition of "implicit theology" as reflective interpretation of religious texts is coherent. Whether this really counts as "theology" depends on definition. Schmid has spent the entire Part B arguing that "theology" has no stable definition — so his definition is as legitimate as any other. But the circularity is obvious: he's defined theology to include what the HB does, then claimed the HB contains theology.

**What's at stake:** This is Schmid's CONTRIBUTION to the field: a definition of theology that fits the HB's actual character as scribal, interpretive literature. If accepted, it resolves the century-long debate about whether the HB has a theology. The resolution is: yes, but it's implicit — it happens through interpretive literary activity, not through abstract doctrinal formulation.

**My assessment:** This is genuinely clever. Schmid's definition sidesteps the deadlock: the HB doesn't have explicit theology (the Scholastic kind), but it has implicit theology (reflective interpretation of religious tradition). The definition emerges naturally from the HB's character as Fortschreibung literature — which Schmid established in §1. The question is whether this is just relabeling "inner-biblical interpretation" as "theology." If all Fortschreibung is theology, the term loses specificity.

### Claim 7: "Stereometric theology" — the HB conveys theological meaning through literary juxtaposition, not explicit statement

**Schmid's claim:** "Implicit theologies can become more explicit over time through the development of a text's redactional history. However, the explication can also be functionally transferred to the audience's imagination." The HB uses "stereometric reading": theological meanings emerge through the juxtaposition of texts in the reader's mind, not through explicit theological propositions.

**Evidence presented:**

- Jer 6:1 ("Flee for safety, Benjaminites!") — political advice with no theological interpretation
- Jer 4:6-7 — reuses Jer 6:1's language but explicitly attributes the evil to God: "I am bringing evil from the north"
- Jer 1:13-14 — the "boiling pot" vision preemptively synthesizes Jer 4 and 6 but remains silent on whether God himself acts. The theological meaning emerges through the reader's synthesis of all three texts.
- Proverbs 11:25-27 — parallelismus membrorum evokes theological dimensions without naming God. "A generous person will be enriched" — the divine passive implies God's action but doesn't state it. The parallelism "swings" the theological dimension between cola.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Jeremiah example is well-chosen — it genuinely shows texts in theological dialogue without any explicit theological proposition. The Proverbs example is more speculative but plausible. The concept of "stereometric" reading is Schmid's most original contribution to theological method.

**What's at stake:** If Schmid is right that the HB's theology is fundamentally stereometric — conveyed through literary juxtaposition rather than explicit doctrine — then traditional theologies that abstract "teachings" from the texts are methodologically wrong. You can't extract "biblical teaching on X" because the HB doesn't teach that way. It teaches by making you hold multiple texts in tension. This has massive implications for how the HB can be used in theological argument.

**My assessment:** This is the most exciting idea in Part B. "Stereometric theology" captures something real about how the HB works: meaning emerges from the space BETWEEN texts, not from individual texts in isolation. The Jeremiah example is compelling because it shows theological development through redaction without ever stating a theological proposition. The Proverbs example is more debatable (is the theological dimension really "stereometric" or just implicit?) but the concept is productive. If Schmid can sustain this reading across the whole canon, he's made a genuine methodological contribution.

---

### Claim 8: The HB is "an observably multi-perspectival theological discussion" — not disconnected theologies but interconnected ones

**Schmid's claim:** "Thanks to the nature of the Hebrew Bible as scribal literature, these theologies are connected and interlinked with one another such that the Hebrew Bible appears as an observably multi-perspectival theological discussion. Therefore, the theology of the Hebrew Bible should not be broken up into the description of its various theologies, even though it comprises these as well."

**Evidence presented:**

- The internal interconnections of Fortschreibung literature create a genuine conversation
- The theologies "do not stand side by side in a disconnected fashion"
- The canonical shape and the scribal process both point toward interconnection

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This claim is the structural warrant for the whole book: that there IS a "theology" (singular) of the HB, not just "theologies" (plural). The evidence for interconnections will need to be demonstrated in Parts E-H. As a programmatic statement, it's clear; as a proven claim, it's pending.

**What's at stake:** If Schmid can't demonstrate genuine interconnection — if the HB is just a collection of independent theological voices that happen to share a canon — then his singular "theology" framing collapses. The book should be "Theologies of the Hebrew Bible."

**My assessment:** Schmid has set himself a high bar. The claim that the interconnections create a genuine "discussion" rather than just proximity is what distinguishes his project from the pluralists (Gerstenberger, Fischer). Whether he clears this bar depends on Parts E-H. The Jeremiah example in §9.2 is a promising start — it shows genuine theological dialogue across texts. But one example isn't enough.

**Strongest section:** §9.2 on stereometric theology. This is Schmid's most original methodological contribution and the Jeremiah example is genuinely illuminating. The concept captures something real about how the HB's literary character generates theological meaning.

**Weakest section:** The engagement with Albertz. Schmid's response is too brief for a serious competitor. Albertz's four advantages for history-of-religion deserve more considered engagement than Schmid gives them.

**Overall for the Truth project:** Part B has established Schmid's working definition of theology — reflective, interpretive, implicit, stereometric. This matters for the Truth project because it shapes how Schmid reads the HB's "historical" narratives. If Genesis-Kings is implicit theology (reflective construction of Israel's past), not history, then using those texts as evidence for pre-exilic religion is a category error. Schmid's definition of theology IS his argument against the American consensus — he's just not saying so directly yet.
