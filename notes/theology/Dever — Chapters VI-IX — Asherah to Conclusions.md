# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Chapters VI-IX

---

## Chapter VI: The Goddess Asherah and Her Cult

### Claim: Asherah was a real goddess, Yhwh's consort — and archaeology proves it

**Dever's claim:** The cumulative archaeological evidence — 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines, Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions, the Arad temple's two standing stones, the Taanach cult stand — demonstrates that Asherah was actively worshipped as Yhwh's consort throughout the monarchy. The biblical writers' embarrassment about a "Hebrew Goddess" led them to systematically obscure her, but they could not erase her entirely.

**Evidence — the Ugaritic background:**
- 14th-13th century texts portray Asherah as "Lady Asherah of the Sea," El's consort, "Mother of the Gods," "Creatress of the Gods"
- She had seventy divine sons
- When El and Yhwh merged, Asherah came with the package

**Evidence — the biblical text itself:**
- The term *'asherah* occurs over 40 times
- In at least several passages, the term MUST refer to a goddess: 1 Kings 15:13; 1 Kings 18:19; 2 Kings 23:4
- "If 'Ba'al' is a deity — as he certainly is — then so is 'Asherah'"
- The Septuagint and Vulgate understood *'asherim* as connected with trees and sacred groves — preserving a memory of the goddess

**Evidence — the inscriptions:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud: "Yhwh of Samaria and his Asherah" — deliberately paired
- Khirbet el-Qom: "blessed by Yhwh and his Asherah" — a burial inscription, deeply personal

**Evidence — the figurines:**
- 3,000+ mass-produced nude female figurines in domestic contexts
- Overwhelmingly from 8th-6th centuries — the period of the prophets and reforms
- "Found in almost every excavated house in Judah"

**Dever's direct challenge to Smith:**
> "It is precisely my intent here to demonstrate that a cult of Asherah did flourish in ancient Israel."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the cumulative case. Dever's evidence significantly strengthens the goddess interpretation.

**What's at stake:** "Did God have a wife?" Dever's answer: Yes.

## Chapter VII: Asherah, Women's Cults, and "Official Yahwism"

### Claim: The suppression of Asherah = the suppression of women's religion

**Dever's claim:** Folk religion was centered in the household, and the household was women's domain. Women were the primary practitioners of domestic religion — tending shrines, using figurines for fertility and healing, maintaining family rituals. "Official" Yahwism — the Temple, the priesthood, the written law — was men's domain. The Deuteronomistic reform was, in effect, a male clerical coup against women's traditional religious authority.

**Evidence:**
- Women's work included "tending to the family shrine and all of the religious rituals, rites of passage, and so forth"
- The figurines are found in domestic contexts — women's spaces — and relate to women's concerns (fertility, childbirth, nursing)
- Men had leisure time for "talking religion and politics" — women did the religious WORK
- "Official religion was a largely male preserve: priests, Temple functionaries, scribes, royal officials — all men. Women were excluded from the priesthood, from writing, from public office."
- "It is women's cults, centering on the veneration of Asherah, that the reformers set out to destroy."

**Dever on feminism:** He distinguishes between "mainstream feminists" (competent scholars focusing on women's issues) and "doctrinaire feminists" (ideology trumps scholarship). He identifies himself as "politically speaking, a feminist" but not a "feminist scholar."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on women as primary domestic practitioners. MEDIUM that the reform was specifically anti-women (the gendered dimension is real but hard to prove was PRIMARY motivation).

**What's at stake:** The Bible's religion — monotheistic, aniconic, male-priest-led — is the religion of the winners. The religion it suppressed — polytheistic, image-rich, woman-led — was the religion of most Israelites. Recovering Asherah is recovering women's history.

## Chapter VIII: From Polytheism to Monotheism

### Claim: Monotheism was a late, elite construction that never fully displaced folk polytheism

**Dever's claim:** "Israelite religion was everywhere and always polytheistic in practice until the very end of the monarchy — and for most people, even beyond." Monotheism was the invention of a small circle of reformers who wrote the Bible. It only became "the religion of Israel" because they won the battle of the books.

**Evidence:**
- The archaeological evidence of polytheistic practice continues UNABATED through the monarchy. Josiah's reform left no archaeological trace.
- "We have no archaeological evidence whatsoever that Josiah's reform had any lasting effect on folk religion"
- The prophets were a tiny minority — "the 'Yahweh-alone' movement was a minority party until the very end"
- The exile created monotheism not because everyone became monotheistic but because the elite who went into exile wrote the Bible
- "The very fact that the biblical writers condemn folk religion so vehemently is the best proof that it was widespread. You don't rail against things that aren't happening."

**The Canaanite background:** Dever emphasizes that Israelite religion grew organically from Canaanite religion. "There was little that was innovative in the biblical sacrificial system." The festivals (Sukkot, Pesach, Shavuot) were originally Canaanite agricultural celebrations later "historicized" by connecting them to events in salvation history.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the persistence of polytheistic practice. HIGH that monotheism was a late elite construction.

## Chapter IX: What Does the Goddess Do to Help?

### Dever's Conclusion

**For understanding the Bible:** "The Hebrew Bible is not a history of Israelite religion. It is a 'minority report' — the polemic of a small group of zealots who eventually won the day."

**For understanding God:** "The God of the Hebrew Bible is a composite — he absorbed the characteristics of many earlier deities. And one of those deities was female."

**For women today:** "Recovering the Goddess is not about reviving ancient Israelite religion — that's impossible and undesirable. It's about recognizing that women's religious experience has been systematically suppressed in the Western monotheistic traditions."

**For faith:** Dever doesn't prescribe. "I have tried to give the forgotten people of ancient Israel — especially the women — their voice back. What you do with that voice is up to you."

**Dever's final word:** The Bible's God once had a wife. Her name was Asherah. She was worshipped in homes and high places, at family shrines and local sanctuaries, by women seeking fertility and families seeking blessing. She was erased by male reformers who wrote a new religion into law and called it the original. Archaeology has brought her back.

## Final Assessment: Dever's Book as a Whole

| Strength | Weakness |
|----------|----------|
| **Overwhelming archaeological evidence** — the figurines, shrines, inscriptions are undeniable | **Rhetorical overreach** — Dever sometimes claims more than the evidence strictly proves |
| **Honest about personal biases** — more methodologically transparent than most scholars | **Dismissive of theology** — his "useless... barrier to understanding" verdict on biblical theology alienates readers who might otherwise be persuaded |
| **Centers women's experience** — a necessary corrective to androcentric scholarship | **Binary thinking** — Book religion vs. folk religion may be too sharp a distinction |
| **Directly engages the faith question** — knows what's at stake for believers | **The Asherah-as-goddess question** — his case is strong but not absolutely decisive on every piece of evidence |

**Dever's contribution to the three-book synthesis:** Smith provided the text-critical framework. Römer provided the historical narrative. Dever provides the material evidence — the figurines, the shrines, the standing stones. Together, they make a cumulative case that is very difficult to dismiss. The biblical God was not always alone.
