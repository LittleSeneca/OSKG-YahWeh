# Römer, The Invention of God — Introduction + Chapter 1

## How This Book Differs from Smith

Römer and Smith agree on the broad outline: Yahweh was not originally the sole God; he emerged from a polytheistic context and became "the only God" through a gradual historical process culminating in the Exile. But they differ in approach:

| Dimension | Smith | Römer |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| **Audience** | Academic/specialist | General educated reader |
| **Method** | Dense text-critical analysis, Ugaritic parallels | Narrative historical reconstruction |
| **Use of texts** | Cautious — texts are "literary vestiges" | More optimistic — texts preserve "traces of memory" |
| **Documentary Hypothesis** | Cautiously retained (with quotation marks) | Explicitly rejected |
| **Key metaphor** | Convergence/differentiation | "Collective invention" — sedimentary layers |
| **Tone** | Methodologically anxious | Confident, accessible |

Römer is the better first read. Smith is the deeper second read.

## Introduction: The Approach

### Römer's Central Question

> "How did one god among others become God? This is the basic, and theologically fundamental, enigma that this book attempts to illuminate."

**Smith's claim (Römer's version):** "It is now beyond doubt that the god of the Bible was not always 'unique,' the one-and-only God." The Bible itself retains traces of polytheism. Judges 11:24 has Jephthah treating Chemosh as a real god alongside Yahweh. Deuteronomy warns against "other gods" without ever claiming they don't exist.

**Evidence presented — the biblical polytheistic traces:**
- Genesis 1: *'ĕlōhîm* has a plural ending — could be translated "gods." "Could the author perhaps be suggesting that the one god subsumes in himself a diversity of gods?"
- Different names for God in different biblical sources reflect different traditions, not just stylistic variation
- Exodus 6:2-3: God tells Moses he appeared to the patriarchs as El Shadday but was NOT known as Yahweh — a trace of distinct deities later merged

**Confidence:** HIGH on the polytheistic traces in the Bible (same evidence as Smith). MEDIUM on Römer's specific interpretive moves (the plural *'ĕlōhîm* as deliberate ambiguity is speculative).

**What's at stake for faith:** Same as Smith — but Römer's framing is more direct. The title "The Invention of God" is provocative. He's not saying God doesn't exist; he's saying the CONCEPT of God as a single universal deity was historically constructed.

---

### Römer's Methodological Position — Pushing Back Against European Minimalism

This is where Römer distinguishes himself. Since the 1970s, European scholarship has tended to treat Pentateuchal texts as late theological constructions with no historical value for earlier periods. Römer pushes back:

> "To take this tack... is to ignore the fact that the narratives contained in the Pentateuch are not inventions proceeding simply from the minds of intellectuals seated in their comfortable chairs. Biblical literature is a literature of tradition."

**His key methodological claim:** Late texts preserve "archaic kernels of fact" — "traces of memory" of events from the distant past. The texts were revised and reinterpreted over centuries, but they weren't fabricated from nothing. "It seems to me perfectly legitimate to reconnect to the older tradition of historical interpretation of the Bible."

**"The Invention of God" — what he means:** Not that scribes fabricated Yahweh. Rather, "a progressive construction arising out of a particular tradition... a series of sedimentary strata gradually laid down over the course of time, which is then sometimes disrupted by historical events." A "collective invention" — "a process in which the conception was continually revised in the light of particular, changing social and historical contexts."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Römer's "traces of memory" approach is defensible but hard to verify. How do you distinguish a genuine archaic kernel from a later invention that LOOKS archaic? He's more optimistic than Smith about what can be recovered, and less deferential to the minimalist challenge than Schmid.

**Who disagrees:** The European minimalists (Schmid, Kratz, the Copenhagen school) would say Römer is too optimistic about recovering pre-exilic history from Persian-period texts.

**Alternative reading:** Schmid's position: the texts ARE largely Persian-period constructions and can't be used to reconstruct earlier periods. Römer's "traces of memory" are unfalsifiable.

**My assessment:** Römer's position is reasonable and more accessible than the minimalist alternative. But it's a judgment call, not a proven methodology. The Schmid challenge remains the single biggest threat to ALL historical reconstructions of pre-exilic Israelite religion.

### Terminology and Historical Framework

Römer is careful about terms in ways that matter:

- **"Israel"** originally meant the northern kingdom (capital Samaria) until 722. Only later did it become a theological term for all Yahweh-worshippers.
- **"Judah"** was the southern kingdom (capital Jerusalem). After 587 it became the Persian province "Yehud."
- **"Jews" / "Judaism"** shouldn't be used before the end of the Persian period (4th century BCE). Before that: "Israelites" or "Judeans."
- **"Canaan"** is a vague biblical term for Syria-Palestine west of the Jordan. Sometimes neutral geographic term, sometimes pejorative.

> This precision matters because it prevents retrojecting later Jewish monotheism onto earlier Israelite religion. The Israelites were not "Jews" and their religion was not "Judaism" — not yet.

## Chapter 1: The God of Israel and His Name

### The Name Problem

Römer explains the YHWH pronunciation mystery clearly:
- Hebrew originally written with consonants only
- The Masoretes (3rd-10th c. CE) added vowels
- By then, pronouncing the divine name was forbidden
- They applied the vowels of *'ădōnāy* ("my Lord") to YHWH as a reading instruction
- "Jehovah" is a medieval Christian mistake — pronouncing the replacement vowels as if they were the original
- The original pronunciation is lost. "Yahweh" is the scholarly reconstruction.

> **The theological significance Römer notes:** "Within the framework of a monotheistic view it is inappropriate for the one god to have a proper name, because if he had one, this might suggest that there was some need to distinguish him from other gods." The name was suppressed BECAUSE monotheism made it theologically awkward. A god with a name is a god among gods.

## Structure of the Remaining Book

Römer's 12 chapters trace a chronological narrative:
1. The Name (Ch. 1)
2. Geographic Origin — southern deserts (Ch. 2)
3. Moses and the Midianites — the Kenite hypothesis (Ch. 3)
4. How Yhwh Became Israel's God (Ch. 4)
5. Entrance into Jerusalem (Ch. 5)
6-7. The Cult of Yhwh in Israel and Judah (Chs. 6-7)
8. The Statue of Yhwh (Ch. 8)
9. Yhwh and His Asherah (Ch. 9)
10. Fall of Samaria and Rise of Judah (Ch. 10)
11. Josiah's Reform (Ch. 11)
12. From One God to the Only God (Ch. 12)

This is a narrative arc: desert god → tribal deity → national god of Israel → god of Jerusalem → the only God. Römer tells a story where Smith builds a case.
