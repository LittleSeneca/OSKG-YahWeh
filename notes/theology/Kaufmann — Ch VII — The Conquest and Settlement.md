# Kaufmann — Ch VII — The Conquest and Settlement

This chapter covers the period from the entry into Canaan through the Judges. Kaufmann makes a series of interlocking arguments: (1) the conquest was a unified national-religious war carried out according to a prearranged plan, not a gradual, piecemeal settlement; (2) the *ḥerem* (ban) was a historical practice that largely annihilated the Canaanite population of the "real land," explaining the absence of Canaanite cultural influence on early Israel; (3) the pre-monarchic tribal confederation was a "kingdom of God" — a theocracy led by charismatic apostle-saviors — not a secular federation; (4) Israelite sanctuaries and agricultural cult were indigenous YHWHistic creations, not Canaanite borrowings; (5) the Judges-era "idolatry" was a superficial, fetishistic phenomenon exaggerated by the biblical historiography's schematic framework; and (6) the conquest-settlement period had a genuinely eschatological quality — a sense that ancient promises were being fulfilled in real time. The chapter is Kaufmann at his most historically specific — he's not just arguing theology here but making concrete claims about what happened on the ground in Iron I Palestine.

---

## Claim 1: The conquest was a unified national plan, not a gradual uncoordinated settlement

**Author's claim:** "It is the accepted view that Canaan was conquered gradually, in several unrelated stages, by individual tribes or tribal bands. The evidence, however, argues for the unified conquest of Canaan by a confederation of tribes that fought to carry out a national plan of conquest." The consciousness of tribal unity cannot be explained as a late development — the centrifugal forces of post-conquest geography (Jordan dividing settlement, Judah's isolation, Transjordanian exposure to Ammon and Moab) would have dissolved any nascent unity. The survival of twelve-tribe consciousness through centuries of division "must have had its roots in the period before the entry into Canaan."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Ideal boundaries predate settlement:** The far-flung northern boundary and the exclusion of Transjordan in the ideal promised land "prove that these limits... were already fixed before the entry into Canaan." No post-conquest political reality produced these boundaries.
- **Judges 1 presupposes a plan:** The chapter records which populations tribes *failed* to dispossess — implying the author "regarded the tribes as following a prearranged plan, each fighting for its own allotted territory." The sum of conquered + unpossessed land equals the complete land of Canaan — a "continuous territory" that "is the standing ideal of a national conquest."
- **Absence of inter-tribal territorial disputes:** "The period of the judges, filled with internecine strife, fails to produce a single boundary dispute or even boundary agreement." The land was divided by lot before conquest — "the settlement followed a prearranged national plan."
- **Archaeology excursus (footnote):** Kaufmann acknowledges the Jericho dating debates (Garstang's early date, Kenyon's corrections, Avigad's 1958 conclusion that "the archaeological findings upon which it is possible to lean are meager enough") but insists his argument doesn't depend on treating the biblical stories as straightforward reports — the "general considerations" prove historical substratum even if individual elements are legendary.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the general claim that the conquest had a coordinated national dimension; LOW-MEDIUM on the claim that a prearranged plan existed before entry.

**What's at stake:** The entire nature of early Israel. If the conquest was a unified national-religious project, the "kingdom of God" concept emerges from a real political-military experience. If settlement was gradual and piecemeal, the unity is a retrojected ideal.

**Who disagrees:** The "accepted view" Kaufmann opposes is the dominant critical consensus — the conquest/settlement models of Alt, Noth, and (later) the "peasant revolt" model of Mendenhall and Gottwald. Archaeology supports gradual, largely peaceful settlement in the highlands with continuity of material culture. Fleming (Ch 6) describes the "people of Yahweh" as a collaborative, non-state grouping that coalesced gradually. Cross (Parts II-III) sees the league as a contingent military alliance that developed into an amphictyony.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite unity was REAL but emerged through the settlement process itself — through shared worship at central sanctuaries, common enemies, and the integrative work of the YHWH cult — not through a prearranged plan. The twelve-tribe scheme is an ideal construct (probably late) that retrojects unity onto earlier periods. The absence of boundary disputes in Judges is an argument from silence — the texts we have may not preserve what didn't serve their theological interests.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann correctly identifies that the biblical texts *present* the conquest as a unified national project, and the question is whether this presentation reflects historical reality or theological idealization. His argument that centrifugal forces would have dissolved unity without a pre-conquest foundation has some force — but it assumes that later political fragmentation is the "natural" state and unity requires special explanation. The opposite model is equally plausible: tribal societies often maintain strong kinship-based identity across political fragmentation (the Arab tribes maintained a sense of common descent across centuries of division). Kaufmann's "ideal boundaries" argument is actually quite good — the limits of the promised land don't match any historical Israelite state, so they must predate the settlement. But this doesn't prove a unified *conquest*; it proves a unified *vision* that shaped how the conquest was later narrated.

## Claim 2: The *ḥerem* (ban) was a historical practice that largely destroyed the Canaanite population of the "real land"

**Author's claim:** The biblical tradition of Canaanite annihilation through *ḥerem* is essentially historical. "The view everywhere is that the Canaanites within the Israelite area of settlement were destroyed; Israel was seduced by those who remained outside and surrounded them." To understand the evidence, one must distinguish the "real land" (Dan to Beersheba, the area actually occupied) from the "ideal land" (including Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, Lebanon, Hermon). Sources that seem to contradict the destruction tradition actually refer to unconquered populations of the *ideal* land, or to an early stage of conquest before the "real land" was fully occupied.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Earliest records know no peaceful relations:** Josh 15:63; 16:10; 17:12-18; Judg 1 "know nothing of alliances and intermarriage with the Canaanites or their influence on the religion of Israel." Later sources (the schematic framework of Judges) that DO mention intermarriage actually refer to nations *surrounding* Israel, not Canaanites *within* — Philistia, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, Amalek.
- **No Canaanites blamed for apostasy:** "Nowhere are Canaanites within the real land blamed for Israel's apostasies." Prophets (Amos 2:9f, Isa 2:6, Ezek 16) attribute idolatrous influence to Philistines and surrounding nations, never to domestic Canaanites. Elijah-Jehu battles are against Sidonian influence. Even Judg 6:8ff and 10:6 refer to "the peoples that were round about them" — Aram, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, Philistines.
- **The Gibeonite exception proves the rule:** The Gibeonites survive only because of an oath; they become hierodules (Nethinim) and remain a distinct, inferior caste through the Second Temple period. "Can we suppose... that the Israelites intermingled amicably with the other Canaanites who are never reported to have made peace with Israel?" The *ḥerem* status is precisely what kept them segregated.
- **No Canaanite military factor after Deborah:** Only one Judges war is with Canaanites (Barak/Deborah, on the disputed boundary). After this, all Israel's enemies are non-Canaanite. "Never did the Canaanites rise from within against their conquerors; never did they unite with their brethren on the boundaries."
- **No class of Canaanite private slaves:** Judges 1 records mass impressment into forced labor (whole communities, for royal service), but "there is no trace of the formation of a class of Canaanite private slaves." Biblical foreign slaves are *purchased*, not captured in war (Exod 21:21; Lev 25:44f) — inconsistent with a surviving Canaanite population.
- **Disappearance of war horse and chariotry:** The Canaanite military technology (horse, iron chariot) "disappears" with Israel's occupation. Joshua hamstrings horses and burns chariots (Josh 11:6, 9); David does the same (2 Sam 8:4). Not until Solomon do these reappear. "Why was this indispensable weapon not adopted by Israel from the Canaanites, if not because the *ḥerem* had destroyed the only class that could teach Israel its use?"
- **Disappearance of the Canaanite city-state:** The city-state political form disappears from within Israelite settlement. Israelite kingship "owes nothing to Canaanite predecessors" — it rules over tribes, not cities.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-LOW on the historical claim of comprehensive destruction. Kaufmann is right that the evidence shows *some* destruction and population replacement in the highlands. But he overstates the completeness and dismisses contrary evidence by definitional fiat (the "real land" vs. "ideal land" distinction).

**What's at stake:** Whether early Israelite religion developed in cultural isolation (as Kaufmann claims) or in continuity and conflict with Canaanite culture (the consensus view). If the Canaanites were largely annihilated, the "Canaanization" model loses its demographic basis.

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological record shows substantial cultural continuity between Late Bronze and Iron I highland settlements — the same pottery traditions, settlement patterns, and agricultural practices. The "peasant revolt" and "gradual emergence" models (Mendenhall, Gottwald, Dever, Finkelstein) see early Israelites as largely indigenous Canaanites who withdrew from the city-state system, not as external conquerors who annihilated the population. Smith (Ch 1) documents that YHWH was worshipped alongside Canaanite deities in the Judges period. Cross (Parts II-III) sees the league absorbing Canaanite cultic traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The *ḥerem* tradition is a theological construct — the language of total destruction serves to express the ideal of separation from Canaanite religion, not a historical report of genocide. The fact that the Bible continually *commands* destruction of Canaanites and *complains* that Israel failed to do so suggests the destruction didn't actually happen. The archaeological evidence supports a model of gradual highland settlement by populations that included both incoming groups and indigenous elements. Kaufmann's "real land"/"ideal land" distinction is clever but ultimately circular — he defines any surviving Canaanite settlement as "outside the real land" by definition, immunizing his thesis against counterevidence.

**My assessment:** This is the most historically consequential claim in the chapter, and also the weakest. Kaufmann is arguing for a genocide that the archaeological record doesn't support. His strongest evidence is negative — the absence of Canaanites as a named class, the disappearance of the city-state, the absence of chariot warfare — but all of these can be explained by the collapse of the Late Bronze city-state system (a regional phenomenon, not uniquely Israelite) and the nature of highland settlement. His "real land"/"ideal land" distinction does genuine exegetical work — it resolves some apparent contradictions — but it also lets him quarantine all counterevidence. The Gibeonite argument is genuinely interesting and underappreciated: if Canaanites were peacefully absorbed, why do the Gibeonites alone survive as a segregated caste for a millennium? But this proves only that the Gibeonites had a special status, not that all other Canaanites were annihilated.

## Claim 3: The pre-monarchic tribal league was a "kingdom of God" — a theocracy led by charismatic apostle-saviors

**Author's claim:** Israel had a political organization before the monarchy — a confederation of independent tribes whose unity "became visible only under certain conditions." Authority was vested in two institutions: (1) the secular "primitive democracy" of elders (tribal council of clan heads, the *nasi'*), unrelated to any temple or sacral function — "unlike that of the Greek amphictyony"; and (2) above this, the "judges" — men of the "spirit," messengers of God, raised up ad hoc by YHWH. "This unparalleled political institution" was "the religious essence of the period." The judges were not local heroes later nationalized; the line between local and national was fluid in a society without institutionalized central authority. "It was a kingdom of the spirit, a theocracy whose divine king exhibited his rule through apostle-saviors, one of the most remarkable manifestations of the idealism of the early religion of YHWH."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Non-consecutive authority:** The judges don't form an unbroken chain. "Non-consecutiveness was the very essence of prophetic authority; it depended upon the action of the divine spirit." The people "looked for the appearance of an apostle-savior and confidently expected that YHWH would raise up such a man by his spirit."
- **Deborah as national figure:** Sisera's pressure targeted Zebulun and Naphtali, but Deborah (based between Ramah and Bethel in Ephraim) "roused the whole confederation." Judg 5 ("the ancient song") treats it as a national event — "the people of YHWH" participate, those who didn't come "to the aid of YHWH" are cursed.
- **Gideon:** Begins as local war with Midian and Amalek, "but it ends by involving Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, Naphtali, and even Ephraim." Centuries later, Isaiah still speaks of "the day of Midian" as national memory.
- **Jephthah:** Ammonites harass Transjordan, Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim — Jephthah defeats them and "delivers all these tribes."
- **Samson:** Even without mustering tribes to battle, he "could still become a national hero."
- **Contrast with Greek amphictyony:** Unlike the Greek model, the elders' authority had no sacral function and was not connected to a temple. The "primitive democracy" was secular — the religious element was the judges.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the description of the judges as charismatic leaders operating in a non-centralized system; MEDIUM on the claim that this constitutes a unique "kingdom of God" political theology; LOW on the claim that this was fundamentally different from Greek amphictyony.

**What's at stake:** Whether the pre-monarchic period genuinely embodied a distinctive religious-political ideal (Kaufmann) or was just a typical segmentary tribal society whose charismatic leaders were later theologized by biblical editors (critical view).

**Who disagrees:** Cross (Parts II-III) explicitly compares Israel's league to Greek amphictyonies, arguing that central sanctuaries (Shechem, Shiloh, Bethel) DID have sacral functions for the league. Fleming (Ch 6) describes the "people of Yahweh" as a collaborative political identity but not a formal theocratic institution. Critical scholarship generally sees the "judges" framework as a Deuteronomistic construction imposed on older, disparate local hero stories.

**Alternative reading:** The judges were local military leaders whose stories were collected, expanded, and given a national-theological framework by later editors. The "kingdom of God" concept is a theological retrojection — it describes how later Israel understood its past, not how that past was actually experienced. The fluidity between local and national that Kaufmann observes is real — but it reflects the nature of segmentary tribal organization generally, not a unique Israelite "theocracy." The Greek amphictyony comparison that Kaufmann rejects actually illuminates the Israelite league's sacral character — as Cross argues, the central sanctuary at Shiloh (where the ark resided) was precisely a league sanctuary.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's description of the judges as charismatic, spirit-empowered, non-consecutive leaders is accurate to the biblical portrayal. His argument against the "local heroes later nationalized" view has some force — the Song of Deborah (Judg 5) is genuinely ancient and already treats the battle as a national event. But Kaufmann's insistence on "uniqueness" again overreaches. The pattern of charismatic military leaders emerging in segmentary societies under external threat is well-attested cross-culturally. The distinctive Israelite element isn't the *pattern* but the *theology* — the interpretation of these leaders as agents of a single divine king. That theology may be early or may be a later interpretive framework; Kaufmann assumes the former without adequately arguing for it.

## Claim 4: Israelite cult and sanctuaries were indigenous YHWHistic creations, not Canaanite borrowings

**Author's claim:** Despite the proliferation of sanctuaries, high places, pillars, and trees after settlement, "none of the prophets ever reproach Israel with having worshiped YHWH in pagan temples." The sanctity of Israelite cult sites doesn't derive from Canaanite tradition — it originates in patriarchal revelations. Temple legends never antedate the patriarchs; "not one Israelite temple has a history antedating the patriarchs." Even the Bethel theophany (Gen 28:10-22), the "closest biblical approach to a pagan temple legend," is "thoroughly Israelite in all its symbols" — the ladder is not a means to apotheosis but serves the angels of YHWH. Agricultural elements in the cult "betray no Canaanite influence" — YHWH blesses produce as universal God, not as a Baal whose life processes are bound to the soil.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Josh 22:9-34 reflects the transitional sanctity crisis:** While the ideal boundary is the Jordan, and only Canaan is "the land of YHWH" where "the tabernacle of YHWH stands," the restriction of sacrifice to the tabernacle altar became obsolete once Israel rooted in the land. The land's sanctity "overshadowed that of the tent," and sanctuaries arose everywhere.
- **No takeover of Canaanite temples:** "The particulars of the conquest speak against this notion." The Josephides slaughtered Bethel's entire population (Judg 1:24ff) — "here, then, there was no possibility of continuing the local cultic tradition." The Danites burned Laish and built new (Judg 18:27-28). Jerusalem's temple is on Araunah's threshing floor with a new altar (2 Sam 24:24f).
- **Temple legends differ from pagan pattern:** Pagan myth links sacred sites to gods' lives — "gods built and lived in the temples; in some cases the temples housed their graves." Israelite temple legends "date the origin of the sanctity of the sites to late times." Patriarchs erect altars and pillars but "do not build temples." Local sanctity "has no autochthonous roots."
- **Bethel analyzed:** The ladder connecting heaven and earth is a familiar pagan motif, but "while the pagan idea represents the ladder as a link between men and the gods and a means to apotheosis, this ladder serves the angels and agents of YHWH." The site's holiness is "primordial" but as "the portal of God's rule over the earth," not a divine dwelling.
- **Agricultural cult not Baalized:** YHWH as fertility source "does not make him a Canaanite Baal." Pagan Baal's life processes are bound to the soil; his festivals "are designed to revive and reinvigorate him." Such motifs are "entirely absent" from Judges-era materials. "Nor is there a hint of a struggle against such ideas and rituals" — they simply aren't there.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the negative observation (the temple legends really ARE different from pagan patterns); LOW on the inference that this proves Israelite cult was always free of Canaanite influence.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite religion was fundamentally transformed by Canaanite agricultural religion (the "Canaanization" thesis) or maintained its distinctive character even as it adopted agricultural forms.

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological and textual evidence for cultic continuity is substantial. Standing stones (*maṣṣebot*), sacred trees (*asherim*), and high places (*bamot*) were standard Canaanite cultic furniture before they were Israelite. Lewis (Ch 4) documents El worship at sites that later became Israelite sanctuaries. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions pair "YHWH and his Asherah" — exactly the kind of consort theology Kaufmann claims is absent. The prophets' constant polemic against high places, pillars, and *asherim* suggests these were indigenous Israelite practices the prophets opposed, not foreign imports.

**Alternative reading:** The Israelite cult DID borrow Canaanite forms (sanctuary types, cult objects, festival patterns) but interpreted them within a YHWH-only theological framework. The "absence of struggle" against Baalized agricultural motifs that Kaufmann notes is actually an argument FROM SILENCE — the texts we have from the Judges period (mostly the Song of Deborah and Judges narrative framework) are so sparse that we can't conclude absence of such ideas from absence of polemic against them. By the time we have substantial texts (8th century prophets), the struggle against Baalization of YHWH cult IS exactly what we find (Hosea in particular). Kaufmann's claim that Bethel's population was "slaughtered" so there was "no possibility" of cultic continuity assumes the biblical conquest account is historically accurate — precisely what's in question.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right about how the biblical texts *narrate* the origins of sanctuary sanctity — it's always through patriarchal theophany, never through primordial divine presence. This IS a genuine difference from pagan temple legends. But he systematically understates the material continuity. Standing stones, sacred trees, high places — these were the standard cultic furniture of the region, and Israelites used them. The question is whether using the same furniture means worshipping the same way. Kaufmann says no — the furniture was reinterpreted. The critical view says yes, at least partially — the furniture carried implicit theological content. The truth is probably in between: forms were borrowed but gradually reinterpreted, with the reinterpretation being uneven and contested (as the prophets' constant polemic demonstrates).

## Claim 5: The Judges-era "idolatry" was a superficial vestigial fetishism — not genuine polytheism

**Author's claim:** Biblical historiography divides Israelite history into alternating periods of loyalty and idolatry — the period of the judges being the first "idolatrous period." But "the artificiality of this period division is apparent." The schematic framework of Judges "knows only of a generalized worship of Baals and Ashtoreths, without being able to supply further details." This "idolatry" was "a vestigial fetishistic idolatry that was found among the ignorant masses throughout the entire pre-exilic period, and to which women seem to have been particularly addicted." Even Joshua's supposedly loyal generation needed his parting demand to "remove the foreign gods which are in your midst" (Josh 24:23). The entire scheme reflects "the historiosophic idealism of the Bible, according to which every national distress is the result of apostasy."

**Evidence presented:**
- **The schematic framework is contentless:** Judges knows only "a generalized worship of Baals and Ashtoreths" — no specifics about myths, rituals, or theology.
- **Joshua's generation wasn't pure:** Josh 24:23 shows that even the "non-idolatrous" conquest generation harbored foreign gods — the division into idolatrous and non-idolatrous periods is artificial. "Since this age enjoyed victory and success, the biblical historiographer could not regard it as guilty of idolatry."
- **No creative pagan influence detectable:** "No trace of any creative influence of paganism can be found in our sources for this period. There is no battle against mythological beliefs. There are no temples or priests to any foreign god." The judges come "in the name of YHWH only; no other god raises prophets or judges to challenge the sole dominion of YHWH over the national life of Israel."
- **Wafer-thin syncretism:** The idolatry Kaufmann concedes existed was the worship of physical objects by the ignorant — not engagement with Canaanite mythology. Zealots regarded even this "tolerated" fetishism as sinful enough to explain national distress.

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Kaufmann's most strained argument in the chapter — he's essentially arguing that all the biblical evidence of Judges-era apostasy describes something so insubstantial it doesn't count as genuine polytheism.

**What's at stake:** Whether the biblical portrayal of the Judges as a period of cyclical apostasy reflects historical reality (in which case Kaufmann's "original monotheism" thesis is in trouble) or a schematic theological overlay (in which case it's compatible with Kaufmann's view that the underlying reality was monotheistic).

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves, read straightforwardly. Judges 2:11-13: "The Israelites did evil in the eyes of YHWH and served the Baals... They forsook YHWH and served Baal and the Ashtoreths." Judges 6:25-32: Gideon's father has an altar to Baal and an Asherah pole — and Gideon tears them down at YHWH's command, nearly getting killed by the townspeople. Judges 8:33: After Gideon's death, "the Israelites again prostituted themselves to the Baals and set up Baal-Berith as their god." This is not "ignorant masses" worshipping objects — it involves named deities, dedicated altars, and community identity. Smith (Ch 1) documents that the Judges period shows active worship of Canaanite deities alongside or instead of YHWH.

**Alternative reading:** The Judges narratives preserve genuine memories of a period when YHWH worship competed with — and often lost to — Canaanite cults. The Deuteronomistic framework *systematizes* this into a cycle of apostasy-punishment-deliverance, but the underlying reality of religious competition is historical. Kaufmann's attempt to reduce this to "vestigial fetishism" fails because the texts describe community-wide adoption of named foreign gods with dedicated cult sites — exactly what genuine polytheism looks like. The "absence of mythological content" in the polemic is Kaufmann's standard argument (from Ch I), but it proves literary conventions, not religious reality.

**My assessment:** This is where the Kaufmannian method shows its deepest cracks. He has defined "genuine polytheism" so that it requires elaborate mythology (theogonies, divine biographies, cosmic dramas). By this definition, almost no popular religion in history would qualify — most people throughout history have worshipped gods without knowing the elite mythological literature about them. The fact that Judges describes Israelites "serving the Baals" without reciting Baal mythology doesn't mean they weren't genuinely polytheistic; it means the biblical authors weren't interested in reporting Canaanite theology. Kaufmann's claim that "no other god raises prophets or judges" is a genuine observation — the judges do operate exclusively in YHWH's name — but this proves only that the *YHWH cult* produced charismatic leaders, not that other gods weren't worshipped. The Gideon story (Judg 6:25-32) is devastating to Kaufmann's thesis: Gideon's own father maintains a Baal altar and Asherah pole in Ophrah, and the townspeople want to kill Gideon for destroying them. That's not "ignorant masses"; that's community-level polytheistic practice.

## Claim 6: The conquest-settlement period had a genuine eschatological quality — ancient promises were being fulfilled

**Author's claim:** "The military success and the settlement gave rise to pride and a sense of well-being. The period had an eschatological quality; ancient promises were being fulfilled. This was 'the end of days,' the final, permanent bliss. All the ancient visionary and prophetic literature is informed by this sentiment." The blessings of Jacob (Gen 49) and Moses (Deut 33) reflect pre-monarchic conditions and give "clear expression" to this eschatological confidence. Gen 49 is "the earliest vision dealing expressly with the 'end of days.'"

**Evidence presented:**
- **Gen 49 and Deut 33 reflect pre-monarchic conditions:** "The tribes fight, but are always victorious; they have settled and enjoy the bounty of the earth."
- **Deut 33:26-29 as eschatological climax:** "YHWH has driven out the enemy and has commanded, 'Destroy!' He has given Israel a land of grain and wine, and has settled it in insulated security. Happy Israel! There is none like God, nor any like Israel, 'a people saved through YHWH.' Its enemies will dwindle away before it and Israel will tread on their backs."
- **The horizon is bright:** "Despite an occasional cloud," the vision is ultimately one of confidence and fulfillment.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the observation that early poetry has an eschatological tone; LOW-MEDIUM on the claim that this reflects *the actual experience* of the conquest-settlement generation rather than later literary retrojection.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israel's eschatological hope developed in response to later historical *failure* (the prophetic critique, the fall of the kingdoms) or was already present as a celebratory mode in the earliest period of success. This matters for dating the origins of Israelite eschatology.

**Who disagrees:** Most critical scholarship dates Gen 49 and Deut 33 — in their current form — considerably later than the settlement period. The blessings of Jacob contain references that seem to presuppose the monarchy (Gen 49:10, "the scepter shall not depart from Judah"). The "end of days" language (*be'acharit hayamim*) is typically associated with later prophetic eschatology. Cross would see the Divine Warrior hymns of the early period (Exod 15, Judg 5) as hymnic celebration rather than "eschatology" in the developed sense.

**Alternative reading:** The early poetry DOES contain a confident, triumphant mode — but calling it "eschatology" is anachronistic. Eschatology proper (the idea of a definitive future transformation of the world order) develops in the prophetic period as a response to the failure of the monarchy and the destruction of the kingdoms. The early poetry's confidence is *prospective* in a different sense — it celebrates current triumphs as evidence of YHWH's power and Israel's election, not as the "end of days." The *be'acharit hayamim* framework in Gen 49 is a later editorial frame, not the original Sitz im Leben of the tribal sayings.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's eschatology claim is creative but underargued — it takes up less than a page in the chapter and feels like an appendix rather than a fully developed argument. He's right that Deut 33:26-29 has a triumphant, even utopian quality. But whether this reflects the "eschatological quality" of the conquest generation or the retrospective idealization of a later poet is not established. The claim that Gen 49 is "the earliest vision dealing expressly with the 'end of days'" depends on dating Gen 49 to the Judges period, which is contested. The "end of days" framing may well be a later editorial addition to earlier tribal sayings. This is the weakest sustained argument in the chapter.

## Ch VII Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Unified national plan of conquest | MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence for gradual settlement; ideal boundaries may reflect vision, not plan |
| 2: Ḥerem historically destroyed Canaanite population | MEDIUM-LOW | Archaeological continuity; "real land"/"ideal land" distinction is definitionally circular |
| 3: Tribal league as "kingdom of God" theocracy | MEDIUM-HIGH (description) / MEDIUM (uniqueness) | Greek amphictyony comparison; judges framework as Deuteronomistic scheme |
| 4: Sanctuaries and cult indigenous, not Canaanite borrowings | MEDIUM (observation) / LOW (inference) | Archaeological continuity in cultic furniture; Kuntillet Ajrud; prophetic polemic against precisely these forms |
| 5: Judges-idolatry as vestigial fetishism | LOW | Gideon's Baal altar (Judg 6:25-32); named deities; community-level worship; Kaufmann defines "polytheism" so narrowly it excludes almost all popular religion |
| 6: Early eschatological quality of conquest-settlement | LOW-MEDIUM | Dating of Gen 49/Deut 33; "eschatology" as anachronistic category; brevity of argument |

**Strongest sections:**
1. **The Gibeonite argument (Claim 2).** Kaufmann's observation that the Gibeonites survived as a segregated caste for nearly a millennium — becoming Nethinim hierodules, returning from exile as a distinct class — is genuinely striking and underappreciated. If Canaanites were peacefully absorbed, why did this ONE group that made a treaty remain permanently distinct? The *ḥerem* status does provide a coherent explanation for the data. This deserves more scholarly attention than it's received.
2. **The absence of Canaanites as a military factor after Deborah (Claim 2).** The observation that Judges-era enemies are non-Canaanite (Moabites, Ammonites, Midianites, Philistines) is a real datum. If Canaanites survived in large numbers, their absence from the military record (except Barak/Deborah, fought on the boundary) requires explanation. The "enclaves only" model at least accounts for this.
3. **The ideal boundaries argument (Claim 1).** The limits of the promised land don't match any historical Israelite state — they're too expansive in the north and exclude Transjordan. This is genuinely hard to explain on a model where the boundaries were invented to justify later territorial ambitions. Kaufmann is right that they seem to predate settlement.

**Weakest sections:**
1. **"Vestigial fetishism" in the Judges period (Claim 5).** This is where Kaufmann's method most visibly breaks down. He has to explain away the entire narrative framework of Judges — repeated cycles of apostasy to named gods with dedicated altars — as describing something so superficial it doesn't count as real polytheism. The Gideon story alone (Judg 6:25-32) is fatal: Gideon's father maintains a Baal altar and Asherah pole, and the community rallies to defend them. This is population-level polytheistic practice, not "ignorant masses" engaged in "vestigial fetishism."
2. **The "no Canaanite cultural influence" claim (Claim 4).** Kaufmann's argument that agricultural cult shows "no Canaanite influence" because it lacks Baal's dying-and-rising mythology is circular — he defines "influence" so that it requires the full mythological package, then triumphantly notes its absence. But influence can operate at the level of practice and form without carrying the full theological freight. The archaeological and epigraphic evidence (standing stones, Asherah poles, "YHWH and his Asherah") shows material continuity that Kaufmann's literary analysis can't dissolve.
3. **The eschatology claim (Claim 6).** Underdeveloped, dependent on contested dating, and arguably anachronistic. Feels tacked on.

**What Kaufmann gets right that critics often miss:**
- The Gibeonite/Nethinim data is genuinely anomalous on a peaceful-absorption model and deserves more attention.
- The absence of inter-tribal territorial disputes in Judges IS striking and not well explained by gradual settlement models.
- The ideal boundaries of the promised land don't match any historical Israelite state — they likely preserve genuinely early tradition.
- The disappearance of Canaanite military terminology, chariotry, and the city-state from within Israelite settlement territory is a real historical datum.
- The temple legends' distinctive pattern (sanctity from patriarchal theophany, not primordial divine dwelling) is a real contrast with ANE norms.

**What Kaufmann misses that critics get right:**
- The archaeological evidence for material and demographic continuity between Late Bronze and Iron I highland settlements is substantial and can't be explained away by the "real land"/"ideal land" distinction.
- The Judges narratives, however schematized by Deuteronomistic editors, preserve genuine memories of religious competition between YHWH worship and Canaanite cults.
- The "kingdom of God" theocracy is a theological idealization of what was likely a more contingent military league — the charismatic judges pattern is real but not unique.
- The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions (discovered after Kaufmann's death) show that even in the monarchy period, YHWH was paired with Asherah in popular religion — a direct challenge to his "no genuine polytheism" thesis.
- The Ugaritic texts show that the divine council, Chaoskampf, and El's patriarchal role — all present in early Israelite poetry — were shared Canaanite mythological frameworks. The Bible's "non-mythological" character was an achievement, not a starting point.

**Methodological note:** This chapter reveals both the strengths and the fatal weaknesses of Kaufmann's method. He is a brilliant close reader of the biblical text — his observations about ideal boundaries, the Gibeonites, the absence of boundary disputes, and the disappearance of Canaanite political forms are genuinely acute and deserve engagement. But his method is fundamentally circular: he uses the Bible's *theological* presentation (the conquest as a unified holy war, the *ḥerem* as total destruction, the judges as YHWH's apostle-saviors) as evidence for *historical* reality, while simultaneously dismissing the Bible's *own* evidence of apostasy and syncretism (the Judges cycles, the Baal altars, the Asherah poles) as theological schematization. He trusts the biblical text when it supports his thesis and explains it away when it doesn't. This is not a method — it's an apologetic dressed as scholarship. The Gibeonite argument is genuinely interesting and should be part of any serious discussion of the conquest. But the chapter as a whole is an object lesson in how a brilliant reader can be led astray by an unfalsifiable thesis.

**Kuntillet Ajrud gap:** The inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud (discovered 1975-76) and Khirbet el-Qom (discovered 1967) — both after Kaufmann's death in 1963 — are the single most significant external challenge to this chapter's thesis. They show that in the 9th-8th centuries BCE, Israelites invoked "YHWH of Samaria and his Asherah" and "YHWH of Teman and his Asherah" in blessing formulas. This is not "vestigial fetishism" — it's everyday religious language pairing YHWH with a consort. If this was the reality in the monarchy period, the Judges period was almost certainly more syncretistic, not less. Kaufmann cannot be faulted for not addressing evidence that didn't exist during his lifetime, but any contemporary evaluation must note that the archaeological/epigraphic record has moved decisively against him on this point.

**Cross-reference engagement:**
- **Cross (Parts II-III)** would agree that the tribal league had a sacral character centered on YHWH as Divine Warrior, but would locate its institutional center at a league sanctuary (Shechem, Shiloh) — a point Kaufmann explicitly rejects. Cross sees the amphictyony model as illuminating, not misleading.
- **Smith (Ch 1)** documents active worship of Canaanite deities in the Judges period — Baal, Asherah, Astarte — directly contradicting Kaufmann's "vestigial fetishism" characterization. The archaeological evidence Smith marshals (figurines, cult stands, inscriptions) shows population-level polytheistic practice.
- **Lewis (Ch 4)** traces El worship at sites that became Israelite sanctuaries — the patriarchal theophanies that Kaufmann treats as evidence of Israelite originality may reflect continuity with El worship at those same sites. The "gate of heaven" at Bethel may have been an El sanctuary before it was a YHWH sanctuary.
- **Fleming (Ch 6)** describes the "people of Yahweh" as a collaborative, non-state identity that coalesced around shared worship and military cooperation — compatible with Kaufmann's "kingdom of God" model in some ways, but Fleming sees it as emerging gradually through practice, not as a prearranged theocratic plan.
