# Day — Chapter 4: Yahweh's Appropriation of Baal Imagery

The longest and most ambitious chapter. Day argues that Yahweh absorbed massive amounts of Baal's mythological portfolio: storm theophany, cloud-riding, thunder-voice, dragon-slaying, the sacred mountain Zaphon, and death-and-resurrection imagery. This is not peripheral borrowing — it's wholesale appropriation of Baal's defining characteristics.

---

## Claim 1: Psalm 29 is a Yahwistic adaptation of Canaanite Baal mythology, including Baal's seven thunders

**Author's claim:** "There can thus be no doubt that Psalm 29 stands remarkably close to the circle of mythological ideas surrounding Baal as they are attested in the Ugaritic texts" (p. 98). Some scholars (Ginsberg, Gaster, Cross, Fitzgerald) go further and claim it's a Canaanite psalm taken over wholesale with Yahweh's name substituted for Baal. Day is more cautious: it's "an Israelite composition largely modelled on the language used by the Canaanites about Baal" (p. 98).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Yahweh's voice = thunder = Baal's voice:** In Ps 29, Yahweh's "voice" (*qôl*) is the thunder — exactly as Baal's voice is thunder in Ugaritic texts (KTU² 1.4.VII.29-31) (p. 97)
2. **Kingship over cosmic waters:** Yahweh enthroned "over the flood" (v. 10) parallels Baal's enthronement "like the flood" in KTU² 1.101.1-3a (p. 97-98)
3. **Seven thunders:** Day's original contribution — the sevenfold *qôl yahweh* (vv. 3a, 4a, 4b, 5, 7, 8, 9) corresponds to Baal's "seven lightnings... eight storehouses of thunder" in KTU² 1.101.3b-4. The Ugaritic 7/8 sequence is "automatic parallelism" — it means seven (pp. 97-98)
4. **Divine assembly:** "Sons of gods" (*benê ʼēlîm*) in v. 1 are the divine council — derived from sons of El at Ugarit (p. 97)
5. **Geographic clues:** The cedars of Lebanon and Sirion (vv. 5-6) are northwest Semitic, not Israelite heartland — consistent with a psalm originally from the Baal-worshipping region (p. 98)
6. **Against wholesale Canaanite origin:** v. 8's reference to "the wilderness of Kadesh" and Yahweh's Sinai theophany "would appear to militate against the view that Psalm 29 is nothing more than a Canaanite psalm with the simple substitution of Yahweh for Baal" (p. 98-99)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is one of the most widely accepted cases of direct Canaanite borrowing in the entire OT. The seven thunders parallel that Day himself discovered adds additional weight.

**What's at stake:** Psalm 29 is in the canonical Psalter. If it's essentially a Baal hymn with Yahweh's name inserted, the line between Yahwism and Baalism was thinner than the OT's own polemic suggests.

**Who disagrees:** S. Mittmann (original psalm had only fivefold *qôl yahweh*) and O. Loretz (six thunders, v. 7 secondary). Day argues the seven-thunder parallel to Ugaritic proves the sevenfold structure is original. Loretz also separates the psalm into three originally independent compositions (El + two Baal traditions) — Day finds this "overly speculative" (p. 99).

**My assessment:** The seven thunders discovery is genuinely important — it means the psalm's structure IS the theology. You can't remove a *qôl yahweh* without breaking the Ugaritic pattern. This is Day's field (he wrote his 1985 monograph *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea* on this material), and his expertise shows.

## Claim 2: The OT dragon/sea conflict derives from Baal's defeat of Yam/Leviathan, NOT from Babylonian Marduk/Tiamat

**Author's claim:** "Since the discovery of the Ugaritic texts from 1929 onwards, it has become generally accepted that the Old Testament's references to a divine conflict with a dragon and the sea are an echo of Canaanite rather than Babylonian mythology" (p. 99-100). Gunkel's late 19th-century Babylonian theory is superseded.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Name correspondence:** OT Leviathan (*liwyātān*) = Ugaritic Litan (*ltn*), the seven-headed dragon defeated by Baal and Anat (KTU² 1.5.I.1-3; 1.3.III.40-42). OT describes Leviathan as "twisting serpent" (*nāḥāš bārîaḥ*) — almost identical to Ugaritic (p. 100)
2. **Also called Rahab in OT** (Ps 89:11; Job 26:12-13) — not attested extra-biblically but presumably alternative name
3. **Multiple heads:** Ps 74:14 refers to Leviathan's multiple heads — at Ugarit he had seven heads
4. **Creation context:** OT associates dragon conflict with creation (Ps 74:12-17; 89:10-15; Job 26:12-13). Ugaritic Baal-Yam conflict is not explicitly a creation account, but the Mari text (Zimri-Lim era) has Adad declaring "I gave you the weapons with which I fought against Sea (*tâmtum*)" — possibly connecting these traditions (p. 100)
5. **Tehom ≠ Tiamat directly:** Gen 1:2's *tehôm* "deep" lacks the feminine ending that Tiamat has. The word *thm* occurs in Ugaritic, so "it is more natural to assume a Canaanite prototype" (p. 102)
6. **Behemoth = Ugaritic Arsh:** The name means "great ox" — Ugaritic texts twice mention a mythical ox-like creature alongside Leviathan called Arsh or "El's calf Atik" (KTU² 1.3.III.40-44; 1.6.VI.51-53). Behemoth dwells in a river (Job 40:23), and in the second Ugaritic allusion Arsh is "in the sea" (p. 103-104)
7. **NOT natural animals:** Behemoth and Leviathan in Job 40-41 are not hippopotamus/crocodile — Leviathan breathes fire (Job 41:10-13), and both are presented as unconquerable by humans (p. 103)

**Three modes of OT reuse:**
- **Creation** (Ps 74, 89, 104; Job 26, 38)
- **Historicization** (Rahab = Egypt in Isa 30:7; Pharaoh = dragon in Ezek 29:3-5; Assyrians = chaos waters in Isa 17:12-14)
- **Eschatologization** (Isa 27:1; Daniel 7; Revelation 12-13)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is the scholarly consensus since Ugarit. The name correspondences (Leviathan/Litan) are precise. The shift from Gunkel's Babylonian theory to the Canaanite theory is one of the most important discoveries of 20th-century biblical scholarship.

**What's at stake:** The Chaoskampf is one of the most pervasive mythological motifs in the OT. If it's Baal's victory over Yam, not Marduk's over Tiamat, then Israel's theological imagination was shaped by Canaan, not Mesopotamia. Genesis 1 is a demythologization of the same tradition.

**My assessment:** Day's expertise on this topic is peerless — his 1985 monograph is the standard reference. The Leviathan/Litan name correspondence alone is close to decisive. The Behemoth/Arsh connection is less secure but still plausible. The "demythologization" thesis (Genesis 1 tames the battle into a "job of work") is elegant and widely accepted.

## Claim 3: Mt Zaphon (Baal's mountain) was transferred to Yahweh's dwelling — both heaven and Jerusalem

**Author's claim:** The name Zaphon (*ṣāpôn*), originally Baal's mountain in Syria (Jebel el-Aqraʻ), was appropriated in the OT to refer both to "the firmament of heaven as the cosmic mountain on which Yahweh dwells" and to Mt Zion/Jerusalem (p. 111-112). The transfer to Jerusalem was likely "mediated through the Jebusite cult of El-Elyon, rather than being directly taken over from Baal" (p. 117).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Identification secure:** Ugaritic administrative tablets in Akkadian and Ugaritic "make certain the identification of Mt Hazi (Casius) and Mt ṣpn (Zaphon)" as Jebel el-Aqraʻ in Syria (p. 108-109)
2. **Ps 48:3 (ET 2):** Jerusalem is "the heights of Zaphon" (*yarketê ṣāpôn*) — prior to Ugarit this made no sense (Jerusalem is not in the far north!). Now understood as applying Baal's sacred mountain name to Zion (p. 109-110)
3. **Papyrus Amherst 63:** A paganized version of Psalm 20 replaces Zion with Zaphon — "Send your emissary from the temple of Arash, and from Zaphon may Horus sustain us" (lines 13-14) vs. Ps 20:3 "May he send you help from the sanctuary and from Zion may he sustain you" (p. 110)
4. **Isa 14:13:** The Shining One tries to ascend to "the mount of assembly on the heights of Zaphon" (*yarketê ṣāpôn*) — Zaphon as the cosmic mountain/firmament (p. 110-111)
5. **Job 26:7:** "He stretches out Zaphon over the void" — Zaphon equated with the vault of heaven (p. 111)
6. **Ezek 1:4:** Storm theophany comes "out of Zaphon" — Yahweh's heavenly dwelling (p. 112)
7. **Exod 15:17:** "The mountain of your inheritance... the place for your dwelling" uses "Zaphonic" language derived from descriptions of Baal's dwelling at Ugarit — most naturally refers to Jerusalem (p. 115-116)

**Against reading Zaphon as simply "north" in these passages:** The word came to mean "north" in Hebrew BECAUSE the mountain was north of Israel. But context in Ps 48, Isa 14, and Job 26 demands the mountain meaning.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Papyrus Amherst 63 evidence (Zion→Zaphon substitution) is a remarkable confirmation. The pre-Ugaritic translations that rendered Ps 48:3 as "far north" demonstrate the philological problem the discovery solved.

**What's at stake:** Jerusalem's sacred geography is built on Baal's. Mt Zion inherited the mythological status of Mt Zaphon — the cosmic mountain where deity dwells, where the forces of chaos attack, and where the divine king reigns victorious. The Jerusalem temple ideology is Canaanite at its foundations.

**Alternative reading:** Eissfeldt and others read Ps 89:13 (ET 12) as naming four sacred mountains including Zaphon. Day argues *ṣāpôn* and *yāmîn* more naturally mean "north and south" here (p. 113), with Tabor and Hermon as the center.

**My assessment:** The Zaphon → Zion transfer is one of Day's most elegant arguments. The combination of Ugaritic geography, Papyrus Amherst 63, and the Isa 14/Job 26 heavenly Zaphon is mutually reinforcing. The El-Elyon mediation theory fits neatly with Chapter 1's arguments.

## Claim 4: Baal was genuinely a dying and rising god, and Hosea appropriated this imagery to describe Israel's exile and restoration — which eventually led (via Isaiah 26) to Daniel's literal resurrection doctrine

**Author's claim (the chain):** Baal (dying and rising god) → Hosea 5-6 and 13-14 (death/resurrection imagery for national exile/restoration) → Isaiah 26:19 (same, still metaphorical) → Daniel 12:2 (literal individual resurrection, "remythologized"). Jewish resurrection belief has a Canaanite genealogy, not a Zoroastrian one.

**Evidence presented (Baal as dying and rising god):**
1. Ugaritic Baal myth: Baal swallowed by Mot (Death), repeatedly declared dead, buried and lamented, land becomes dry — then El has vision, declares "Mightiest Baal is alive," Baal resumes throne (KTU² 1.4.VIII-1.6.VI) (p. 118)
2. Against Mark Smith (merely "disappearing and returning"): The two myths use "clearly distinct language" — Telepinus VANISHES; Baal DIES and is declared ALIVE. "The contrast between life and death is basic to the myth" (citing Mettinger) (p. 118-119)
3. Against the "substitute" theory (de Moor, Gibson): "There is no indication of this in the text" (Waterston) (p. 119)
4. Zech 12:11: "The mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the valley of Megiddo" — Hadad = Baal, confirming death-and-mourning ritual survived into monarchic period (p. 119)

**Evidence presented (Hosea uses Baal's death/resurrection imagery):**
1. **Hos 13:1 is deliberately ironical:** "Ephraim incurred guilt through Baal and DIED." For Hosea, it's not Baal who dies and rises — it's ISRAEL who dies through Baal worship, then (if repentant) rises through Yahweh (p. 121)
2. **Death, not illness:** Hiphil of *qûm* "raise up" and piel of *ḥāyâ* "revive" in Hos 6:2 — "All the other places in the OT where these two verbs appear as word pairs the meaning clearly relates to resurrection from death, not simply healing" (Isa 26:14, 19; Job 14:12, 14) (p. 120)
3. **Lion imagery = death:** In Hos 5:14 Yahweh is a lion carrying off prey "and none shall rescue" — elsewhere in OT (Amos 3:12; Jer 2:30) this implies certain death. Confirmed by Hos 13:7-9 where lion = destruction (p. 120)
4. **Parallel images between Hos 5-6 and 13-14 prove death:** Both use lion imagery, "return" language, healing imagery, rain/dew — and Hos 13 explicitly speaks of death. Therefore Hos 5-6 must also mean death, not illness (pp. 120-121)
5. **Rain/dew connection:** Israel's resurrection associated with rain (Hos 6:3) and dew (Hos 14:6) — "This is striking, since in the Ugaritic Baal myth we read that Baal took the rain and two of the dew goddesses with him when he went into the underworld, and it is implied that they reappeared when he rose again" (p. 121)
6. **The third day:** NOT derived from a fertility god's resurrection timetable (Osiris, Attis, Adonis — all too late). "After two days... on the third day" is "a poetic way of saying 'after a short while'" — cf. Hebrew *ʼetmôl šilšōm* "formerly" and Lk 13:32-33 (pp. 122-123)

**Evidence presented (Isaiah 26 depends on Hosea 13-14):**
- Eight parallels between Hosea 13-14 and Isaiah 26-27, all in the same order (with one minor exception) — Day's own discovery, published in *JTS* 1980 (pp. 122-123)
- Hos 13:14 ("Shall I ransom them from Sheol?") is the opposite of Isa 26:19 ("Your dead shall live") — but the LXX and ancient versions interpret Hos 13:14 positively, and "this was clearly how the author of Isa 26:19 interpreted it" (p. 123-124)
- Both passages have birth-pangs imagery immediately before (Hos 13:13; Isa 26:17-18) — "something too remarkable to be ascribed to mere coincidence" (p. 124)
- Isa 26:19 is STILL metaphorical (national restoration, like Ezekiel 37). The decisive proof: the very next chapter (Isa 27:8) speaks of exile — and both verses correspond to adjacent verses in Hosea 13 (vv. 14 and 15) (p. 125)

**Evidence presented (Daniel 12 depends on Isaiah 26):**
- Both use "awake" (*qîṣ*) from "dust" (*ʻāpār*) (p. 125)
- Daniel 12:2's unique word *dērāʼôn* "abhorrence" occurs only one other place: Isa 66:24, also eschatological judgment (p. 125)
- Dan 12:3's "those who turn many to righteousness" (*maṣdîqê hārabbîm*) echoes Isa 53:11's suffering Servant (vicarious death applied to Maccabean martyrs) (p. 125-126)
- Now "remythologized" — literal individual resurrection, not national restoration

**Against Zoroastrian origin:** Jewish resurrection speaks of "awakening from sleep" (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2; 4 Ezra 7:32) — "such a concept is completely alien to the Zoroastrians." But Canaanite tradition preserves exactly this: Melqart's resurrection festival was called *egersis* "awakening" (Menander, in Josephus *Ant.* 8.5.3), and Ugaritic texts call Aqhat's death "sleep" (*šnt*) (KTU² 1.19.III.45) (pp. 126-127)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the overall chain. The Hosea-Baal connection is strong. The Isaiah-Hosea dependence (eight parallels in order) is convincing. The Daniel-Isaiah dependence is solid. The weakest link is whether Baal was genuinely a "dying and rising" god or merely "disappearing and returning" — Day himself engages this debate and argues persuasively for the former, but it's contested.

**What's at stake:** This is the single most important chain of influence Day traces in the entire book. If correct, the doctrine of bodily resurrection — arguably the most distinctive feature of Jewish and Christian eschatology — originated in Canaanite Baal mythology, was demythologized by Hosea into political metaphor, and was remythologized by Daniel into literal afterlife hope. This is a radically different genealogy than either Zoroastrian influence or divine revelation.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (Baal is "disappearing and returning," not dying and rising). H.M. Barstad (the words don't imply resurrection). Those favoring Zoroastrian origin (Bousset, Boyce, Lang). Those who read Hos 5-6 as illness/healing, not death/resurrection (Mays, Wolff, Rudolph, Davies).

**My assessment:** Day's eight-parallel argument for Isaiah's dependence on Hosea is airtight — that's his own research and it's been well-received. The Baal-as-dying-god argument is the most contested link. Mettinger's upcoming book (which Day references as forthcoming) would later provide the most thorough defense of dying and rising gods. The Zoroastrian counter-argument is weakened by the "sleep/awakening" language difference — if Israel got resurrection from Persia, why does the vocabulary differ so dramatically?

## Claim 5: Yahweh-as-cloud-rider derives from Baal's epithet *rkb ʻrpt* "rider of the clouds"

**Author's claim:** Yahweh riding a cloud chariot in Ps 104:3 and elsewhere "ultimately derives from Baal, whose stock epithet, *rkb ʻrpt*... may be accepted with the majority of scholars to mean 'rider of the clouds'" (p. 91-92). However, the widely-held equation of Ps 68:5's *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* with *rkb ʻrpt* is probably wrong.

**Evidence presented (for Baal origin):**
1. De Moor: The name Be-'-li-ra-kab-bi "Baal of the chariot" is known from Samʻal, and Ramesses III compared himself to Baal driving a chariot (p. 93)
2. Ugaritic *rkb* elsewhere always means "ride, mount" — not "gather" (p. 93)
3. OT texts associating *rkb* with clouds: Deut 33:26; Ps 18:11; 68:34; 104:3; Isa 19:1; 66:16 — "since the Israelites were far more intimately related to the Canaanites than the Greeks, the Old Testament parallels involving rkb are far more relevant" than Zeus epithets (p. 93)
4. Hab 3:8: Yahweh rides horses/chariot of victory against the sea — Zeus defeating Typhon also mounts a winged horse, the conflict taking place at Mt Casius (Zaphon). This motif "is ultimately derived from Baal mythology" (p. 94-95)
5. KTU² 1.5.V.6b-11: Mot commands Baal to descend to the underworld taking "your clouds, your wind, your chariot team (*mdl*), your rain" — Day argues *mdl* = "chariot team" drawing the cloud chariot (pp. 95-96)

**Evidence presented (against Ps 68:5 = cloud rider):**
- *ʻărābâ* in Hebrew means "desert." "It is a sound principle that if a Hebrew word makes good sense in its normally attested meaning, it should be accepted" rather than creating a hapax legomenon (p. 93-94)
- Context fits: vv. 8-9 echo wilderness traditions, v. 7 refers to "the wilderness" (p. 94)
- Isa 40:3 has *bāʻărābâ mesillâ* "a highway in the desert" — using *sll* (same root as Ps 68:5's *sollû*), supporting the "desert" reading (p. 94)
- Nevertheless, "it is still likely that Yahweh is here conceived as riding on a cloud... through the deserts" (p. 94). Day even suggests *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* "may be a deliberate distortion of the epithet *rkb ʻrpt*" (p. 94)

**Confidence:** HIGH for Baal origin of cloud-rider imagery. MEDIUM for the Ps 68:5 emendation debate. Day's position here is admirably restrained — he defends the minority conservative reading while acknowledging the cloud-riding context.

**What's at stake:** "Rider of the clouds" was one of Baal's most characteristic epithets. Its transfer to Yahweh is one of the clearest cases of direct mythological appropriation in the OT. Daniel 7:13 ("one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven") is the apocalyptic endpoint of this same tradition.

**My assessment:** Day's restraint on Ps 68:5 is characteristic — he doesn't let his enthusiasm for Baal parallels override sound philology. The cloud-chariot-horses chain (Ps 104 → Hab 3 → Zeus/Typhon → Baal) is well-constructed. His *mdl* = "chariot team" reading is tentative but plausible.

## Chapter 4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Psalm 29 = Baal hymn adaptation (seven thunders) | VERY HIGH | Loretz/Mittmann's source-critical fragmentations |
| Dragon/sea conflict = Canaanite, not Babylonian | VERY HIGH | Mari *tâmtum* text could complicate the neat Babylonia-vs-Canaan dichotomy |
| Zaphon transferred to Yahweh (heaven + Jerusalem) | HIGH | Ps 89:13 reading debate; El-Elyon mediation is plausible but speculative |
| Baal as dying/rising god → Hosea → Isaiah → Daniel resurrection | MEDIUM-HIGH | Baal as "dying" god contested by Smith/Barstad; Zoroastrian alternative still has proponents |
| Cloud-rider imagery from Baal | HIGH | Ps 68:5 debate; Ullendorff/Brock's "gatherer" reading (minority) |

**Strongest section:** The Hosea → Isaiah → Daniel chain (Day's own research). The eight structural parallels between Hosea 13-14 and Isaiah 26-27 are a genuine scholarly discovery that has held up well.

**Weakest section:** The Baal-as-dying-and-rising-god debate. Day acknowledges the controversy and engages it, but the evidence is more contested than he suggests. Mettinger's subsequent work would help, but the "disappearing and returning" counter-position is not simply rhetorical.

**Most important methodological move:** Day demonstrates a three-stage process: (1) mythological appropriation (Baal → OT), (2) demythologization (cosmic battle → national restoration metaphor), (3) remythologization (metaphor → apocalyptic literal resurrection). This is a genuinely sophisticated literary-historical model, not a crude "borrowing" claim. The entire trajectory from Baal to Daniel 12 is one of Day's most original contributions to the field.
