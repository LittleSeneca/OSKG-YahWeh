# Smith, Early History of God — Introduction

## Claim 1: Israelite culture WAS Canaanite culture — there was no radical separation

**Smith's claim:** The older scholarly consensus (Albright, Kaufmann, Tigay) treated Israel and Canaan as distinct cultures, with Baal/Asherah worship as foreign "syncretism." This is wrong. The archaeological, linguistic, and epigraphic evidence shows Israelite culture was a subset of Canaanite culture. "One cannot maintain a radical cultural separation between 'Canaanites' and Israelites for the Iron I period."

**Evidence presented:**
- Scripts: both linear and cuneiform alphabetic scripts in highlands AND coast/valleys
- Language: Hebrew and Canaanite "cannot be easily distinguished" in Iron I; Isaiah 19:18 calls Hebrew "the language of Canaan"
- Material culture: four-room house, collared-rim jar, hewn cisterns — once thought distinctively Israelite, now attested in coastal and Transjordanian sites
- Burial patterns: continuous from Late Bronze through Iron Age
- Cultic terminology: *zebah*, *šělāmîm*, *minḥah*, *kālîl*, *'ōlāh* all have Ugaritic/Phoenician cognates. *Kōhen* (priest), *qādēš* (cultic functionary), *rb khnm* (chief priest) are Canaanite terms.
- The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208) distinguishes Israel and Canaan — but this shows political distinction, not cultural.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is now archaeological consensus. Even conservative scholars generally accept material culture continuity. The question is what it MEANS for religion, not whether the continuity exists.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the foundation under everything else. If Israelite culture IS Canaanite culture, then the biblical narrative of Israel as a distinct people with a distinct God entering a foreign land is historically false. Israel emerged FROM Canaan. Its God emerged from the Canaanite pantheon. You can't maintain "God revealed himself to a separate people" if the people weren't separate.

**Who disagrees:** Almost no one disputes the material continuity. The disagreement is about whether material culture continuity = religious continuity. Conservatives (Hess, Block, Walton) argue shared material culture doesn't prove shared theology. Minimalists (Thompson, Lemche) argue we can't know enough to draw ANY conclusions.

**Alternative reading:** Shared ANE culture doesn't equal shared religion. Modern Americans and Canadians share language, architecture, and consumer goods but have meaningfully different cultures and self-conceptions. The Israelites could have shared pots and houses with Canaanites while worshiping a fundamentally different God.

**My assessment:** The material continuity is overwhelming and must be the starting point. But Smith sometimes elides "shared material culture" with "shared religion." These are different claims requiring different evidence. The cultic terminology parallels are stronger evidence for religious continuity than the pottery parallels. Track when Smith shifts between "culture" and "religion" — they're not the same thing.

## Claim 2: Baal and Asherah worship was indigenous Israelite religion, not foreign syncretism

**Smith's claim:** This follows directly from Claim 1. If Israel IS Canaanite, then Baal and Asherah weren't foreign imports corrupting pure Yahwism. They were part of Israel's own religious heritage. "Baal and Asherah were part of Israel's 'Canaanite' heritage, and the process of the emergence of Israelite monolatry was an issue of **Israel's breaking with its own 'Canaanite' past.**"

**Evidence presented:** This is the argument the rest of the book builds. The Introduction just states it programmatically. The evidence comes in Chapters 1-4.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that Baal and Asherah were worshipped in Israel (biblical and archaeological evidence is strong). MEDIUM that this represents "indigenous heritage" rather than "syncretism" — the distinction may be semantic. If a practice is 200 years old, is it "indigenous" or "syncretistic"? Depends on your timeframe.

**What's at stake for faith:** Enormous. If Smith is right, the biblical prophets weren't calling Israel back to original purity — they were calling Israel to ABANDON its own heritage for something new. "Yahweh-alone" wasn't conservation; it was revolution. The Bible's own story (pure origins, later corruption) is exactly backward.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue the prophets preserved an original monotheism that had been corrupted. Tigay's onomastics suggests popular religion was more Yahwistic than Smith allows. Kaufmann argued monotheism was the original revolution, not a late development. See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins|the full directory]].

**My assessment:** This is the central claim of Smith's entire project, and it depends entirely on Claim 1. If Israelite culture = Canaanite culture, this follows naturally. If the cultures were more distinct than Smith allows, the "indigenous vs. syncretistic" framing shifts. The evidence for Baal/Asherah WORSHIP (not just cultural borrowing) is strong but not uniform — track when Smith has direct evidence of cultic devotion vs. when he infers it from literary parallels.

## Claim 3: The monarchy played a constructive role in monotheism's development, not just a corrupting one

**Smith's claim:** "The monarchy fostered the inclusion of various deities, or their features, into the cult of Yahweh... In the first half of its existence, the monarchy fostered some features of convergence in exalting Yahweh as the national god." Kings like Hezekiah and Josiah drove the centralization that made monotheism possible. The monarchy gets blamed for the polytheism but deserves partial credit for the monotheism.

**Evidence presented:** Mesopotamian and Egyptian parallels: Marduk absorbed other gods' traits as Babylon rose; Amun-Re did the same in Egypt. Royal psalms (Ps 2, 18, 89) use Baal's storm-warrior language to exalt Yahweh as the dynastic patron. Hezekiah's and Josiah's reforms centralized worship in Jerusalem, creating the conditions for monotheism.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The ANE parallels are solid. The claim that the monarchy "fostered convergence" is plausible but hard to prove — we don't have pre-monarchic texts to compare against. Smith acknowledges this: pre-8th century evidence is "barely enough."

**What's at stake for faith:** If the monarchy drove monotheism's development, then monotheism is a POLITICAL achievement, not a revelation. The God of Israel became "one" because kings needed a unified national cult, not because God revealed his oneness to prophets. This doesn't disprove revelation — God could work through politics — but it removes the "supernatural intervention" necessity from the historical explanation.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives would argue the prophets drove monotheism AGAINST the monarchy (Elijah vs. Ahab is the paradigm). Smith's counter is that the monarchy's relationship to religion was complex, not uniformly hostile.

**My assessment:** Smith is right to complicate the "monarchy = villain" narrative. But his evidence for the monarchy's CONSTRUCTIVE role is thinner than his evidence for its role in promoting Baal. The ANE parallels (Marduk, Amun-Re) show that state religion CAN absorb other deities — they don't show that it DID in Israel. Track whether Smith demonstrates this or just asserts it by analogy.

## Claim 4: Tigay's onomastic argument doesn't disprove widespread polytheism

**Smith's claim:** Tigay's finding that 94%+ of Hebrew personal names use YHWH as the theophoric element does NOT prove Israel was monotheistic or even monolatrous. It only proves Yahweh was the most popular national deity. Personal names are "notoriously difficult to assess for historical purposes" and reflect naming conventions, not personal theology.

**Evidence presented:**
- **Ugaritic counter-example:** Few Ugaritic names contain Asherah (*'atrt*) as a theophoric element, yet ritual texts prove she was actively venerated. Absence in names ≠ absence in worship.
- **Punic counter-example:** Tannit was the most popular Punic goddess, yet Punic names "likewise rarely contain *tnt* as a theophoric element."
- **General principle:** Naming conventions are governed by factors other than religious devotion. A child named "Yohanan" (YHWH is gracious) could grow up in a household that also venerated Asherah.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Smith's counter-examples are real and damaging to a naive onomastics = theology equation. But they cut against Smith too: if Ugaritic names don't reflect worship, then ISRAELITE names also might not reflect worship — including the predominance of YHWH names. Smith uses onomastic silence to argue AGAINST Asherah worship (no one named their kid after her) while dismissing onomastic predominance as evidence FOR Yahwism. That's inconsistent.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the strongest empirical challenge to the polytheism consensus. If Tigay is right that 94%+ names are Yahwistic in the 8th-6th centuries, then popular religion was far more Yahwistic than Smith's model predicts. Smith NEEDS to neutralize this evidence for his case to work.

**Who disagrees:** Tigay, obviously. His book *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986) is the definitive statement. Smith's counter hasn't persuaded most onomastics specialists.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data + the archaeological evidence together suggest a society where Yahweh dominated PUBLIC identity (names) but private practice was more diverse (figurines, household shrines). This is actually Smith's own distinction between "official" and "popular" religion — but applied to onomastics, it weakens his case.

**My assessment:** This is the weakest part of Smith's Introduction. His counter-examples are real but they're a defensive move, not a positive case. He's saying "onomastics can't DISPROVE polytheism" — which is true. But he's not saying "here's positive evidence FOR polytheism from names." The onomastic data remains a genuine problem for the consensus that Smith hasn't fully solved. Flag for further investigation.

## Methodological Presuppositions (Smith's Own List)

Smith explicitly states 6 assumptions. Here's what matters:

1. **"The Bible is not a modern history book"** — fair, but Smith then uses it AS a historical source. The tension between these moves is unresolved.

2. **"The evidence is radically incomplete — a puzzle missing most pieces"** — genuinely humble, but doesn't stop him from drawing strong conclusions.

3. **"Ancient Israelite religion was communal and national, not private"** — important correction to modern assumptions. Most of the evidence we have is about state cult, not household practice.

4. **"Practice over belief"** — Smith argues the monotheism/monolatry distinction (belief vs. practice) is problematic because ancient religion was about practice. He retains the distinction anyway. This is intellectually honest but creates an unresolved tension.

5. **"No 'essence' of Israelite religion apart from the data"** — Smith rejects the idea that there's a "true" Israelite religion vs. "syncretistic corruption." All practice is legitimate historical data. This is a methodological commitment, not a neutral observation — it rules out the conservative position by definition.

6. **"The picture is partial and subjective"** — genuine acknowledgment of limits.

**The unspoken assumption:** Smith's framework assumes the biblical narrative's picture of Israel's religious history is systematically misleading. The Bible says Israel was always Yahwistic and other gods were foreign. Smith says the opposite. This isn't a conclusion he reaches after examining evidence — it's the lens through which he reads the evidence. Worth tracking: does he ever find evidence that contradicts his framework, or does all evidence get interpreted to fit?

## Overall Assessment of the Introduction

**Strongest claim:** Israelite material culture was Canaanite (HIGH confidence, archaeological consensus).

**Weakest claim:** Tigay's onomastics are irrelevant to the polytheism question (Smith's counter is defensive, not decisive).

**Most theologically explosive:** Baal/Asherah worship was indigenous, not foreign — the Bible's narrative is exactly backward.

**Most methodologically important:** Smith's stated assumptions. Track whether he follows them consistently or violates them when convenient.
