# Smith, Early History of God — Introduction

## Chunk 2: The Framework

### Smith's Central Argument

Smith opens with a direct challenge to the older consensus (Albright, Kaufmann, Tigay, Ringgren, Fohrer, Ahlström). That consensus held:

> "Israel had always been essentially monolatrous; Israel worshiped only Yahweh, although it did not deny the existence of other deities."

In this view, Baal and Asherah worship was "syncretism" — foreign contamination of an originally pure Yahwism. The biblical narrative of Israel as ethnically separate from Canaanites, with Yahweh as its exclusive deity from the beginning, was taken as historically reliable.

**Smith's counter:** Three major shifts in perspective make this view untenable.

### Shift 1: Israel WAS Canaanite

> "Israelite culture largely overlapped with, and derived from, 'Canaanite' culture... Given the information available, one cannot maintain a radical cultural separation between 'Canaanites' and Israelites for the Iron I period."

The evidence: scripts (both linear and cuneiform alphabetic), language (Hebrew and Canaanite "cannot be easily distinguished" in Iron I — Isaiah 19:18 calls Hebrew "the language of Canaan"), pottery, burial patterns, architecture (the four-room house and collared-rim jar, once thought distinctively Israelite, are now attested on the coast and in valleys), and cultic terminology. Sacrificial terms like *zebah*, *šělāmîm*, *minḥah*, *kālîl*, and *'ōlāh* all have Ugaritic/Phoenician cognates. The word for priest (*kōhen*) is Canaanite. Even the "tent of meeting" has Canaanite prototypes.

> This is the **archaeological ground-truth** that undercuts the biblical narrative of ethnic separation. If the culture is continuous, the religion probably is too.

### Shift 2: Baal and Asherah Were Indigenous, Not Foreign

This is the crucial move. If Israelite culture IS Canaanite culture, then:

> "Baal and Asherah were part of Israel's 'Canaanite' heritage, and the process of the emergence of Israelite monolatry was an issue of **Israel's breaking with its own 'Canaanite' past** and not simply one of avoiding 'Canaanite' neighbors."

Baal and Asherah worship wasn't syncretism with foreigners. It was **old Israelite religion.** The biblical polemic against "Canaanite" practices was Israel rejecting ITS OWN heritage, not foreign contamination.

> "In early Israel, the cult of Yahweh generally held sway. However, this statement does not fully characterize pre-exilic Israelite religion as a whole. Rather, **Israelite religion apparently included worship of Yahweh, El, Asherah, and Baal.**"

### Shift 3: The Monarchy Wasn't Just the Villain

The Books of Kings depict monarchs as the worst offenders — importing foreign gods, tolerating Baal worship. Smith complicates this:

> "The monarchy fostered the inclusion of various deities, or their features, into the cult of Yahweh... In the first half of its existence, the monarchy fostered some features of convergence in exalting Yahweh as the national god."

The monarchy PLAYED A ROLE in monotheism's development. Royal religion was both "conservative and innovative" — it incorporated traditional popular practices AND drove centralization under Hezekiah and Josiah. The monarchy gets blamed for the polytheism but partial credit for the monotheism.

**Caveat:** Our evidence is southern/Judean. We know much less about northern religious practice except when southern scribes chose to record it (usually to condemn it).

### The Convergence/Differentiation Framework

This is the book's central theoretical contribution:

| Process | What Happens | When | Examples |
|---------|-------------|------|----------|
| **Convergence** | Other deities' characteristics absorbed INTO Yahweh | Period of Judges through early monarchy | El's titles, Baal's storm imagery, Asherah's symbol |
| **Differentiation** | Features originally Israelite rejected as "Canaanite" and non-Yahwistic | 9th century onward, intensifying 8th-6th centuries | Baal condemned, asherah torn down, high places destroyed, solar worship suppressed |

> "Israelite monolatry developed through **conflict and compromise** between the cults of Yahweh and other deities. Israelite literature incorporated some of the characteristics of other deities into the divine personage of Yahweh. **Polemic against deities other than Yahweh even contributed to this process.**"

This last point is counterintuitive: polemic AGAINST Baal actually absorbed Baal's characteristics into Yahweh. By claiming "Yahweh, not Baal, is the real storm god," the polemicists implicitly conceded that storm-god characteristics belonged to the true deity — and therefore transferred them to Yahweh.

### Smith's Direct Rebuttal to Tigay's Onomastics

This is worth pulling out separately because Tigay's onomastic argument (94%+ YHWH names in inscriptions) has been a major challenge to the consensus. Smith addresses it directly:

> "The distribution of 'theophoric' elements in proper names lends credence, however, only to the notion that Yahweh was Israel's **most popular god, its national deity.** There is more to the evidence than proper names, which, however suggestive, are **notoriously difficult to assess** for historical purposes."

**Smith's counter-evidence:**

1. **Ugarit:** Few proper names contain Asherah (*'atrt*) as a theophoric element — yet Ugaritic ritual texts prove she was venerated. Absence of her name in personal names doesn't mean she wasn't worshipped.

2. **Punic world:** Tannit was the most popular goddess, yet Punic names "likewise rarely contain *tnt* as a theophoric element."

3. **General principle:** "The giving of names was subject to conventions governed by factors other than religious concerns."

> This is a **direct, specific rebuttal** to one of the strongest empirical challenges to the consensus. Smith's counter is: onomastics tell you which god is most popular for naming children, not which gods are worshipped. The archaeological and textual evidence for other deities must be weighed alongside the names, not dismissed because of them.

### Methodological Presuppositions (Section 2)

Smith explicitly states his assumptions — worth noting because they shape everything that follows:

1. **The Bible is not a modern history book** — but it contains historical information. The biblical authors were "no less historians than Herodotus or Thucydides" (citing Halpern), but they had different methods and theological commitments.

2. **The evidence is radically incomplete.** Smith uses a puzzle metaphor: "working with a puzzle that is missing many or most of its pieces. Worse yet, scholars do not know how many pieces there are."

3. **Religion in ancient Israel was communal and national, not private.** Modern Westerners think of religion as personal. Ancient Israelite religion was about the nation, the monarchy, the cult. This affects what evidence we have and what it means.

4. **Practice over belief.** "The Bible more frequently stresses correct practices than correct beliefs or internal attitudes." Christian scholars have tended to project their emphasis on belief backward. Smith argues the monotheism/monolatry distinction (belief vs. practice) is therefore problematic — but he retains it because it's "a matter of current interest."

5. **No "essence" of Israelite religion apart from the data.** Claims about what "true" Israelite religion was versus "syncretistic corruption" are "statements of personal faith and not historical description." Both "official" and "popular" religion are legitimate subjects of historical inquiry.

6. **The picture is partial and subjective.** Smith closes the introduction by stating this explicitly — not false modesty, but a genuine acknowledgment of the limits of the enterprise.

## Key Methodological Observations

### Smith vs. the Older Consensus

The older consensus (Albright through Tigay) accepted the **biblical narrative's framework**: Israel was separate from Canaan, Yahweh was Israel's exclusive deity, and other gods were foreign intrusions. Smith's argument is that **this framework is itself a product of the Deuteronomistic polemic** — it's the victors' history. The archaeological and epigraphic evidence tells a different story.

### Smith vs. Tigay (Onomastics)

This is one of the few places where Smith directly engages a specific empirical challenge. His counter is: names don't equal worship. The Ugaritic and Punic parallels are strong — if a goddess can be popular without appearing in personal names, the same could be true in Israel.

### Smith's Epistemological Humility

Smith repeatedly emphasizes how little we know. Pre-8th century reconstruction is "largely conjectural." The puzzle is missing most of its pieces. This is not rhetoric — it affects how he weights evidence throughout the book. He's more willing than many scholars to say "we don't know" rather than filling gaps with speculation.

### The Unspoken Assumption

Smith doesn't say this explicitly, but his framework assumes that **the biblical narrative's picture of Israel's religious history is systematically misleading.** The Bible says Israel was always Yahwistic and other gods were foreign. Smith says the opposite: Israel was Canaanite, other gods were indigenous, and the "foreign" framing was a later polemical construction. This is a methodological commitment, not a neutral observation — and it shapes which evidence he finds persuasive.
