# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapter 3

## Moses and the Midianites

---

## Claim 1: Moses is a constructed figure with an Egyptian name — no extra-biblical evidence exists for him

**Römer's claim:** "Moses is a figure of tradition, of whom, however, there are no historical traces" (quoting Assmann). He has no extra-biblical attestation in Egyptian or any other ancient texts. But his NAME is genuinely Egyptian and may preserve an archaic transcription that takes us back to the second millennium. Moses is "a 'construct' in which memory traces of various different historical events and figures are combined."

**Evidence presented:**

**The name:**
- "Moses" derives from the Egyptian root *msj* ("to engender, beget"), found in names like Ramses ("Ra has begot him")
- The Egyptian consonant "s" in "Moses" is rendered in Hebrew with the letter *šin* — whereas in first-millennium texts, this sound is normally expressed by *sāmek*. "The name Moses thus takes us perhaps back to the second millennium."
- This is philological evidence for antiquity, not for historicity. An old name doesn't mean an old person.

**Two possible Egyptian historical parallels — neither is a perfect match:**

1. **Beya:** A Canaanite chancellor under Seti II (1203-1197 BCE). After the king died, Beya proclaimed the child Siptah as pharaoh and essentially reigned himself. "The close relation between Moses and the Egyptian court described in Exodus 2 may well be a reflection of this situation." Beya hired Canaanite mercenaries (*'apiru*, possibly related to "Hebrew") and seized Egyptian silver and gold (compare Exodus 11:2 — Israelites take silver and gold from Egypt). BUT: a newly published document indicates Beya was EXECUTED in Egypt, "which would make any identification with Moses arbitrary, despite a certain number of common motifs."

2. **Ben-Ozen:** A Semite from Transjordan under Ramses II who held the post of "Carver Esquire." His Egyptian name contained the element "m-s-s." "He intervened as a mediator in a conflict between some groups of Shasu who were performing forced labor and their Egyptian overseers. This might bring to mind the episode in Exodus 2 where Moses comes to the defense of the Hebrew slave."

**Römer's conclusion:** "None of the possible candidates could really provide a model for all aspects of the biblical Moses, who must rather be understood as a 'construct' in which memory traces of various different historical events and figures are combined."

**The Exodus narrative as literary art:**
- The story of Moses fleeing Egypt is "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance"
- Moses killing the Egyptian overseer REVERSES the standard Egyptian iconographic motif of pharaoh striking down an enemy — the biblical author "certainly knew this convention and reversed it; by doing so he affirmed the authority of Moses vis-à-vis the power of Egypt"
- "It is very difficult to reconstruct any historical event that might lie behind this treatment"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that Moses has no extra-biblical attestation (this is simply a fact). HIGH that the name is Egyptian and may preserve archaic transcription. MEDIUM that Beya or Ben-Ozen are genuine historical kernels behind the Moses figure (plausible parallels but impossible to prove).

**What's at stake for faith:** The founder of Israelite religion — the man who spoke to God face to face, who received the Torah on Sinai, who led Israel out of Egypt — has no footprint outside the Bible. None. The best we can say is that his name might be genuinely old and that some aspects of his story might echo real Egyptian political figures. For a faith tradition that depends on Moses as the unique mediator of divine revelation, this is a serious problem.

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives who maintain Mosaic authorship and historicity. Scholars who argue for a historical Moses behind the legend (the "kernel of truth" approach). Minimalists who say the entire Moses narrative is Persian-period fiction with no historical value at all.

**Alternative reading:** The Egyptian name doesn't need to indicate a historical person — it could be a literary choice by an author familiar with Egyptian naming conventions. The Beya and Ben-Ozen parallels could be coincidental rather than sources.

**My assessment:** Römer is admirably honest here. He presents the Egyptian parallels (Beya, Ben-Ozen) that might support a historical Moses, then immediately acknowledges their limitations. He doesn't pretend the evidence is stronger than it is. His conclusion — Moses as a "construct" combining multiple "memory traces" — is reasonable but, as he himself notes, unprovable. This is the kind of historical reconstruction where you can't tell the difference between a genuine trace and a coincidental parallel.

## Claim 2: The Midianites were a real historical people in northwestern Arabia who may have mediated Yhwh worship to Israel

**Römer's claim:** The Midianites were "nomadic peasants" who combined agriculture, stock raising, and camel domestication. They lived in a confederation in northwestern Arabia/southern Transjordan. They produced distinctive pottery (13th-first centuries BCE), were involved in trade along major caravan routes, and possibly participated in copper and gold mining at Timna. They are the most plausible bridge between the southern Yhwh (Chapter 2) and Israel.

**Evidence presented:**

**Geographic location:**
- 1 Kings 11 places Midian south of Edom, on the route from Edom to Egypt through Wadi Feran
- Greco-Roman and Arab geographers knew a town "Midama/Madyan" east of the Gulf of Aqaba, identified with al-Bad in Wadi 'Afal
- Two major commercial routes traversed the region: a coastal route from Aqaba through Wadi 'Afal (archaeological remains from 13th century onward) and an inland route through the Ḥismā

**Archaeological evidence:**
- Distinctive "Midianite pottery" dated to 13th-11th centuries BCE, decorated with camels, human figures, and ostriches
- Found as far afield as the Levant — evidence of trade networks
- Wadi Sadr probably marks the southern frontier; Wadi Šarma and al-Qurayya were major population centers

**Economic activities:**
- Camel domestication (first domesticated in SE Arabia in 3rd millennium, spread to western Arabia by late 2nd millennium)
- Combined agriculture and stock raising
- Possibly involved in copper and gold mining at Timna under Egyptian control
- A "Midianite holy place" was discovered at the site of an Egyptian sanctuary to Hathor — "the later occupants of this site seem to have tried to erase the Egyptian presence"

**The connection to the Shasu:** "The Midianites, like the Shasu (among whom the Midianites were probably counted by the Egyptians), were perhaps also involved in mining." The Midianites may have been a subgroup of the broader Shasu phenomenon.

**The biblical connection — Jethro:**
- Moses marries Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, "priest of Midian" (Exodus 2-3)
- Jethro already worships Yhwh and offers sacrifice to him (Exodus 18)
- The narrative implies Yhwh was ALREADY known among the Midianites before Moses
- The Kenites (a Midianite subgroup) appear as metalworkers associated with Israel (Judges 1:16, 4:11)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that the Midianites were a real historical group (archaeological evidence is solid). MEDIUM that they mediated Yhwh worship to Israel (the Jethro narrative is the only evidence, and its historical value is debated).

**What's at stake for faith:** If the Midianites already worshipped Yhwh before Moses, then Moses didn't receive a revelation of a previously unknown God. He encountered a god his Midianite father-in-law already knew. Yhwh was not revealed TO Israel through Moses — Yhwh was brought TO Israel FROM Midian. "Revelation" becomes "cultural transmission."

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the Jethro narrative as a late literary construct (Persian period) with no historical value. Scholars who argue Yhwh was indigenous to Canaan and the Midianite connection is fictional.

## Claim 3: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is the best available explanation, but it remains unproven

**Römer's claim:** The tradition connecting Moses to Midian "is perhaps based on a historical recollection of the importance of the Midianites and of some kind of close contact between them and Moses." The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis — Yhwh was a Midianite god whom Moses encountered through his Midianite father-in-law and then brought to Israel — connects the Soleb evidence (Chapter 2) with the biblical narrative in a coherent way. But it can't be proven.

**Römer's caution:** The Moses narrative is "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance." The Jethro story may be a literary device rather than a historical report. The hypothesis is plausible, elegant, and underdetermined.

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The hypothesis elegantly connects the dots but depends on taking the biblical narrative as preserving genuine historical memory. Given Römer's own acknowledgment that the Moses story is literary artifice, this is a significant tension.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the hinge of Römer's entire historical reconstruction. If the Kenite hypothesis is wrong, the mechanism by which Yhwh moved from the southern deserts to Israel is unknown. We have a starting point (Soleb) and an endpoint (Yhwh as Israel's god) with no bridge between them.

**My assessment:** This is where Römer's "traces of memory" methodology faces its hardest test. The Kenite hypothesis is attractive because it makes sense of scattered data. But attractive explanations aren't necessarily true. The Jethro narrative could be a late attempt to explain how a foreign god became Israel's god, rather than a memory of how it actually happened. Römer's honesty about the uncertainty is commendable. His willingness to proceed with the hypothesis anyway is questionable.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Moses has no extra-biblical attestation | VERY HIGH | None — this is simply a fact |
| Moses' name is Egyptian, possibly archaic | HIGH | Name could be a literary choice, not a historical trace |
| The Midianites were a real historical group | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaeological evidence is solid |
| The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis | LOW-MEDIUM | Depends on treating literary narrative as historical memory |
| Beya/Ben-Ozen as models for Moses | LOW | Parallels are interesting but impossible to verify |

**Strongest section:** The archaeological-geographic description of Midian — grounded in material evidence.

**Weakest section:** The Kenite hypothesis — elegant, unprovable, and in tension with Römer's own skepticism about the Moses narrative.

**The fundamental tension in Römer's project:** He wants the biblical texts to preserve genuine historical memories (so he can reconstruct pre-exilic history), but he also acknowledges they are literary artifice shaped by ideology. He can't have it both ways, and Chapter 3 is where this tension is most visible.
