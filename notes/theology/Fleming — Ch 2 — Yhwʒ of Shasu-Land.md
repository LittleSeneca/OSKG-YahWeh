# Fleming — Chapter 2: Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land

This is the foundation chapter of the entire book. Fleming undertakes the most thorough review of the Egyptian evidence in the Yahweh-origins literature, reaching conclusions that overturn 70+ years of scholarly consensus: the Soleb list of Amenhotep III (c. 1400 BCE) probably did NOT include Seir, the Shasu-land names are kinship-based people-groups (not towns or landmarks), and Yhwʒ was a people-name, not a divine name — only later did the god Yahweh carry this people-name forward. The chapter's two driving questions: (1) Does Yhwʒ = Yhwh phonologically? (Answer: almost certainly yes.) (2) What is the relationship between the Egyptian people-name and the later deity? (Answer: the deity inherited the name of a Shasu kinship group.)

---

## Claim 1: Yhwʒ is phonologically the same name as the Hebrew Tetragrammaton YHWH

**Author's claim:** "In agreement with a significant majority of scholars, I conclude that the first question demands with high likelihood an affirmative response: the names are truly the same" (p. 43).

**Evidence presented:**
- The hieroglyphic writing y-h-wʒ is "clearly syllabic": <y> (double reed leaf) always renders /ya/, <h> is a single consonant without vowel marker, <wʒ> is almost certainly /wa/ — per Thomas Schneider (p. 59, n.a)
- Final <wʒ> could also represent /we/; in either case the name ends in an open vowel
- The match is "not particularly strong" only to skeptics (Goedicke 1994, Ahlström 1986), while a long list accepts it: Weippert (1974), Astour (1979), Redford (1992: 272-73), van der Toorn (1999: 911-12), Schneider (2007), Adrom and Müller (2017)
- The occurrence as "a major identity among the Shasu people" supports the connection

**Confidence:** HIGH. The phonological match is precise and the scholarly consensus is overwhelming. Even Adrom and Müller (2017), who are cautious Egyptologists without stake in the Yahweh debate, accept the reading without objection.

**What's at stake:** If the match is rejected, the earliest evidence for the name Yahweh disappears entirely, and the entire historical investigation loses its oldest anchor. Fleming's argument doesn't strictly require the match (he could argue from the poetic/biblical evidence alone), but it gives his reconstruction a 14th-century BCE starting point.

**Who disagrees:** Goedicke (1994), Ahlström (1986: 57-60). Mark Smith (2001a: 25) calls the evidence "not particularly strong."

**Alternative reading:** The phonetic similarity could be coincidental — a Shasu subgroup name that happens to resemble the later divine name but has no historical connection.

**My assessment:** The phonological case is strong, and the burden of proof is on skeptics. The consonants y-h-w are too distinctive for coincidence, and the Egyptian syllabic orthography maps cleanly. Fleming's argument doesn't depend on certainty here — he acknowledges it remains a probability — but the rest of the book is built on the assumption that the match is real.

## Claim 2: Seir (S'rr) was probably NOT in the original Amenhotep III Soleb list — it was added in the 13th century Ramses II copy

**Author's claim:** "Considerations of space make it highly unlikely that S'rr could have been part of the Soleb list, which would then require uniquely mismatched α and β sets of six and three names" (p. 84). "Aside from the debate over identification of ś'rr from 'Amarah West... the published drawings for the Soleb temple make it difficult to restore 'Seir' to the older Shasu list" (p. 64).

**Evidence presented:**
- Column N4 at Soleb has space for exactly 9 prisoners (Fleming's own measurement calculations, done with Elizabeth Knott and Nancy Fleming, pp. 63-64)
- Every other column at Soleb divides names into sets of 4+5 or 5+4 — a 6+3 split would be unique
- The 'Amarah West list has 6 names, the Soleb list has at most 5 (and probably 4: Trbr, Yhwʒ, Smt, Pyspys)
- Giveon (1964: 245) already doubted room for six cartouches
- The reverse order in the Ramses II copy (S'rr at the head) suggests scribal reorientation
- Seir only becomes associated with Shasu in the 13th century (Ramses II obelisk, Papyrus Harris I)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The physical measurement argument is clever and original but depends on drawings at 1:10 scale with acknowledged imprecision. However, Giveon independently doubted six names would fit. The convergence of physical + historical + pattern arguments (13th-century Seir-Shasu association only) is strong.

**What's at stake:** Enormous. If Seir was NOT in the original list, the sole geographical anchor for Yhwʒ disappears. Without Seir, Yhwʒ is somewhere in "the inland back country" — could be south, could be east, could be north. The Midianite Hypothesis's Egyptian pillar collapses.

**Who disagrees:** Giveon (1971), Ahituv (1984), Redford (1992), and essentially everyone who has worked on these texts. They all restore Seir to the Soleb list from the Amarah West copy. Grdseloff (1947) made this identification first and it's been canonical since.

**Alternative reading:** The duplicated -rr is normal Late Egyptian orthography (Redford 1992: 272 n.67), the "careless orthography of the whole list" explains it (Ahituv 1984: 169), and Seir was always there. Fleming's space calculation is based on reconstructed measurements from drawings — a single error in column diameter transforms the conclusion.

**My assessment:** This is Fleming's most original and consequential argument. If he's right, the entire geography of Yahweh's origins shifts. The physical measurement is ingenious but vulnerable — I'd want an Egyptologist to verify the column dimensions independently. The historical argument (Seir-Shasu connection only emerges in 13th c.) is independent of the measurement and may be stronger.

## Claim 3: The Shasu-land names represent kinship-based people-groups ("families" = mhwt), not towns or landmarks

**Author's claim:** "It is central to my analysis that the names for the distinct Shasu units were not assigned by Egyptian scribes but rather are taken from indigenous Semitic identities" (p. 68). "The names represent groups of people not identified by towns" (p. 69). "I insist that they are groups within the Shasu 'land,' and that the names only make sense in relation to people" (p. 85).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Egyptian term mhwt ("families/tribes") is applied to Shasu subdivisions in Papyrus Anastasi VI and Papyrus Harris I (late 13th-12th c.)
- O'Connor (1987: 35): mhwt "emphasizing the primacy of kinship within the social and political organization" — applied to Libyans and Shasu alike
- The names are entirely unknown — not part of any attested Late Bronze Age landscape of towns or landmarks (p. 65)
- This is the ONLY list naming Shasu subdivisions — unique and precious
- Anne Porter's work on kinship creating social bonds across distance (2009, 2012) provides a theoretical framework
- The Mari archives show exactly this pattern: kinship-based pastoralist groups integrated with settlement and agriculture
- None of the attempts to identify the names geographically (Astour's northern locations, Görg's animal traits, biblical cross-references) has produced convincing results

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Egyptian use of mhwt is solid evidence for how Egyptians perceived Shasu organization. But we can't be certain the names reflect indigenous self-identification rather than Egyptian administrative categories. The argument that they're "not towns" is stronger than the positive claim that they're kinship groups.

**What's at stake:** If Yhwʒ is a kinship-based people-group, then Yahweh-the-god is the god OF a people — the deity carries the people's name, not vice versa. This inverts the usual assumption (that a place or sanctuary named after the god gave rise to the name). It also aligns with Fleming's Ch 6 argument about 'am Yhwh.

**Who disagrees:** Betsy Bryan (personal communication to Fleming): the choice is between "tribe" and "locale." Most Egyptologists and biblical scholars have treated the names as toponyms — place names, perhaps sanctuaries (Giveon: "Beth Yahweh, the house of Yahweh").

**Alternative reading:** The names could be geographical subdivisions of "Shasu-land" without implying kinship organization — simply administrative districts defined by Egyptian scribes for their own purposes.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is plausible and well-argued but not proven. The Egyptian evidence for mhwt is later than Amenhotep III. The key evidence is really negative: every attempt to locate the names geographically has failed. That doesn't prove they're kinship groups, but it removes the main alternative. The Mari comparison is illuminating but analogical, not evidential.

## Claim 4: The Soleb and 'Amarah West lists derive from a SINGLE moment of Egyptian contact with the Shasu — one military encounter

**Author's claim:** "It is equally important to recognize what lies behind the textual commonality of the Soleb and the 'Amarah West lists: a single moment of Egyptian contact with the Shasu people in circumstances that could generate knowledge of real Shasu group-names" (p. 70). "This one list produces the opposite effect [of the Shasu stereotype], acknowledging the known individuality of each named entity. It did not happen again, which suggests all the more that there was some particular purpose to the administrative occasion" (p. 73).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Shasu-land names are Semitic — Egyptians wouldn't have made them up
- Their recurrence in two related Nubian temple displays indicates a single source
- The Soleb version is earlier (Amenhotep III), so the contact occurred during or before his reign
- No other Egyptian text names Shasu subdivisions — this list is unique
- The list's specificity (actual named groups) contrasts with the normal Egyptian practice of stereotyping the Shasu as a generic category

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The single-source inference is methodologically sound — the two temple lists are clearly related, and their specificity is unique. But "single military encounter" vs. "ongoing administrative relationship" is acknowledged by Fleming as uncertain (p. 71).

**What's at stake:** If the names come from a single encounter, they give us a genuine snapshot of Shasu political organization at one moment in the early 14th century. We're not looking at a evolving tradition but at a fixed data point. This makes the evidence both more precious (genuine contact) and more limited (one moment, one Egyptian perspective).

**Who disagrees:** This is Fleming's own synthesis. The uniqueness of the list hasn't been emphasized this way before.

**Alternative reading:** The names could derive from ongoing Egyptian administration of Shasu territory — not a single battle but accumulated administrative knowledge over decades.

**My assessment:** The "single encounter" reading is elegant and fits the evidence well, but it's speculative. The key point — that these are real indigenous names, not Egyptian inventions — is more important than the "single moment" framing.

## Claim 5: Early Shasu references (15th-14th centuries) point NORTH toward Syria, not south — the southern association only emerges in the 13th century

**Author's claim:** "This evidence for the geography of the Shasu in the 15th and 14th centuries does not settle the location of 'Shasu-land'... The point is that in the earlier phases of Egyptian involvement in Asia... the geographical associations of mobile Shasu people tended to be more northern" (p. 81).

**Evidence presented:**
- Thutmose IV's chariot list places Shasu among Syrian sites: Naharin, Babylon, Tunip, Kadesh, Takhsi — all in the Orontes region (p. 80)
- Amenhotep II's prisoner list (15,200 "living Shasu") appears alongside Nuhašše (northern Levant, east of the Orontes, south of Aleppo) — "the Amenhotep II list offers only a land far north of Palestine for its one point of reference" (p. 78)
- Amenhotep III's Document 5a lists "Shasu Spring" alongside Dothan and Šamhuna (Jezreel Valley, northern Palestine) — not southern (p. 79)
- The Soleb column N4 is on the north side of the hypostyle hall — Asian sphere, not African (south side has African peoples) (p. 53)
- Ramses II fought Shasu at Kadesh on the Orontes (p. 80)
- Only in the 13th century do Seir, Edom, and Moab appear in connection with Shasu

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is data-driven and verifiable from Giveon's corpus. The early texts genuinely point north. But "pointing north" doesn't mean Yhwʒ specifically was northern — Shasu were everywhere in the inland back country. The argument establishes that there's no default southern location for early Shasu, not that Yhwʒ was northern.

**What's at stake:** If early Shasu generally appear in northern/Syrian contexts, the default assumption that Yhwʒ must be southern (driven by the Bible) loses its Egyptian support. The southern Shasu association is a 13th-century development, not an original feature.

**Who disagrees:** Giveon (1971) read the early texts through a southern lens, assuming the Shasu were always associated with Edom/Seir. Most biblical scholars have followed Giveon.

**Alternative reading:** The Shasu category was applied to mobile pastoralists across the entire inland Levant. Southern Shasu existed alongside northern Shasu. The fact that Egyptian military interests were in Syria in the 15th century doesn't mean Shasu weren't also in the south — it means Egypt wasn't campaigning there yet.

**My assessment:** Fleming's point is more modest than it seems. He's not proving Yhwʒ was northern; he's deconstructing the assumption that it must be southern. The geographical agnosticism is methodologically sound: we simply don't know where Amenhotep III's Shasu-land was, and the default should be the entire inland back country, not the deep south.

## Claim 6: The personal name 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh ("My Lord is the Shepherd of Yah") confirms Yhwʒ as a people-name, not a divine name

**Author's claim:** "The personal name 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh would reduce the plausibility of a conclusion that Yhwʒ already represented the deity Yahweh... It is the people, not a god, that need a 'shepherd.' Most simply, Yhwʒ is one subdivision of some association of groups" (p. 84).

**Evidence presented:**
- Thomas Schneider (2007) published the name from New Kingdom Egypt (late 18th or 19th Dynasties, c. 1330-1230)
- The divine element in the theophoric name would be 'adōnī ("My Lord"), not yāh — like Amorite Aduna-Addu, "The Lord is Addu"
- Yāh is a hypocoristic (shortened) form of Yhwʒ
- The name means "My (Divine) Lord is the Shepherd of (the) Yah (People)"
- Schneider himself, in comments on Fleming's draft, agreed this reading "would suit well my interpretation of Yhwʒ as a people"
- The name occurs in six variants — it wasn't a one-off

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The reading is grammatically plausible and Schneider (who discovered the name) endorses Fleming's interpretation. But it's one personal name, and alternative readings are possible. Schneider's original 2007 interpretation read it as evidence for early Yahweh worship.

**What's at stake:** If correct, this is the only evidence from the period between Soleb (c. 1400) and the Mesha Stele (c. 840) that bears on the divine-name-vs-people-name question. It tilts the scales toward "people."

**Who disagrees:** Schneider's original (2007) reading treated Yhwʒ as "a mountainous region linked to the worship of a god named Yahweh" — the opposite of Fleming's reading.

**Alternative reading:** Yāh IS a divine name (short form of Yahweh), and the name means "My Lord is the Shepherd of Yah" — evidence for early Yahweh worship, not for Yhwʒ-as-people.

**My assessment:** The name is fascinating but can't carry much weight on its own. It's too ambiguous. Fleming's interpretation is clever but depends on his prior conclusion that Yhwʒ is a people-name. The argument risks circularity: we know Yhwʒ is a people because this name mentions a shepherd; we know the shepherd is for a people because Yhwʒ is a people-name.

## Claim 7: The Egyptian evidence is "before Israel" because Merneptah's Israel (c. 1207) and Yhwʒ (c. 1400) have nothing to do with each other

**Author's claim:** "Yhwʒ is attested before Israel, which is not a name linked to the Shasu. As encountered by Egypt, the Israel people from the Merneptah Stele (ca. 1207) and the Yhwʒ group of the Shasu (early 14th century) have nothing to do with each other" (p. 85).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Merneptah Stele uses a people-determinative for Israel (man + woman + plural strokes)
- Israel was encountered in Canaan; Yhwʒ was encountered somewhere in Shasu-land
- No Egyptian text connects the two names
- Two centuries separate the attestations

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is simply factual: the Egyptian record attests Yhwʒ (c. 1400) before Israel (c. 1207), and they're separate entries in separate contexts.

**What's at stake:** This establishes the chronological priority of the name Yahweh over the name Israel in the historical record. Yahweh really was "before Israel" — not just theologically but chronologically, in the oldest available evidence.

**Who disagrees:** Na'aman (2011), who "conflates Israel with the Shasu category" (p. 85 n.90). Some scholars argue the early Israelites WERE Shasu.

**Alternative reading:** Israel may have emerged FROM Shasu populations, so the chronological separation is artificial — Israel and Yhwʒ are phases of the same underlying reality.

**My assessment:** Factually solid but interpretively limited. The names are separate in the Egyptian record, but that doesn't mean the populations were separate — it means Egyptian scribes used different categories at different times for different purposes. Fleming acknowledges this complexity (p. 85: "groups identified with Yahweh and with Israel may have been affiliated or closely aligned").

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Yhwʒ = Yhwh phonologically | HIGH | Requires accepting Egyptian syllabic orthography |
| 2: Seir not in Soleb list | MEDIUM-HIGH | Measurement error in column circumference |
| 3: Names as kinship groups | MEDIUM | Alternative: Egyptian administrative districts |
| 4: Single encounter origin | MEDIUM | Could be ongoing administrative knowledge |
| 5: Early Shasu point north | MEDIUM-HIGH | Doesn't rule out concurrent southern Shasu |
| 6: 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh as people evidence | MEDIUM | Circular dependency on prior people-name conclusion |
| 7: Yhwʒ before Israel chronologically | HIGH | Na'aman's conflation argument |

**Strongest section:** The physical argument about Soleb column N4 space and the exclusion of Seir (pp. 63-64). This is original, testable, and if verified by Egyptologists, transformative. The historical argument that Seir-Shasu association only emerges in the 13th century (pp. 74-77) is independent and equally powerful.

**Weakest section:** The 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh interpretation (pp. 83-84). Too much weight on one ambiguous personal name, and the reasoning flirts with circularity.

**Overall assessment:** This is the most important chapter in the book and arguably the most original contribution to the Yahweh-origins debate in decades. If Seir wasn't in the Soleb list, the southern-origin hypothesis loses its oldest and most "objective" anchor. The kinship-group reading of the Shasu names is less certain but opens up genuinely new interpretive possibilities. The chapter's caution ("we cannot locate Yhwʒ of Shasu-land") is admirable and honest.
