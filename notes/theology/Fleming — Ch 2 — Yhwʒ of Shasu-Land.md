# Fleming — Chapter 2: Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land

This is the foundation chapter of the entire book. Fleming undertakes the most thorough review of the Egyptian evidence in the Yahweh-origins literature, reaching conclusions that overturn 70+ years of scholarly consensus: the Soleb list of Amenhotep III (c. 1400 BCE) probably did NOT include Seir, the Shasu-land names are kinship-based people-groups (not towns or landmarks), and Yhwʒ was a people-name, not a divine name — only later did the god Yahweh carry this people-name forward. The chapter's two driving questions: (1) Does Yhwʒ = Yhwh phonologically? (Answer: almost certainly yes.) (2) What is the relationship between the Egyptian people-name and the later deity? (Answer: the deity inherited the name of a Shasu kinship group.)

---

## Claim 1: Yhwʒ = Yhwh phonologically
→ [[claim-yhw3-phonologically-same-as-yhwh-tetragrammaton]] | **fleming-ybi-2.1** | Confidence: high
  The Egyptian hieroglyphic writing y-h-wʒ maps cleanly to the Hebrew Tetragrammaton — the consonants are too distinctive for coincidence and scholarly consensus is overwhelming.
  Supports: [[claim-shasu-yhwh-people-name-not-divine-name]]

## Claim 2: Seir not in the original Soleb list
→ [[claim-seir-absent-original-soleb-list-column-space-calculation]] | **fleming-ybi-2.2** | Confidence: medium-high
  Column N4 at Soleb has space for exactly 9 prisoners; a 6+3 split would be unique. Seir was added to the 13th-century Ramses II copy. If correct, the Midianite Hypothesis loses its oldest geographical anchor.
  Contradicts: [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]], [[claim-yhwh-southern-desert-origin-soleb-poetry-synthesis]]

## Claim 3: Shasu names = kinship-based people-groups
→ [[claim-shasu-land-names-kinship-people-groups-not-toponyms]] | **fleming-ybi-2.3** | Confidence: medium
  The Egyptian term mhwt ("families/tribes") describes Shasu subdivisions. Every attempt to locate the names geographically has failed. If Yhwʒ is a people-name, Yahweh-the-god is the god OF a people — the deity carries the people's name.
  Supports: [[claim-shasu-yhwh-people-name-not-divine-name]], [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]]

## Claim 4: Soleb and Amarah West derive from single encounter
→ [[claim-soleb-amarah-west-lists-single-shasu-encounter]] | **fleming-ybi-2.4** | Confidence: medium
  The two temple lists are clearly related, their specificity is unique, and the names are Semitic — Egyptians wouldn't have invented them. A single 14th-century military-administrative contact produced both lists.

## Claim 5: Early Shasu point north, not south
→ [[claim-early-shasu-references-northern-syria-not-southern]] | **fleming-ybi-2.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  Thutmose IV, Amenhotep II, and Amenhotep III texts place Shasu among Syrian sites (Orontes, Nuhašše, Jezreel). The southern Shasu association only emerges in the 13th century. The default assumption that Yhwʒ must be southern loses its Egyptian support.
  Contradicts: [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]]

## Claim 6: 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh as people-name evidence
→ [[claim-adoni-roe-yah-people-name-not-divine-name]] | **fleming-ybi-2.6** | Confidence: medium
  Thomas Schneider's New Kingdom personal name means "My Lord is the Shepherd of the Yah (People)" — "shepherd" makes more sense for a people than a deity. The only onomastic evidence between Soleb and the Mesha Stele.
  Supports: [[claim-shasu-yhwh-people-name-not-divine-name]]

## Claim 7: Yhwʒ before Israel chronologically
→ [[claim-yhw3-attested-before-israel-merneptah-stele]] | **fleming-ybi-2.7** | Confidence: high
  Yhwʒ (c. 1400) predates Israel (c. 1207) in the Egyptian record by two centuries. The names are separate entries in separate contexts — the name Yahweh really was "before Israel" in the oldest available evidence.
  Supports: [[claim-merneptah-stele-first-mention-israel-ethnic-group]]

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Yhwʒ = Yhwh phonologically | HIGH | Requires accepting Egyptian syllabic orthography |
| 2: Seir not in Soleb list | MEDIUM-HIGH | Measurement error in column circumference |
| 3: Names as kinship groups | MEDIUM | Alternative: Egyptian administrative districts |
| 4: Single encounter origin | MEDIUM | Could be ongoing administrative knowledge |
| 5: Early Shasu point north | MEDIUM-HIGH | Doesn't rule out concurrent southern Shasu |
| 6: 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh as people evidence | MEDIUM | Circular dependency on prior people-name conclusion |
| 7: Yhwʒ before Israel chronologically | HIGH | Na'aman's conflation argument |

**Strongest section:** The physical argument about Soleb column N4 space and the exclusion of Seir (pp. 63-64). This is original, testable, and if verified by Egyptologists, transformative. The historical argument that Seir-Shasu association only emerges in the 13th century (pp. 74-77) is independent and equally powerful.

**Weakest section:** The 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh interpretation (pp. 83-84). Too much weight on one ambiguous personal name, and the reasoning flirts with circularity.

**Overall assessment:** This is the most important chapter in the book and arguably the most original contribution to the Yahweh-origins debate in decades. If Seir wasn't in the Soleb list, the southern-origin hypothesis loses its oldest and most "objective" anchor. The kinship-group reading of the Shasu names is less certain but opens up genuinely new interpretive possibilities. The chapter's caution ("we cannot locate Yhwʒ of Shasu-land") is admirable and honest.

**Cross-reference to primary sources:** [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]] Section 4 (Soleb Shasu Inscription) needs updating: the primary source note currently states "yhwA could be a place name or a divine name" and assumes the Seir connection. Fleming's argument that Seir was absent from the original list and that Yhwʒ is a people-name should be noted there.
