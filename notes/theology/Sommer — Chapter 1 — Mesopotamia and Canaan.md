# Sommer — Chapter 1

## Fluidity of Divine Embodiment and Selfhood: Mesopotamia and Canaan

---

## Claim 1: ANE gods had "fluid selves" — multiple local manifestations with partial independence

**Sommer's claim:** "A single deity could exist simultaneously in several bodies. Further, a deity could have a fragmented or ill-defined self, for gods were not fully distinct from each other, at least not all of the time."

**Evidence — Ishtar's fragmentation:**
- Assyrian treaties list "Ishtar of Arbela," "Ishtar of Nineveh," and Venus (planet = Ishtar) as SEPARATE witnesses with separate curse obligations
- Assurbanipal's hymn addresses them as plural ("their lips," "their names") — grammatically distinct goddesses
- Yet functionally "one and the same": "The Lady of Nineveh, the mother who bore me... The Lady of Arbela, my creator" — both claim motherhood. The next lines use feminine PLURAL verbs: "They decreed as my fate..."
- "Somehow, it was possible for various local and even heavenly manifestations of a single god to be effectively identical with each other and also distinct from each other"

**Sommer clarifies what this is NOT:**
1. NOT Ishtar's contradictory personality (love AND war) — that's complex characterization, not fragmented selfhood
2. NOT syncretism (historically independent gods merging) — he's studying how worshippers experienced these gods synchronically, not diachronic development

**Evidence — divine overlap/merger:**

1. **Ninurta's body as all gods:** A late 2nd-millennium hymn describes other gods as Ninurta's body parts. "Your face is Shamash, your head is Adad, your neck is Marduk." This is not monotheism — it's imagistic theology where divine selves overlap.

2. **Marduk absorbs the high gods:** In *Enuma Elish*, Anu, Ea, and Enlil are equated with Marduk. "Your word is Anu!" — not "like Anu's" but IS Anu. Ea says Marduk "shall have the name Ea, just like me." In Mesopotamia, "name and essence were inseparable." BUT "all these deities act quite independently of each other in the epic... In one sense, Marduk and Ea share a self, but at the same time Ea has a self that is not Marduk."

3. **The *tākultu* ritual (K. 252):** Lists offerings to combined deities like "Dagan-Ashur" — single divine determinative, single deity in the ritual moment. "The texts are intriguing because of the curiously ambivalent concept of the divine as both one and many that they present."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Akkadian/Ugaritic texts are unambiguous.

## Claim 2: ANE gods had MULTIPLE BODIES — cult statues were the god, not representations of the god

**Sommer's claim:** "In Mesopotamian religions, divine bodies differ from nondivine ones in that a deity's presence was not limited to a single body; it could emerge simultaneously in several objects."

**Evidence — the *mīs pî* and *pīt pî* rituals:**

The "mouth-washing" and "mouth-opening" rituals transformed a crafted statue into a living god:

- Before the ritual: "This statue without its mouth opened cannot smell incense, cannot eat food, nor drink water"
- During: The human artisans' hands are "symbolically cut off" — emphasizing that "in reality the idol was created by the gods"
- After: "The statue was in fact divine." The priest whispers into the statue's ear: "You are counted among your brother gods... From today may your destiny be counted as divinity"
- The wood was called "the flesh of the gods" and "the bones of the gods"

**Key point — the statue IS the god, not a symbol:**
> "A ṣalmu, then, did not merely direct the worshipper's mind toward a god who dwelled in some other sphere; it did not depict the god. Rather, once the *mīs pî* or *pīt pî* ritual was complete, the divine presence entered into the statue, and the ṣalmu was the god."

**The consequence — multiple simultaneous embodiments:**
- The god dwelled in heaven AND in its cult statue AND potentially in other statues at other temples
- "The god is indeed the statue, but also is not only the statue"
- Idol polemics in the Bible miss this: "The idol was not an idol, i.e., merely an object of worship, it was the god"

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The ritual texts are explicit and unambiguous.

## Claim 3: ANE fluidity was ABSENT from ancient Greece — the key religious divide isn't mono/polytheism

**Sommer's claim:** "Greek culture provides no evidence that multiple objects could contain the presence of a particular deity at any one moment. Ancient Greek religion furthermore maintained that deities' selves were consistently distinct from each other." The Greek gods had discrete bodies and discrete selves LIKE HUMANS. The ANE gods were fundamentally UNLIKE humans in their fluidity.

**The implication:** The traditional distinction between monotheism and polytheism is less fundamental than the distinction between FLUID and NON-FLUID divinity. Greece was polytheistic but non-fluid. Israel was monotheistic but fluid (in J/E). This cross-cuts the standard taxonomy.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the ANE evidence. MEDIUM-HIGH on the Greek contrast (Sommer's documentation is persuasive but some classicists might challenge the generalization).
