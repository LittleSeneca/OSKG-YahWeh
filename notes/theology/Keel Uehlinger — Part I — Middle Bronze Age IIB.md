# Keel & Uehlinger — Part I: Equality of the Sexes — Middle Bronze Age IIB (1750–1550 BCE)

Part I covers the first flourishing period of Canaanite urban culture with a distinct iconographic tradition, establishing the baseline against which all later Iron Age developments must be measured. The title — "Equality of the Sexes" — signals the core finding: male and female deities, symbols, and worshipers appear in roughly balanced proportions. The goddess is not marginal, not a consort-afterthought, but a fully independent power represented more frequently in metal figurines than any male deity. This is the religious world into which — centuries later — Yahweh would enter.

The chapter covers nine topics: caprids and lions (§10–12), the Hippopotamus Goddess and Q-symbol (§13–14), the Naked Goddess, goddess heads, and trees (§15–17), cult installations at Nahariyah and Gezer (§18–20), the temples of Tel Kitan and Megiddo (§21–22), the weather god and his consort (§23–24), the falcon-headed figure (§25), the ruler with worshipers (§26–27), and couples (§28).

## Claim 1: Caprids and lions are gender-ambiguous symbols whose meaning depends entirely on whether the interpretive framework is Egyptian or Near Eastern — revealing a religious world without rigid gender boundaries

**Authors' claim:** "Whether the figure is to be assigned to the male or to the female sphere depends in each case on the overall classification of the motifs and the tradition history, in other words, on whether one detects stronger influences from Egypt or from the Near East."

Caprids (wild goats, ibexes) appear on scarabs since MB IIA and surge in MB IIB. They are symbols of virility — "strength and power... symbols of virility" (p. 19). But they also belong unmistakably to the sphere of the goddess. On Old Syrian cylinder seals, reclining caprids appear in the same scene with a goddess. A scarab from Jericho depicts the "Naked Goddess" with ram's horns, making her "the source of the power of life in the plants and animals" (p. 20). Keel & Uehlinger note that even in the Song of Songs (2:7; 3:5), lovers are adjured "by the gazelles and the wild does" — animals in the goddess's sphere.

Lions are more complex. On the 42 known examples, 57% combine lion with uraeus (apotropaic/protective) and only 14% with branches (fertility). The lion "primarily symbolize[s] male potency and aggressiveness" (p. 24). The Egyptian tradition shows the lion as triumphant pharaoh — striding over enemies, attacking humans, standing above a crocodile hacked to pieces. "The lion is always the victor, never the vanquished, and even homo sapiens is defeated" (Tufnell, cited p. 23). But Near Eastern tradition places the lion in the goddess's sphere: as "Mistress of the Animals" (bēlet ṣēri), an epithet of Ashratum (the precursor of Asherah). A scarab from Tell el-Ajjul shows the goddess between a lion and a vulture — the "Mistress of the Steppe."

**Evidence presented:**
- 53 caprid scarabs: 39.6% with branch (fertility emphasis), 15% with uraeus (protection)
- 42 lion scarabs: 57% with uraeus (defense dominant), 14% with branch
- 9 examples show lion attacking caprid from behind — aggression motif
- Caprids have a deep indigenous Palestinian tradition: Chalcolithic ibex depictions, Early Bronze Age examples, horns alone as symbol
- Old Syrian cylinder seals show lions in goddess scenes (8 references to Winter 1983 catalog)
- The lion-striding-over-crocodile motif is completely without Near Eastern parallel — unambiguously Egyptian
- One unique scarab from Jericho (illus. 7) shows a hybrid: lion raised on hind legs facing a worshiping man — "reminiscent of Old Syrian compositions" but the lion lays its paw on the man and the man venerates

**Confidence:** HIGH. The statistical breakdown of branch vs. uraeus combinations provides hard data. The dual tradition (Egyptian royal lion vs. Near Eastern goddess-associated lion) is well-established in the comparative literature.

**What's at stake:** This gender ambiguity — the same animal belonging to male or female divine spheres depending on cultural context — reveals a pre-monotheistic religious imagination where divine domains weren't sharply segregated by gender. The rigid "Yahweh = male warrior, Asherah = female fertility" binary that many scholars retroject onto preexilic Israelite religion is not present in MB IIB iconography. The goddess could be aggressive (lion); the male deity could be associated with vegetation (branch). This fluidity is what monotheism would eventually erase.

**Who disagrees:** Hornung and Staehelin (1976) group all these animals together as "wild beasts of the desert" representing generic life-force with no attention to gender. Keel & Uehlinger explicitly critique this: "This interpretation pays no attention to the gender of the animals, nor does it show any interest in identifying the specific powers that the animals might be representing" (p. 20).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that the dual attributions reflect different local traditions rather than genuine gender fluidity — that a lion in a goddess scene at one site means something different from a lion at another site, and that Keel & Uehlinger are over-reading the co-occurrence data.

**My assessment:** The quantitative approach (branch vs. uraeus percentages) is methodologically sound and reveals real patterns. The Egypt vs. Near East duality is a genuinely useful heuristic. But the key finding is the one Keel & Uehlinger themselves emphasize: that these symbols *cannot* be assigned to a single gender sphere without examining the full iconographic context. This is a lesson that applies directly to interpreting Iron Age Israelite iconography — a seal with a lion doesn't automatically mean "Yahweh as lion"; it could just as easily mean "goddess as mistress of lions." Context is everything.

## Claim 2: The Naked Goddess was transformed from a Syrian cylinder-seal consort into a uniquely Canaanite "Branch Goddess" — a mass-market fertility deity for personal devotion

**Authors' claim:** "Fertility and prosperity are accentuated on the scarabs, not the sexuality and eroticism of palace life. The goddess is more patently and directly associated with fertility on the scarabs, especially the fertility of the vegetation, than is the case on the Old Syrian cylinder seals."

This is the most important iconographic transformation documented in Part I. The "Naked Goddess" originates in Old Syrian cylinder seal glyptic — elite art for palace culture. On cylinder seals, she typically holds her arms bent across her abdomen or holds her breasts. She appears before a male consort (usually the weather god), often disrobing or opening her clothing — the emphasis is on erotic encounter.

On the 44 MB IIB scarabs recovered in Palestine, everything changes. The goddess now appears "in splendid isolation" (p. 27). She is depicted frontally on two-thirds of specimens — "the direct frontal view is extremely rare in the ancient Near East" (p. 28). Her arms hang down alongside her body on most examples, a posture "more typical of depictions of Egyptian maidservants" than of goddesses. Her genitalia are frequently emphasized while breasts are not depicted. Crucially, she is flanked by branches on 36 of 44 specimens (82%) — hence Schroer's designation "Branch Goddess." On one piece, branches sprout from her genitalia; on another, her genitalia are covered by a leaf or branch.

**Evidence presented:**
- 44 Naked Goddess scarabs total; 22 from controlled excavations or surface finds in Palestine; the other half purchased (many in Jerusalem)
- Two from Tell el-Yehudiyeh in the eastern Nile Delta — either Egyptian exports to Canaan or Canaanite manufacture
- 36 of 44 (82%) flank the goddess with branches/little trees
- Two-thirds show frontal view; exaggeratedly large ears — interpreted by Schroer as "willingness of the goddess to hear" worshipers, not a Hathor relic
- Arms hang down alongside body on most specimens (contrast cylinder seals where arms are bent or hold breasts)
- Genitalia emphasized, breasts not depicted — "the real reason why the goddess lets her arms hang down... is because of the effect caused by the exaggeratedly long arms as they provide a frame for her genitalia" (p. 28)
- On cylinder seals, the goddess disrobes for copulation; on scarabs, genitalia are "associated with vegetation"
- Goddess heads (often misidentified as "Hathor fetish") undergo parallel transformation: cow's ears become large human ears, horns become palm leaves or blossoms (Schroer 1989, 139–185)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The statistical data (82% branch association, two-thirds frontal) is robust. The contrast with cylinder seal conventions is clear and well-documented. Schroer's (1989) monograph provides the detailed typology.

**What's at stake:** This transformation reveals the democratization of goddess religion. The cylinder seal goddess belongs to palace elites — her sexuality is displayed for a royal consort. The scarab goddess belongs to ordinary people — she faces the worshiper directly, hears their prayers, and guarantees vegetation and fertility. This is the material evidence for the "folk religion" that Dever argues for in Iron Age Israel, but rooted a thousand years earlier. The Judean Pillar Figurines (JPFs) of the 8th–6th centuries — nude females holding breasts — are the Iron Age descendants of this MB IIB Branch Goddess tradition. The MB IIB evidence shows that female-centered fertility religion was not a late corruption of "pure" Yahwism but the indigenous religious baseline.

**Who disagrees:** The "Hathor fetish" interpretation of goddess heads — that these are Egyptian imports, not Canaanite transformations. Schroer's arguments for local adaptation (human ears, palm-leaf horns) challenge this. Scholars who see all nude female iconography as generic "fertility amulets" without specific divine reference would also push back.

**Alternative reading:** The branch-flanked goddess might be an entirely separate iconographic type from the cylinder seal goddess — a local Palestinian invention that borrowed some visual vocabulary but represents a different deity or concept. Keel & Uehlinger assume continuity; the alternative is parallel independent development.

**My assessment:** The transformation argument is convincing because it explains both continuities (the figure type, the nudity, the genital emphasis) and differences (frontal view, branch flanking, isolation from consort) within a single coherent model. The key insight is that the medium shapes the message: a cylinder seal rolls out a narrative scene with multiple figures, but a scarab base is a single static image meant for personal contemplation. The goddess adapted to her new medium by becoming more intimate, more directly present to the worshiper. This is a genuinely important finding for understanding how religion actually functions through material culture.

## Claim 3: The Q-symbol and Hippopotamus Goddess reveal a female-centered apotropaic religion concerned with protecting mothers and children — a parallel religious sphere to the male-dominated official cult

**Authors' claim:** The Q-shaped mark "originated in Babylonia and may have symbolized a mother's womb. This sign was associated with ancient mother goddesses such as Ninhursag or Nintu."

This section introduces two categories of evidence for a specifically female-oriented religious practice. First, the Hippopotamus Goddess (Thoeris, tꜣ wr.t "the big one") — a hybrid creature with aspects of crocodile and lion, whose "principal duty was to keep all types of evil from mothers and small children" (p. 24). She appears on Egyptian "magic knives" (apotropaic wands) found at Tell el-Ajjul (~1750 BCE), on bone inlay from a tomb south of Jaffa (~1650–1550), and on a scarab from Azor.

Second, the Q-symbol — a distinctive mark on seal amulets produced in northern Syria or southeastern Anatolia around 1750 BCE, exported throughout the Levant. An Old Babylonian terracotta plaque depicts a mother goddess between two miscarried infants with the Q-sign above each one. "Putting this sign above miscarried infants fits the pattern detected in the rather common practice of placing seal amulets with the Q-shaped symbol in the graves of children" (p. 26). The symbol persists for over a millennium: it appears on a mother goddess pendant in the Late Bronze Age (§44) and in Judean graves of Iron Age IIC, where "its function might be to characterize the grave as a mother's womb" (§356f.).

**Evidence presented:**
- Hippopotamus Goddess on magic knife fragment from Tell el-Ajjul (~1750, Altenmüller 1965)
- Two examples on bone inlay from a tomb 14 km south of Jaffa (~1650–1550) — the excavator misidentified them as "Hyksos horses" (Ory 1945)
- Scarab from late MB IIB necropolis at Azor confirms identification
- Q-symbol seal amulets: oval plaque with ring handle in Anatolia, rudimentary scarab shape in southern Palestine — produced at a faience export facility ~1750
- Old Babylonian plaque: mother goddess with Q-signs above miscarried infants
- Continued use: Late Bronze Age mother goddess pendant with Q-symbol; Iron Age IIC Judean graves
- The Q-symbol's function "might be to characterize the grave as a mother's womb" (Keel et al. 1989, 39–87 for full study)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Hippopotamus Goddess material is well-dated and clearly Egyptian in origin — the identification is secure. The Q-symbol's womb interpretation is more inferential, supported by the Old Babylonian plaque and the grave context but ultimately an interpretation of an abstract symbol.

**What's at stake:** This section shows that MB IIB religion had a distinctly female sphere of concern — pregnancy, childbirth, infant mortality — addressed by specifically female-coded religious objects (Hippopotamus Goddess, womb-symbol amulets). This is not "goddess religion" in the abstract but concretely functional religion addressing the highest-stakes experiences in an ancient woman's life. The persistence of the Q-symbol from MB IIB through Iron Age IIC (a span of ~1200 years) demonstrates extraordinary religious continuity at the level of personal/family piety. This continuity is invisible in texts, which are concerned with official cult and national deities. If we only had the Hebrew Bible, we would know nothing of this tradition. The iconographic evidence reveals a layer of religion the biblical authors either didn't know about or chose not to record.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who interpret the Q-symbol differently — as a potter's mark, a decorative element, or an abstract sign without specific religious meaning. The womb interpretation, while plausible, is not universally accepted.

**Alternative reading:** The Q-symbol might have multiple meanings depending on context — womb symbolism in one setting, something else in another. The 1200-year continuity might reflect reuse of older objects (heirlooms) rather than continuous religious practice.

**My assessment:** The magic knife/Hippopotamus Goddess evidence is strong — these are well-understood Egyptian apotropaic objects with clear function. The Q-symbol interpretation is plausible but less certain. The most important contribution here is methodological: by connecting objects found in children's graves to iconography of mother goddesses and womb symbolism, Keel & Uehlinger demonstrate how to extract religious meaning from archaeological context without texts. This is exactly the methodology the Introduction promised. The connection to Iron Age IIC Judean graves is particularly striking — it suggests that womb/rebirth symbolism persisted in popular religion long after official Yahwism had supposedly eliminated goddess worship. Dever's JPF evidence from Iron Age Judah tells the same story from the other end: female fertility religion survived every reform.

## Claim 4: The Nahariyah and Gezer cult installations were dedicated to a goddess — and the standing stones (maṣṣēbôt) could represent female, not just male, deities

**Authors' claim:** "Stelae made of stone are not necessarily images of male deities... The figure on this stele... is represented more realistically by means of sculpted genital lips" (Tel Kitan stele, p. 35).

At Nahariyah, a cult site near a spring in far northern Palestine, the key find is a steatite mold (22 cm high) for mass-producing metal statuettes of a naked female figure. Her hands rest on her thighs flanking her genitalia — the same emphasis seen on the Branch Goddess scarabs. Nineteen goddess figurines (or fragments) were found in a vessel under plaster pavement, some solid-cast (using the mold), others cut from sheet-silver or sheet-bronze. "The figures are 4–10 cm high; those cut from sheet metal often have a little hole cut into them near the top end, which shows that these figures of the goddess were worn as pendants" (p. 31). Dove figurines (in flight, wings outstretched) were discovered "in the same quantities as the goddess figures" (p. 31). On Old Syrian seals, doves fly from the disrobing goddess toward the weather god — "messengers of her love" (Keel 1984, 58–62). Lion and vulture amulets were also found at Nahariyah.

At Gezer, the famous row of ten monoliths (up to 3.25 m tall) has been traditionally interpreted as phallic symbols or memorial stelae for male rulers. Keel & Uehlinger challenge this: a bronze figurine found in a treasure cache at Gezer is "iconographically very similar to those made from the steatite mold at Nahariyah" — naked female, tall head covering, hands on thighs. This "suggests... that the massebahs might also portray female deities" (p. 33).

At Tel Kitan, a village south of the Sea of Galilee (~1750–1650 BCE), a row of about ten small maṣṣēbôt stood before a temple building. One of the stelae "was shaped, in very rough fashion, in the form of a naked woman" — 45 cm high, with "sculpted genital lips" and barely indicated breasts (p. 35). This confirms the Gezer inference: maṣṣēbôt can represent goddesses.

**Evidence presented:**
- Nahariyah: steatite mold for mass-producing naked goddess figurines (22 cm high, found on platform)
- 19 goddess figurines/fragments in a vessel hoard; some solid-cast, some sheet-metal with pendant holes
- Dove figures in equal quantities to goddess figures; dove amulet also from Tell el-Ajjul
- Lion amulet (possibly 12th Dynasty Egyptian) and vulture amulet — both in goddess sphere
- Gezer: 10 monoliths up to 3.25 m, dated ~1600 BCE by ceramic evidence
- Bronze female figurine from Gezer treasure cache: naked, tall head covering, hands on thighs — same type as Nahariyah mold
- Gold sheet figures from Gezer (largest 16 cm): naked except head covering, necklace, sash; genitalia "shown in larger scale" with continuous line framing them; narrowed at bottom — possibly "made to imitate the form of wooden or stone figures that were stuck into the ground" (p. 33–34)
- Macalister found 7 similar naked female figures at Gezer; one appears pregnant (hand supporting protruding abdomen — rare fertility emphasis)
- Tel Kitan: stele shaped as naked woman among row of maṣṣēbôt — sculpted genital lips, stump arms
- Two lead figures from Tell el-Ajjul: goddess with hands on breasts, high horned head covering

**Confidence:** HIGH. The convergence of evidence across three sites (Nahariyah, Gezer, Tel Kitan) with different media (stone mold, sheet-metal, gold, stone stele) is compelling. The Tel Kitan stele is the clincher — an unambiguous female maṣṣēbâ.

**What's at stake:** This directly challenges a long-standing assumption in biblical archaeology that maṣṣēbôt represent male deities (or are phallic symbols). If maṣṣēbôt can represent goddesses, then every Iron Age Israelite site with standing stones must be re-evaluated. The two maṣṣēbôt in the Arad temple's holy of holies — which Dever identifies as "Yhwh and another deity" (almost certainly Asherah) — could both potentially represent female deities, or one male and one female. The biblical prohibition of maṣṣēbôt (Deut 16:22; Lev 26:1) takes on new meaning if maṣṣēbôt were associated with goddess worship specifically. The reform movements (Hezekiah, Josiah) may have been targeting not just "Canaanite" religion generically but goddess religion specifically.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that maṣṣēbôt are exclusively male/phallic symbols. Kempinski's attempt to date the Gezer installation to MB IIA (earlier) — Keel & Uehlinger reject this based on Giveon's demonstration that the Egyptian statuette used for dating is found in later contexts too (p. 33, note 11).

**Alternative reading:** The female stele at Tel Kitan might be exceptional — one goddess among many male stelae. The Gezer monoliths, being abstract/aniconic, might not be gendered at all. The presence of a female figurine in a cache at Gezer doesn't prove the monoliths represent goddesses.

**My assessment:** The Tel Kitan stele is genuinely important evidence that should change how archaeologists interpret maṣṣēbôt. It's a single example, but it proves possibility — once you know maṣṣēbôt *can* represent goddesses, you can't assume they always represent gods. The Nahariyah mold is equally important because it shows mass production of goddess figurines for a cult site — this is organized, institutional goddess worship, not private folk practice. Combined, these sites demonstrate that MB IIB Canaan had substantial, well-funded goddess cults with permanent installations, professional workshops, and standardized iconography. This is the religious infrastructure that later Israelite religion either built upon or reacted against.

## Claim 5: The MB IIB weather god is primarily a vegetation/fertility deity holding a branch — not the warrior-smiter he becomes in the Late Bronze Age

**Authors' claim:** "The weather god appears less frequently on Middle Bronze Age IIB scarabs. Where he does appear, his relationship with fertility is accentuated, not his aggressive nature; that aspect will become more important during the Late Bronze Age. He does in fact raise his right hand but he holds no weapon, so that the gesture depicts more likely a victory rather than a battle. In his other hand, stretched out in front of himself, the deity holds a branch or a blossom."

This is a critical chronological finding. The weather god (Baal/Hadad type) is well-known from Ugaritic texts as a warrior who defeats Yamm (Sea) and Mot (Death), wielding a club and lightning. Scholars routinely project this warrior persona backward onto MB IIB Canaanite religion. But the iconographic evidence complicates this narrative.

The Megiddo cylinder seal (illus. 30) shows the weather god in a loincloth and high pointed cap, holding a club in his raised left hand and an axe in his right — but he also holds a rope tied around a reclining bull. He faces the goddess who "pushes her clothing aside" while bulls' heads emphasize their connection. On the Hazor seal fragment (illus. 31a), the goddess "pulls up ostentatiously" her clothing, and a secondary motif shows a suckling cow with turned head — "the weather god appears in a context associated with both sexuality and fertility" (p. 39).

But on the scarabs (illuss. 32a–d), the weather god appears without weapons. He raises his right hand but "holds no weapon, so that the gesture depicts more likely a victory rather than a battle." His other hand holds a branch or blossom — explicitly linking him to vegetation. "This suggests a natural link to the 'Branch Goddess,' who obviously must be his consort" (p. 39).

**Evidence presented:**
- Old Syrian hematite cylinder seal from Megiddo (illus. 30): weather god with weapons AND bull; goddess disrobing; ruler and horned worshiper as secondary figures
- Hazor hematite cylinder seal fragment (illus. 31a): weather god approaching goddess who lifts clothing; suckling cow secondary motif
- Tell el-Far'ah cylinder seal (illus. 31b): goddess disrobing; two worshipers under winged disk
- Scarabs (illuss. 32a–d): weather god without weapons, raised hand = victory gesture, branch/blossom in left hand
- 22 metal deity figurines from Megiddo sacral area: 16 female, only 6 male (Negbi 1976, 131) — "male deities are here as well, but they are by far in the minority" (p. 36)
- Weather god with weapons in "Anatolian posture" dates to MB IIA at Megiddo (Loud 1948, pl. 233.1) — the armed type exists, but the branch-holding type dominates the scarab evidence
- Limestone casting mold from Stratum XIIIB (1800–1750): god with axe — mass-produced armed god figurines existed alongside branch-holding depictions

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The scarab evidence is clear: the weather god holds a branch, not a weapon. The cylinder seals from Megiddo and Hazor complicate the picture — they show an armed weather god in erotic encounter with the goddess. The resolution may be that both types coexisted: an armed warrior type (cylinder seals, metal figurines) and a vegetation/fertility type (scarabs). The claim that the warrior aspect "will become more important during the Late Bronze Age" is a prediction the rest of the book must support.

**What's at stake:** This complicates the standard scholarly narrative about Yahweh's origins. If Yahweh is modeled on the Canaanite weather god (as Smith, Day, and Cross argue), but the MB IIB weather god is primarily a vegetation deity holding a branch, then the "Yahweh as storm/warrior god from the southern deserts" narrative may be projecting Late Bronze Age warrior imagery backward. The MB IIB weather god looks more like a fertility consort than a conquering warrior. This would support Römer's argument that Yahweh's warrior aspect developed gradually, not all at once. It also raises the possibility that Yahweh absorbed the vegetation/fertility aspect of the weather god before acquiring the warrior aspect — which would invert the standard narrative.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (2001), who argues that the West Semitic weather god is fundamentally a warrior and that Yahweh's warrior identity is primary. Day (2000), who traces Yahweh's Baal-like characteristics. Cross (1973), who sees Yahweh as a divine warrior from the earliest poetry. All rely primarily on Ugaritic texts (14th–13th c. BCE) which postdate MB IIB by centuries.

**Alternative reading:** The branch-holding weather god may be a specifically Canaanite variant, while the armed weather god is the Syrian/North Mesopotamian type. The scarabs, being largely local products, reflect the local variant. The cylinder seals, being imports from northern Syria, reflect the Syrian variant. The distinction would then be geographical, not chronological.

**My assessment:** The chronological argument is the most interesting but also the hardest to prove — it requires demonstrating that the armed weather god type genuinely gains prominence in LB, which depends on evidence not yet presented in Part I. The more solid finding is simply that a branch-holding weather god existed in MB IIB and was closely paired with the Branch Goddess. This shows that the weather god and goddess were conceived as a complementary fertility pair — he brings rain, she brings vegetation — long before any "battle with the sea" mythology dominates the iconography. For the Truth Project, this matters because it shows the religious world into which Yahweh was eventually slotted was a world of divine pairs, not solitary male deities.

## Claim 6: The falcon-headed figure represents a deliberate merging of the Egyptian royal god Horus with the Canaanite weather/fertility god — ideological groundwork for Hyksos rule

**Authors' claim:** "The self-confident Canaanites who lived at the end of the eighteenth century and during the seventeenth and sixteenth centuries associated their royal god, their fertility god, with the chief Egyptian royal god, so they placed a branch or blossom scepter into the hand of their own royal god" (p. 41).

The falcon-headed figure appears much more frequently than the weather god on MB IIB scarabs. German scholars identify it as the Canaanite god Horon; English-speaking scholars identify it as the Egyptian sun god Re. Keel & Uehlinger argue both sides are wrong because they ignore the iconographic tradition.

The key evidence comes from the "jasper group" — cylinder seals and scarabs made from green jasper (green slate facies) produced in Byblos or further south, dated 1720–1650 BCE (Collon 1986). The falcon-headed figure with Egyptian double crown appears on Old Syrian cylinder seals as "clearly shown as the royal god, which means he is probably Horus" (p. 41). On scarabs, the double crown disappears but the falcon head remains — "this seems to change his identity but little" (p. 41). The non-Egyptian element is the branch or flower in the figure's hand — "probably a branch scepter or a blossom scepter, adapted for the falcon-headed figure from the Canaanite weather god, who was a royal god as well." The result: "a merging of attributes from the Egyptian royal god with those of the Canaanite royal god" (p. 41).

The same merging appears in reverse: a scarab from Tell el-Dab'a (Hyksos capital) shows a pharaoh wearing the crown of Lower Egypt, holding a club in his right hand AND a branch in his left — "quite unlike what the Egyptians would have included in such a scene" (p. 41).

The falcon-headed figure also appears victorious over the crocodile (illuss. 34a–b) — an Egyptian motif, but one that was taboo in Egypt because the crocodile was holy. "In Canaan, however, Baal was acclaimed as victor over litanu/Leviathan, the chaos monster depicted as a reptile. This motif may have encouraged portrayal (though discreet) of this victory" (p. 43). And the Horus falcon with outstretched wings dominates jewelry in the Byblos royal tombs — "the kings of Byblos fancied themselves, in their puny little territory... as if they were in fact little pharaohs" (Chéhab, cited p. 43).

**Evidence presented:**
- Falcon-headed figure on jasper group cylinder seal (Collon 1986, 58 no. 4)
- Impression from Alalakh Stratum VII palace (1720–1650; Collon 1975, 78f. no. 144)
- Seal of Sumirapa of Tuba (same period; Nougayrol/Amiet 1962)
- On scarabs: falcon-headed figure without double crown but with branch/blossom (illuss. 33a–c)
- Falcon-headed figure victorious over crocodile (illuss. 34a–b; Keel et al. 1989, 268–275)
- Horus falcon with outstretched wings on jasper group scarabs (illus. 34c)
- Five gold earrings in falcon-with-outstretched-wings form from Tell el-Ajjul (Petrie 1931, pl. 15)
- Tell el-Dab'a scarab: pharaoh with club AND branch
- Egyptian reaction: "regarded this equating of deities as impudent and insolent" (p. 41)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The identification of the falcon-headed figure with the jasper group is securely established by Collon's work. The "merging" interpretation is inherently plausible given the Hyksos context. The key uncertainty is whether Canaanites themselves understood the falcon-headed figure as a merged Horus-Baal or simply as Horus with local attributes added — there's a difference between deliberate theological syncretism and casual borrowing of visual elements.

**What's at stake:** This is evidence for religious syncretism driven by political ambition. The Hyksos ("Rulers of Foreign Lands") needed ideological legitimation to rule Egypt, and one strategy was to claim that their god was the same as the Egyptian royal god — just with a branch instead of an ankh. This is the same strategy that later produced Greco-Egyptian Sarapis and Roman-Imperial syncretisms. It also foreshadows the later question of whether Yahweh absorbed solar and royal imagery from Egyptian models — if Canaanites were already merging their royal god with Horus in MB IIB, then the solarization of Yahweh in Iron Age IIB–C (which Keel & Uehlinger will document in later chapters) has deep precedent.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who identify the falcon-headed figure as purely Egyptian (Re) or purely Canaanite (Horon) — both positions refuse the syncretism thesis. The Egyptians themselves clearly rejected the equivalence: Egyptian reaction to Hyksos-era syncretism was hostile.

**Alternative reading:** The falcon-headed figure with a branch might simply depict an Egyptian deity (Horus) in a Canaanite artistic style where branches were a standard attribute of any deity associated with vegetation. This would be iconographic borrowing without theological syncretism — the same god, different visual conventions.

**My assessment:** The Byblos evidence is the strongest support for the syncretism thesis — the kings of Byblos were literally wearing Egyptian royal symbols while ruling a Canaanite city. The branch/blossom scepter in the falcon-headed figure's hand is genuinely non-Egyptian and requires explanation. Keel & Uehlinger's explanation — deliberate merging of Canaanite and Egyptian royal gods — is the most economical. But I'm cautious about over-reading political theology from scarab designs. The fact that these are mass-produced amulets, not royal inscriptions, suggests the merging may have been more organic and popular than centrally directed. Either way, the evidence shows that MB IIB Canaanites were comfortable combining Egyptian and Canaanite divine attributes — a religious flexibility that monotheism would later foreclose.

## Claim 7: The ruler in the fringed garment represents a deified Canaanite kingship — a mediating figure between divine and human spheres that both prefigures and contrasts with later Israelite royal ideology

**Authors' claim:** "Politically, this led to the founding of the Hyksos Dynasty in Egypt. Religiously, the sovereigns appear as some sort of mediating figures who bridge the gap between the divine and earthly worlds. Perhaps they were deified after they died" (p. 43).

The "ruler wearing the garment with thick fringes" is the dominant human figure in MB IIB glyptic art, appearing on both cylinder seals and scarabs of the jasper group. On Old Syrian cylinder seals, the fringed garment is worn by both worshipers and the worshiped — the same figure can appear as supplicant and as object of veneration. The ruler appears alone on numerous scarabs, sometimes with an enemy prostrate beneath his throne (illus. 36d) — a motif that "reminds one of the enemies that are under the royal lion" (p. 44). Stone stelae fragments from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem portray the fringed-garment ruler in raised relief — "perhaps these stelae were used in the cultic worship of the person who is depicted" (p. 45).

The chronological context is crucial: this iconography emerges ~1700–1650 BCE, exactly when Canaanite city-state kings were asserting independence from the weakening Egyptian 13th Dynasty — the process that culminated in Hyksos rule over northern Egypt (~1650 BCE). "Schroer has correctly determined that the depiction of the ruler who is wearing the garment with thick fringes and appears all alone provides an indication of the strong position that the Canaanite city-state kings assumed" (p. 43).

Male and female worshipers also appear frequently on scarabs — holding branches/flowers, standing before deities, offering sacrifices, or dancing (illuss. 39a–c; 40a–b). The weather god on one oval plaque is mirrored on the reverse by three dancers, one of whom "assumes the posture of the weather god and even holds a branch in his hand" (p. 45) — ritual imitation of the deity.

**Evidence presented:**
- Jasper group cylinder seals (1720–1650 BCE): ruler in fringed garment as central figure (Collon 1986, illuss. 1–2, 5–6, 11)
- Barqai scarab (~1650 BCE): only example where ruler wears both fringed garment AND tall Syrian cap (illus. 36a)
- Scarabs with ruler alone (illuss. 36b–c) — interpreted as evidence of strong kingship and possible deification
- Unique scarab (illus. 36d): enemy prostrate under ruler's throne
- Stelae fragments from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem: ruler in fringed garment in raised relief (illuss. 37–38; Merhav 1985)
- Male and female worshipers with branch/flower on numerous steatite scarabs (illuss. 39a–c)
- Dancers depicted on scarabs (illuss. 40a–b) — "enthusiastic activity taking place in the cult, expressed by scenes with dancers, is most likely to be attributed to worship of the weather god" (p. 45)
- Oval plaque: weather god on one side, three dancers on reverse — one dancer imitates weather god's posture

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The iconographic type is well-defined and the link to political developments is plausible. The evidence for actual deification (cultic worship of the ruler's image) is more inferential — stelae fragments could be commemorative rather than cultic.

**What's at stake:** This is the iconographic evidence for divine or semi-divine kingship in MB IIB Canaan — a concept the Hebrew Bible explicitly rejects (the Davidic king is adopted as Yahweh's son, not deified; the anti-monarchic strain in 1 Samuel 8 treats kingship itself as rebellion against divine rule). The biblical polemic against Canaanite kingship (Deuteronomy 17:14–20 limits the king's power, horses, wives, and wealth) may be reacting specifically against the kind of kingship depicted on these MB IIB seals — a king who mediates between divine and human worlds, receives homage, and is depicted with attributes that blur the line between ruler and god. The fact that male and female worshipers carry branches/flowers — the same attributes held by deities — suggests a religious system where the boundary between human and divine was permeable in ways that Israelite religion would later police much more strictly.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the fringed-garment figure as simply a human worshiper or local ruler without divine claims. The interpretation of these figures as "deified" is Keel & Uehlinger's (and Schroer's), not universally shared.

**Alternative reading:** The fringed-garment figure might represent an ideal type — "the pious ruler" — rather than a specific deified individual. The stelae could commemorate royal patronage of the cult rather than cultic worship of the king himself. The prostrate enemy under the throne is borrowed from Egyptian royal iconography and may be conventional rather than theological.

**My assessment:** The evidence for some form of exalted kingship is strong — the ruler appears as a central, independent figure on seals; receives homage; is depicted with a defeated enemy underfoot. Whether this amounts to full deification or "sacral kingship" is harder to determine. For the Truth Project, the important point is that MB IIB Canaan had a political-religious system where the king occupied a semi-divine mediating role. This is the ideological background against which Israelite kingship defined itself — either by adopting similar imagery (the royal psalms' language of the king as God's son sitting at his right hand) or by rejecting it (the prophetic critique of kingship, the Deuteronomistic restrictions). Either way, you can't understand Israelite royal ideology without understanding MB IIB Canaanite royal ideology first.

## Claim 8: Erotic couples on seals and scarabs mirror divine intimacy on the human plane — showing that sexuality was integrated into MB IIB religion in ways the Hebrew Bible would later suppress

**Authors' claim:** "Perhaps we should treat a series of representations of couples that convey strongly erotic connotations as analogous to the encounter of the weather god with the 'Naked Goddess'" (p. 45).

The divine couple — weather god and goddess — are shown on cylinder seals in explicitly sexual encounter: the goddess pushes aside or lifts up her clothing (Megiddo seal, illus. 30; Hazor fragment, illus. 31a; Tell el-Far'ah seal, illus. 31b). The parallel on the human level is a series of couple depictions: an Old Syrian hematite cylinder seal from Tell el-Ajjul (illus. 41) shows a man and woman drinking together from a shared vessel through tubes — with palms and an eight-rayed star (symbols of Ishtar/Astarte). A Krakow cylinder seal replaces the star with a flying dove (Keel/Uehlinger 1990, 126 illus. 158). Scarabs from late MB IIB "depict a man and a woman being intimate with one another, sometimes showing this more graphically, sometimes less" (illus. 42; Keel/Schroer 1985, 94f. illuss. 67–70). A Megiddo Stratum XII scarab (1750–1700 BCE) shows a fringed-garment ruler with a woman with a thick braid, flanked by a flower bud and a fully opened bloom (illus. 43).

The authors explicitly note that these erotic representations "are common for the Middle Bronze Age but disappear in the later periods" (p. 45).

**Evidence presented:**
- Old Syrian hematite cylinder seal from Tell el-Ajjul (illus. 41): couple drinking together; palms and eight-rayed star (Ishtar/Astarte symbols)
- Krakow cylinder seal: couple drinking together; flying dove replaces star
- Multiple MB IIB scarabs with erotic couples (illus. 42; Keel/Schroer 1985, 94f.)
- Megiddo Stratum XII scarab (illus. 43): ruler + woman with braid + flower bud + full bloom
- Divine-level parallel: Megiddo seal (illus. 30) — goddess disrobing before weather god
- Hazor fragment (illus. 31a) — goddess lifting clothing; suckling cow (fertility secondary motif)
- "Such representations are common for the Middle Bronze Age but disappear in the later periods"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The cylinder seals with divine-erotic scenes are well-documented in Winter's (1983) catalog. The scarab couple depictions are more scattered but consistent enough to establish a pattern. The claim that these "disappear in later periods" is one the rest of the book must support.

**What's at stake:** This is evidence that MB IIB Canaanite religion integrated sexuality into its symbolic system at both divine and human levels. The goddess's sexuality — her disrobing, her genitalia, her love-messenger doves — was celebrated, not hidden. Human couples mirrored divine couples in erotic intimacy, and this intimacy was considered auspicious enough to place on seal amulets. This stands in stark contrast to the Hebrew Bible's treatment of sexuality in religious contexts: the prophets' metaphorical language of Israel as Yahweh's unfaithful wife (Hosea, Jeremiah, Ezekiel) treats female sexuality as shameful promiscuity; the Priestly tradition separates sexuality from the sacred through purity regulations; Canaanite religion is condemned precisely for its sexual character ("sacred prostitution" polemics). The MB IIB evidence shows what the biblical authors were reacting against — a religious world where divine and human sexuality were continuous, not compartmentalized.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue that the "sacred marriage" or hieros gamos was a literary motif rather than an actual practiced ritual. Also those who see the erotic cylinder seals as palace entertainment rather than religious iconography — the scenes might depict court life, not cult.

**Alternative reading:** The couple scenes could depict wedding celebrations or romantic ideals rather than sexual acts. The drinking-together motif might represent hospitality or alliance rather than eroticism. The "disappearance in later periods" might reflect changes in seal production and use rather than changes in religious attitudes toward sexuality.

**My assessment:** The connection between divine and human couple depictions is suggestive but not airtight. The cylinder seals showing the weather god and disrobing goddess are clearly religious — they include divine attributes (horned head coverings, bulls, worshipers). The scarabs with human couples are more ambiguous — they could be secular romantic imagery. But the shared vocabulary (branches, flowers, drinking together) and the explicit appearance of Ishtar/Astarte symbols (star, dove, palm) on the couple scenes supports Keel & Uehlinger's reading that these are religiously meaningful — human eroticism participating in divine eroticism. For the Truth Project, the key point is that MB IIB religion was comfortable with divine sexuality in ways that the Hebrew Bible is not. Whether this represents a genuine shift in religious sensibility or just different media/genres is a question that needs the rest of the book's evidence.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Caprids and lions are gender-ambiguous, context-dependent symbols | HIGH | Evidence that all lions = male, all caprids = female in every context |
| 2. Naked Goddess transformed into Branch Goddess for mass-market scarabs | HIGH | Alternative interpretation of branches as decorative rather than identifying |
| 3. Q-symbol and Hippopotamus Goddess = female-centered apotropaic religion | MEDIUM-HIGH | Non-religious interpretation of Q-symbol; Egyptian imports vs. local adoption |
| 4. Nahariyah/Gezer cult installations dedicated to goddess; maṣṣēbôt can be female | HIGH | Tel Kitan stele = single exception; Gezer monoliths = gender-neutral |
| 5. MB IIB weather god is vegetation deity, not warrior | MEDIUM-HIGH | Armed figurines from same period contradict branch-holding scarab evidence |
| 6. Falcon-headed figure = Canaanite-Egyptian royal god syncretism | MEDIUM-HIGH | Artistic borrowing without theological syncretism; purely Egyptian identification |
| 7. Fringed-garment ruler = deified/sacral Canaanite kingship | MEDIUM-HIGH | Stelae = commemorative not cultic; deification = over-reading |
| 8. Erotic couples mirror divine intimacy; sexuality integrated into religion | MEDIUM-HIGH | Scenes = secular romance; "disappearance" = change in media, not religion |

**Strongest claims:** 1 (gender ambiguity — hard statistical data on branch/uraeus combinations), 2 (Branch Goddess transformation — 82% branch association is compelling), and 4 (goddess cult installations — Tel Kitan stele is a genuine discovery that reframes maṣṣēbôt interpretation).

**Weakest claims:** 5 (weather god as non-warrior — the armed figurines and Megiddo seal complicate the picture; the claim depends on showing that the warrior type increases in LB, which Part I hasn't demonstrated yet), and 8 (erotic couples — the religious interpretation of human couple scenes is the most inferential).

**Key methodological contribution:** The chapter demonstrates Keel & Uehlinger's methodology in action: treating iconography as a system where meaning is determined by combinations (branch + goddess = fertility, uraeus + lion = protection), context (Egyptian vs. Near Eastern tradition history), and medium (cylinder seal vs. scarab = different audiences and functions). This is the approach that will make the Iron Age chapters so powerful — by the time they reach Iron Age IIB–C, they've established how to read combinations, contexts, and media shifts.

**Cross-reference connections:**
- **Dever (Chapter V):** The JPF evidence from Iron Age Judah is the clear Iron Age descendant of the MB IIB goddess figurine tradition documented at Nahariyah and Gezer. Dever's "folk religion" has a 1,000-year prehistory that Keel & Uehlinger are documenting here. The transformation from MB IIB Branch Goddess (genitalia emphasis, arms at sides, branch flanking) to Iron Age JPFs (breast emphasis, hands supporting breasts) is a story the later chapters will need to tell.
- **Smith (Origins):** Smith's West Semitic conceptual structures (divine family, divine council) are reconstructed from Ugaritic texts (14th–13th c. BCE). Keel & Uehlinger's MB IIB evidence shows a divine pair (weather god + goddess) that precedes the Ugaritic texts by centuries. This suggests Smith's conceptual structures have deeper roots than Ugarit, which supports his argument for broad West Semitic patterns — but the MB IIB evidence is geographically closer to Israel than Ugarit, which supports Keel & Uehlinger's argument that you don't need Ugarit to understand Canaanite religion.
- **Römer (Introduction):** Römer argues Yahweh was not always Israel's god and that the Bible preserves polytheistic traces. Keel & Uehlinger's MB IIB evidence shows the polytheistic baseline — this is what religion looked like in Canaan before Yahweh arrived. The weather god + goddess pair, the female-centered fertility cult, the divine kingship — all of these are the religious structures that Israelite religion either inherited, adapted, or rejected. The "equality of the sexes" in the chapter title is significant: monotheism around a male deity would represent a radical break from this balanced system.

**For the Truth Project:** Part I establishes that the pre-Yahwistic religious baseline in Canaan was characterized by (1) roughly balanced male and female divine representation, (2) a goddess cult with substantial institutional infrastructure (molds, workshops, permanent installations), (3) a weather god who was primarily a fertility/vegetation consort, not a warrior, (4) permeable boundaries between divine and human (king as mediator, human couples mirroring divine couples), and (5) a religious imagination comfortable with combining Egyptian and Near Eastern elements. Every one of these features will be transformed, suppressed, or polemicized against as Yahwism develops. The later chapters will show how — and how incompletely.

**Summary note (pp. 47–48):** The authors' own summary emphasizes the creative merging of Egyptian and Near Eastern traditions, the goddess's dominance over the male deity in representational frequency, and the crucial observation that "the religious system of signs that used massebahs, sacred trees, deities and rituals that had a patently sexual character, regarded by many as typical of 'the' Canaanite religion for the entire period from 1500 to 500, may in reality have played a decisive role only during Middle Bronze Age IIB and might have merely undergone radical modifications during the following periods, surviving only in a marginal way as the religion of the pagani, the villagers" (p. 48). This is a warning against flattening "Canaanite religion" into a timeless monolithic category — what biblical texts condemn as "Canaanite" may actually describe religious practices that were already marginal or transformed by the time the texts were written. The biblical polemic may be against a memory, not a living reality.
