# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter V: The Hidden God, Victorious Gods, and the Blessing of Fertility (Iron Age I, c. 1200–1000 BCE)

The transitional chapter. Keel and Uehlinger trace the iconographic record across the pivotal Iron Age I period — the era of the Judges, the Philistine arrival, the collapse of Egyptian imperial control, and the emergence of early Israelite settlement in the hill country. The chapter makes a startling argument: the anthropomorphic goddess, so prominent in the Late Bronze Age, **virtually disappears from glyptic art** during Iron Age I. In her place, fertility is expressed through depersonalized symbols — suckling mother animals, trees, scorpions — while male deities become exclusively dominating, triumphant, and aggressive. The "hidden god" Amun-Re, propagated through mass-produced seal amulets from the great temple at Gaza, dominates the period's iconography alongside Seth-Baal and Reshef, now shown standing ON their animal opponents rather than fighting them. This chapter establishes the Late Bronze-to-Iron Age transition in iconography and lays the groundwork for understanding how Yahweh will eventually absorb both the masculine domination motifs AND the depersonalized fertility symbols during Iron Age II.

---

## Claim 1: Iron Age I is a genuine transitional period — the Late Bronze urban system collapsed, but the iconographic repertoire shows both continuity with Egyptian traditions and radical innovation

**Author's claim:** The period is "a typical transitional period, marked by overlapping trends" (line 2-3). At Hazor, "the settlement located in the lower city ceased to exist and from that point on people lived almost exclusively up on the tell. The next strata, XII and XI, preserve a few remains from poorer, unfortified settlements that were little more than villages" (lines 16-19). At Megiddo, the Late Bronze city was destroyed by the mid-twelfth century, and Str. VIB preserves "a small and poorly built village" (line 25). At Lachish, "a gap in settlements followed, lasting some two hundred years" (line 40-41). Beth-Shean seems to skip Iron Age I village settlement entirely: "politically speaking, [Iron Age I] did not take place in Beth-Shean at all" (lines 42-43).

Yet iconographically, "despite all the efforts to distance themselves from Egypt and their desire to be emancipated from this colonial power that had ruled in the previous age, the inhabitants in the southern coastal plain continue to use Egyptian-style glyptic art themes" (lines 488-490). Egyptian traditions survived — but specific Egyptian markers (the ram sphinx, the falcon-head of Horus, the uraeus and crown on the archer) disappeared. The result is a hybrid: Egyptian-derived motifs stripped of their most identifiably Egyptian features and adapted into a local repertoire.

**Evidence presented:**
- Settlement archaeology: Hazor Str. XII-XI (semi-nomadic villages), Megiddo Str. VIB (poor village) → VIA (Philistine center) → VB (poor again), Lachish (200-year gap), Beth-Shean (no true Iron I village stratum)
- The Megiddo hoard (Str. VI A, 11th century): 32 seal amulets found in a painted vessel with seven handles, 25 identifiable — "the name 'Amun' dominates, by far, all other motifs" (lines 203-204), appearing on 36% of the pieces
- Continuity of Egyptian themes (Amun cryptography, pharaoh-as-lion, chariot/archer motifs) alongside disappearance of specifically Egyptian iconographic markers
- New local production: black calcite conoids, crude limestone seals — indigenous manufacture replacing Egyptian imports

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The archaeological evidence for settlement disruption is independently established. The iconographic pattern of Egyptian continuity-with-stripping is documented across multiple site strata and seal types.

**What's at stake:** This establishes that Iron Age I is not a "dark age" devoid of religious evidence but a period with its own coherent iconographic system — one that both inherits from the Late Bronze Age and transforms that inheritance in ways that will shape Iron Age II. The "gap" is in urban architecture, not in religious imagery.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat Iron Age I as archaeologically invisible for religious history (a common tendency given the paucity of temple architecture). Keel and Uehlinger's seal-amulet approach fills precisely this gap.

**Alternative reading:** The disappearance of Egyptian-specific markers could reflect loss of technical skill by local seal-cutters rather than deliberate theological choice. The "adaptation" may be accidental degradation.

**My assessment:** The Megiddo hoard alone — 32 seal amulets from a single stratified context, with Amun cryptography dominating — is a powerful data point. The authors are right that Iron Age I has been underrated as a period of religious change because the evidence is glyptic rather than architectural.

## Claim 2: The Amun temple at Gaza propagated a "hidden god" theology throughout Palestine via mass-produced seal amulets — a possible precursor to Israelite aniconism

**Author's claim:** The Papyrus Harris I (compiled under Ramesses III's successor) records a temple in Gaza — "a 'house that no one can enter,' dedicated to Amun in the country D3hy (Palestine)" — where "the foreigners from Rtnw [the Levant] come to him [to the cultic image of Amun] and present him with their gifts" (lines 74-77). This temple was supported by tribute from the southern coastal plain and Shephelah. The mass-produced seal amulets bearing Amun's name (explicit and cryptographic) were likely produced in its workshops. The Philistines "apparently continued to emphasize the cult of the god" after Egyptian withdrawal (lines 218-219).

Crucially, Amun's nature as the "hidden one" (Egyptian *Ymn* = "the hidden") was expressed iconographically through cryptographic writing: "Amun's nature could be praised simultaneously in cryptographic writing by using the lion both to show him as the 'hidden one' and as the one who had the power of a lion" (lines 213-215). The hiddenness WAS the theology — and the cryptography WAS the expression of that theology.

**Evidence presented:**
- Papyrus Harris I, 9.1-3: the "house that no one can enter" for Amun in Gaza
- Three scarabs from Beth-Shemesh and Tell el-Far'ah (south) indicating tribute to the Gaza temple (Uehlinger 1990a)
- Hieratic ostraca from Tel Sera and Tel Haror connected with the tribute system
- A unique scarab from Tell el-Far'ah (south) apparently depicting a Philistine prince before a ram-headed god — "classic Amun iconography" (lines 86-88, illus. 129)
- Truncated pyramid seals (new in Iron Age I, found only in Philistine-controlled areas): explicit Amun-Re inscriptions on one seal from Tel Qasile Str. X (illus. 130), cryptographic Amun writing on a seal from Tel Gerisa (illus. 131), and a third from near Jaffa
- The Megiddo hoard: 9 of 25 identifiable pieces bear Amun's name (5 cryptographic via lion, 1 via uraeus acrophony, 4 as Mn-hpr-r' throne name of Tuthmosis III) — "36% of the pieces" (lines 202-204)
- Mass-produced Ramesses Period goods concentrated on the coastal plain but penetrating the Shephelah and hill country

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Papyrus Harris reference is unambiguous. The concentration of Amun seals in Iron Age I strata is statistically significant. The cryptographic writing-as-hiddenness argument is philologically sound (Egyptian *imn* = "hidden").

**What's at stake:** This is a bombshell for the aniconism debate. If a "hidden god" theology — expressed through concealment, cryptography, and a temple "no one can enter" — was operating in 12th-11th century Palestine, then Israelite aniconism may not have been a unique theological innovation but rather an adaptation of an existing Egyptian-Canaanite theological tradition. The connection the authors draw to El and eventually Yahweh is explicit: "The cult of this Egyptian deity figure could have influenced the cult of the Canaanite El, who was thought of in a similar way and then consequently might have had an impact on the way Yahweh was conceived of as well" (lines 230-235). This directly feeds into the [[Romer — Chapter 8 — Statue of Yhwh]] debate about whether Yahweh was originally aniconic.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israelite aniconism as a radical break with Canaanite/ANE tradition (e.g., Mettinger's "programmatic aniconism") would resist the idea of Egyptian theological influence. The "house that no one can enter" might refer to a restricted-access inner sanctuary rather than a truly imageless cult — the text mentions a "cultic image" (line 76-77).

**Alternative reading:** The cryptographic writing could simply be a stylistic fashion in mass-produced seal amulets, not a theological statement. The dominance of Amun in the glyptic record may reflect production centers rather than popular devotion — people bought what was available.

**My assessment:** This is one of the chapter's most original and consequential arguments. Keel and Uehlinger don't overclaim — they say Amun's cult "could have influenced" El and Yahweh, not that it DID. But the convergence of (a) a temple described as unenterable, (b) a god whose very name means "hidden," (c) cryptographic writing that conceals while revealing, and (d) the eventual emergence of aniconic Yahwism in the same geographic region is too suggestive to dismiss. See [[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]] for the related argument that Iron Age I cult sites lack anthropomorphic divine images.

## Claim 3: The Iron Age I divine iconographic repertoire is exclusively masculine, domineering, and triumphant — the anthropomorphic goddess virtually disappears

**Author's claim:** "By comparison with the repertoire of deities in the Late Bronze Age, that of Iron Age I is reduced markedly. Ptah, whose presence is noted at many different sites during the Late Bronze Age, disappears completely" (lines 63-66). More dramatically: "As the Egyptians departed, their goddesses, such as Hathor, and also the Egyptian versions of goddesses, such as the lady Anat, disappeared from Palestinian iconography. Images of female deities made using costly materials (metals) are entirely absent in Iron Age I" (lines 712-715). And most starkly: "If one sets aside consideration of the terracottas for the moment, anthropomorphic figures in Iron Age I show deities that are exclusively masculine, domineering, and triumphant" (lines 766-769).

The Philistine "Ashdoda" — a seated goddess figure fused with her throne, derived from Mycenaean prototypes — was popular during the 12th century at Ashdod, Ekron, and other coastal cities, but "after this time period the 'Ashdoda' type disappears. It apparently did not survive the move to accommodate Philistine symbolism to the indigenous system" (lines 519-521). The one fragment of a nursing mother goddess from Tell Qasile Str. X (late 11th century) is the exception that proves the rule — it's the last gasp of a dying tradition.

**Evidence presented:**
- Complete absence of Hathor, Anat, Astarte, and Qudshu from Iron Age I glyptic art — all major LB figures
- "Even goddess depictions (reduced to being of one piece with a throne) that were introduced by the Philistines from Mycenaean contexts disappeared totally at the end of Iron Age I" (lines 716-718)
- The Ashdoda type: found at Ashdod, Ekron, Aphek — 12th century only; absent from later strata
- The only surviving female divine images are on "inexpensive terracottas... only the simplest forms of the 'naked woman,' without attributes" (lines 726-728) — used in "personal piety" settings, primarily by women
- By contrast, male deities proliferate: Amun-Re, Seth-Baal, Reshef, the "Lord of Crocodiles," the archer, the enthroned god

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is the chapter's central empirical finding, documented across every site and medium surveyed. The contrast with Late Bronze Age (Ch IV) — where goddesses were abundant and diverse — is stark and undeniable.

**What's at stake:** If the anthropomorphic goddess disappears from the official/elite iconographic record during Iron Age I — BEFORE the emergence of Israel as a state — then the absence of a divine consort for Yahweh in Iron Age II may not result from "Yahweh-alone" polemics suppressing her, but from a prior, broader Canaanite cultural shift away from anthropomorphic goddess imagery. This would significantly complicate Dever's argument ([[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]]) that the Judean Pillar Figurines represent the popular survival of Asherah worship suppressed by orthodox Yahwism. If the goddess was ALREADY depersonalized in Iron Age I, the JPFs of Iron II may represent something different from simple continuity.

**Who disagrees:** Dever argues that goddess figurines show CONTINUITY of Asherah worship from Bronze Age through Iron II. Keel and Uehlinger's claim that anthropomorphic goddess imagery "disappeared" depends on privileging glyptic art (seals, metalwork) over terracotta figurines. They acknowledge this tension: "We now find images of the female deity only on inexpensive terracottas" (line 726-727) — but they treat terracottas as a separate, lower-status phenomenon.

**Alternative reading:** The goddess didn't disappear; she migrated from elite media (seals, metal) to popular media (terracotta). This could reflect changes in production and patronage rather than changes in belief. The "disappearance" is an artifact of Keel and Uehlinger's seal-centric methodology.

**My assessment:** This is the most important finding in the chapter, and also the most debatable. Keel and Uehlinger are right about the glyptic record — the goddess IS gone from seals. But their dismissal of terracottas as merely "personal piety" may underestimate their significance. In a period when seal ownership may have been narrowing (fewer literate elites), terracottas may have been the PRIMARY medium of popular religious expression. The "disappearance" may be a class phenomenon, not a religious one. See cross-reference: [[Stavrakopoulou — Part I — Feet and Legs]] for the argument that divine embodiment persisted in non-glyptic forms.

## Claim 4: Seth-Baal and Reshef transition from warrior gods who FIGHT to gods of domination who STAND — a shift from conflict to command

**Author's claim:** "Images of Reshef wielding a weapon and Baal-Seth in battle are not typical of the Iron Age I. Images showing them in command and standing on animals become the norm" (lines 319-321). Seth-Baal is shown as "a winged deity with horned cap, occasionally standing on a lion" (lines 240-241). Reshef appears "on top of a horned animal" — carefully executed pieces identify the animal as a gazelle (lines 299-302). The lion under Seth-Baal's feet "is not his attribute animal, but his opponent, known from Late Bronze Age glyptic art and probably to be identified as Mot, the Canaanite god of summer drought and death" (lines 249-252).

This shift from active combat to static domination has a theological dimension: "the weather and fertility aspects of Baal that had been emphasized in Middle Bronze Age IIB receded in importance, in deference to military themes, when Baal was associated with Seth in the Late Bronze Age" (lines 258-261) — and in Iron Age I, even the military action recedes in favor of a posture of achieved victory. The god no longer needs to FIGHT; he stands on his defeated enemy.

Furthermore, the relationship between Seth-Baal and the sun god (now replaced by Amun) changes: "The relative ranking of the deities that was apparent during the Late Bronze Age, with Baal-Seth in the foreground and the sun god as the more distant deity in the background, was lost in Iron Age I when Baal-Seth was associated with Amun" (lines 756-759). The Wenamun story (c. 1075) captures this: "Behold, Amun can thunder in the heaven and send Seth at his time" (lines 273-274) — Amun commands, Seth executes.

**Evidence presented:**
- Seth-Baal on truncated pyramid seals (illuss. 130-131), winged with horned cap and long tassel, standing on lion, flanked by uraei
- Seth-Baal on Ramesses Period mass-produced scarabs (illuss. 134a-c)
- Reshef on scarabs: god on gazelle (illuss. 137a-b, 138a-b)
- Bronze Reshef figurine from Megiddo Area BB Str. V B (c. 1050-975): "appears with a hand weapon in his raised hand and holds a shield" (lines 312-313) — but even this may be an heirloom from Late Bronze Age
- The Baal-Seth / Amun relationship in Wenamun II 19: Amun thunders, sends Seth
- Cryptographic seals combining Amun and Seth names (illus. 136)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The iconographic pattern is consistent across multiple media and sites. The shift from combat to standing-on-animal is well-documented.

**What's at stake:** This provides an iconographic parallel to the biblical shift from Yahweh as divine warrior who fights FOR Israel (Exodus 15, Judges 5) to Yahweh as enthroned king who rules OVER the nations (Psalms 93-99). If Seth-Baal's iconography shifted from combat to command during Iron Age I, this represents a broader West Semitic theological development that Israelite religion participated in, not a uniquely Israelite innovation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize the distinctiveness of Yahweh's kingship would resist the implication that it derives from Canaanite models. The Baal cycle at Ugarit (Late Bronze) already shows Baal enthroned after defeating Yamm — the "standing on" motif may simply be a new ICONOGRAPHIC convention for an old theological concept.

**Alternative reading:** The shift from combat to standing may reflect changes in seal-carving fashion (static poses are easier to execute on small stamp seals) rather than theological change. The "standing on" motif could simply be a compositional convenience.

**My assessment:** The authors' reading is more persuasive than the "artistic convenience" alternative because the motif appears consistently across multiple media (pyramid seals, scarabs, scaraboids) and the same shift affects MULTIPLE deities (Seth-Baal, Reshef, Horus-as-crocodile-master). A fashion change might affect one figure; a systematic pattern across the entire pantheon suggests a conceptual shift.

## Claim 5: The disappearance of the anthropomorphic goddess from glyptic art represents a genuine theological transformation — fertility becomes a depersonalized "numinous power" rather than a personal divine force

**Author's claim:** This is the chapter's most important theoretical argument. "As far as the glyptic art is concerned, the female aspect of fertility was no longer represented in anthropomorphic form, but only by means of symbols, substitute entities, and other entities from nature such as the suckling mother animal, the tree, or the scorpion" (lines 718-723). And the key interpretive move: "As the goddess disappeared, being represented only by the use of her attribute symbols, the personality of this power faded into the background as well. Blessing and fertility were no longer directly connected with a personal power who was acting consciously but was thought of increasingly as a numinous power and force" (lines 812-816).

This is a genuinely new claim — not just that the goddess IMAGES disappeared (Claim 3), but that their disappearance reflects a change in how fertility itself was CONCEPTUALIZED. The goddess's attributes (tree, suckling animal, scorpion) survive, but detached from personhood — they become abstract symbols of a depersonalized blessing-force.

The payoff: "The disappearance of the depiction of the goddess in anthropomorphic form is an important precondition for what will have to be demonstrated in the succeeding chapters. During the Iron Age II, the icons that depict blessing will be assigned to a male deity (Yahweh), will be virtually taken over by him, and will finally be used as a way to express his blessings" (lines 817-821). The goddess had to be depersonalized FIRST before Yahweh could absorb her functions.

**Evidence presented:**
- The "Branch Goddess" and Qudshu type disappear completely from glyptic art (line 658-660)
- The suckling mother animal appears on conoids (new medium, new material — quartz, black calcite, limestone) from Megiddo Str. VI, Tell el-Far'ah (south) Grave 134, Beth-Shemesh Str. III, Tel Eitun Grave C1 (illuss. 151-152)
- The suckling animal is accompanied by a scorpion — "the scorpion appeared frequently already on Middle Bronze Age Syrian cylinder seal glyptic art with the goddess of love" (lines 608-611)
- The stylized tree flanked by caprids, which "during the Late Bronze Age [was interpreted] as showing the goddess and her activity," continues on Iron Age I conoids (lines 646-650) — but WITHOUT the anthropomorphic goddess figure
- "The crude, light-colored limestone seals that are typical of Iron Age I use, as an icon that is supposed to bring blessing, only the stylized tree" (lines 724-725)
- The archer + suckling mother animal combination (illuss. 155a-b) may link to the Ben-Anat warrior class — but even here, the goddess is represented by an animal, not anthropomorphically

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The iconographic pattern (attributes without goddess) is empirically solid. The inference that this represents a conceptual shift from personal to impersonal fertility is more interpretive but well-argued.

**What's at stake:** This is the theoretical bridge between Chapters V and VI. If Keel and Uehlinger are right, then Yahweh's absorption of fertility functions in Iron Age II was possible PRECISELY BECAUSE those functions had already been detached from the goddess's personhood. Yahweh didn't have to defeat a goddess to take her powers — he simply stepped into a role that had been vacated and depersonalized. This is a very different model from the "Yahweh-alone movement suppressed Asherah" narrative. It suggests theological evolution rather than theological revolution.

**Who disagrees:** [[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]] argues that Asherah worship persisted robustly through Iron I and II, evidenced by figurines and cult stands. [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] likely argues for polytheistic continuity. If the goddess was being actively worshipped through figurines, the "depersonalization" Keel and Uehlinger see in glyptic art may reflect elite/scribal theology only, not popular religion.

**Alternative reading:** The survival of goddess ATTRIBUTES without the goddess herself could be read differently — not as depersonalization, but as the goddess becoming so closely identified with her symbols that the symbols BECOME her. The tree IS the goddess, not a replacement for her. This interpretation would be more congruent with Dever's Asherah-as-sacred-tree argument.

**My assessment:** Keel and Uehlinger's reading is elegant but vulnerable. The glyptic evidence supports it, but glyptic evidence is only one stratum of religious expression. The terracotta figurines (which they acknowledge but downgrade) may tell a different story. The truth may be that BOTH processes occurred simultaneously: elite glyptic art depersonalized the goddess while popular terracotta art kept her anthropomorphic. This would parallel the later Iron II pattern where seals show no consort for Yahweh but thousands of JPFs suggest popular goddess devotion. The chapter's limitation is that it cannot fully account for non-glyptic evidence within its own methodological framework.

## Claim 6: The Amun cryptography in Iron Age I seals reveals a sophisticated theology of divine hiddenness that predates and may have influenced Israelite aniconism

**Author's claim:** Building on Claim 2, the authors argue that the specific MECHANISM of Amun's hiddenness — cryptographic writing — was itself a theological practice. "The name Ymn-R' 'Amun-Re' recurs on numerous mass-produced pieces from the Ramesses Period... The name is frequently written cryptographically; for example, the sun disk instead of y, mn and the beetle (ntry 'divine') for n, which can be read simultaneously as the throne name of Tuthmosis III, Mn-hpr-r" (lines 129-135). The Tel Gerisa seal (illus. 131) writes Ymn twice on the base: "partly explicit, partly cryptographic, we read the name Ymn twice (reed stalk = y + m3y 'lion' = m + nb 'Lord' = n; ytn 'sun disk' = y + mn)" (lines 108-110).

The lion plays a dual role in this cryptography: it writes the name AND expresses the god's nature. The Wenamun story quotes Amun's spokesman: "The sea is his, and Lebanon is his... Truly, a lion loves his possessions" (lines 208-210). The lion "both show[s] him as the 'hidden one' and as the one who had the power of a lion" (lines 214-215). The cryptography doesn't just encode the name — it encodes a THEOLOGY in which the god is simultaneously hidden (requiring decoding) and powerful (the lion).

This practice was not confined to Egypt. The mass-produced goods "could have been produced, among other places, in the workshops of the Amun Temple at Gaza, where the Philistines apparently continued to emphasize the cult of the god" (lines 217-219). The goods "are found concentrated on the plains and have been found especially in the southern coastal plain. But they also gained access into the Shephelah and into the hill country" (lines 221-223). The "hidden god" theology was thus disseminated throughout Palestine — including into the very hill country where early Israel was emerging.

**Evidence presented:**
- Multiple seal amulets with Amun cryptography: Tel Qasile (illus. 130), Tel Gerisa (illus. 131), Jaffa piece, Megiddo hoard pieces
- Cryptographic techniques: reed stalk = y, lion = m3y/m, nb = n, sun disk = ytn/y+mn, red crown = y, Horus falcon + flagellum = r'
- Uraeus used acrophonically from y'r.t for y (illus. 133e)
- Wenamun II 24, 34: Amun's spokesman claims universal dominion with lion metaphor
- Amun praised as "king of the gods" and "lion" in other contemporary texts (lines 211-212)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The cryptographic readings are philologically sound (Jaeger 1982, Drioton's earlier work on Egyptian cryptography). The theological interpretation of the cryptography as expressing hiddenness is supported by Amun's very name. The dissemination into the hill country is archaeologically documented.

**What's at stake:** This claim bridges Egyptian theology and Israelite aniconism in a very specific way. If the "hidden god" concept was circulating in the hill country during Iron Age I — the period when Israelite identity was forming — then the eventual Israelite prohibition of divine images may have drawn on a pre-existing theological vocabulary of divine hiddenness. This is NOT the same as saying "Yahweh IS Amun" — but it does suggest that the CONCEPT of a god whose true form is inaccessible was available in the cultural environment. [[Romer — Chapter 8 — Statue of Yhwh]] argues that Yahweh was originally represented by a statue; Keel and Uehlinger's evidence here could be used to argue the opposite — that aniconic concepts were circulating in Palestine centuries before the Deuteronomistic reform.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who insist on the uniqueness of Israelite aniconism (e.g., Mettinger) would resist any Egyptian genealogy. The "hidden god" of Amun theology is not truly aniconic — Amun HAS a cult image, it's just hidden in a sanctuary no one can enter. Israelite aniconism involves the ABSENCE of a cult image, not its concealment.

**Alternative reading:** The cryptography may reflect Egyptian scribal playfulness rather than disseminated theology. The presence of Amun seals in the hill country could indicate trade, not religious influence. People may have worn Amun seals for their apotropaic value without understanding the cryptographic theology.

**My assessment:** The distinction between "hidden image" and "no image" is real and important — Keel and Uehlinger don't fully address it. But their broader point stands: Iron Age I Palestine had a LIVED EXPERIENCE of a god whose form was inaccessible, whose name was concealed in puzzles, and whose sanctuary could not be entered. Whether this directly influenced Israelite aniconism or merely created a cultural environment in which aniconism was THINKABLE, it complicates any narrative of aniconism as a sui generis Israelite invention.

## Claim 7: The Ben-Anat warrior class provides epigraphic evidence for the continuing significance of goddess traditions — but ONLY within a specific, socially restricted military stratum

**Author's claim:** "About two dozen arrowheads from Lebanon and el-Hadr near Bethlehem, dated to the eleventh century, are inscribed with their owner's names" (lines 679-681). The pieces from el-Hadr "apparently belonged to members of the warrior class Ben-Anat" (line 681-682). Arrowhead no. 5 bears the inscription 'bdlb't / bn 'nt; nos. 1-4 carry hṣ 'bdlb't, "arrow of 'Abdlabi'at" (lines 683-684). The personal name 'Abdlabiat — attested at Ugarit (KTU 4.63 III 38) — means "servant of the 'lioness,'" where lb't is "an epithet of the goddess" (lines 685-696).

This is significant because Anat was described "on the one hand, in a Late Bronze Age Egyptian text as a suckling cow... and, on the other hand... as a warrior goddess and patroness of the soldier" (lines 671-676). The combination of warrior (archer) and suckling mother animal on Iron Age I conoids (illuss. 155a-b) may therefore represent the Ben-Anat class — men whose identity was defined by service to a goddess who was both nurturer and warrior.

But the authors immediately narrow the claim: "Whoever wants to use this evidence to show the continuing significance of one or more goddesses in early Iron Age Palestine must immediately narrow the focus and recognize that this evidence can be linked to traditions that are at home only within specific social classes. Even in such cases, the themes seem to be a carryover, by and large, from what has come down by inheritance from the Late Bronze Age (family names or personal names!)" (lines 703-709). The goddess survives in personal names and military clan identity — NOT in public cult or iconographic representation.

**Evidence presented:**
- Arrowheads from el-Hadr (near Bethlehem): inscribed with Ben-Anat names, 11th century
- Arrowhead no. 5: 'bdlb't bn 'nt — "Servant of the Lioness, son of Anat"
- Arrowheads nos. 1-4: hṣ 'bdlb't — "arrow of 'Abdlabi'at"
- Ugaritic parallel: 'Abdlabiat as archer's name (KTU 4.63 III 38)
- Dagger blade from Giloh watchtower: stamped with stylized tree (illus. 157) — "just like the inscribed names, [these stampings] are to be regarded as ways to identify the owner" (lines 699-700)
- Conoids combining archer + suckling mother animal: Gezer (illus. 155a), Tell el-Far'ah south (illus. 155b)
- Biblical references: Ben-Anat in Judg 3:31 (Shamgar ben-Anat), Judg 5:6
- Akkadian labbatu as Ishtar epithet (line 742-743 note 8)

**Confidence:** HIGH for the existence of a Ben-Anat warrior class with Anat-devotion. MEDIUM for the conoid interpretation — the archer+suckling-animal combination could represent other things.

**What's at stake:** This provides a nuanced alternative to both "the goddess disappeared" (Keel and Uehlinger's own glyptic argument) and "the goddess survived robustly" (Dever's figurine argument). The goddess DID survive — but in a specific, socially narrow context (military aristocracy with Late Bronze cultural inheritance), not as a widely worshipped public deity. This could explain why Anat appears in biblical personal names (Shamgar ben-Anat) and place names (Beth-Anat) even though she has no temple or cult in the biblical narrative. [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] may address this phenomenon.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the biblical references to Anat as purely literary or vestigial might resist the Ben-Anat warrior class interpretation. The arrowhead inscriptions could be elite fashion rather than evidence of active devotion.

**Alternative reading:** The archer + suckling animal conoids may simply juxtapose two popular motifs (aggression + fertility) without any connection to Anat or the Ben-Anat class. The authors acknowledge this possibility: paratactic combination rather than theological statement.

**My assessment:** This is a brilliant piece of detective work that exemplifies the best of Keel and Uehlinger's method — combining glyptic, epigraphic, onomastic, and biblical evidence to reconstruct a specific social-religious phenomenon. The Ben-Anat class provides a model for how goddess devotion could persist within specific social niches even as public iconography shifted away from anthropomorphic goddesses. This has implications for understanding how Asherah worship may have survived in similar niches (women's domestic religion?) during Iron II.

## Claim 8: The two dominant themes of Iron Age I iconography — aggressive domination and agricultural/herd fertility — correspond to the dual concerns of the Judges era in the Hebrew Bible

**Author's claim:** In their summary (§80), the authors synthesize the entire chapter into two factors: "The iconographic symbol system used during Iron Age I highlights two factors that each take their own unique form. One factor is aggressiveness, oriented toward superiority and domination; it appears in the Hebrew Bible, roughly speaking, in the exodus narratives, in the book of Judges, and in the narratives describing the battles against the Philistines in 1 Samuel" (lines 783-788). "The second factor is evident in a desire for human, animal, and agricultural fertility, as can be detected most clearly in the imagery of the suckling mother animal. This is shown most obviously in the Hebrew Bible in the patriarchal histories in Genesis (cf., e.g., the significance of fertility among the flocks in the Jacob cycle, Genesis 25-35)" (lines 793-797).

Crucially, "these two emphases are not mutually exclusive. This is shown, for example, in the Gideon traditions in the book of Judges (Judges 6-8), as well as in the evidence discussed in §78 for the existence of the Ben-Anat warrior class" (lines 798-800). Gideon is both warrior AND owner of vast flocks — the two themes coexist in a single figure.

The significance of the Baal-(Seth) tradition for the hill country settlers "appears also in the names of the numerous newly established sites in the region that made up their names by using the pattern Baal-X" (lines 790-792). The onomastic evidence (Baal place-names) converges with the iconographic evidence (Seth-Baal dominance seals) to suggest that Baal, not Yahweh, was the primary deity of the early Iron Age I hill country.

**Evidence presented:**
- Aggression/domination iconography: Seth-Baal on lion, Reshef on gazelle, pharaoh striking enemies, lion attacking caprids, archer dominating animals and humans, bull's military prowess (NOT fertility) emphasized (line 412-414)
- Fertility iconography: suckling mother animal (all variations), stylized tree, scorpion, caprids at tree — but notably WITHOUT anthropomorphic goddess
- Gideon cycle (Judges 6-8): warrior who also has concerns about flocks and harvest
- Baal-X toponyms in hill country: Isserlin 1957, Rosen 1988
- Jacob cycle (Genesis 25-35): fertility of flocks as central concern

**Confidence:** HIGH. The correlation between the two iconographic themes and the two dominant concerns of the Judges narratives is persuasive and well-supported.

**What's at stake:** This is the capstone synthesis of the chapter. It shows that the iconographic evidence is not just an independent dataset but one that correlates meaningfully with the biblical picture of the period — WITHOUT depending on the Bible's theological framework. The Baal toponym evidence is particularly important: if early Iron Age hill country settlers named their villages after Baal, then the Baal-Yahweh conflict of later centuries (Elijah, Hosea) may reflect a real religious history in which Yahweh gradually displaced Baal as the primary deity of the hill country. [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] almost certainly addresses this period.

**Who disagrees:** Biblical maximalists might argue that the Judges narratives are late and reflect Iron II concerns projected backward. But Keel and Uehlinger are not using the Bible to DATE the iconography — they're using the iconography to independently establish what Iron Age I concerns WERE, and then noting the convergence with biblical themes. The correlation doesn't depend on the historicity of any specific Judges story.

**Alternative reading:** The two-theme synthesis may be too neat. Other concerns (lineage, land tenure, tribal identity) may have been equally important but didn't leave iconographic traces.

**My assessment:** This is a model of how iconography and biblical studies can converge without circular reasoning. The authors don't claim the seals PROVE the Bible — they claim the seals show what Iron Age I people cared about, and those concerns happen to align with what the Judges narratives care about. That's a legitimate correlation. The Baal toponym evidence is the strongest specific link: it independently confirms that Baal was the dominant deity in the very region where early Israel was forming.

## Chapter V Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Iron Age I as genuine transitional period | VERY HIGH | None — independently confirmed by settlement archaeology |
| 2: Amun temple at Gaza and "hidden god" theology | HIGH | "House no one can enter" may refer to restricted sanctuary, not imageless cult |
| 3: Anthropomorphic goddess disappears from glyptic art | VERY HIGH | Depends on privileging glyptic over terracotta evidence |
| 4: Seth-Baal/Reshef shift from combat to command | HIGH | Could be artistic fashion rather than theological change |
| 5: Fertility becomes depersonalized "numinous power" | MEDIUM-HIGH | Terracotta figurines may tell a different story about popular religion |
| 6: Amun cryptography as theological practice | MEDIUM-HIGH | Cryptography could be scribal play, not disseminated theology |
| 7: Ben-Anat warrior class as socially restricted goddess survival | HIGH (existence) / MEDIUM (conoid interpretation) | Conoid archer+animal may be paratactic, not Anat-related |
| 8: Two themes (aggression + fertility) correlate with Judges era | HIGH | May over-simplify; other concerns left no iconographic traces |

**Strongest section:** §§62-64 on the Amun temple at Gaza and the "hidden god" theology. This is original, well-documented, and has the most far-reaching implications — connecting Egyptian theology, Philistine cult, Canaanite El, and eventually Israelite aniconism. The Megiddo hoard analysis (36% Amun) is a model of quantitative glyptic argument.

**Weakest section:** §78 on the Ben-Anat warrior class. The arrowhead evidence is excellent, but the connection to the archer+suckling-animal conoids is speculative. The authors acknowledge this with appropriate hedging ("leads one to assume"), but the section reads as an intriguing hypothesis rather than a demonstrated finding.

**Most consequential finding:** The disappearance of the anthropomorphic goddess from glyptic art (Claim 3), combined with the depersonalization-of-fertility argument (Claim 5). This is the finding that the rest of the book builds on — if the goddess was depersonalized in Iron Age I, Yahweh's absorption of fertility functions in Iron Age II was filling a vacuum, not displacing a rival. This fundamentally changes how we understand the "Yahweh and his Asherah" question.

**Most debated finding:** The relationship between glyptic evidence (goddess gone) and terracotta evidence (goddess persists in cheap clay figurines). Keel and Uehlinger acknowledge but downgrade the terracottas. Dever's entire argument ([[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]]) rests on taking terracottas seriously as evidence of POPULAR religion. The tension is unresolved and will run through the rest of the book.

**Tension I'll watch in later chapters:** Keel and Uehlinger argue that the goddess's ATTRIBUTES survived while her PERSONHOOD faded (Claim 5). But in Chapter VI (Iron IIA), they will need to explain how these depersonalized attributes were "assigned to a male deity (Yahweh)" and "virtually taken over by him" (lines 819-821). The mechanism of this takeover — was it gradual absorption, deliberate polemic, or something else? — is not explained in Chapter V, and the answer has major implications for whether we see Israelite religion as evolving organically from Canaanite roots or breaking from them through conscious reform.

**Connection to project themes:** This chapter is CRITICAL for understanding the Late Bronze-to-Iron Age transition in Israelite religion. It establishes that:
1. The anthropomorphic goddess was already in decline BEFORE Israel emerged as a state — complicating narratives that blame "Yahweh-alone" reformers for suppressing her
2. Aniconic/hidden-god concepts were circulating in Palestine (via Amun theology) centuries before the Deuteronomistic reform
3. Baal, not Yahweh, was the dominant deity of the early hill country settlers (per Baal-X toponyms)
4. The two great themes of Iron Age I religion — military domination and agricultural fertility — will both be absorbed into Yahwism during Iron Age II

The chapter is a necessary prequel to understanding the "Yahweh and his Asherah" debate, the origins of aniconism, and the relationship between Canaanite and Israelite religion.
