# Keel & Uehlinger — Chapter V: The Hidden God, Victorious Gods, and the Blessing of Fertility (Iron Age I, c. 1200–1000 BCE)

The transitional chapter. Keel and Uehlinger trace the iconographic record across the pivotal Iron Age I period — the era of the Judges, the Philistine arrival, the collapse of Egyptian imperial control, and the emergence of early Israelite settlement in the hill country. The chapter makes a startling argument: the anthropomorphic goddess, so prominent in the Late Bronze Age, **virtually disappears from glyptic art** during Iron Age I. In her place, fertility is expressed through depersonalized symbols — suckling mother animals, trees, scorpions — while male deities become exclusively dominating, triumphant, and aggressive. The "hidden god" Amun-Re, propagated through mass-produced seal amulets from the great temple at Gaza, dominates the period's iconography alongside Seth-Baal and Reshef, now shown standing ON their animal opponents rather than fighting them. This chapter establishes the Late Bronze-to-Iron Age transition in iconography and lays the groundwork for understanding how Yahweh will eventually absorb both the masculine domination motifs AND the depersonalized fertility symbols during Iron Age II.

## Chapter V Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Iron Age I as genuine transitional period | VERY HIGH | None — independently confirmed by settlement archaeology |
| 2: Amun temple at Gaza and "hidden god" theology | HIGH | "House no one can enter" may refer to restricted sanctuary, not imageless cult |
| 3: Anthropomorphic goddess disappears from glyptic art | VERY HIGH | Depends on privileging glyptic over terracotta evidence |
| 4: Seth-Baal/Reshef shift from combat to command | HIGH | Could be artistic fashion rather than theological change |
| 5: Fertility becomes depersonalized "numinous power" | MEDIUM-HIGH | Terracotta figurines may tell a different story about popular religion |
| 6: Amun cryptography as theological practice | MEDIUM-HIGH | Cryptography could be scribal play, not disseminated theology |
| 7: Ben-Anat warrior class as socially restricted goddess survival | HIGH (existence) / MEDIUM (conoid interpretation) | Conoid archer+animal may be paratactic, not Anat-related |
| 8: Two themes (aggression + fertility) correlate with Judges era | HIGH | May over-simplify; other concerns left no iconographic traces |

**Strongest section:** §§62-64 on the Amun temple at Gaza and the "hidden god" theology. This is original, well-documented, and has the most far-reaching implications — connecting Egyptian theology, Philistine cult, Canaanite El, and eventually Israelite aniconism. The Megiddo hoard analysis (36% Amun) is a model of quantitative glyptic argument.

**Weakest section:** §78 on the Ben-Anat warrior class. The arrowhead evidence is excellent, but the connection to the archer+suckling-animal conoids is speculative. The authors acknowledge this with appropriate hedging ("leads one to assume"), but the section reads as an intriguing hypothesis rather than a demonstrated finding.

**Most consequential finding:** The disappearance of the anthropomorphic goddess from glyptic art (Claim 3), combined with the depersonalization-of-fertility argument (Claim 5). This is the finding that the rest of the book builds on — if the goddess was depersonalized in Iron Age I, Yahweh's absorption of fertility functions in Iron Age II was filling a vacuum, not displacing a rival. This fundamentally changes how we understand the "Yahweh and his Asherah" question.

**Most debated finding:** The relationship between glyptic evidence (goddess gone) and terracotta evidence (goddess persists in cheap clay figurines). Keel and Uehlinger acknowledge but downgrade the terracottas. Dever's entire argument rests on taking terracottas seriously as evidence of POPULAR religion. The tension is unresolved and will run through the rest of the book.

**Tension I'll watch in later chapters:** Keel and Uehlinger argue that the goddess's ATTRIBUTES survived while her PERSONHOOD faded (Claim 5). But in Chapter VI (Iron IIA), they will need to explain how these depersonalized attributes were "assigned to a male deity (Yahweh)" and "virtually taken over by him." The mechanism of this takeover — was it gradual absorption, deliberate polemic, or something else? — is not explained in Chapter V, and the answer has major implications for whether we see Israelite religion as evolving organically from Canaanite roots or breaking from them through conscious reform.

**Connection to project themes:** This chapter is CRITICAL for understanding the Late Bronze-to-Iron Age transition in Israelite religion. It establishes that:
1. The anthropomorphic goddess was already in decline BEFORE Israel emerged as a state — complicating narratives that blame "Yahweh-alone" reformers for suppressing her
2. Aniconic/hidden-god concepts were circulating in Palestine (via Amun theology) centuries before the Deuteronomistic reform
3. Baal, not Yahweh, was the dominant deity of the early hill country settlers (per Baal-X toponyms)
4. The two great themes of Iron Age I religion — military domination and agricultural fertility — will both be absorbed into Yahwism during Iron Age II

The chapter is a necessary prequel to understanding the "Yahweh and his Asherah" debate, the origins of aniconism, and the relationship between Canaanite and Israelite religion.
