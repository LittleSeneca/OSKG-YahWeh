# Keel & Uehlinger — Part IV: Iron Age IIA

## Anthropomorphic Deities Recede and Are Replaced by Their Attribute Animals and Entities

Iron Age IIA (ca. 1000-925 BCE) — the period of the United Monarchy under David and Solomon — marks a decisive turning point. Anthropomorphic deity images largely disappear from the local repertoire, replaced by attribute animals, cultic symbols, and empty space. This is not yet monotheism, but it is the beginning of the aniconic trajectory that will culminate in the image-free orthodoxy of the 7th-6th centuries. The chapter covers: the decline of divine statues, the "Lord of the Ostriches" as the dominant indigenous male deity, the goddess's substitution by her attribute entities, the Taanach cultic stands, the Jerusalem Temple iconography, and the northern Syrian/Phoenician cultural reorientation that drove these changes.

---

## Claim 1: Bronze and terra-cotta anthropomorphic deity figurines cease production — every Iron IIA find is a Late Bronze Age heirloom

**Authors' claim:** "It is more important, when one assesses the piece religio-historically, to note that god-figurines of this type were no longer being produced in Iron Age II."

The rich Canaanite tradition of bronze deity figurines — male "striking gods" and female "naked goddesses" — stops during Iron Age IIA. Every anthropomorphic bronze found in Iron II contexts proves on examination to be a residual Late Bronze Age piece, not new production.

**Evidence presented:**
- A bronze clenched fist (4 cm) from Jerusalem Str. 14 (10th century) — originally part of a ~38 cm "striking god" statue. But "little statues that were assembled customarily by using several pieces would have had the arm cast as a single piece" — this separate fist is so rare it must have been a repair piece, and analogous artifacts from Megiddo and Kamid el-Loz are LB II. "The bronze fist from Jerusalem ought to be dated to Late Bronze Age II"
- An enthroned blessing figure from Tell el-'Oreme Str. II (8th century) — dated to LB IIB, "found as an antique" in an Iron II context, perhaps in a "cultic room at the gate" (cf. 2 Kgs 23:8)
- A figurine from Tel Zeror (8th century house) — possibly Iron I origin "at the very latest"
- A Hazor Str. IX B figurine (late 10th century) — "neither an interpretation nor a date for it are possible"
- "Anthropomorphic depictions of the chief deities are attested in literary texts that refer to neighboring states (Dagon in Philistia, Milcom in Ammon)... but they were apparently no longer produced in Israel during Iron Age IA"
- Bronze goddess images "had not been produced in Canaan since the end of the Middle Bronze Age"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The converging dating of every single Iron II anthropomorphic bronze as an heirloom is statistically significant. This is not a gap in preservation — it's a genuine cessation of production.

**What's at stake:** The famous "no graven images" commandment has a material precursor. The aniconic turn was not a theological innovation imposed from above — it was a cultural shift already underway by the 10th century. This pushes the timeline centuries earlier than the exile-centered model most biblical scholars assume.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the image ban to the exile or later. Galling (1977) and others have argued for Iron II production of anthropomorphic bronzes.

**Alternative reading:** The absence could reflect economic factors (bronze became scarce/expensive after the Bronze Age collapse) rather than religious aniconism. Different media might have been used (wood, which doesn't survive).

**My assessment:** The economic argument is weakened by the fact that glyptic art ALSO shows the decline. Seals were cheap and mass-produced — if people wanted deity images, they would appear on seals. They don't. The shift is cultural, not economic.

## Claim 2: The "Lord of the Ostriches" is the dominant indigenous male deity in Iron Age IIA glyptic — and his steppe associations align with early Yahweh traditions

**Authors' claim:** The "Lord of the Ostriches" appears as the most common indigenous anthropomorphic figure in Iron IIA glyptic art. Unlike the Egyptian-influenced solar figures on Philistine scarabs, this figure belongs to the local steppe/desert tradition — and may represent an early iconographic expression of Yahweh.

**Evidence presented:**
- "The ostrich, whose relation to humans is not always easy to figure out and that is tamed only with great difficulty, represents not only a deserted, dangerous and sinister world but also a numinous power that commands respect and honor because it can survive mysteriously at the edge of habitable land"
- The motif develops from an anthropomorphic "Lord" (center, frontal, raised arms) to the ostrich alone, without the human/divine figure — "the likelihood that this substitution did occur is shown, for example, on two scaraboids made at the same workshop in Lachish. One shows the traditional constellation while the other shows two ostriches... without any human figure"
- Ostrich + caprid combinations without any divine figure appear on 10th-9th century scaraboids
- The steppe/desert habitat of the ostrich connects to early Yahweh traditions associated with Seir, Paran, Teman, and Midian (cf. Deut 33:2; Judg 5:4-5; Hab 3:3)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The steppe associations are suggestive but indirect. There's no inscription identifying the "Lord of the Ostriches" as Yahweh specifically.

**What's at stake:** If this figure IS an early Yahweh, it suggests Yahweh's earliest iconography was not borrowed from Baal or El but was indigenous — a steppe/desert deity associated with the ostrich, a creature of the wilderness. This aligns with the "Yahweh from the south" (Midianite/Kenite) hypothesis.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Yahweh as originally a Canaanite deity assimilated from El or Baal. Also those who argue Yahweh was aniconic from the beginning.

**Alternative reading:** The "Lord of the Ostriches" could represent any number of local numinous powers, not necessarily Yahweh. The ostrich motif could be decorative rather than theological.

**My assessment:** The substitution pattern (anthropomorphic lord → ostrich alone) is the same mechanism we see for goddess → tree. It's part of the broader aniconic trend. Whether specifically Yahweh or not, it shows that even male deity figures were being abstracted into attribute animals during Iron IIA.

## Claim 3: The goddess is replaced by her attribute symbols — suckling animals, scorpions, and trees become autonomous "icons of blessing"

**Authors' claim:** The anthropomorphic naked goddess of the Bronze Age is "substituted by the entities through which she worked" — suckling mother animals, caprids at trees, scorpions, and branches. The symbols become autonomous, detached from any specific goddess identity.

**Evidence presented:**
- The stylized palmetto tree flanked by caprids (the substitute symbol for Asherah) appears on the Taanach cultic stands, a Megiddo terra-cotta plaque (Str. V), and numerous seals
- The tree-caprids constellation blends "two different themes: the constellation of the 'Mistress of the Caprids' with the caprids at the tree"
- "Most often, only the incarnated form of the blessings she was providing is portrayed" — suckling mother animal alone, caprid alone, tree alone
- "Substitute entities, such as a horse or a suckling mother animal, can then be worshiped in her place"
- "Sometimes, only a scorpion placed with the caprid, or a branch, or a worshiping human even give a hint that the goddess is really in the background behind these substitute creatures or activities"
- Terra-cotta figurines of women holding tambourines replace the naked goddess — "Just as Judges 4-5 describe Deborah as a 'mother in Israel,' replacing the warrior goddess, perhaps Anat... so also the portrayal of the female tambourine player replaces the iconographic representations of the goddess in Israel"
- The clothed "woman with disk" figurines: the disk is a tambourine (not a solar disk or sacrificial cake), identifying the figure as a human cult participant, not a goddess

**Confidence:** HIGH. The pattern of goddess → tree/caprid/lion/nursing-animal is documented across multiple media and sites. The tambourine player interpretation is well-argued.

**What's at stake:** This is the mechanism by which the goddess becomes the asherah (cultic symbol). The same power that was depicted anthropomorphically in the Bronze Age is now represented by her attributes. The worshipers may still experience her as a personal divine presence, but the official iconography has abstracted her into a tree. This is how "his asherah" can function as BOTH a cultic symbol AND carry the goddess's meaning — the symbol IS the goddess, made transparent.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who interpret the tambourine-holding women as goddesses (Amiran's solar goddess theory). Also those who see the tree-caprids motif as an independent symbol unrelated to goddess traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The tree and nursing animals could be generic fertility/blessing symbols, not substitutes for a specific goddess. The "substitution" framing may overstate the connection to Asherah specifically.

**My assessment:** The iconographic continuity is too strong for the "generic symbol" reading. The same constellation (tree + caprids + lions) has clear goddess associations in LB contexts. In Iron IIA, the goddess figure disappears from the constellation — but the constellation persists. This is abstraction, not replacement. The goddess is still there — she's just become transparent. This connects to Deuteronomy 28:4's "blessed shall be the šagar of your cattle and the 'aštārōt of your flock" — where the goddess's name has become a common noun for fertility blessing.

## Claim 4: The Taanach cultic stands show the goddess, Baal, and an intentionally empty space — domestic cult with an emerging aniconic center

**Authors' claim:** The two cultic stands from Taanach (10th century) are the most important Iron IIA cult objects. Stand #1 shows the goddess as stylized tree with caprids (lowest register) and Baal as a subordinate, off-center figure strangling a snake. Stand #2 has an INTENTIONALLY EMPTY space between cherubs — possibly the earliest material evidence for aniconic Yahweh worship.

**Evidence presented:**

*Stand #1 (Sellin):*
- Five vertically arranged zones, alternating winged sphinxes (cherubs) with lions as guardian figures
- Lowest register: stylized palmetto tree flanked by caprids eating from it — the substitute symbol for Asherah
- Baal appears as an appliqué on the RIGHT SIDE ("since the image is off to the side, Baal is not shown as the object of direct veneration by the cult; it suggests rather a certain subordination of that god")
- "In contrast to [Middle Bronze Age] iconography, [the stand] does not show male and female deities in any relationship to one another" — the divine couple has been dissolved

*Stand #2 (Lapp):*
- Four registers: lowest = naked "Mistress of the Lions" (the goddess directly); third = tree flanked by caprids; second = "only a pair of cherubs and a space and no depiction is missing between them"
- "The space appears to have been left empty intentionally"
- Taylor (1988): the empty space depicts an "invisible deity" — Yahweh enthroned on the cherubs. Keel & Uehlinger are cautious: "yōšēb ha-kĕrûbîm does not mean 'who dwells (between) the cherubim'... but 'who is enthroned on the cherubs' or 'who sits on a cherub throne'"
- Nevertheless: the empty space is deliberate, and it occupies the central position where a deity figure would appear

**Confidence:** HIGH for the iconographic analysis. MEDIUM for the "Yahweh" identification of the empty space — Keel & Uehlinger are appropriately cautious.

**What's at stake:** If the empty space represents Yahweh, this is the earliest material evidence for aniconic Yahwism — 10th century BCE, centuries before the exile. The cherub throne as aniconic cult object would be the original "seat" of Yahweh, not a later replacement for a lost statue.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue there WAS a statue in the First Temple (Niehr). Also those who interpret the empty space differently (unfinished, damaged, held a lost appliqué).

**Alternative reading:** The empty space could have held a perishable object (wood, textile). It could be a manufacturing flaw. It could be a space for ritual action rather than a depiction of divine absence.

**My assessment:** Caution is warranted. But the convergence is striking: empty center between cherubs on a cultic stand from Taanach + empty space between cherubs in the Jerusalem Temple's Holy of Holies + no statue of Yahweh described in any biblical text + the broader archaeological pattern of declining anthropomorphic images. Aniconic Yahwism in the 10th century is not proven — but it's more plausible than the alternative.

## Claim 5: The Jerusalem Temple iconography is gender-neutral and royal — the goddess is structurally absent

**Authors' claim:** "All the decoration in the Solomonic temple is predicated upon the idea that this is the place of divine order and blessing, but the motifs are not gender specific or else are associated with a 'male' royal god."

The biblical description of Solomon's Temple (1 Kings 6-7) describes cherubs, palm trees, open flowers, lions, and oxen — motifs well-attested in Iron IIA iconography. What's absent is equally telling: NO doves, NO caprids at trees, NO suckling animals, NO "Mistress of the Animals" — all motifs associated with the goddess in Bronze Age and Iron I iconography.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Temple was "built in the tradition of the Syrian long-room style" with "privileged trade relations and technological ties with the Syro-Phoenicians"
- The Holy of Holies (dĕbîr): an enclosed, windowless wooden cube ~10m per side — "an adaptation of an Egyptian tradition about a chapel of the gods"
- Two gold-covered wooden cherubs (1 Kgs 6:23-28) with inner wings held horizontally to form the throne for the invisibly present Yahweh
- "The cherub throne, not the ancient Israelite ark... became 'the new symbol for the presence of God'"
- Cherub thrones are attested archaeologically: LB ivories from Megiddo (illuss. 65, 66b), Iron I terra-cotta fragments from Megiddo, and the well-known sarcophagus of King Ahiram of Byblos (~1000 BCE) showing him on a cherub throne
- Phoenicia maintained "widespread and fairly continuous use" of (sometimes empty) cherub thrones from the end of the 2nd millennium into the Hellenistic Period
- The palm trees, flowers, lions, and oxen are standard Syro-Phoenician royal decorative motifs — associated with kingship, not fertility goddesses
- "The tree no longer represents the goddess but stands for the kingdom"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the iconographic parallels. MEDIUM for the biblical description reflecting 10th-century reality (vs. later retrojection).

**What's at stake:** If the Temple's iconography matches the Iron IIA archaeological pattern, the Jerusalem cult was aniconic from the beginning — and the goddess was structurally excluded from its decorative program. This is the architectural correlate of the broader aniconic trend.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Temple descriptions to Josiah or the exile. Also those who argue for Asherah's presence in the First Temple (based on 2 Kgs 21:7 — Manasseh's installation — and 2 Kgs 23:6 — Josiah's removal).

**Alternative reading:** The Temple descriptions could be a post-exilic retrojection that happens to preserve accurate Iron Age iconographic details. Manasseh's asherah (7th century) may represent a late introduction of the goddess into a Temple that was previously goddess-free.

**My assessment:** The iconographic parallels between the Temple description and the Iron IIA archaeological record are independent of the Solomon question. Whether the Temple was built in the 10th, 9th, or even 8th century, its decorative program matches what we find archaeologically. And the structural absence of goddess motifs — doves, caprids at trees, suckling animals — is consistent with the broader pattern of goddess abstraction in Iron IIA. Even in the Temple, the goddess is present only through what she's become: royal blessing symbols that no longer carry her name.

## Claim 6: Northern Syrian and Phoenician influence — not Egyptian — drove the aniconic turn

**Authors' claim:** The reorientation of Israel's cultural ties from Egypt toward Phoenicia and northern Syria during Iron IIA brought a new iconographic system in which attribute animals and astral/royal symbols replaced anthropomorphic deities.

The Egyptian solar iconography that had dominated the Late Bronze Age and persisted in Philistine production gives way to a Syro-Phoenician repertoire where the emphasis is on cherub thrones, guardian lions, and abstract royal symbols rather than anthropomorphic gods and goddesses.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Egyptian-influenced solar deity scarabs (Claim 2 of my original note) are Philistine-produced, not Israelite — they're found in Israel/Judah as imports, not local products
- The Taanach cultic stands use "a Syro-Phoenician tradition when it uses cherubs and lions together" — paralleling a small ivory box from Megiddo (end of LB)
- The crescent moon above bovines and the crescent moon on a staff (emblem of the Moon God of Haran) are "typical of northern Syria"
- "Even in the traditional, inexpensive terra-cotta production, goddesses appear less frequently. Clothed votive figures appear in place of the naked goddesses, portraying a woman with a tambourine"
- "The enthroned, clothed mother-with-child makes her appearance" — a figure type that "became an extremely popular figure in Egypt and throughout the Phoenician world during the second half of the first millennium"
- The cherub throne tradition is Phoenician — "where (sometimes empty) cherub thrones appear in widespread and fairly continuous use"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Egyptian-to-Syrian shift is clear in the iconographic record. Whether this shift CAUSED the aniconic turn or merely coincided with it is harder to establish.

**What's at stake:** If the aniconic turn was driven by Syro-Phoenician cultural influence, Israelite aniconism is not unique or revolutionary — it's an adaptation of a broader regional trend. The distinctive Israelite contribution is not the aniconic impulse itself, but its radicalization (eventually no images at all).

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israelite aniconism as unique and revolutionary (the biblical model). Also those who see Egyptian influence continuing through Iron II.

**Alternative reading:** The Syro-Phoenician reorientation may have been primarily political and economic (Solomon's alliance with Hiram of Tyre), with the religious-iconographic effects being secondary and unintended.

**My assessment:** The direction of causality is the key question. Did Israel adopt Syro-Phoenician iconography because it was already moving toward aniconism, or did Syro-Phoenician iconography cause the aniconic turn? Keel & Uehlinger's evidence favors the latter: the new political alliances brought new artistic conventions, and those conventions happened to be less anthropomorphic than the Egyptian conventions they replaced. Israelite aniconism may have been partly an accident of geopolitics.

## Claim 7: There was no goddess consort in the Solomonic Temple — neither literary nor iconographic evidence supports Asherah's presence before Manasseh

**Authors' claim:** The Temple's iconographic program is systematically gender-neutral. Motifs associated with goddesses in earlier periods (doves, caprids at trees, suckling animals, the naked goddess herself) are "conspicuously absent." The goddess had already receded into gender-neutral symbols of royal blessing.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Temple decorations: cherubs, palm trees, open flowers, lions, oxen, pomegranates, lilies — all attested in Iron IIA Syro-Phoenician royal iconography. None specifically associated with a goddess
- Doves: associated with the goddess in LB iconography (illuss. 16, 19-21) but absent from Temple decoration
- Caprids at trees: the substitute symbol for the goddess — absent
- Suckling mother animals: associated with Anat/Astarte — absent
- The "Mistress of the Animals" — absent
- The evidence for Asherah in the Temple comes from later biblical texts: 1 Kgs 15:13 (Asa removes Maacah's "abominable image") and 2 Kgs 21:7 (Manasseh installs an asherah image). Both postdate Solomon's Temple by centuries
- "The tree no longer represents the goddess but stands for the kingdom" — the goddess's symbols have been re-semanticized as royal symbols
- The goddess has been "demoted" from temple to household — she appears on domestic cultic stands (Taanach) and terra-cotta figurines, not in monumental temple decoration

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The absence of goddess motifs from the Temple description is striking. But the description is textual, not archaeological — we don't have the actual Temple to excavate.

**What's at stake:** If the Solomonic Temple was goddess-free, then the "Yahweh had a consort in his own Temple" narrative is wrong for the early monarchy. The goddess came LATER (Manasseh, 7th century) — she was an innovation, not a survival. This inverts the usual narrative: the goddess wasn't suppressed; she was INTRODUCED, then suppressed.

**Who disagrees:** Dever, Römer, and others who see Asherah as an original partner of Yahweh from the beginning of the monarchy. Also scholars who argue the Temple description is a post-exilic idealization.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical description of the Temple may be a post-exilic text that deliberately omits goddess elements that were actually present. The deuteronomistic editors had every reason to erase Asherah from the Temple's history.

**My assessment:** This is a genuine unknown. We can't excavate the Temple. But the iconographic pattern Keel & Uehlinger document — the goddess's retreat into substitute symbols during Iron IIA — is consistent with a Temple that was goddess-free from the start. If Asherah was introduced under Manasseh, that would represent the SAME goddess revival we see in Iron IIC (Part VI), just at the official Temple level. The goddess's absence from the early Temple and her introduction under Assyrian influence is a coherent narrative that fits the archaeological evidence better than the "original consort" model.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Iron Age IIA

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Bronze figurines cease production | HIGH | New Iron IIA finds of locally produced deity bronzes |
| 2. "Lord of the Ostriches" as early Yahweh | MEDIUM | Inscriptions identifying the figure differently |
| 3. Goddess replaced by attribute symbols | HIGH | Evidence the tree motif predates and is independent of goddess |
| 4. Taanach stands — empty space as Yahweh | MEDIUM | Discovery the space held a now-lost appliqué |
| 5. Temple iconography gender-neutral/royal | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaeological discovery of the actual Temple |
| 6. Syro-Phoenician reorientation drove aniconism | MEDIUM | Evidence Egyptian influence continued strongly |
| 7. No goddess in Solomonic Temple | MEDIUM-HIGH | Discovery of Asherah imagery in Temple-period Jerusalem |

**Strongest section:** Claims 1 and 3 together. The cessation of anthropomorphic deity production AND the substitution of goddess by attribute symbols are the two most securely documented developments of Iron Age IIA. They provide the developmental mechanism for everything that follows.

**Weakest section:** Claim 2 (Lord of the Ostriches as Yahweh). The steppe associations are suggestive but the identification is speculative without inscriptional confirmation.

**Key contribution to the project:** Iron IIA is the period when the structural conditions for biblical monotheism were established — not through theological revolution, but through the gradual abstraction of deities into symbols and the reorientation of Israel's cultural ties from Egypt (anthropomorphic solar gods) to Phoenicia/Syria (aniconic royal symbols). The goddess didn't die — she became a tree, a nursing cow, a tambourine. Yahweh didn't become aniconic by commandment — his throne was empty from the beginning because the culture that built his Temple no longer made statues of gods.
