# Keel & Uehlinger — Part IV: Iron Age IIA

## Anthropomorphic Deities Recede and Are Replaced by Their Attribute Animals and Entities

Iron Age IIA (ca. 1000-925 BCE) is the period of the United Monarchy under David and Solomon — the least archaeologically visible and most textually contested period in Israelite history. Keel & Uehlinger's iconographic evidence shows a pivotal development: anthropomorphic deity images largely disappear from the local repertoire, replaced by attribute animals, cultic symbols, and empty space. This is the beginning of the aniconic trajectory that will culminate in the image-free orthodoxy of the 7th-6th centuries.

---

## Claim 1: Bronze and terra-cotta anthropomorphic deity figurines cease production in Iron Age IIA

**Authors' claim:** "It is more important, when one assesses the piece religio-historically, to note that god-figurines of this type were no longer being produced in Iron Age II."

The rich tradition of bronze and terra-cotta deity figurines — male "striking gods" and female "naked goddesses" that were abundant in Middle and Late Bronze Age Canaan — essentially stops during Iron Age IIA in Israel and Judah. The few figurines found in Iron II contexts are residual Late Bronze Age pieces, not new production.

**Evidence presented:**
- A bronze clenched fist from Jerusalem (Str. 14, 10th century) — probably a Late Bronze Age piece, separated from its statue, kept as an heirloom/amulet
- An enthroned blessing figure from Tell el-'Oreme (Str. II, 8th century) — dated to LB IIB, found "as an antique" in an Iron II context
- A figurine from Tel Zeror (8th century house) — possibly Iron I origin at latest
- A Hazor Str. IX B figurine (late 10th century) — "neither an interpretation nor a date for it are possible"
- "Anthropomorphic depictions of the chief deities are attested in literary texts that refer to neighboring states (Dagon in Philistia, Milcom in Ammon)... but they were apparently no longer produced in Israel during Iron Age IA"
- Bronze goddess images "had not been produced in Canaan since the end of the Middle Bronze Age"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The absence is consistent across multiple sites and contexts. It's not a gap in the archaeological record — it's a genuine change in production patterns.

**What's at stake:** If anthropomorphic deity images stop being produced in Iron IIA, the famous "no graven images" commandment has a material precursor — not a theological innovation imposed from above, but a cultural shift already underway. This is the iconographic evidence for the aniconic trajectory that Römer argues for textually.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for continuity of anthropomorphic cult images into the monarchy. The biblical descriptions of cherubim in the Temple could imply figural decoration, if not free-standing statues.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of bronze figurines could reflect economic changes (bronze became expensive) rather than religious aniconism. Terra-cotta figurines might have been the cheap alternative that simply doesn't survive as well.

**My assessment:** The economic argument is weakened by the fact that glyptic art (seals) also shows a decline in anthropomorphic deities during this period. If it were just bronze cost, seals (cheap, mass-produced) would still show gods. The decline across multiple media points to a cultural shift.

## Claim 2: Egyptian solar motifs persist in Philistine-produced amulets — the sun god on the throne remains the most visible anthropomorphic deity

**Authors' claim:** "Anthropomorphic depictions of a male deity that follow an Egyptian pattern are found quite commonly on just one particular group of steatite amulets... from the Philistine region of southern Palestine in the tenth century."

The exception to the decline of anthropomorphic deity images is a group of steatite scarabs/amulets produced in Philistine southern Palestine. These show a male figure (the sun god or deified pharaoh) enthroned, surrounded by four protective falcons with wings spread.

**Evidence presented:**
- The figure sits on a throne above a nbw ("gold") sign, one arm raised in blessing, a small sun disk under the arm
- A uraeus snake comes from the figure's MOUTH (not forehead) — "characteristic of the manifestation of the god who comes as a scorching breath"
- Four falcons with spread wings surround the figure — the winged solar disk above him
- Distribution: Achzib, Megiddo, Taanach, Tel Zeror, Gezer, Tell el-Ajjul, Tell el-Yehudiyeh — all in the southeastern Levant
- "The seal amulets with the angular, stylized royal sun god were manufactured in Palestine; this type of scarab was unknown in Egypt"
- An early name seal from Revadim (belonging to a certain *'b'*) appears to be the next developmental stage — the enthroned figure is now flanked by worshipers, Egyptian motifs (nbw, falcon) absent

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the Philistine origin and solar interpretation.

**What's at stake:** Egyptian solar theology persists as the dominant anthropomorphic iconography even as local Canaanite deity images disappear. The sun god is the last anthropomorphic deity standing in the Iron IIA iconographic record. This sets the stage for the solarization of Yahweh in Iron IIB.

**Who disagrees:** The identification as sun god vs. deified pharaoh is debated. Keel (1982) argued for deified king; Keel & Uehlinger here lean toward sun god based on the boat-throne and solar disk.

**Alternative reading:** These scarabs may have been produced for export and tell us little about Philistine religion specifically.

**My assessment:** The Philistine origin is significant. These amulets show that anthropomorphic Egyptian solar imagery survived in the region even as Israel/Judah moved toward aniconism. The cultural boundary between Philistia and Israel/Judah was also a religious boundary in terms of image use.

## Claim 3: The Taanach cultic stands show the goddess as stylized tree and Baal as subordinate — the divine couple has been dissolved

**Authors' claim:** "In contrast to [Middle Bronze Age] iconography, [the Taanach stand] does not show male and female deities in any relationship to one another."

The two cultic stands from Taanach (10th century) are the most important Iron IIA cult objects. They show the goddess through her symbols (stylized tree flanked by caprids, lions) while Baal appears as a subordinate, off-center figure strangling a snake. The divine couple that was prominent in MB/LB iconography has been separated and hierarchized.

**Evidence presented:**
- Stand #1 (Sellin): five vertical zones — pairs of winged sphinxes (cherubs) alternating with lions as guardian figures. Lowest register: stylized palmetto tree flanked by two caprids eating from it — the substitute symbol for the goddess Asherah
- Baal appears as an appliqué on the RIGHT SIDE (not center): "a youthful warrior who strangles a snake with both hands (litanu/Leviathan)" — "since the image is off to the side, Baal is not shown as the object of direct veneration"
- Stand #2 (Lapp): four registers. Lowest: naked "Mistress of the Lions." Third: tree flanked by caprids. Second register: "only a pair of cherubs and a space" — "no depiction is missing between them" — the space is INTENTIONALLY empty
- Taylor (1988) interprets the empty space as depicting an "invisible deity" — possibly Yahweh enthroned on cherubs. Keel & Uehlinger are skeptical of this specific identification but acknowledge the empty space is deliberate

**Confidence:** HIGH for the iconographic analysis. MEDIUM for the "invisible deity" interpretation of the empty space — it's suggestive but not provable.

**What's at stake:** If the second Taanach stand deliberately depicts an empty space where a deity should be, this is the earliest material evidence for aniconic Yahwism — circa 10th century BCE, centuries before the exile. The "missing statue" that Römer discusses would have been missing from the beginning, not removed later.

**Who disagrees:** Taylor (1988) and Hadley (1989) interpret the empty space as Yahweh. Keel & Uehlinger are cautious — the epithet "enthroned on the cherubs" doesn't necessarily mean an empty space between them.

**Alternative reading:** The empty space could simply be an unfinished or damaged section of the stand, or a space meant for a now-lost appliqué.

**My assessment:** The empty space is tantalizing but ambiguous. Even if it doesn't depict Yahweh specifically, it DOES show that the cultic stand's designers chose NOT to fill the central space with a deity image — in a context where the surrounding registers show cherubs, lions, and the goddess's symbols. The absence is meaningful even if the specific referent is uncertain.

## Claim 4: The goddess is "substituted" by the entities through which she worked — trees, caprids, and lions replace the anthropomorphic female form

**Authors' claim:** "The substitution of the goddess by entities through which she worked" is one of the defining features of Iron IIA iconography.

The naked goddess with accentuated breasts and pubic triangle — dominant in MB/LB iconography — is replaced by her attribute entities: the stylized tree, caprids nibbling at it, guardian lions. The goddess hasn't disappeared; she's been abstracted into her symbols.

**Evidence presented:**
- Stylized palmetto tree with caprids appears on Taanach stand #1, Taanach stand #2, and a Megiddo terra-cotta plaque (Str. V)
- "The manner in which these motifs are combined in many different ways will be visible in the glyptic art from Iron Age I well into Iron Age II"
- The tree-caprids constellation functions as "substitute symbols for the goddess Asherah"
- This substitution continues the pattern from Iron I (see Part III) but accelerates in Iron IIA
- The "Mistress of the Lions" on the Taanach stand is one of the last anthropomorphic goddess images — already flanked by her attribute animals rather than centered as a nude figure

**Confidence:** HIGH. The pattern is consistent across media and sites.

**What's at stake:** This is the mechanism by which the goddess becomes the asherah (cultic symbol). The same goddess who was depicted anthropomorphically in the Bronze Age is now represented by a tree. The worshiper may still experience her as a goddess, but the official iconography has abstracted her. This is the key to understanding how "his asherah" can be BOTH a cultic symbol AND function as a goddess in popular piety — the symbol carries the goddess's meaning without depicting her body.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the tree as an independent symbol unrelated to the goddess tradition. Also those who argue the substitution represents the goddess's suppression, not her abstraction.

**Alternative reading:** The tree and caprids motif could have become a generic blessing symbol, detached from any specific goddess association.

**My assessment:** The iconographic continuity is too strong for the "generic blessing symbol" reading. The same constellation (tree + caprids + lions) appears with clear goddess associations in MB/LB contexts, then without the goddess figure in Iron IIA. This is abstraction, not replacement. The goddess is still there — she's just become transparent.

## Claim 5: The Jerusalem Temple iconography — cherubs, palm trees, and flowers — fits the Iron IIA pattern of aniconic divine representation

**Authors' claim:** The iconography of the Jerusalem Temple as described in 1 Kings 6-7 (cherubs, palm trees, open flowers, lions, oxen) matches the Iron IIA iconographic repertoire documented archaeologically.

The biblical description of Solomon's Temple — cherubs guarding the ark, palm trees and flowers on the walls, the bronze sea on twelve oxen — is consistent with what we know from excavated Iron IIA iconography. The notable absence: no anthropomorphic image of Yahweh.

**Evidence presented:**
- The Temple description in 1 Kings features: cherubs (winged sphinxes), palm trees, open flowers, lions, oxen — all attested in Iron IIA glyptic art
- "These motifs all belong to the standard repertoire of Syro-Phoenician art during Iron Age IIA"
- No statue of Yahweh is described — only the empty space above the cherub throne
- The Taanach cultic stands provide the closest archaeological parallel to the Temple's decorative program
- The empty space between cherubs on the Taanach stand is the small-scale version of the empty space between the cherubs in the Holy of Holies

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the iconographic parallels. MEDIUM for the Temple description reflecting actual 10th-century practices (vs. later retrojection).

**What's at stake:** If the Temple's iconography matches the Iron IIA archaeological record, this supports the historicity of the Solomonic Temple's decorative program — not as proof of Solomon, but as evidence that the biblical description preserves genuine Iron IIA iconographic conventions. More importantly, it means the Jerusalem cult was aniconic from the beginning.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Temple descriptions to the Josianic or post-exilic period. Also those who argue there WAS a statue of Yahweh in the First Temple (see Niehr, Römer's Ch 8 discussion).

**Alternative reading:** The biblical description could be a post-exilic retrojection that happens to preserve accurate Iron Age details through scribal memory.

**My assessment:** The iconographic parallels are striking and independent of the Solomon question. Whether the Temple was built in the 10th, 9th, or 8th century, its decorative program matches what we find in Iron IIA-B iconography. And critically: no statue of Yahweh is described. This is exactly what the archaeological record would predict for an Israelite sanctuary of this period.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Iron Age IIA

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Anthropomorphic figurines cease production | HIGH | New Iron IIA bronze deity finds |
| 2. Solar amulets from Philistia persist | MEDIUM-HIGH | Better dating showing these are Iron I, not IIA |
| 3. Taanach stands show dissolved divine couple | HIGH | Discovery that empty space held a lost appliqué |
| 4. Goddess substituted by attribute entities | HIGH | Evidence tree/caprid motif predates goddess association |
| 5. Temple iconography matches Iron IIA pattern | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence of anthropomorphic statue in First Temple |

**Strongest section:** Claim 4 — the substitution mechanism. This is the most important theoretical contribution of Part IV. It explains how aniconism developed organically from within Canaanite iconographic conventions, not as a revolutionary imposition.

**Weakest section:** The empty space on the Taanach stand as "Yahweh." Keel & Uehlinger are appropriately cautious about this interpretation, and I share their caution.

**Key connection to broader project:** The Iron IIA aniconic turn provides the developmental pathway for everything that follows. The goddess becomes transparent (tree). The male warrior god becomes subordinate (Baal on the side of the stand). The central space is left empty. This is not yet monotheism — the goddess and Baal are still present through their symbols. But the hierarchy is clear: one central, invisible deity; others visible but subordinate. This is the iconographic matrix from which biblical monotheism will emerge.
