# Smith — Chapter 7: El, Yahweh, and the Original God of IsraEL and the Exodus

The chapter where Smith makes his most audacious historical claim: El, not Yahweh, was the original god of Israel — and possibly the original god of the Exodus. The name "IsraEL" itself is the primary evidence. Yahweh was a southern warrior-god (Edom/Teman) who merged with El at northern cult sites (Shiloh, Shechem, Jerusalem). The three-stage process — (1) El as original god, (2) El as pantheon head with Yahweh as warrior-son, (3) merger — is the structural mechanism behind monotheism. "With a distinct father-god erased, the son's identity as son was also erased. And then there was only One."

---

## Claim 1: El was the original god of Israel — the name "IsraEL" is the primary evidence

**Author's claim:** "The name of Israel contains not the divine element of Yahweh but El's name, with the element *'ēl. If Yahweh had been the original god of Israel, then its name might have been *yiśrâ-yahweh, or perhaps better *yiśrâ-yāh in accordance with other Hebrew proper names containing the divine name."

**Evidence presented:**
- The name Israel is very old — attested at Ebla and Ugarit.
- Distribution of divine names in early poems: Genesis 49:24-25 presents El epithets separate from Yahweh (v. 18). El and Yahweh are named separately in the same poem but not identified.
- Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (LXX/DSS): Elyon allots nations to "divine sons," with Yahweh receiving Israel. El/Elyon is the head; Yahweh is one member. "This reading points to an old phase of Israel's religion when El held a pre-eminent position apart from the status of Yahweh."
- Exodus 6:2-3 (priestly): Yahweh explicitly says "I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." The patriarchs worshipped El, not Yahweh. This is a late text that PRESERVES the memory of the distinction.
- Shiloh: El language throughout — tent tradition (Psalm 78:60), incubation dreams (Samuel), divine gift of child (Hannah), El-name of Elqanah. Seow: Shiloh was an El cult site.
- Shechem: local god was 'ēl bĕrît ("El of the covenant," Judges 9:46). Title 'ilbrt appears in Late Bronze Age Ugaritic (1.128.14-15) as title for El. When Shechem became Yahwistic, Yahweh inherited El's title.
- Jerusalem: El Elyon and El "creator of earth" in Genesis 14:18-22. "At a variety of sites, Yahweh was incorporated into the older figure of El, who belonged to Israel's original West Semitic religious heritage."
- Tigay's onomastic data: 557 Yahwistic names, 77 with *'l — but Tigay reads *'l as title for Yahweh. Smith argues some may be genuine El names.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the name "Israel" contains the El element and that this is significant. MEDIUM-HIGH for the overall thesis that El was the ORIGINAL god (the name could be pre-Israelite rather than evidence of Israel's earliest religion). MEDIUM for the reconstruction of specific cult sites.

**What's at stake:** If El was Israel's original god, the traditional narrative is inverted. Israel didn't start with Yahweh and then add El characteristics — it started with El and then added Yahweh. Monotheism is the merger of an old northern god (El) with a young southern god (Yahweh). The name "Israel" itself carries the memory of a pre-Yahwistic past.

**Who disagrees:** Tigay reads 'l in names as Yahweh's title. Albright/Cross identify Yahweh as an El epithet from the beginning. Van der Toorn argues El's cult was largely rhetorical in the first millennium. Evangelical scholars who maintain Mosaic Yahwism.

**Alternative reading:** The name "Israel" could predate the people — it might have been a geographical or tribal name later adopted, with no religious significance. The 'ēl element in early poems could be generic ("god") rather than the proper name El. The distinction between El and Yahweh in Genesis 49 might be poetic parallelism, not evidence of separate gods.

**My assessment:** The name "Israel" is a genuinely powerful piece of evidence. It's hard to explain why a people devoted to Yahweh from the beginning would name themselves after El. Smith's cumulative case — the name + Deut 32:8-9 + Exodus 6:2-3 + cult site evidence — is strong. The weakest link is the cult site reconstruction, which requires inferring pre-Yahwistic El worship at Shiloh, Shechem, and Jerusalem from texts written much later. But the pattern across multiple sites is suggestive.

## Claim 2: Three-stage process: (1) El as original god → (2) El as pantheon head, Yahweh as warrior-son → (3) identification/merger

**Author's claim:** "We may posit three hypothetical stages (not necessarily discrete in time or geography)." The merger of El and Yahweh was "a convergence of divine identities" that "succeeded the older paradigm of divine succession in the ancient Middle East."

**Evidence presented:**
- Stage 1 (El as original god): Name Israel, separate mentions of El and Yahweh in early poems (Gen 49, Ps 82).
- Stage 2 (El as pantheon head, Yahweh as warrior): Deut 32:8-9 places Yahweh among the divine sons. Psalm 82 shows Yahweh as prosecutor in El's assembly. This is "an early accommodation of the two in some early form of Israelite polytheism."
- Stage 3 (identification): Judges 5 (Song of Deborah) — Yahweh, the southern divine warrior, is attributed victory in the central highlands. "The merger probably took place at different rates in different parts of Israel."
- The merger predates the monarchy: "relatively early in the area where Judges 5 was composed, but possibly later elsewhere."
- "Israelite highland cult sites of El assimilated the outsider, southerner Yahweh." The direction matters: El's cult sites absorbed Yahweh, not vice versa.
- Parallel with Ugarit: "Yahweh in ancient Israel and Baal at Ugarit were both outsider warrior gods who stood second in rank to El, but they eventually overshadowed him in power. Yet Yahweh's development went further. He was identified with El: here the son replaced and became the father whose name only serves as a title for the son."
- The erasure of the father: "With a distinct father-god erased, the son's identity as son was also erased. And then there was only One."
- The triangle collapses: when El and Yahweh are no longer distinguished, "there was no longer a triangle of relations, pitting the head god and his beloved monstrous enemies against the young warrior-god. In biblical terms, this triangle is part of an order swept aside, leaving only Yahweh with such enemies."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the general model. MEDIUM for the specific timing and geography. The three-stage model is a heuristic, not a demonstrated sequence.

**What's at stake:** This is the structural engine of monotheism. Convergence isn't just Yahweh absorbing characteristics — it's the ELIMINATION OF A GENERATION. The father becomes the son. Genealogy is erased. This is not divine succession (Marduk replacing Ea) but divine identification (Yahweh BECOMING El). Smith's phrase "there was only One" is freighted: monotheism requires not just the elimination of rival gods but the elimination of Yahweh's own father.

**Who disagrees:** Cross sees the identification as very early and essentially original — Yahweh WAS an El epithet from the start. In Cross's model, there was never a stage where El and Yahweh were separate gods. Smith is explicitly pushing back against Cross here.

**Alternative reading:** The three stages could be a literary phenomenon rather than a historical sequence. Genesis 49 and Psalm 82 might reflect different genres (blessing, legal proceeding) using different divine names, not different historical stages. The identification of El and Yahweh could have been original, with the "separate" texts reflecting poetic convention rather than religious history.

**My assessment:** Smith's model is more historically plausible than Cross's. The name "Israel" containing 'ēl rather than yāh is genuinely hard to explain if Yahweh was always the chief god. And the distinct regional origins (El = north/indigenous, Yahweh = south/imported) make a merger scenario more likely than original identity. The three stages are a useful heuristic, but Smith admits the timing is fuzzy. The most important contribution is the DIRECTION of the merger: El's cult sites absorbed Yahweh, not the reverse. This means monotheism grew from El's established northern cult, with Yahweh as the junior partner who eventually consumed the senior.

## Claim 3: Yahweh originated as a southern storm/warrior god — possibly more like Athtar than Baal

**Author's claim:** "Yahweh originated at the southern sites of Seir/Edom/Teman/Sinai... located by many scholars today in the northwestern Arabian peninsula east of the Red Sea." His original character may have been closer to Athtar (inland desert warrior) than Baal (coastal storm-god).

**Evidence presented:**
- Biblical passages placing Yahweh's origins in the south: Deut 33:2, Judges 5:4-5, Ps 68:9, Hab 3:3. Kuntillet 'Ajrud: "Yahweh of Teman."
- The far southern region "does not seem propitious as a home for a storm-god such as Baal, because this region has relatively low annual rainfall in contrast to the high rainfall for the Levantine coast."
- "Perhaps Yahweh's original character approximated the profile of Athtar, a warrior-and precipitation-producing god associated with mostly inland desert sites with less rainfall."
- "The momentous evidence provided by the Ugaritic texts may have steered research toward El and Baal to seek Yahweh's original profile; this direction may be partially misleading."
- Judges 5:14 mentions kinship between Ephraim and Amalek — Amalekites were a southern group. Judges 5:24 mentions Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite — Kenites are placed south of Judah, "precisely the area of ancient Yahwistic cult."
- Mechanism of transmission: trade. Judges 5:6 mentions trade as part of the problem leading to conflict. "Perhaps trade, enhanced by some kinship ties, provided the mechanism by which a far southern tradition of the deity in Se'ir/Edom/Sinai/Teman/Midian came to be celebrated originally at northern sites such as Shiloh and Bethel."
- "Part of the original profile of Yahweh may be permanently lost, especially if the earliest biblical sources reflect secondary developments in the history of this deity's profile."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM for the southern origin (the biblical passages are consistent but late). LOW-MEDIUM for the Athtar comparison (speculative). The trade/kinship mechanism is plausible but underdetermined.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh was not originally a Canaanite/coastal deity but a southern desert god, then his entry into Israel was a cultural encounter between highland El worshippers and southern Yahweh traders. The merger was cross-cultural, not internal evolution. This has implications for how we understand Israel's origins — it wasn't one people with one god but a fusion of different groups with different gods.

**Who disagrees:** Albright, Cross, and others identify Yahweh as an El epithet from the start (no southern origin needed). Some minimalist scholars question whether "early Israel" was a coherent entity at all. The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis has a long pedigree but remains debated.

**Alternative reading:** The southern origin texts (Deut 33:2, Judges 5:4-5, Hab 3:3) could be late liturgical formulations, not historical memories. Yahweh might have originated in the central highlands and later been associated with the south for theological reasons (Sinai covenant tradition).

**My assessment:** Smith is refreshingly honest about uncertainty: "part of the original profile of Yahweh may be permanently lost." The southern origin tradition is too consistent across multiple biblical sources to dismiss, but the specifics are unrecoverable. The Athtar comparison is the most creative part — and the most speculative. Smith's real contribution is not solving Yahweh's origins but showing that Yahweh and El had DIFFERENT origins, which required a merger.

## Claim 4: El, not Yahweh, may have been the original god of the Exodus

**Author's claim:** "El may have been the original god connected with the Exodus from Egypt and that this event was secondarily associated with Yahweh when the two gods coalesced."

**Evidence presented:**
- Numbers 23:22 and 24:8: "El who freed them from Egypt has horns like a wild ox." This associates the Exodus with EL, not Yahweh. The description "horns like a wild ox" evokes El's attribute animal at Ugarit (Bull El).
- In Numbers 23-24, El is attested almost three times as often as Yahweh (El: 23:8, 19, 22, 23; 24:4, 8, 16, 23 vs. Yahweh: 23:8, 21; 24:6). "These poems preserve an old repertoire of El tradition, now synthesized with references to Yahweh" (Levine).
- Egyptian names in Shilohite priesthood: Moses, Phinehas, Hopni, Merari — pointing to the Egyptian background of Levitical Shilohite priests. J.M.P. Smith (1918): "The name of 'Phinehas,' the son of Eli, priest at Shiloh, is Egyptian and points to some Egyptian connections with the Levitical priesthood of Yahweh at Shiloh."
- Exodus 6:2-3: El Shadday was the god of the patriarchs; Yahweh was revealed later. This preserves the memory that the exodus god was El.
- The Exodus narrative's divine warrior profile may be a "secondary application (albeit an early one) of Yahweh's identity as a divine warrior." Originally, the Exodus god was the family god, the "god of the fathers" — a characterization that fits El better than the southern warrior Yahweh.

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is the most speculative claim in the chapter. The Numbers 23-24 evidence is real but could be explained by poetic convention (using 'ēl as generic "God"). The Exodus-El connection requires multiple inferential steps.

**What's at stake:** If El was the original Exodus god, then the central event of Israel's national identity was originally associated with El, not Yahweh. Yahweh was retroactively inserted into the foundational narrative. For theology, this means the God who "brought Israel out of Egypt" was El — and Yahweh became that God through merger. The creedal heart of the Hebrew Bible (Exodus 20:2) would preserve a theological appropriation, not an original historical memory.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who maintains the Exodus narrative as historically reliable in its current form. Even critical scholars may resist this claim as too speculative. The Numbers 23-24 texts are notoriously difficult to date.

**Alternative reading:** The Numbers 23-24 poems could use 'ēl as a title for Yahweh (="God who freed them from Egypt"), not as a distinct deity. The predominance of El over Yahweh in these poems could reflect archaic poetic style rather than religious history. The Egyptian names in the Shilohite priesthood don't require an El-Exodus connection — they could reflect Egyptian influence on Yahwistic priests.

**My assessment:** This is Smith's most provocative claim and his weakest. The Numbers 23-24 evidence is genuine but ambiguous — 'ēl can mean "God" generically. The Shilohite-Egyptian connection is interesting but the chain of inference (Egyptian names → Levitical priests → El as their god → El as Exodus god) is long. I'm not convinced, but I appreciate that Smith presents it as a hypothesis ("may have been") rather than a settled conclusion. The claim is more important for what it reveals about Smith's method than for its historical probability: he's willing to follow the evidence wherever it leads, even if it overturns the most fundamental Israelite tradition.

## Claim 5: The El-Yahweh merger was not succession but convergence — the father becomes the son, erasing both roles

**Author's claim:** "This paradigm of convergence of divine identities succeeded the older paradigm of divine succession in the ancient Middle East (for example, Ea's replacement by his son Marduk in Enuma Elish). Indeed, the erasure of the father, with his transformation into the son, was a requisite condition for the monotheistic identity of the son."

**Evidence presented:**
- Mesopotamian model: divine SUCCESSION — Marduk replaces Ea but Ea remains a distinct god (weakened but not erased). The older generation persists.
- Israelite model: divine CONVERGENCE — El IS Yahweh. El's characteristics become Yahweh's (aged patriarch enthroned, compassionate father, tent-dwelling, creator, healer). "Many, if not all, features of the father and son could be incorporated into the one divine leader."
- What's displaced: some El features are transferred to the divine "Name" (Ch 4) or the guardian angel of Israel.
- The erasure is total: "With a distinct father-god erased, the son's identity as son was also erased. And then there was only One."
- The cosmic triangle (El-favors-monsters vs. Baal-fights-monsters) collapses when El and Yahweh merge: "there was no longer a triangle of relations, pitting the head god and his beloved monstrous enemies against the young warrior-god."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the descriptive contrast (succession vs. convergence). MEDIUM-HIGH for the claim that convergence required erasure of both father and son identities.

**What's at stake:** This is the theological heart of Smith's argument. Monotheism isn't just "believing in one God" — it's a specific kind of theological move that ELIMINATES divine genealogy. Yahweh has no father, no sons (except metaphorically), no family. The "One" is not just numerically singular but genealogically isolated. This is what distinguishes Israelite monotheism from Mesopotamian "mono-polytheism" (Marduk as supreme but with acknowledged divine family).

**Who disagrees:** Cross might argue the identification was so early that "convergence" is the wrong model — there was never a separate El and Yahweh to converge. Heiser would say the divine council structure preserved the father-son relationship under a different vocabulary.

**Alternative reading:** The convergence model might overstate the erasure. Yahweh retains fatherly characteristics; Israel is called God's "son" (Exodus 4:22, Hosea 11:1). The divine family isn't erased — it's displaced onto the relationship between God and Israel. The family metaphor persists; only the divine members are eliminated.

**My assessment:** Smith's distinction between succession and convergence is genuinely illuminating and I think it's right. The Mesopotamian pantheon preserves older generations; the Israelite one doesn't. The erasure of El as a separate entity — and the corresponding erasure of Yahweh's sonship — is a structural difference, not just a numerical one. The phrase "there was only One" captures the radicality of the move: Yahweh becomes a god without origin, without father, without peer. That's not "believing in one God" — it's an ontological transformation of what it means to be a god at all.

## Chapter 7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: El was original god of Israel (name Israel) | HIGH (name) / MEDIUM-HIGH (overall) | 'ēl as generic "god" not proper name |
| 2: Three-stage merger process | MEDIUM-HIGH (model) / MEDIUM (specifics) | Stages as heuristic, not sequence |
| 3: Yahweh as southern warrior (Athtar-like) | MEDIUM (south) / LOW-MEDIUM (Athtar) | Original profile unrecoverable |
| 4: El as original Exodus god | LOW-MEDIUM | Ambiguous evidence; multiple inferential steps |
| 5: Convergence vs. succession distinction | HIGH | Displacement of family onto God-Israel relationship |

**Strongest section:** Section 5 (Was El Israel's Original God?). The name "Israel" argument is simple, elegant, and hard to dismiss. The three-stage model, even if schematic, provides the clearest framework in the book for understanding how monotheism emerged structurally.

**Weakest section:** Section 7 (Was El the Original God of the Exodus?). The most speculative claim in the book, and the evidence is thin. The Numbers 23-24 poems are genuinely interesting but the inference to "El was the Exodus god" requires too many steps.

**Most important theoretical move:** The succession vs. convergence distinction. This is the conceptual advance over Cross. Cross saw the El-Yahweh identification as original; Smith sees it as achieved. And the ACHIEVEMENT required erasing the father to make the son ontologically singular. Monotheism is not the triumph of the father or the son — it's the elimination of the distinction.

**Cross-reference:** This chapter is the historical payoff for Part I's structural analysis. Ch 2 established that Yahweh was originally tier 2 under El; Ch 3 established the divine family model; Ch 7 shows how the family was collapsed. Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* is the primary interlocutor — Smith accepts Cross's framework (El and Yahweh merge) but rewrites the timeline and mechanism. Römer's *The Invention of God* makes compatible arguments about Yahweh's southern origins and merger with El. Levine's work on Numbers 23-24 and Transjordanian El traditions is crucial to Smith's Exodus argument.
