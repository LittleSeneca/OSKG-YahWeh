# Meta-Analysis: The Scholars Behind the Scholarship

A directory tells you *what* scholars argue. A meta-analysis asks *why* they might argue it — what shapes their conclusions before they even look at the evidence. This note examines the presuppositions, academic genealogies, methodological commitments, institutional incentives, and national/intellectual traditions that produce the field's conclusions.

---

## 1. Academic Genealogy: Who Trained Whom

The field of Hebrew Bible / Israelite religion is small enough that academic lineages matter enormously. A single advisor can shape a generation.

### The Albright → Cross → Smith Lineage

This is the dominant lineage in American scholarship, and it tells a fascinating story of intellectual drift.

| Generation | Scholar | Institution | Position on Monotheism | Religious Background |
|------------|---------|------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | **William F. Albright** (1891–1971) | Johns Hopkins | Early Mosaic monotheism — Israel was distinctive from the beginning | Confessional Protestant |
| 2 | **Frank Moore Cross** (1921–2012) | Harvard (student of Albright at Hopkins) | Yahweh emerged from within the El tradition; evolutionary model, but still saw early distinctiveness | Presbyterian background |
| 3 | **Mark S. Smith** (b. 1956) | Princeton Seminary / NYU (student of Cross at Harvard) | Thoroughgoing evolutionary model — Israelite religion as subset of Canaanite religion | Roman Catholic |

**The trajectory is striking.** Albright argued for early Mosaic monotheism. His student Cross moved substantially toward the evolutionary model. Cross's student Smith completed the journey — Smith's work is the most thorough articulation of the position that Israelite religion was fundamentally Canaanite and polytheistic in origin.

**Cross's other students:** William Dever (archaeology, "not even a theist"), Richard Elliott Friedman (moderate critical), Emanuel Tov (leading textual critic, Hebrew University). Cross trained an entire generation — and they span the spectrum from Friedman's moderate position to Dever's atheism.

**This is not a conspiracy.** It's how academic fields work. But it means that a single intellectual lineage has had disproportionate influence on what counts as "the consensus."

### Competing Lineages

| Lineage | Key Figures | Character |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| **Harvard / Cross** | Smith, Dever, Friedman, Tov, Hendel | Dominant in American critical scholarship |
| **Tel Aviv** | Finkelstein, Na'aman, Koch | Archaeology-first; skeptical of biblical narratives |
| **Hebrew University / Kaufmann** | Kaufmann, Tigay (UPenn, but Israeli-trained) | Emphasized Israelite distinctiveness; Tigay's onomastics |
| **German / Swiss critical** | Schmid (Zurich), Kratz (Göttingen), Römer (Lausanne/Collège de France), Albertz (Münster) | Rigorous historical-critical; late dating of sources |
| **Copenhagen minimalists** | Thompson, Lemche | Bible as Persian/Hellenistic fiction |
| **Evangelical** | Heiser, Block, Hess, Walton, Longman, Kitchen | Various degrees of engagement with critical scholarship |

**The German/Swiss school is particularly important.** Konrad Schmid's late dating of the Pentateuch — if accepted — would fundamentally undermine how the American consensus uses biblical texts as evidence for pre-exilic polytheism. Yet Schmid's work is less cited in American scholarship than Smith's. Is this because Smith's arguments are stronger, or because the American guild is institutionally invested in the evolutionary model?

## 2. Confessional Mapping: Who Believes What

A scholar's religious commitments (or lack thereof) don't determine their conclusions, but they shape what questions get asked, what evidence counts, and what conclusions feel plausible.

### Scholars by Religious Stance

| Stance | Scholars | Pattern |
|--------|----------|---------|
| **Confessional Christian (evangelical)** | Heiser, Block, Hess, Walton, Longman, Kitchen, Hoffmeier, Millard | Tend to challenge consensus; argue for earlier monotheism or divine council framework |
| **Confessional Christian (mainline/Catholic)** | Mark S. Smith (Catholic), Cross (Presbyterian), Albright (Protestant) | Smith is the consensus leader despite being Catholic — his conclusions cut against traditional doctrine |
| **Jewish** | Sommer (JTS), Kaufmann (Hebrew U), Tigay (Jewish background), Levenson (Harvard), Segal (Columbia), Zevit (AJU), Kugel (Orthodox Jewish) | Diverse: Kaufmann argued early monotheism; Sommer offers third way; Segal complicates the endpoint |
| **Atheist / Non-theist** | Dever ("not even a theist"), Stavrakopoulou (Humanists UK patron), Thompson (minimalist) | Dever's atheism is notable — he has no theological stake, yet his archaeological conclusions strongly support polytheistic folk religion |
| **Unknown / Secular context** | Römer (Collège de France is secular), Schmid (Zurich), Lewis (Hopkins), Fleming (NYU), Kratz (Göttingen) | European scholars operate in more secular institutional contexts than American ones |

### Patterns Worth Noting

1. **Smith is a believing Catholic who argues against his own tradition's theology.** This is rhetorically powerful — it's hard to accuse him of anti-religious bias. But Smith's Catholicism may also matter: Catholic biblical scholarship has a tradition of engaging historical criticism that evangelical Protestantism often lacks. Smith can hold his faith and his scholarship in tension in ways that would be harder in traditions that require biblical inerrancy.

2. **Evangelical scholars almost uniformly challenge the consensus.** Is this because the evidence supports their position, or because their theological commitments require them to find arguments against it? The same question cuts the other way: do critical scholars favor the evolutionary model because *dis*proving traditional theology is personally satisfying?

3. **Jewish scholars don't fit a single pattern.** Kaufmann's early monotheism was influential in Israeli scholarship. Sommer complicates without rejecting. Segal shows the endpoint was never stable. Jewish scholars have less stake in whether "monotheism" originated with Moses or in the exile — the question is historical, not salvation-dependent.

4. **Atheist scholars split.** Dever finds polytheistic folk religion everywhere. Stavrakopoulou argues Yahweh had a physical body. Both conclusions align with a non-religious worldview. But Thompson (atheist/agnostic minimalist) argues we can't know anything — which undermines both the consensus AND its critics.

## 3. Methodological Tradeoffs: What Counts as Evidence?

The single biggest driver of disagreement may not be presuppositions about God — it's presuppositions about **method**. Different methods produce different answers, and every scholar has a methodological home turf.

### The Four Methods and Their Built-In Biases

| Method | What It Excels At | What It Misses | Key Practitioners |
|--------|-------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Text Criticism** | Recovering original readings; identifying editorial layers | Tells us what scribes thought, not what ordinary people believed | Smith, Cross, Hendel, Tov, Ulrich |
| **Archaeology** | What people actually DID (figurines, shrines, inscriptions) | Can't tell us what people THOUGHT; material remains don't speak | Dever, Finkelstein, Zevit, Keel/Uehlinger |
| **Comparative Religion (ANE)** | Shows shared cultural matrix; identifies borrowings | Risks assuming similarity = identity; can miss distinctiveness | Day, Smith, Stavrakopoulou, Cross |
| **Onomastics (personal names)** | What ordinary people named their children | Names reflect tradition, not personal theology | Tigay |

**Each method tilts toward a different conclusion:**

- **Text critics** see editorial layers and conclude the religion evolved. (Smith: "Look at how Deut 32:8 was altered from 'sons of God' to 'sons of Israel' — they were covering up polytheism!")
- **Archaeologists** find figurines and multiple shrines and conclude folk religion was polytheistic. (Dever: "Thousands of Asherah figurines don't lie.")
- **Comparative religion scholars** see Ugaritic parallels and conclude Israel borrowed from Canaan. (Day: "Psalm 29 is a Baal hymn with Yahweh's name pasted in.")
- **Onomasticians** see 90%+ YHWH names and conclude popular religion was monolatrous. (Tigay: "If everyone worshipped Baal, why did nobody name their kid after him?")

**These methods genuinely conflict.** The archaeology says polytheism. The onomastics says monolatry. Both can't be straightforwardly correct — someone's method is missing something. Either the figurines don't mean what archaeologists think, or the names don't mean what onomasticians think, or "what people did" and "what people believed" were genuinely disconnected in ways our categories can't capture.

### The Methodological Naturalism Debate

A deeper meta-question: should scholars assume methodological naturalism (no supernatural explanations allowed) as a working principle, or is that a metaphysical commitment masquerading as a method?

- **Most critical scholars** treat methodological naturalism as non-negotiable. You can't invoke "God revealed monotheism to Moses" as a historical explanation because it's unfalsifiable.
- **Evangelical scholars** argue this rules out the very thing under investigation. If God DID reveal monotheism, methodological naturalism guarantees you'll never find it. The method predetermines the conclusion.
- **The moderate position** (associated with John J. Collins, Michael V. Fox): methodological naturalism is a practical necessity for academic work, not a metaphysical claim. You bracket supernatural explanations procedurally, not because you've proven them false.

**How this plays out in the debate:** Smith's evolutionary model "works" within methodological naturalism — it explains the data without invoking revelation. Heiser's divine council model ALSO works within methodological naturalism — it's a textual/historical argument, not a "God told me" claim. Kaufmann's early monotheism is harder to sustain without some version of "something unusual happened." This may be one reason Kaufmann's position lost influence: it's harder to operationalize within the methodological constraints of the modern academy.

## 4. National and Institutional Schools

### American vs. European Scholarship

American and European biblical scholarship have different characters, shaped by different institutional contexts.

| Dimension | American | European (esp. German/Swiss) |
|-----------|----------|------------------------------|
| **Institutional context** | Mix of secular universities, divinity schools, and seminaries | Mostly state-funded secular universities |
| **Relationship to faith communities** | Closer; many scholars teach in seminaries or divinity schools | More distant; theology faculties are state-funded but secular in orientation |
| **Dominant methods** | Archaeology, comparative religion, literary approaches | Rigorous historical criticism, redaction criticism, late dating |
| **Attitude toward the Bible** | More varied; includes confessional and critical voices | Consistently critical; less need to accommodate faith commitments |
| **Key example** | Smith can be a Catholic teaching at Princeton Seminary | Römer teaches at the Collège de France, a thoroughly secular institution |

**The German/Swiss school's late dating (Schmid, Kratz) is a genuine challenge to the American consensus.** If the Pentateuch is Persian-period (5th–4th c. BCE), not pre-exilic, then the biblical texts Smith uses as evidence for polytheistic origins are compositions by monotheists, not fossilized records of polytheism. This argument has gained ground in Europe but has been slower to penetrate American scholarship — partly because American scholars are more invested in the texts as historical sources.

### Israeli Scholarship

Israeli biblical scholarship has its own character:

- **Kaufmann's influence:** His argument for early, revolutionary monotheism was enormously influential in Israel. The Hebrew University was built around his framework. Even critical Israeli scholars engage Kaufmann seriously in ways American scholars often don't.
- **Archaeology-first:** Finkelstein and the Tel Aviv school are archaeology-driven and deeply skeptical of biblical narratives — but for historical reasons (the ground doesn't match the text), not theological ones.
- **No Christian theological stake:** Israeli scholars have no investment in whether Jesus fulfills Old Testament prophecy or whether Paul was right about monotheism. This freedom from Christian theological constraints cuts both ways — less pressure to defend orthodoxy, but also less pressure to attack it.

### Institutional Prestige Hierarchy

Where a scholar publishes and teaches shapes their influence:

| Tier | Institutions | Character |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Elite secular / Ivy** | Harvard, Yale, Chicago, Johns Hopkins, UPenn, NYU, Columbia, Berkeley, Brown | Sets the agenda; graduates place at other elite institutions |
| **Elite European** | Collège de France, Oxford, Cambridge, Zurich, Göttingen, Lausanne, Edinburgh | Parallel elite track; Schmid, Römer, Day |
| **Major research universities** | Tel Aviv, Hebrew University, Arizona, Exeter, Georgia, Münster, Amsterdam | Produce substantial research; Finkelstein, Dever, Stavrakopoulou |
| **Divinity schools / seminaries** | Princeton Seminary, JTS, Harvard Divinity, Yale Divinity, Union Theological | Bridge academic and confessional worlds; Smith, Sommer, Levenson |
| **Evangelical seminaries** | Wheaton, Denver Seminary, Trinity Evangelical, Westmont | Parallel ecosystem; Block, Hess, Walton, Longman |
| **Think tanks / religious orgs** | Faithlife Corporation (Logos) | Heiser's institutional home — not a university |

**This hierarchy matters.** Scholars at elite institutions train the next generation, control the major journals, and set the terms of debate. The Albright → Cross → Smith lineage managed to place students at Harvard, NYU, Princeton, and Berkeley. When a consensus forms within this lineage, it self-perpetuates — not through conspiracy, but through the normal operation of academic prestige and mentorship.

## 5. The Heiser Problem: Scholar or Apologist?

Michael Heiser presents a methodological puzzle. His PhD is from UW-Madison (a legitimate secular university). His textual arguments about Deut 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 are essentially the SAME as Smith's — he agrees the MT was altered. His framework (divine council) engages the same data as Smith but reaches different conclusions.

**Yet Heiser is almost entirely ignored by critical scholarship.** Why?

**Possible explanations:**

1. **Institutional location:** Heiser worked for Faithlife Corporation (Logos Bible Software) and Celebration Church, not a university. He published with Lexham Press, not Oxford or Cambridge. In the academic prestige economy, this matters.

2. **Confessional framework:** Heiser was an evangelical Christian who believed in biblical inerrancy. Critical scholars may dismiss him as doing apologetics rather than disinterested scholarship — even when his textual arguments are sound.

3. **Popular audience:** Heiser wrote for a general evangelical audience (*The Unseen Realm*), not for the guild. This is often seen as a mark against scholarly seriousness.

4. **The "both sides" problem:** Heiser's conclusions happen to align with what his faith community wants to hear. Is that because the evidence supports it, or because he found a sophisticated way to reconcile evidence with orthodoxy?

**The counter-argument:** Heiser's reading of Deut 32:8-9 is actually IDENTICAL to Smith's on the textual question. Both agree the original read "sons of God." Heiser just interprets it differently — as evidence of a divine council of created beings rather than evidence of polytheism. If Heiser is wrong, it's not because he's an apologist — it's because his interpretation is weaker on the merits. But the dismissal of Heiser by the guild often looks like it's about his institutional location and confessional identity, not about the quality of his arguments.

**This cuts both ways.** If critical scholars dismiss Heiser because he's evangelical, are they any less biased than evangelicals who dismiss Smith because he's Catholic? The question isn't who's biased — everyone is. The question is whose biases produce better arguments.

## 6. The Smith Problem: How a Catholic Argued Against His Own Tradition

Mark S. Smith presents the inverse of the Heiser problem. Smith is a believing Roman Catholic whose scholarly conclusions — Israelite religion was polytheistic, monotheism was a late development, Yahweh absorbed a Canaanite goddess as his consort — directly contradict traditional Catholic (and Christian) doctrine.

**This gives Smith enormous rhetorical credibility.** When a Catholic scholar argues that Yahweh had a wife (Asherah), it's hard to dismiss him as an anti-religious axe-grinder. When a Princeton Seminary professor argues that monotheism emerged from polytheism, it carries more weight than when an atheist says the same thing.

**But Smith's Catholicism may matter in subtler ways:**

- **Catholic biblical scholarship** has a long tradition of engaging historical criticism that evangelicalism lacks. Catholic scholars are allowed — even expected — to hold faith and critical scholarship in tension. Smith doesn't need to reconcile every conclusion with inerrancy.
- **Smith's framework may reflect Catholic comfort with development.** Catholic theology accepts doctrinal development (Newman's "development of doctrine"). The idea that understanding of God developed over time may feel less threatening to a Catholic than to an evangelical for whom the Bible must be consistent from Genesis onward.
- **Is Smith's position genuinely costly?** He teaches at Princeton Seminary (Presbyterian, not Catholic) and his work is celebrated by the critical guild. His conclusions align with the academic prestige structure. There's no professional cost to his position — only theological tension that he seems to navigate comfortably.

## 7. The Journal and Gatekeeping Question

Academic fields are controlled through journals, conferences, and hiring committees. Some patterns worth investigating:

### Major Journals

| Journal | Focus | Editorial Character |
|---------|-------|---------------------|
| *Journal of Biblical Literature* (JBL) | Flagship SBL journal | Critical, mainstream |
| *Vetus Testamentum* (VT) | Hebrew Bible | European, historical-critical |
| *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* (CBQ) | Catholic scholarship | Critical but with Catholic tradition |
| *Journal for the Study of the Old Testament* (JSOT) | Hebrew Bible | British, critical |
| *Zeitschrift für die alttestamentliche Wissenschaft* (ZAW) | Hebrew Bible | German, rigorously critical |
| *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* (BASOR) | Archaeology | Dever, Zevit publish here |
| *Themelios* | Evangelical | Hess's critique of Smith appeared here |

**Do evangelical scholars publish in the same journals as critical scholars?** Generally, no. Evangelical scholarship has its own ecosystem — different journals, different conferences (ETS vs. SBL), different presses (Baker, Eerdmans, Zondervan vs. Oxford, Cambridge, Brill). This parallel ecosystem means the two camps often talk past each other rather than engaging directly.

**The exception:** Eerdmans publishes both critical and evangelical works. Smith's *Early History of God* and Dever's *Did God Have a Wife?* are Eerdmans publications, but so are works by Block and Walton. This crossover press is one of the few places where the ecosystems meet.

### The SBL (Society of Biblical Literature)

The SBL is the main professional organization. Its annual meeting is where careers are made. **The SBL is methodologically pluralistic** — evangelical, Catholic, Jewish, and secular scholars all present. But the program units (the panels that accept or reject papers) are controlled by senior scholars, and the prestige gradient clearly favors critical over confessional work.

## 8. Generational Drift: How the Field Has Moved

| Era | Dominant View | Key Figures | What Changed |
|-----|--------------|-------------|-------------|
| **1950s–1960s** | Early Mosaic monotheism | Albright, Bright, Wright | Confessional scholarship dominant in America |
| **1970s–1980s** | Transitional; Canaanite connections acknowledged | Cross, Freedman | Ugaritic texts discovered; comparative method gains ground |
| **1990s–2000s** | Evolutionary consensus established | Mark Smith, Day, Dever | Smith's *Early History of God* (1990) crystallizes the new consensus |
| **2010s–2020s** | Consensus refined and challenged | Römer, Lewis, Fleming, Schmid, Sommer | More nuance; late-dating challenge from Europe; onomastic challenge from Tigay |

**The pattern is toward increasing complexity.** The simple "polytheism → monotheism" story of the 1990s has been complicated by:
- **Schmid's late dating** (if texts are late, they can't be used as evidence for early polytheism)
- **Tigay's onomastics** (if most Israelites had YHWH names, popular religion may have been more monolatrous than archaeology suggests)
- **Sommer's divine fluidity** (if even monotheists believed in God's multiple bodies, our categories don't map well)
- **Fleming's complication of the Kenite hypothesis** (Yahweh may not have been "imported" in the simple sense)

**The direction of travel is toward messier models, not cleaner ones.** This is typical of maturing scholarship — the early paradigm is simple and bold; later refinements complicate it.

## 9. The Quality Question: Are Consensus Arguments Better?

A meta-analyst should ask: when you compare the actual arguments of consensus scholars vs. challengers, is there a quality differential?

### Where the consensus is strongest:

1. **Archaeological evidence for polytheistic practice.** Dever's case is overwhelming: thousands of female figurines, multiple temples, inscriptions naming Asherah alongside Yahweh. The counter-arguments (cult objects rather than goddess, isolated sites) are strained.

2. **Text-critical evidence for scribal alteration.** Deut 32:8-9 is a slam dunk. The MT was altered. Even Heiser agrees. The only question is what the original meant.

3. **Ugaritic parallels.** The density and specificity of parallels between Ugaritic texts and the Hebrew Bible make coincidence implausible. Psalm 29 as a repurposed Baal hymn is widely accepted.

### Where the consensus is weaker:

1. **Timing.** The claim that "true monotheism only emerged in the exile" has to explain Elephantine (polytheistic Yahwism in the 5th c. BCE), Tigay's onomastics (monolatrous naming patterns centuries earlier), and the fact that Deutero-Isaiah's monotheism is so fully-formed it's hard to see as an "emergence."

2. **Mechanism.** The evolutionary model describes WHAT happened but is vague on WHY. Why did THIS people, alone in the ANE, reduce their pantheon to one? "Political centralization" and "exile trauma" are plausible pressures, but they produced different results elsewhere.

3. **The texts as evidence.** Schmid's late-dating challenge is serious. If the Pentateuch is Persian-period, Smith's use of Genesis and Exodus as windows into pre-exilic religion is methodologically questionable.

### Where the challengers are strongest:

1. **Tigay's onomastics.** The data is the data. 94%+ YHWH names in Hebrew inscriptions demands explanation. The counter ("names don't reflect belief") is possible but ad hoc.

2. **Sommer's fluidity model.** It explains the "polytheistic" language in ostensibly monotheistic texts without requiring an evolutionary framework. It's elegant and textually grounded.

3. **Schmid's late dating.** If accepted, it doesn't disprove polytheistic origins (archaeology still shows that), but it removes the main textual evidence for the evolutionary timeline.

### Where the challengers are weakest:

1. **Heiser's divine council as "not polytheism."** Distinguishing "acknowledging other divine beings who are created/subordinate" from "polytheism" may be a distinction without a difference. Most ANE polytheistic systems had hierarchies too.

2. **Conservative dating of texts.** The argument that Moses wrote the Pentateuch (or even that it's substantially pre-exilic) is not taken seriously by critical scholarship for reasons that are well-established, not just prejudicial.

## 10. How to Read a Scholar: Questions to Ask

When evaluating any scholar in this debate, don't just ask "what do they argue?" Ask:

1. **Who trained them?** What lineage are they in? What assumptions did they inherit?

2. **Where do they work?** What pressures does their institution create? Do they need to please a confessional constituency? An academic tenure committee? Both?

3. **What are their religious commitments?** Not to dismiss them as "biased," but to understand what questions feel urgent to them and what conclusions would be costly.

4. **What method do they favor?** Text criticism, archaeology, onomastics, and comparative religion each have built-in tilts. A scholar who only does text criticism will see different things than one who digs in the ground.

5. **Who publishes them?** Oxford University Press and Lexham Press have different audiences, different peer review standards, and different prestige.

6. **Who cites them?** Are they cited by scholars on the OTHER side? If not, why not? Is it because their arguments are weak, or because the other side doesn't read them?

7. **What would falsify their position?** If a scholar can't articulate what evidence would change their mind, they're not doing scholarship — they're doing advocacy.

8. **What's their trajectory?** Have they changed their mind over their career? In which direction? Scholars who have moved TOWARD complexity (rather than toward certainty) are generally more trustworthy.
