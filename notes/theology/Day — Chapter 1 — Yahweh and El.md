# Day — Chapter 1: Yahweh and El

Day's foundational chapter. He argues that Yahweh and El were originally separate deities who became equated, that Yahweh originated in the south (Midian/Edom), and that Israel appropriated massive amounts of El language and imagery — aged god, wisdom, creator, divine council, paradise — while eventually rejecting only two El elements: the bull cult and El's consort Asherah.

---

## Claim 1: Yahweh and El were originally DISTINCT deities, not the same god

**Author's claim:** "El and Yahweh were originally distinct deities that became amalgamated" (p. 15). Cross and de Moor are wrong to think Yahweh was originally a form or epithet of El. Day holds this was the view "as long ago as F.K. Movers" (1841) and has been argued since by Eissfeldt and Mettinger.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Character difference:** In Ugaritic texts El is "wholly benevolent," whereas Yahweh has a fierce as well as a kind side (p. 14, citing Løkkegaard)
2. **Storm association:** The earliest evidence (Judg 5:4-5) associates Yahweh with the storm — something El is NOT connected with. This is reminiscent of Baal, not El (p. 15, citing Mettinger)
3. **Cross's theory is "pure speculation":** The formula *ˀil ḏū yahwī ṣabaˀôt* ("El who creates hosts") is "nowhere attested, whether inside or outside the Bible" (p. 15). Cross's grammatical objection (that a proper name shouldn't appear in the construct) is wrong — "Yahweh of Teman" and "Yahweh of Samaria" at Kuntillet Ajrud show proper names CAN appear in construct (citing Emerton). Further, *hyh* (hwh) is not attested in Hebrew in the hiphil ("cause to be, create"), though it is in Aramaic; the name more likely means "he is" (qal)
4. **The OT itself signals discontinuity:** E and P sources say the patriarchs did NOT know the name Yahweh; it was first revealed to Moses (Exod 3:13-15; 6:2-3), unlike J where Yahweh was known from primaeval times (Gen 4:26)

**Confidence:** HIGH. Day's case is strong on grammatical grounds against Cross; Emerton's observation about Kuntillet Ajrud usage of proper names in construct is decisive. But the *character* argument is weaker — "wholly benevolent" is an overstatement about Ugaritic El.

**What's at stake:** This is the foundational question for the entire field. If Cross is right, Yahweh IS El — Israel's god was the Canaanite high god from the beginning, just reformulated. If Day is right, Yahweh was a foreign import who was later *grafted onto* El, which means the character of Yahwism came from outside Canaan. Most scholars today follow Day/Eissfeldt/Mettinger on this, not Cross.

**Who disagrees:** P.M. Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, pp. 60-75); J.C. de Moor (*The Rise of Yahwism*, pp. 223-60). Cross was Day's primary target and remains the most important counter-voice.

**Alternative reading:** Sommer's "divine fluidity" model partially reconciles both: Yahweh IS El in some sense but manifests differently. Cross's theory, while grammatically speculative, captures the genuine continuity between El and Yahweh that Day himself documents throughout the chapter.

**My assessment:** Day wins on grammar — Cross's unattested formula is a real problem, and the Kuntillet Ajrud parallels are strong evidence. But Day's admission that the OT "has no qualms in equating Yahweh with El" (p. 15) shows the distinction is more theoretical than practical. They became the same god so thoroughly that the original separation barely matters for biblical interpretation. The character arguments (benevolent vs. fierce) are the weakest part of his case — Yahweh has plenty of benevolent passages, and ANE gods weren't so one-dimensional.

## Claim 2: Yahweh originated OUTSIDE Canaan, in the south (Midian/Edom)

**Author's claim:** "Most scholars who have written on the subject during recent decades support the idea that Yahweh had his origins outside the land of Israel to the south, in the area of Midian" (p. 16). Evidence points to N.W. Arabia rather than the Sinai peninsula.

**Evidence presented:**
1. Biblical poetry: Judg 5:4-5; Deut 33:2; Hab 3:3, 7 — all have Yahweh "marching from" Edom/Seir/Paran/Teman
2. Kuntillet Ajrud inscription with "Yahweh of Teman" (*yhwh tmn*), Teman being a southern region (p. 17)
3. Egyptian references to "the Shasu of Yhw" alongside "the Shasu of Seir" — an Egyptian text from 14th c. BCE listing Yahweh-land among Shasu (nomadic) territories in the Edom/Seir region (p. 17)
4. Archaeological evidence: civilization in the Hejaz area (N.W. Arabia/Midian) in the Late Bronze Age/Early Iron Age, contrasted with "general lack of this in this period in the Sinai peninsula" (p. 17)
5. Even "arch-'minimalist' N.P. Lemche feels confident about this" (p. 16 n. 12)

**Confidence:** HIGH. The convergence of Egyptian onomastics (Soleb), biblical poetry, and the Kuntillet Ajrud epithet is unusually strong. The Soleb inscription (14th c. BCE) antedates any Israelite presence by centuries — this is the strongest single piece of evidence.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh came from the south, then Israel's god was NOT indigenous to Canaan. This undercuts any claim that Israelite religion was simply "Canaanite religion." It also complicates the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis — was Moses historical or just a narrative vehicle for a genuine cultural memory of southern origins?

**Who disagrees:** M.C. Astour questioned the reading of *Seir* in the Egyptian texts, claiming the reference was to Sarara in Syria, not Seir in Edom (p. 17). Day counters that "on balance... the Egyptian *S'rr* still seems more likely to be a slip for *S'r* (Seir) than the name Sarara." Daniel Fleming (*Yahweh Before Israel*, 2021) offers the most recent major challenge — arguing Yahweh was NOT taken from outsiders but the name reflects Israel's own diverse origins. Christian Frevel also complicates the southern origins thesis.

**Alternative reading:** Fleming's view that Yahweh's name emerged from within the diverse herding peoples who became Israel, rather than being imported from Midian. This preserves non-Canaanite origins without requiring the Kenite hypothesis.

**My assessment:** The Soleb evidence is genuinely difficult to dismiss. But Day concedes Astour's objection only with "on balance" — he doesn't demolish it. The strongest point is convergence: Soleb + biblical poetry + Kuntillet Ajrud + archaeology of Hejaz all point south. Fleming's 2021 challenge is important but doesn't explain the Egyptian data.

## Claim 3: The OT's divine council derives from the seventy sons of El

**Author's claim:** The "sons of God" in the OT (Gen 6:2; Job 1:6; Ps 29:1; Deut 32:8) are directly inherited from the Ugaritic concept of the seventy sons of El and his consort Asherah. "That this is certain can be established from the fact that both were seventy in number" (p. 24).

**Evidence presented:**
1. At Ugarit: "the seventy sons of Asherah (Athirat)" (*šbʻm. bn. ʼaṯrt*, KTU² 1.4.VI.46). Since Asherah was El's consort, these are El's sons (p. 24)
2. Deut 32:8 (original): "When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance... he fixed the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the **sons of God**" (*bene ʼelohim*). This reading is supported by 4QDeut (Qumran), LXX, Symmachus, Old Latin, Syro-Hexaplaric manuscript (p. 24)
3. MT's "sons of Israel" (*bene yiśraʼel*) is a deliberate scribal alteration driven by discomfort with polytheistic overtones (p. 24)
4. Genesis 10 lists precisely seventy nations, corresponding to seventy guardian deities
5. Later Jewish apocalyptic texts (1 Enoch, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan) explicitly speak of seventy guardian angels of the nations — confirming the tradition (p. 25)
6. The OT NEVER calls them "sons of Yahweh" — always "sons of God" with words containing *ʼl* (*bene haʼelohim, bene ʼelohim, bene ʼelim*). This "finds a ready explanation in their origin in the sons of the Canaanite god El" (p. 25)

**Replies to critics:**
- **Whybray:** Claims "seventy" is a conventional large number. Day: No — Genesis 10 lists precisely seventy nations. This is specific, not generic.
- **Block:** Suggests the seventy gods are a back-projection from the seventy nations of Gen 10. Day: But seventy sons of El are "already attested prior to Deut 32:8, as the Ugaritic texts prove" — so Block has the direction of influence reversed (p. 25)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is one of the most secure parallels in the entire book. The same number (70), the same name pattern ("sons of El/God," never "sons of Yahweh"), MT alteration confirming embarrassment, and later Jewish tradition all converge.

**What's at stake:** This directly connects Israel's worldview to Canaanite mythology. The divine council is not a Hebrew innovation — it's inherited furniture. The MT scribes' alteration is the smoking gun: you don't censor what doesn't bother you.

**Who disagrees:** D.I. Block (*The Gods of the Nations*, 1988) — the only named counter-voice in this section, and Day dispatches him efficiently. Heiser (not mentioned here but in the broader debate) agrees about the "sons of God" reading but interprets them as created beings, not rival deities.

**Alternative reading:** Heiser's view: these are legitimate divine beings but subordinate, created, and part of Yahweh's bureaucracy — not a polytheistic pantheon in the Canaanite sense. The number 70 is shared but the *ontology* differs.

**My assessment:** Day is clearly right about the derivation. The MT alteration alone proves the original was embarrassing. Where Day overstates slightly is the implication that this *necessarily* proves Israelite polytheism — Heiser's alternative (subordinate created beings) is theologically distinct but textually possible. The shared number cannot be coincidence — Ugarit and Israel are drawing from the same conceptual reservoir.

## Claim 4: The Garden of Eden story derives from El's dwelling place

**Author's claim:** "The thesis I shall defend... is that the garden of Eden — or garden of God, as it is sometimes called — derives from the dwelling place of El" (p. 33). El dwelt at the source of the rivers in Armenia, and this is exactly where Genesis places Eden.

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Geographic precision:** Gen 2:10-14 places Eden at the source of the Tigris and Euphrates, which points to Armenia (not the Persian Gulf / Sumerian Dilmun). Albright: Hebrew *rāšîm* "refers solely to the headwaters of a river" (p. 30-31)
2. **Mountain location:** Ezek 28:14, 16 places Eden on a mountain — fits Armenia (mountainous) not the Persian Gulf (flat)
3. **Hittite-Canaanite Elkunirsa myth:** El (= Elkunirsa, "El, creator of the earth") "lives in a tent at the source of the Mala river (Euphrates)" — which "must also be in Armenia, since El characteristically dwells on a mountain" (p. 31-32)
4. **Noah's Ark landed on Ararat** (Urartu/Armenia, Gen 8:4). In the Mesopotamian flood story, the hero Utnapishtim was translated to Paradise at *pî nārāti* "the mouth of the rivers" — naturally the Armenian source, not the Gulf mouth (p. 31)
5. **Job 15:7-8:** The first man's dwelling is "in the council of God" — the divine council meets on El's mountain at Ugarit
6. **Wisdom emphasis in Eden** (Gen 3:6, 22; Ezek 28:12, 17) connects to El, who was particularly noted for wisdom
7. **Eden associated with creation** — consonant with El as creator god
8. **Gihon** (one of Eden's rivers) shares its name with Jerusalem's spring, associated with El/Elyon in Ps 46:5 (ET 4)

**Regarding Ezekiel 28:** Day demonstrates that both oracles (vv. 2-10 and vv. 12-19) reflect El traditions:
- God called *ʼel* (rare in Ezekiel — only here and Ezek 10:5)
- "seat of God in the heart of the seas" reflects El's dwelling at the source of rivers/deeps (Ugaritic *mtb ʼil*)
- Emphasis on divine wisdom mirrors El
- Ezek 28:12-19 is a variant of the Eden story (cherub, casting out, Eden)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The geographic argument is strong (rivers + mountain = Armenia, not Dilmun), and the Elkunirsa myth is direct evidence for El at the Euphrates source. But multiple sacred mountains competed in tradition (Hermon, Lebanon, Amanus — see Day's lengthy footnote on pp. 32-33 where he engages Pope, Cross, Naccache).

**What's at stake:** The Eden story is not a Hebrew original — it's a transformed Canaanite myth about El's cosmic mountain. This means one of the most foundational biblical narratives (creation, fall, paradise) has a pre-Israelite Canaanite source. Theologically significant for doctrines of revelation and biblical uniqueness.

**Who disagrees:** Those locating Eden in the Persian Gulf (Speiser, following Sumerian Dilmun tradition); Westermann (who thinks the Yahwist had no particular location in mind — Day says the "precise geographical indications" refute this). For competing locations of El's mountain: Pope (Khirbet Afqa), Cross (Mt Amanus), Naccache (Mt Lebanon) — all disputed in Day's footnote (pp. 32-33).

**Alternative reading:** The Eden story could draw from MULTIPLE ANE paradise traditions (Sumerian Dilmun, Canaanite El's mountain, Jerusalem temple symbolism) without being reducible to any single one. Day's derivation is plausible but too monocausal.

**My assessment:** Day makes a strong cumulative case. The convergence of geography (Armenia = rivers + mountain), the Elkunirsa myth (direct textual evidence), and the El-specific themes (wisdom, divine council) is compelling. But the confidence should be slightly tempered — paradise myths were widespread, and Genesis may fuse multiple sources. The Ezekiel 28 connection to El is the most persuasive single link.

## Claim 5: El-Shaddai means "El, the Mountain One," not "God Almighty"

**Author's claim:** "The most likely interpretation of the divine name El-Shaddai is 'El, the mountain one', with reference to El's dwelling place on a mountain" (p. 33). The traditional "God Almighty" (LXX *Pantokratōr*, Vulgate *omnipotens*) is a later misunderstanding.

**Evidence presented:**
1. Akkadian *šadû* = "mountain," with derived forms *šaddāʼu/šadduʼa* = "mountain inhabitant" (p. 33)
2. Hurrian hymn: El described as "El, the one of the mountain" (*ʼIl paban-ḫi-wi-ni*) (p. 34, citing Cross)
3. Amorite parallel: *bēl šadê* "lord of the mountain" used of Amurru, whom Cross identifies as the Amorite El — Amurru's consort is Asratum (Asherah), El's consort (p. 34)
4. Deir ʻAlla inscription (I.5-6): "the Shaddayyin took their places as the assembly" — the *šdyn* are parallel with "gods" (*ʼlhn*), and the divine assembly meets on a mountain. El, as supreme deity, would be Shaddai "par excellence" (p. 34)
5. Shaddai appears parallel with El "a remarkable number of times, especially in Job" (Num 24:4, 16; Job 8:3, 13:3, 15:25, etc.) (p. 34-35)
6. The competing etymology (Hebrew *śādeh* "field") is problematic because "field" has *ś* (sin) while Shaddai has *š* (shin). The "mountain" derivation from "breast" (*šad*) has the right *š* (p. 33-34)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Day himself hedges: "though we cannot be certain, a plausible case can be made" (p. 35). The Deir ʻAlla evidence is the strongest point; the Amorite parallel is suggestive but depends on Cross's identification of Amurru as El, which is itself debated.

**What's at stake:** If correct, every time the OT calls God "Shaddai" (or "El-Shaddai"), it's invoking a Canaanite epithet for El as the mountain-dwelling god — not a statement about omnipotence. This changes how we read P's deliberate use of "El-Shaddai" for the patriarchal period.

**Who disagrees:** Those favoring "El of the field" etymology (M. Weippert, W. Wifall, O. Loretz). The traditional "Almighty" reading is confessional, not linguistic.

**Alternative reading:** Shaddai could simply be an archaic divine name whose etymology was lost early. Day's "mountain" derivation is the best linguistic proposal but not certain.

**My assessment:** This is a "plausible but not proven" claim, and Day is appropriately cautious. The Deir ʻAlla evidence is genuinely strong — the Shaddayyin as divine assembly members. The Amorite *bēl šadê* parallel is weaker (depends on Cross's chain of identifications). I'd say MEDIUM-HIGH, upgrading from Day's own caution because the Ugaritic El-mountain association is independently strong.

## Claim 6: Jeroboam's golden calves were Yahwistic images (not pedestals), derived from El's bull symbolism

**Author's claim:** "Jeroboam's golden calves derived from the old bull cult associated with El-Bethel at Bethel, which was traced back to Jacob" (p. 39). They were images, not pedestals.

**Evidence presented (Yahwistic, not Baal):**
1. Jeroboam was trying to stop people from going to Jerusalem to worship *Yahweh* — wouldn't impose an alien god (p. 37)
2. Jeroboam's words: "Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" — it's Yahweh who is the God of the Exodus (p. 37)
3. Exodus 32 (polemicizing against Jeroboam's calves) connects them to Yahweh: "Tomorrow shall be a feast to the LORD" (Exod 32:5) (p. 37)
4. Jeroboam's own son has a Yahwistic name (Abijah, 1 Kgs 14:1)
5. Samaria ostracon 41 has the personal name *ʻglyw* — "calf of Yahweh" or "Yahweh is a calf" (p. 38)
6. Jehu's revolution "wiped out Baal from Israel" (2 Kgs 10:28) but did NOT remove the golden calves (2 Kgs 10:29) — clearly not perceived as Baal symbols (p. 37)
7. Elijah, fierce anti-Baal prophet, never condemns the calves (p. 37)

**Evidence presented (El-derived, not Baal-derived):**
1. Bull symbolism at Ugarit is *supremely* associated with El ("Bull El," *tr ʼil*) — not primarily Baal
2. One of the two calf sites was **Bethel** — literally "house of El." The god was called El-Bethel (Gen 35:7; cf. 31:13); Jer 48:13 refers to the deity "Bethel" (p. 38-39)
3. Jacob's god is *ʼabîr yaʻaqob* "the Mighty One of Jacob" (Gen 49:24) — *ʼabîr* is closely connected to *ʼabbîr* "bull." Jacob's god was likely originally "the Bull of Jacob" (p. 39)

**Evidence presented (images, not pedestals):**
1. The OT consistently represents them as images: "Behold your gods... who brought you up" (1 Kgs 12:28); "sacrificing to the calves he had made" (p. 41)
2. Hos 8:6: "A workman made it; it is not God" — pointless unless people DID consider the calf a god (p. 41)
3. El at Ugarit is called "Bull El" — the epithet is applied to EL HIMSELF, "not to his pedestal" (p. 41)
4. The Jacob-Bethel connection: if Jacob's god was "the Bull of Jacob," the bull was "more than a mere pedestal" (p. 41-42)

**Confidence:** HIGH for Yahwistic (not Baal); MEDIUM-HIGH for El derivation specifically; HIGH for images over pedestals.

**What's at stake:** This establishes that ancient Israelites worshipped Yahweh in the form of a bull image — a direct material violation of the second commandment that was apparently mainstream in the Northern Kingdom for two centuries. It also reveals that El bull cult survived at Bethel specifically, the very place associated with Jacob's vision.

**Who disagrees:** Those who think the calves represented Baal (Tobit 1:5 already attests this view; modern advocates include T.J. Meek, H.M. Barstad). Obbink/Albright pedestal theory (the calves were thrones for invisibly enthroned Yahweh, not images — citing Hadad-on-bull Syrian iconography). Eissfeldt: calves were on standards/poles. Lewy/Bailey: calves represented the moon-god Sin.

**Alternative reading:** The pedestal theory has genuine iconographic support from Syrian Hadad depictions. Day's objection that "Bull" is applied to El himself (not his pedestal) doesn't fully engage with how visual cult representation actually works in the ANE — a deity could be both represented by and enthroned upon the same animal.

**My assessment:** Day's case is strongest on the Yahwistic identification (Jehu's purge is the trump card). The El connection is persuasive through Bethel specifically but weaker at Dan. The images-vs-pedestals debate is a false binary: in ANE worship, the animal could function as BOTH symbol and throne. The pedestal theory isn't as easily dismissed as Day suggests. But overall, Day's position represents the current scholarly majority and his arguments are well-reasoned.

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Yahweh and El originally distinct | HIGH | Cross/de Moor's counter-arguments about shared identity |
| Yahweh originated in the south (Midian) | HIGH | Fleming (2021) and Frevel's complications |
| Divine council = seventy sons of El | VERY HIGH | Heiser's ontological reinterpretation |
| Eden = El's dwelling in Armenia | MEDIUM-HIGH | Multiple competing sacred mountains; monocausal explanation |
| El-Shaddai = "El, the mountain one" | MEDIUM | Etymological uncertainty; competing "field" derivation |
| Golden calves = Yahwistic images from El | HIGH | Pedestal theory from Syrian iconography |

**Strongest section:** The divine council / seventy sons argument. The convergence of Ugaritic numbers, Deut 32:8 textual criticism, MT alteration, and later Jewish tradition makes this nearly unassailable.

**Weakest section:** The "character" argument for El/Yahweh distinction. "Wholly benevolent" is a caricature of Ugaritic El, and the ferocity/kindness contrast collapses when you consider the full biblical portrait of Yahweh.

**Most important insight:** Day's observation that the OT "has no qualms in equating Yahweh with El" — in marked contrast to its "vehement opposition to Baal." This selective appropriation (El = good, Baal = bad, Asherah = rejected, bull = eventually rejected) reveals a deliberate theological filtering process, not passive cultural absorption.

**Cross-reference notes:** Smith (*Early History*, Ch 1) agrees on El/Yahweh separation and convergence but emphasizes sociological mechanisms (convergence and differentiation) more than Day's comparative mythology. Dever barely engages Day's textual methodology but reaches similar conclusions archaeologically. Cross (*Canaanite Myth*) is the primary dissenting voice Day targets throughout.
