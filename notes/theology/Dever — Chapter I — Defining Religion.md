# Dever, Did God Have a Wife? — Chapter I

## Defining and Contextualizing Religion

---

## Claim 1: Religion must be defined by what people DO, not what they believe

**Dever's claim:** Most works on Israelite religion fail even to define "religion." Dever adopts Hans Penner's functional definition: "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." This focuses on PRACTICE — the observable, material, archaeological dimension of religion — rather than theology or belief.

**Evidence:** Surveys definitions from Tylor, Frazer, Durkheim, Evans-Pritchard, Douglas, Geertz, James, Marx, Eliade, and others. Notes that Zevit is the rare exception who attempts a definition.

**Confidence:** HIGH that this is a useful operational definition for an archaeological study. MEDIUM that it captures all dimensions of Israelite religion (it deliberately excludes theology).

---

## Claim 2: The harsh realities of daily life are essential context for understanding Israelite religion

**Dever's claim:** You cannot understand Israelite religion without understanding how people lived. Most scholars ignore this entirely. Dever provides a detailed reconstruction of daily life — the four-room house, the extended family compound, the diet, the diseases, the gender roles, the life expectancy — as the necessary context for understanding what religion MEANT to ordinary people.

**Evidence presented:**

| Aspect of Daily Life | Detail | Religious Implication |
|---------------------|--------|---------------------|
| **The four-room house** | 850-1700 sq ft, animals stabled below, family slept above | Religion practiced AT HOME, not at Temple |
| **Family structure** | 15-20 people in extended family compound; kin-based society | Religion was domestic, not institutional |
| **Diet** | Bread, olives, yogurt, occasional meat; one bad year = hardship, two = disaster | Fertility and agricultural success were existential religious concerns |
| **Health** | Arthritis in teenagers, carpal tunnel in women, bad teeth from stone-ground flour, malnutrition | Healing was a primary religious function |
| **Life expectancy** | ~30 years; a man of 50 was a revered "elder" | No afterlife to look forward to — religion had to deliver NOW |
| **Literacy** | Below 5%; earliest Hebrew inscriptions 10th-11th century; "functional literacy" only from 8th century | "Ordinary people could not have read the Bible if they had had it" |
| **Sanitation** | "No sanitary facilities at all, not even 'outhouses.' And there was, of course, no running water." | Life was "nasty, brutish, and short" in the most literal sense |
| **Women's work** | "Tending to the family shrine and all of the religious rituals" | Women were the primary practitioners of domestic religion |
| **Men's leisure** | "Probably in the village common-house, talking religion and politics" | Women did the religious WORK; men did the religious TALKING |

**Dever's key quote:**
> "The 'good life' is the simple, everyday joy of being alive, well, secure, surrounded by one's family, taking pleasure in good things. But the vision of Micah and Amos is also a deeply religious one, in which physical and spiritual realities are in harmony."

> "Only as the full materiality of ancient Israel is more securely grasped will we be able to make proper sense of its spirituality."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the archaeological data (houses, diet, health, demography are well-attested). MEDIUM on Dever's inference that all Israelites experienced life this uniformly (there was variation).

**What's at stake:** "If the sketch of the lives of most people that I have attempted here is even close to the reality, it is evident that for most people it was not the orthodox, biblical theology promulgated by clerics and reformers that mattered, but the 'good life' that they hoped to enjoy."

---

## Claim 3: Women were the primary practitioners of household religion

**Dever's claim:** While men controlled the official cult (priesthood, Temple, writing), women controlled the domestic cult — the family shrine, the rituals of fertility and healing, the care of figurines. "Women played a much larger role in all this family 'maintenance' than usually thought." The suppression of folk religion was, in part, the suppression of women's religious authority.

**Evidence:**
- "Women's work was mainly although not exclusively at home: harvesting some things; preserving, preparing, and cooking food... spinning, weaving... making baskets and pottery; caring for both the elders and a house full of children; and, as I shall show, tending to the family shrine and all of the religious rituals, rites of passage, and so forth."
- Men had leisure time in the off-season — "probably in the village common-house, talking religion and politics"
- The figurines are found in DOMESTIC contexts — women's spaces
- "It is archaeology, and archaeology alone, that can bring back those anonymous, forgotten folk of antiquity and give them their long-lost voice, allowing them to speak to us of their ultimate concerns. We must listen, especially to women, whom patriarchy has rendered largely mute."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (archaeological context + ethnographic parallels support this, but direct evidence is limited).

## Chapter I Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| Religion defined by practice, not belief | HIGH (for archaeological study) |
| Daily life context essential for understanding religion | HIGH |
| Women as primary domestic religious practitioners | MEDIUM-HIGH |

**Key contribution:** Dever provides the material context that Smith and Römer lack. When Smith talks about "convergence" and Römer about "political construction," Dever shows you the four-room house where the figurine sat on a shelf, the grinding stone where the woman with carpal tunnel prayed for healthy children, the oven where she baked offerings to the goddess. This is what "folk religion" actually looked like.
