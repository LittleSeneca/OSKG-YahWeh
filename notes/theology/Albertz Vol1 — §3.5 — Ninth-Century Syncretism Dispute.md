# Albertz — §3.5: The Dispute Over Official Syncretism in the Ninth Century

The Omride dynasty's diplomatic syncretism — a Baal temple in Samaria alongside Yahweh worship — provoked the first organized prophetic opposition in Israelite history. Elijah and Elisha, economically independent prophetic outsiders, combined social criticism (defense of traditional land rights) with religious criticism (defense of exclusive Yahwism). The conflict culminated in Jehu's bloody revolution (c. 842 BCE), which exterminated the Omrides, destroyed the Baal temple, and marked the first time Yahwism showed the "intolerance" that would become its hallmark.

## Claim 3: The Carmel Narrative Is a Late Theological Construction, Not Historical
→ [[claim-carmel-narrative-late-theological-construction-not-historical]] | **albertz-hir-3.5.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  The Carmel narrative is not a cult legend but a late theological construction. No king appears; the Yahweh altar is destroyed not preserved; the anti-idol polemic presupposes exilic monotheism. Alt's theory should be abandoned. The real Elijah was a rainmaker, not a theologian.
  Depends on: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]]. The narrative retrojects exilic monotheistic rhetoric into the 9th century.

## §3.5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Omride policy = diplomatic ditheism, not persecution | MEDIUM | Sources entirely from Deuterononomistic side; we don't know Omride theology |
| 2. Elijah/Elisha as déclassé outsiders enabled systemic critique | MEDIUM | Sparse sources; sociological profile is inferential |
| 3. Carmel narrative is late fiction; Alt's theory should be abandoned | MEDIUM-HIGH | Strong literary-critical argument; Alt's reconstruction is speculative |
| 4. Jehu's revolution = first Yahwistic intolerance; pyrrhic victory | HIGH (events); MEDIUM (interpretation) | Exclusivity may predate Jehu; "intolerance" is a normative judgment |

**Strongest section:** The deconstruction of the Carmel narrative (Claim 3). Albertz's literary-critical case is crisp and convincing.

**Weakest section:** The sociological profile of Elijah/Elisha (Claim 2). Interesting but thin on direct textual evidence.

**Cross-reference:** This section is where Albertz most closely parallels the "Yahweh-alone party" model of Morton Smith and Bernhard Lang — a zealous minority enforcing exclusivity against a syncretistic majority. Albertz's contribution is the sociological grounding: the exclusivists were déclassé economic outsiders, not just theological purists.
