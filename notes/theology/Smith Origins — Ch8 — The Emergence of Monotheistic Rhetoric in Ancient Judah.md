# Smith — Chapter 8: The Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric in Ancient Judah

This is where the book's title is directly addressed. Smith defines what monotheism IS in the biblical texts (a specific kind of exclusivity rhetoric), shows its rarity and late dating, and argues that it emerged as "inner community discourse" in response to the collapse of the old national theology. Two historical conditions enabled it: the erosion of the lineage system (making the individual deity intelligible) and the imperial crisis (making the national-god model obsolete). Monotheism is not a "stage" of religion — it's a rhetorical strategy born from political catastrophe.

---

## Claim 1: Monotheistic statements in the Bible are surprisingly rare, concentrated in the exilic/post-exilic period, and always rhetorical

**Author's claim:** "Because of the post-biblical importance of monotheism, the relative rarity of its expression in the Bible is quite striking." Monotheism "is a kind of inner community discourse establishing a distance from outsiders; it uses the language of Yahweh's exceptional divine status... to absolutize Yahweh's claim on Israel."

**Evidence presented:**
- Two categories of monotheistic language: (1) exclusivity — Yahweh "alone" (*lĕbadd-) or no god "apart from/besides" Yahweh (*zûlat-); (2) denial of reality — other gods are "not" ('ên), "nothings" ('ĕlîlîm), or "dead" (mētîm).
- Category 1 examples: Deut 4:35, 2 Kings 19:15/Isa 37:16, Neh 9:6, Ps 86:10, 2 Sam 7:22/1 Chr 17:20. All but Deut 4:35 are prayers — "monotheism here belongs to the rhetoric of praise."
- Category 2 examples: Deut 4:39, 1 Sam 2:2, Jer 16:19-20, Ps 96:5/1 Chr 16:26, Ps 82:7.
- The Shema (Deut 6:4) is explicitly EXCLUDED: "It is difficult to gauge to what degree the monotheistic interpretation of the Shema is due to later readings." Tigay: "This is not a declaration of monotheism, meaning that there is only one God."
- The First Commandment (Exod 20:3) is also excluded — it prohibits veneration of other gods without denying their existence.
- Dating: "Most of the references to monotheism derive from the exilic period or later." Jer 16:19-20 may be late pre-exilic. 1 Sam 2:2 and Ps 82 are undatable. "On the whole the late monarchy and exile seem to represent the general period for the emergence of monotheistic rhetoric."
- Halpern: "Had Second Isaiah... not had Jeremiah's (and Deuteronomy's) voice crying on his own arrival from the steppe, his explicit monotheistic claims would have fallen on deaf ears."
- Seitz: "This is not a sublime monotheism capable of differentiation from a more concrete henotheism — rather it is henotheism of a particularly potent stripe."
- "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The rarity of monotheistic statements is a textually verifiable fact. The exclusion of the Shema and First Commandment is well-argued and follows Tigay's careful philology.

**What's at stake:** If monotheistic rhetoric is rare and late, then "biblical monotheism" is not the Bible's default position. Most of the Bible operates within a polytheistic/monolatrous framework. Monotheism proper — the denial that other gods exist — is a minority rhetoric concentrated in crisis literature. This inverts the common assumption that the Bible is "a monotheistic book."

**Who disagrees:** Albright and his school (Mosaic monotheism from Sinai). Those who read the Shema and First Commandment as monotheistic declarations. Scholars who backdate monotheistic texts to the monarchy.

**Alternative reading:** The rarity of monotheistic statements might reflect editing — later monotheists may have SUPPRESSED polytheistic texts rather than ADDED monotheistic ones. The concentration in exilic texts might reflect preservation bias, not historical emergence.

**My assessment:** Smith's philological precision is admirable. The exclusion of the Shema is methodologically important — it prevents the most famous "monotheistic" text from being used as evidence for early monotheism. The two-category typology is clear and useful. The weakest link is the dating argument, which depends on scholarly consensus about biblical books that is itself contested. But even if some texts are earlier than Smith thinks, the overall pattern (monotheistic statements are rare and clustered in crisis contexts) holds.

## Claim 2: Monotheistic rhetoric emerged against a background of robust Judean polytheism — the prior context was national theology with divine council

**Author's claim:** "The period of the monarchy sustained various forms of Israelite polytheism... The Bible hardly provides an objective or complete picture of Israel's religion, because of significant editorial selection."

**Evidence presented:**
- Spectrum of polytheistic options in late monarchy: from cultic devotion to many deities, to "ditheistic" model (Yahweh + Asherah as divine couple), to single national god with servant-gods.
- All models had Yahweh as king of the heavenly host. But "only scattered references to a number of other deities who belong to the middle levels of the pantheon have survived."
- Extra-biblical evidence: Kuntillet 'Ajrud ("Yahweh and his asherah"), female pillar figurines, iconographic evidence for goddess.
- "Even if we did not know of the biblical references to other deities, this extra-biblical evidence alone suggests a polytheistic situation."
- The older national theology (Deut 32:8-9): Elyon presides; each divine son gets a nation; Yahweh gets Israel. This theology was TOLERANT of other gods as long as they stayed in their own nations. Elijah, Amos, Hosea opposed foreign gods ON ISRAELITE SOIL but didn't deny foreign gods in their own territory.
- Royal theology reinforced the national god: Psalms 2, 18, 45, 72, 89, 110 — divine and human kings in parallel. Cosmic enemies parallel human enemies. The king participates in Yahweh's divine warrior power (Ps 89:26: "I will set his hand on Sea").
- Isaiah 9:5: 'ēl gibbôr and 'ăbî'ad applied to the Davidic king — divine titles drawn from El ('ăbî'ad = "eternal father") and Baal ('ēl gibbôr = "warrior god").
- ḥērem warfare: Anat's battle (1.13) provides the divine model for Israelite holy war. The king fights Yahweh's ḥērem.
- "In short, the developments issuing in clear expressions of biblical monotheism involved many changes spanning centuries."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The polytheistic background is well-established by both biblical polemics and archaeological evidence.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism emerged from WITHIN a functioning polytheistic system, it's not a "restoration" of original purity but a NOVEL development. The national theology wasn't monotheism in embryo — it was a different system that had to be dismantled.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see monotheism as original (Mosaic) and polytheism as later corruption. Those who minimize the extra-biblical evidence for Asherah worship.

**Alternative reading:** The national theology might have been more monolatrous than Smith allows. The existence of other nations' gods was acknowledged but they weren't considered competitors to Yahweh WITHIN Israel. This is closer to "henotheism" than polytheism.

**My assessment:** Smith's survey is thorough and balanced. The most important point is that the national theology was FUNCTIONAL — it worked as long as nations existed in balance. Only when imperial powers disrupted that balance did the theology break. This is a genuinely historical explanation, not a theological one.

## Claim 3: Two historical conditions enabled monotheism — (a) erosion of the lineage system, (b) imperial crisis making the national-god model obsolete

**Author's claim:** "A culture with a diminished lineage system, one less embedded in traditional family patrimonies due to societal changes in the eighth through sixth centuries, might be more predisposed both to hold to individual human accountability for behavior and to see an individual deity accountable for the cosmos."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Condition 1 (social):** Seventh-century lineage erosion — royal power over patriarchal authority, land consolidation by upper class, warfare on countryside. Post-exilic bêt 'ābôt ("fathers' house") replaces older bêt 'āb — signaling decline. Prophetic shift to individual accountability (Jer 31:29-30; Ezek 18; Deut 24:16): children not punished for fathers' sins. "I view this individual accountability at the human and divine levels as concomitant developments."
- **Condition 2 (political):** Rise of Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian empires made the national-god model obsolete. Tigay: "The need to emphasize the monotheistic idea in this period was probably due to the increased exposure of Israel to the triumphant Assyrian and Babylonian empires, which attributed their victories... to their gods." Looming empires suggested the model of the SUPER-national god — Assur and Marduk as sum-total of all deities. "Israel's political and social reduction in the world... further altered its social structure in a manner that had a serious impact on its traditional theology."
- The shift: "As Judah's situation on the mundane level deteriorated in history, the cosmic status of its deity soared in its literature." This fits Jaspers's "Axial Age" (ca. 800-200).
- Against Persian influence: Zoroastrian dualism (Ahura Mazda vs. Angra Mainyu) "does not truly resemble biblical monotheism." "The language of biblical monotheism appears to represent... developments of older language exalting the national god." Persian religion may have reinforced but was not the progenitor.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the correlation between lineage decline and monotheism. MEDIUM for the claim that imperial crisis was the specific trigger. Both are correlations, not demonstrated causes.

**What's at stake:** This is Smith's causal account. If correct, monotheism is a contingent historical development driven by social and political changes, not an inevitable theological evolution. Different historical circumstances might have produced a different outcome. Monotheism is not the "destination" of religious development but one particular adaptation to one particular set of crises.

**Who disagrees:** Those who see monotheism as primarily a theological breakthrough (prophetic insight, covenant logic). Persian influence theorists (though Smith's critique of Zoroastrian dualism is strong). Scholars who date lineage changes differently.

**Alternative reading:** The causal arrow could run in either direction — monotheistic theology might have CONTRIBUTED to lineage erosion (by emphasizing individual accountability before one God) rather than being caused by it. The correlation doesn't establish which caused which.

**My assessment:** Smith's two-condition model is elegant and historically grounded. The lineage argument (first made in Ch 4's Afterword) is the more original of the two. The imperial crisis argument is more conventional but still important — it explains the timing. Smith's rejection of Persian influence is well-argued: Zoroastrian dualism is structurally different from biblical monotheism, and the biblical language clearly develops from older Israelite discourse about the national god. But the causal claims remain correlations. We can say these conditions made monotheism INTELLIGIBLE; we can't prove they CAUSED it.

## Claim 4: Monotheism is a rhetoric of persuasion aimed at fellow Judeans, not a philosophical position about the number of gods

**Author's claim:** "Monotheistic statement attempted to persuade Judeans still unconvinced of this perspective. Perhaps these declarations represent the efforts of a minority of 'monotheists' to persuade a majority of Judeans who held Yahweh as the head of a larger group of divinities or divine powers."

**Evidence presented:**
- Genre analysis: monotheistic statements appear in prayers (rhetoric of praise) and sermons (rhetoric of persuasion), not in philosophical treatises.
- The pun in Psalm 96:5 ('ĕlōhîm → 'ĕlîlîm, "gods" → "nothings") is a rhetorical device — wordplay designed to persuade, not analyze.
- "Israelites continued to worship deities other than Yahweh both before and possibly after the exile." Monotheistic rhetoric was aspirational, not descriptive.
- "Monotheism is therefore not a new cultural step but expresses Israel's relationship with Yahweh."
- "Perhaps the main point of such statements was not simply to move the latter into the 'monotheistic camp' but to convince them of the reality of Yahweh's power in the world."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The rhetorical reading of monotheistic statements is Smith's central methodological contribution and it's well-supported by genre analysis.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism is rhetoric, then modern debates about "monotheism vs. polytheism" as belief SYSTEMS are anachronistic. The biblical authors weren't making ontological claims about how many divine beings exist — they were making political-theological claims about Yahweh's supremacy. "There is no god besides me" means "no power rivals Yahweh," not "the category 'deity' has only one member."

**Who disagrees:** Philosophers of religion who read biblical monotheism as ontological monotheism. Conservative theologians who see monotheistic statements as revealed truth claims. Scholars who treat monotheism as an evolutionary "stage" of religion.

**Alternative reading:** Rhetoric and ontology aren't mutually exclusive. "There is no other god" IS an ontological claim even if its PRIMARY function is rhetorical. The distinction between "making an ontological claim" and "using ontological language rhetorically" may be one of emphasis, not kind.

**My assessment:** Smith's rhetorical reading is the most important methodological move in the book. It reframes the entire debate: the question isn't "when did Israel become monotheistic?" but "what was the function of monotheistic language in its ancient context?" This is genuinely illuminating. But I think Smith may slightly overstate the rhetoric/ontology distinction. The claim "other gods are nothings" (Ps 96:5) is BOTH a rhetorical strategy AND a truth claim. The two aren't incompatible. Smith's point is that the rhetorical function is PRIMARY and the ontological claim is SECONDARY — which I think is right.

## Chapter 8 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Monotheistic statements rare, late, rhetorical | HIGH | Dating of specific texts contested |
| 2: Prior context was robust polytheism | HIGH | Degree of polytheism debated |

**Strongest section:** Section 1 (Defining Monotheism). The two-category typology, the exclusion of the Shema and First Commandment, and the dating survey establish the methodological rigor that the rest of the chapter builds on.

**Weakest section:** Section 4 (National God and Royal Ideology). Informative but overlong — the detailed discussion of Isaiah 9:5 and Psalm 45:7, while interesting, doesn't advance the central argument as much as Sections 1, 2, and 5.

**Central thesis of the book, stated here:** "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms." This is the sentence the entire book exists to defend. Everything before it (Parts I-II) describes the polytheistic system that monotheistic rhetoric emerged within. Everything after it (Ch 9-10) shows how that rhetoric was deployed in specific genres and contexts.

**Cross-reference:** The lineage erosion argument (Claim 3) connects back to Ch 4's Afterword — this is where Smith cashes the check he wrote at the end of Part I. The exclusion of the Shema from monotheism connects to the Introduction's claim that "monotheism" is a modern category the ancients wouldn't recognize. The royal theology discussion (Claim 2) provides the baseline against which Ch 9's priestly, wisdom, and apocalyptic theologies are measured. Römer's *The Invention of God* makes very similar arguments about the exilic emergence of monotheism.
