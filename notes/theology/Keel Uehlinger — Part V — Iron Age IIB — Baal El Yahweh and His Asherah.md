# Keel & Uehlinger — Part V: Iron Age IIB

## Baal, El, Yahweh, and "His Asherah" in the Context of Egyptian Solar and Royal Imagery

The longest and most consequential chapter in the book. Iron Age IIB (ca. 925-720 BCE) is the period of the divided monarchy, the Omride dynasty, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, and the beginning of Assyrian pressure. It is the period in which the question of Yahweh's relationship to Asherah is both most intensely debated and — according to Keel & Uehlinger — most widely misunderstood. Their conclusion: the iconographic and epigraphic evidence converges on "his asherah" as a cultic symbol (stylized tree), not a goddess consort. The dominant religious development of Iron Age IIB is not polytheistic goddess worship but the *solarization* of Yahwism through Egyptian-influenced royal iconography.

**This chapter is the iconographic evidence that Smith, Dever, and Römer argue about without having systematically examined.**

---

## Claim 1: Anthropomorphic goddess imagery is extremely rare in Iron Age IIB Israel and Judah — and completely absent from Hebrew glyptic art
→ [[claim-goddess-imagery-minimal-iib-absent-hebrew-glyptic-seals]] | **keel-ggi-5.1** | Confidence: high
  Of 8,500 catalogued seals, not a single Hebrew name seal depicts a goddess. The chronological distinction between IIB (minimal goddess) and IIC (pillar figurines) is devastating to Dever's continuity argument. Supports: Iron I goddess disappearance, his-asherah-as-cultic-symbol (5.5), JPF revival-not-continuity (6.3).

---

## Claim 2: The Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi paintings are paratactic — individual motifs by different artists, NOT a unified mythological scene
→ [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-pithoi-paratactic-motifs-not-unified-scene]] | **keel-ggi-5.2** | Confidence: very-high
  P. Beck (1982) identified three different artists with different styles, colors, and skill levels. Inscriptions and paintings are spatially correlated but compositionally unrelated. Every unified-scene interpretation collapses. Supports: Bes-not-Yahweh (5.3), lyre-player-human (5.4).

---

## Claim 3: The Bes figures are apotropaic daemons, NOT Yahweh and Asherah
→ [[claim-bes-figures-apotropaic-daemons-not-yahweh-asherah]] | **keel-ggi-5.3** | Confidence: very-high
  The Bes = Yahweh identification was always a stretch. Bes features are diagnostically leonine, not bovine. The inscription runs across them coincidentally. Supports: his-asherah-as-cultic-symbol (5.5). Challenged by: Lewis's cautious admission that Bes figures are "possible" Yahweh candidates.

---

## Claim 4: The lyre player is a human woman, not a goddess — Dever's "enthroned Asherah" is based on weak parallels
→ [[claim-lyre-player-human-woman-not-goddess-dever-asherah-enthroned]] | **keel-ggi-5.4** | Confidence: high
  No "goddess with lyre" iconographic type exists in the entire ANE. The chair is a Phoenician courtly armchair, not a cherub throne. The visual case for a goddess at Kuntillet Ajrud collapses. Supports: his-asherah-as-cultic-symbol (5.5). Contradicts: Dever (will edge when extracted).

## Claim 5: "His asherah" (*'šrth*) is a cultic symbol (stylized tree), NOT the goddess Asherah — the grammatical, biblical, and iconographic evidence converges
→ [[claim-his-asherah-cultic-symbol-stylized-tree-not-goddess-consort]] | **keel-ggi-5.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  THE central claim. Hebrew never uses possessive suffixes with proper names. Every biblical description treats asherah as a manufactured object. The stylized tree flanked by caprids on Pithos A (motifs E-H) is the visual correlate. Singular verbs in blessing formulas: Yahweh alone is the active agent. Supports: Smith's asherah-as-symbol. Contradicts: Dever, Römer (will edge when extracted). Depends on: goddess-minimal-in-IIB (5.1), goddess-to-attribute-substitution (4.3).

---

## Claim 6: The dominant religious iconography of Iron Age IIB is SOLAR, not Baal-oriented — and solar imagery was absorbed into Yahwism
→ [[claim-solar-imagery-dominant-iib-absorbed-yahwism-egyptian-royal]] | **keel-ggi-5.6** | Confidence: high
  The sun child in lotus is the most common anthropomorphic deity depiction. A Judean official (*'byw 'bd 'zyw*) paired his Yahwistic name with solar imagery — a deliberate religious statement. Supports: Smith's solar-language-royal-development, no-distinctive-Yahweh-iconography (Ch X).

---

## Claim 7: The bull images at Bethel and Dan were Yahwistic, not Baalistic — and the aniconic turn begins in Iron Age IIA-IIB with the substitution of attribute animals for anthropomorphic deities
→ [[claim-bull-images-bethel-dan-yahwistic-not-baalistic-aniconic-substitution]] | **keel-ggi-5.7** | Confidence: high
  Jeroboam's formula uses Exodus language — Yahwistic identification. The attribute-animal substitution provides an internal developmental mechanism for Israelite aniconism that does not depend on exilic innovation. Supports: Day's Jeroboam-bulls-as-Yahwistic, Kaufmann's golden-calf-fetishism.

---

## Claim 8: Baal, not Asherah, was the primary rival to Yahweh in Iron Age IIB — the goddess question has been overemphasized
→ [[claim-baal-primary-rival-yahweh-iib-not-asherah-goddess-overemphasized]] | **keel-ggi-5.8** | Confidence: medium-high
  Hosea never names Asherah; repeatedly names Baal. NW Semitic inscriptions never pair Baal and Asherah — they appear together only in Deuteronomistic redaction. The real religious competition was about which male god would be supreme. Supports: Baal-Yahweh-coexisted-in-Judges, challenges Kaufmann's no-genuine-syncretism.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Iron Age IIB

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Goddess imagery minimal in IIB; absent from Hebrew seals | HIGH | New finds of goddess-heavy Hebrew seals from controlled excavations |
| 2. Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi are paratactic, not unified scenes | VERY HIGH | Nobody seriously challenges Beck's analysis |
| 3. Bes figures are apotropaic daemons, not Yahweh & Asherah | VERY HIGH | Discovery that Bes = Yahweh in some previously unknown context |
| 4. Lyre player is human, not a goddess | HIGH | Discovery of a "goddess with lyre" iconographic type |
| 5. "His asherah" is cultic symbol, not goddess consort | MEDIUM-HIGH | New Ugaritic/Hebrew evidence for divine name + possessive suffix |
| 6. Solar imagery is dominant and absorbed into Yahwism | HIGH | Evidence that solar motifs are just decorative fashion |
| 7. Bull images are Yahwistic; aniconic turn begins in IIA-IIB | MEDIUM-HIGH | Better dating showing anthropomorphic deities persist longer |
| 8. Baal is primary rival, not a goddess | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence of widespread goddess worship that somehow evaded the seal record |

**Strongest section:** Claims 1-5 together form a devastating cumulative case. The grammatical argument (no possessive suffix on proper names) + the iconographic argument (asherah = stylized tree) + the epigraphic argument (singular verbs, Yahweh alone blesses) + the chronological argument (pillar figurines are IIC, not IIB) → the "consort" reading is wrong. This is the systematic, evidence-based refutation that Smith's position always needed and never had.

**Weakest section:** Claim 8 (Baal as primary rival). The evidence is solid but the framing feels reactive — a pendulum swing against the goddess-obsession of recent scholarship. The question isn't "Baal OR Asherah" but "what was the shape of Israelite polytheism?" And Keel & Uehlinger's own evidence shows it was hierarchical: Yahweh at the top, with subordinated mediating entities (asherah, cherubs, Bes figures, solar powers) below him.

**Most important finding for the project:** The asherah as cultic symbol, not goddess. This is the iconographic evidence that lets us adjudicate the Smith vs. Dever debate. Smith was right about the grammar. Dever was right that the archaeological evidence matters — but wrong about what it shows for Iron Age IIB specifically.

## Chapter-Level Assessment

**Overall quality of argument:** HIGH. This is the book's central chapter and Keel & Uehlinger bring their full methodological arsenal to bear. The integration of iconographic, epigraphic, biblical, and grammatical evidence is exactly what the field needs and rarely gets. The restraint — refusing to identify uncertain figures, acknowledging the limits of the evidence — is methodologically exemplary.

**What shifts for the project:**
- **Smith vindicated on the grammatical point.** His reading of *'šrth* as "his asherah" (cult symbol) rather than "his Asherah" (goddess) is supported by the iconographic evidence he didn't use.
- **Dever partially vindicated, partially corrected.** The archaeological evidence IS primary and the textual scholars ARE missing half the puzzle — but Dever's specific interpretation of that evidence (Asherah as goddess consort) is wrong for Iron Age IIB. His pillar figurine evidence belongs to Iron Age IIC.
- **Solar religion is the missing piece in the Asherah debate.** Both Smith and Dever focus on the Asherah question. Keel & Uehlinger show that the real religious development of Iron Age IIB was solarization — the absorption of Egyptian solar theology into Yahwism through royal ideology.
- **The aniconic turn begins earlier than most scholars think.** The substitution of attribute animals for anthropomorphic deities in Iron Age IIA-B provides a developmental mechanism for Israelite aniconism that doesn't depend on exilic theological innovation.
