# Keel & Uehlinger — Part V: Iron Age IIB

## Baal, El, Yahweh, and "His Asherah" in the Context of Egyptian Solar and Royal Imagery

The longest and most consequential chapter in the book. Iron Age IIB (ca. 925-720 BCE) is the period of the divided monarchy, the Omride dynasty, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, and the beginning of Assyrian pressure. It is the period in which the question of Yahweh's relationship to Asherah is both most intensely debated and — according to Keel & Uehlinger — most widely misunderstood. Their conclusion: the iconographic and epigraphic evidence converges on "his asherah" as a cultic symbol (stylized tree), not a goddess consort. The dominant religious development of Iron Age IIB is not polytheistic goddess worship but the *solarization* of Yahwism through Egyptian-influenced royal iconography.

**This chapter is the iconographic evidence that Smith, Dever, and Römer argue about without having systematically examined.**

---

## Claim 1: Anthropomorphic goddess imagery is extremely rare in Iron Age IIB Israel and Judah — and completely absent from Hebrew glyptic art

**Authors' claim:** "Contrary to what is often thought to be the case, terra-cottas from Iron Age IIB with images of a 'naked goddess' have not been found 'by the hundreds,' but are relatively rare by comparison with the so-called pillar figurines that appear in the following periods... In the glyptic art of Iron Age IIB from Israel and Judah, there is not a single seal known to us on which a goddess might have been depicted anthropomorphically. The absence of the goddess from Hebrew name seals is especially significant."

This is the finding that should reframe the entire Asherah debate. During the very period when the "Yahweh and his Asherah" inscriptions were written (ca. 850-735 BCE), the material evidence for goddess worship in Israel and Judah is minimal.

**Evidence presented:**
- Only 2-3 specimens of female deity depictions in Israel/Judah during Iron Age IIB: an ivory ointment spoon from Hazor Str. VI showing a goddess transparently (palm tree on front, face with doves on back), and an ivory "woman at the window" plaque from Samaria
- The "Naked Goddess" plaques (with accentuated genitalia and breasts) are found in Philistine territory (Ashdod, Tel Batash/Timnah), NOT in Israel or Judah — "they seem not to have been accepted into the repertoire in Israel or Judah"
- The Samaria plaque with a "naked goddess" dates to the very end of Iron Age IIB (destroyed 701), right at the transition to IIC
- "Not a single seal" from Israel/Judah glyptic art depicts a goddess anthropomorphically
- Hebrew name seals show zero goddesses — one possible exception (illus. 331) is probably an imported piece with a Hebrew inscription added later
- The pillar figurines (Judean Pillar Figurines) "came into Palestine in great numbers only toward the end of the eighth century" — i.e., Iron Age IIC, not IIB

**Confidence:** HIGH. The authors have catalogued 8,500 seals. The absence of goddess imagery from Hebrew name seals is a statistically significant finding — these were personal items chosen by their owners to represent their religious identity. If goddess worship was widespread, it should show up on personal seals.

**What's at stake:** The entire "Yahweh had a consort" thesis depends on the assumption that Asherah was widely worshiped as a goddess in monarchic Israel. If the period's own iconography shows minimal goddess representation, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions must mean something other than what Dever and Römer claim. This is the iconographic evidence Smith needed but didn't have.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1990, 2005) argues that the pillar figurines ARE Asherah and represent widespread goddess worship. But Keel & Uehlinger's crucial point is that pillar figurines belong to Iron Age IIC (7th century), not IIB (9th-8th centuries) — the period of the inscriptions. Dever conflates the periods.

**Alternative reading:** The "absence of evidence" could be evidence of aniconism specifically for seals, not for religion in general. Seals are elite objects; goddess worship could have been a non-elite phenomenon.

**My assessment:** The chronological distinction between IIB and IIC figurines is devastating to Dever's argument. He uses 7th-century pillar figurines as evidence for 8th-century goddess worship. Keel & Uehlinger's catalogue-based dating is far more precise. The near-total absence of goddess imagery from Hebrew name seals during Iron Age IIB is extremely hard to reconcile with the "Yahweh had a consort" thesis.

## Claim 2: The Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi paintings are paratactic — individual motifs by different artists, NOT a unified mythological scene

**Authors' claim:** "Even a quick glance at illuss. 219-221 shows that the various paintings on each side of the jars do not form a coherent composition, but are rather made up largely of a set of motifs that are positioned paratactically either next to one another or above one another."

The famous pithoi from Kuntillet Ajrud — which scholars have used to argue that Yahweh and Asherah were depicted together — are not a single coherent scene. Different artists painted different motifs at different times. The inscription "Yahweh... and his asherah" runs across a Bes figure, but inscription and painting are unrelated — they happened to be on the same jar.

**Evidence presented:**
- Three different artists identified by P. Beck (1982): different brush styles, colors (red on Pithos A, red+black on Pithos B), and skill levels
- "A significant time period must have elapsed between the time when [some motifs] were painted... and when [others were] later added to the vessel. During this time period, some of the older drawings would have faded"
- The motifs include: chariot horses, boar, lioness, suckling cow with calf, caprids at lotus tree, Bes figures, female lyre player, archer, five male worshipers
- The Egyptian solar/astral motifs that dominate Israelite glyptic art are "completely absent from both the pithoi and the wall paintings at Kuntillet Ajrud" (Beck 1982, 44)
- The artists were likely northern Syrian or Phoenician, not Israelite

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. Beck's analysis is art-historical and has not been seriously challenged. The different hands are visible in the published photographs.

**What's at stake:** Every interpretation that reads the pithoi as a unified theological statement ("Here is Yahweh and his Asherah depicted together!") collapses if the images are random, accumulated over time, and unrelated to the inscriptions written across them.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984), Margalit (1990), Coogan (1987a) — all of whom treat the inscriptions and paintings as related. Margalit even describes the paintings as a "graphic commentary on the word *'šrh*."

**Alternative reading:** Even if the paintings were done by different artists at different times, the inscriptions were intentionally placed on a vessel that was used in a Yahwistic cultic context — the vessel itself might have been chosen because its imagery was relevant to the religious message.

**My assessment:** Beck's analysis is rigorous and the paratactic nature of the paintings is hard to dispute. The fact that Egyptian solar motifs — the dominant iconographic language of Iron Age IIB Israel — are completely absent from Kuntillet Ajrud suggests this was a provincial outpost, not a major cult center. The pithoi are fascinating but they're not a Rosetta Stone for Israelite religion. They're the doodles of travelers at a caravanserai in the desert.

## Claim 3: The Bes figures are apotropaic daemons, NOT Yahweh and Asherah

**Authors' claim:** "An identification of the two Bes images... with 'Yahweh... and his Asherah' is impossible. The iconography and inscriptional evidence known thus far offer no evidence for a connection between Yahweh and Bes, though this has often assumed, especially not for Yahweh being depicted in the shape of a Bes figure."

The two frontal figures on Pithos A (labeled M and N) — the ones the "Yahweh and his Asherah" inscription runs across — are Bes-type Egyptian dwarf deities. Grotesque lion faces, protruding ears, arms akimbo, crooked legs, tail. They are apotropaic protective daemons, not Israelite gods.

**Evidence presented:**
- Full iconographic analysis: head ornamentation (blossom/feather crown), grotesque grimacing face, protruding ears, beard or collar, arms akimbo, crooked legs, tail — all standard Bes features
- Gilula (1979) and Margalit (1990) tried to interpret the heads as bovine (connecting to the Bethel bull cult), but this "cannot be correct" — the features are clearly leonine, not bovine
- No evidence for any connection between Bes and Asherah in the entire iconographic record — a few "very hypothetical references" at most
- The solar motifs that accompany Bes in other contexts (Egyptian ntr '3, "Great God") are absent from Kuntillet Ajrud — further evidence these are generic protective daemons, not solarized high gods
- The dots on their bodies are decorative (probably indicating clothing), not apotropaic wedjat-eyes

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The iconographic identification of Bes figures is among the most secure in ancient Near Eastern art history. The features are diagnostic.

**What's at stake:** The Bes = Yahweh identification has been one of the most sensational claims in the field — "the only depiction of Yahweh ever found!" If the Bes figures are just protective daemons that happen to share a jar surface with a Yahwistic inscription, the sensationalism evaporates.

**Who disagrees:** Gilula (1979 — bovine interpretation), Meshel (1979 — partly agrees with bovine), Coogan (1987a — "Yahweh and his Asherah"), McCarter (1987), Margalit (1990) who called it a "graphic commentary." Korpel (1990) identified Bes as Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** Bes figures do sometimes appear in connection with major deities in Egyptian and Phoenician art. Their presence at a Yahwistic site could indicate they were integrated into Yahwistic protective iconography, even if they aren't Yahweh himself.

**My assessment:** The Bes = Yahweh claim was always a stretch, driven by the coincidence of the inscription's placement. Keel & Uehlinger's systematic iconographic analysis demolishes it. The Bes figures simply happened to be on the jar before the inscription was added — a completely mundane archaeological reality that got turned into a theological bombshell by scholars who didn't separate image from text.

## Claim 4: The lyre player is a human woman, not a goddess — Dever's "enthroned Asherah" is based on weak parallels

**Authors' claim:** "We have no clear evidence that ever shows a goddess playing a lyre... it is only with a measured degree of confidence that one can say that this is more likely a human figure rather than a divine one."

Dever (1984) identified the seated lyre player on Pithos A as the goddess Asherah enthroned on a cherub throne. Keel & Uehlinger systematically dismantle this: the figure has small but clear breasts (so female), but the chair is a courtly armchair (not a cherub throne), the garment length doesn't match enthroned goddess conventions, and no known iconographic type shows a goddess playing a lyre.

**Evidence presented:**
- The figure has "two small, but clearly drawn, breasts" — favoring female identification (contra Hadley who argues male)
- Dever's parallels are three Ugaritic LB statuettes/pendants of enthroned goddesses — but none holds a lyre, they're from a different region and 500+ years earlier
- The chair has a slightly angled back, no footrest, grill-work panelling — this is Gubel's Type II-g Phoenician courtly armchair, not a cherub throne
- "Lion's feet are not enough to make this a cherub throne since they are included on all kinds of furniture during the first millennium"
- A Phoenician scarab from Acre shows the same chair type — with a woman seated on it, not necessarily a goddess
- A scaraboid from Nippur shows a lyre player on a similar chair, probably playing for the moon god — a human musician, not a deity
- No iconographic type of "Enthroned Goddess with a Lyre" exists in the entire ancient Near Eastern record

**Confidence:** HIGH. The art-historical analysis is precise and the parallels are better than Dever's. The absence of a "goddess with lyre" iconographic type is particularly damning.

**What's at stake:** If the lyre player is human, then neither of the two humanoid figures near the inscription (the Bes figures and the lyre player) can be identified as a goddess. The entire visual case for a goddess at Kuntillet Ajrud collapses — there is no goddess in the paintings.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984, 1990), Hadley (1987b, 1989 — argues the figure could be male), some feminist scholars who want the lyre player to be a goddess.

**Alternative reading:** Even if not a goddess, a female musician playing in a cultic context near Yahwistic inscriptions is still significant for understanding women's roles in Iron Age religion. The lyre player doesn't need to be a goddess to be important.

**My assessment:** Dever's identification was always the weakest part of his Kuntillet Ajrud analysis. He wanted to find Asherah in the paintings so badly that he overlooked basic art-historical conventions. Keel & Uehlinger's restraint is methodologically superior: when you can't identify a figure definitively, say so. The lyre player is probably human. She's interesting for what she tells us about musical performance at the site — not for goddess worship.

## Claim 5: "His asherah" (*'šrth*) is a cultic symbol (stylized tree), NOT the goddess Asherah — the grammatical, biblical, and iconographic evidence converges

**Authors' claim:** "The controversial reference to *'šrth* in the inscription from Kuntillet Ajrud thus most likely refers to such a cultic object... 'Yahweh's asherah' does not have equal rank with Yahweh but is rather a mediating entity that brings his blessing and is conceived in the mind in the shape of a stylized tree that was thus subordinate to Yahweh."

This is the central claim of the entire book. Keel & Uehlinger analyze the Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions and conclude, based on grammar, biblical usage, and iconography, that "his asherah" refers to a wooden cult object — a stylized tree symbol — not the goddess Asherah. The asherah is a *medium* of Yahweh's blessing, not his consort.

**Evidence presented:**

*Grammatical evidence:*
- Hebrew never uses a possessive suffix with a proper name. You can't say "his Asherah" any more than you can say "his Mary." The suffix *-h* on *'šrth* forces "asherah" to be a common noun, not a personal name
- In all biblical passages where "asherah" might refer to a goddess, it has the article (*hā'ăšērâ* or *lā'ăšērâ*) — double determination (article + proper name) is impossible in Hebrew
- The Ugaritic parallel 'nth (Anat + possessive suffix) is sometimes cited as a counterexample, but it's a single debated text (KTU 1:43.13) and doesn't establish a pattern

*Biblical evidence:*
- Every biblical description of an asherah treats it as a manufactured object: made of wood, "made" or "set up," "planted" (Deut 16:21), "torn out," "chopped down," "burned"
- The biblical texts never describe the asherah's appearance — they treat it as a known artifact, not a personal deity
- The deuteronomistic texts that polemicize against "the asherah" are centuries later than Kuntillet Ajrud and refer to Jerusalem, not Samaria/Teman

*Iconographic evidence:*
- The asherah is consistently depicted as a stylized tree in the iconographic record
- Pithos A at Kuntillet Ajrud shows a stylized tree flanked by caprids, above a lion (motifs E-H, the largest and most prominent composition on the jar) — this is the visual correlate of "his asherah"
- Seal images (illuss. 222, 230, 233a) and a pithos from Lachish (illus. 223) show the same stylized tree motif in Judean contexts, confirming the asherah was known in this form in Judah as well
- The connection between goddess and stylized tree in the history of traditions supports the identification — but the tree has become a *symbol* that *replaces* the anthropomorphic goddess

*The blessing formulas:*
- The blessing formula in the pithos inscriptions says "I bless you by/to Yahweh... and his asherah" — but the concluding wish for blessing in both inscriptions uses SINGULAR verbs: "may HE bless you and keep you" — Yahweh alone is the active agent
- A votive inscription on a stone bowl from the same room mentions only Yahweh: "Blessed is/be he of/to Yahweh" — no asherah
- The Khirbet el-Qom inscription: "Blessed is Uriyahu by Yahweh... by his asherah, he has saved him" — singular verb, Yahweh is the active agent, the asherah is the *medium through which* salvation occurs

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The grammatical argument is strong but not universally accepted (some Ugaritic counterexamples exist). The iconographic identification of asherah = stylized tree is very strong. The singular-verb pattern in the blessing formulas is a genuinely important observation that other scholars have overlooked.

**What's at stake:** This is THE debate. If Keel & Uehlinger are right, Smith's position (asherah = cult symbol) is vindicated by the iconographic evidence, and Dever/Römer's position (Asherah = goddess consort) is wrong. The entire "Yahweh had a wife" narrative collapses — or at minimum, must be radically reframed as "Yahweh had a cultic symbol that mediated his blessing."

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984, 1990, 2005), Römer (2015), Day (1986, 2000), Coogan (1987a), M. Weippert (1990), Olyan (1988), Zevit (1984), and most scholars in the "Asherah as consort" camp. The majority of the field reads the evidence differently.

**Alternative reading:** The grammatical argument, while elegant, may be too rigid. Languages have exceptions. The singular verb in the blessing formula could simply be default singular agreement with the nearest subject. The Ugaritic material does show divine names with possessive suffixes in at least a few cases. The "consort" reading remains plausible even if Keel & Uehlinger have shifted the burden of proof.

**My assessment:** After reading Keel & Uehlinger's complete analysis alongside Dever's and Smith's arguments, I find Keel & Uehlinger MORE convincing than Dever but with an important caveat. Their reading accounts for ALL the evidence — grammatical, biblical, iconographic, and epigraphic — while Dever's reading requires dismissing the grammatical problem and treating the iconographic evidence selectively. *However*, Keel & Uehlinger may be underplaying the experiential reality: even if the asherah was "officially" a cultic symbol mediating Yahweh's blessing, ordinary Israelites may have experienced it as a goddess. The distinction between "mediating entity" and "deity" may have been blurrier on the ground than it is in scholarly analysis. The truth is probably: the asherah was a cultic symbol that *functioned* as a goddess for many worshipers, even if the official theology framed it as subordinate to Yahweh.

## Claim 6: The dominant religious iconography of Iron Age IIB is SOLAR, not Baal-oriented — and solar imagery was absorbed into Yahwism

**Authors' claim:** "The strong influence that came from Egypt must not be underestimated, producing a pronounced tendency toward the celestialization and solarization of divine powers."

This is the finding that connects directly to Smith's convergence thesis and Cross's Divine Warrior solar imagery. The dominant religious iconography of Iron Age IIB in Israel/Judah is not Baal, not Asherah, but *Egyptian solar motifs* — and these were integrated into official Yahwism through royal ideology.

**Evidence presented:**
- The sun god as a child in a lotus blossom "is seen most frequently" on the Samaria ivories — the most common anthropomorphic deity depiction
- The motif appears on Hebrew name seals: a seal belonging to a certain *'yw bn ywqm*, another belonging to *'byw 'bd 'zyw* — possibly an official of the Judean king Azariah/Uzziah (ca. 773-735). The owner's title *'bd* ("servant, minister") confirms high status
- On these seals, the sun child holds one hand to his chest and raises the other "in the gesture of greeting or blessing" — sometimes with cow horns and solar disk (Hathor/Isis attributes adapted for solar theology)
- Winged protective powers (falcons, uraei, sphinxes/cherubs) are the most characteristic motifs of the period, combining "unapproachable distance and effective protection" — the celestial aspect of the divine
- Falcon-headed sphinxes with solar disks, winged uraei flanking royal cartouches, and the falcon with spread wings (royal symbolism derived from Egyptian New Kingdom ideology)
- Gender "plays no special role and opposition between male and female deities is not an issue" — solarization absorbs both male and female divine figures into a unified solar sphere
- Osiris-Re union iconography (Djed pillar flanked by Isis and Nephthys with solar disks) — the integration of solar and chthonic divine aspects

**Confidence:** HIGH. The evidence is overwhelming and based on the largest catalogue of Iron Age seals ever assembled. The solar motifs are quantitatively dominant, not just occasional.

**What's at stake:** This directly supports Smith's argument in *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (Ch 8) that solar imagery was absorbed into Yahwism during the monarchy. It also connects to Cross's Divine Warrior as a solar figure and to the "Yahweh as sun god" traditions detectable in the Psalms (e.g., Psalm 84:11: "Yahweh is a sun and shield"). The solarization of Yahweh was NOT a late or fringe phenomenon — it was the *mainstream* religious development of Iron Age IIB.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who minimize Egyptian influence on Israelite religion. Also those who see Baal as the primary influence on Yahwistic imagery. Keel & Uehlinger's data shows Baal imagery exists (the "four-winged Baal") but is quantitatively secondary to solar imagery.

**Alternative reading:** The solar motifs on seals may reflect fashion rather than theology — people bought seals with Egyptian-looking designs because they were prestigious, not because they were solar worshipers. The name seals with solar motifs could simply be using conventional decorative patterns.

**My assessment:** The prestige argument works for anepigraphic seals but fails for name seals. A Judean official who commissioned a seal with his Yahwistic name (*'byw* = "Yahweh is my father") chose to pair that name with solar imagery. That's a deliberate religious statement, not just fashion. The solarization of Yahweh in Iron Age IIB is one of the most important and underappreciated findings in the study of Israelite religion. Smith intuited it from texts; Keel & Uehlinger prove it from artifacts.

## Claim 7: The bull images at Bethel and Dan were Yahwistic, not Baalistic — and the aniconic turn begins in Iron Age IIB with the substitution of attribute animals for anthropomorphic deities

**Authors' claim:** "According to the Old Testament, worship of Yahweh that included the use of one or more bull calf images (*'ēgel*, *'ăgālîm*) was the officially established state religion in the Northern Kingdom."

The bull images that the biblical texts polemicize against (1 Kings 12; Hosea) were not foreign Baal worship — they were Yahwistic. The bull was Yahweh's attribute animal, just as it had been El's in Ugarit. The biblical polemic is not against worship of the wrong god but against worship of the right god through the wrong image.

**Evidence presented:**
- 1 Kings 12:28 — Jeroboam's formula: "Here are your gods/God, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt" uses the same language as the golden calf episode (Exodus 32), indicating Yahwistic identification
- The bull as divine pedestal/attribute animal has deep roots in Syrian/Canaanite iconography — El is called "Bull El" (*tr 'il*) at Ugarit
- The general trend in Iron Age IIA → IIB: "anthropomorphic deities recede and are replaced by their attribute animals and entities" — the aniconic turn begins here, not in the exile
- "Lord of the Ostriches" and "Lord of the Caprids" type figures decline; the animals alone appear, without the anthropomorphic deity
- This substitution of attribute animal for deity is a key mechanism in the development of aniconism — the animal becomes the symbol that stands for the god

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Yahwistic identification of the bull images. MEDIUM for the aniconic trajectory claim — it's a pattern but the timing is debated.

**What's at stake:** The aniconic turn in Israelite religion is usually dated to the exile or later. Keel & Uehlinger's evidence pushes it back to Iron Age IIA-IIB — centuries earlier. If anthropomorphic deity images were already being replaced by attribute animals in the 10th-8th centuries, the later "image ban" has deep cultural roots, not just theological innovation. This connects to Römer's argument about the missing statue of Yahweh (Ch 8) — perhaps there was no statue because the aniconic turn had already shifted representation from anthropomorphic to symbolic.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the aniconic turn late (exilic/post-exilic). Also those who interpret the bull images as evidence of Baal worship rather than Yahwism.

**Alternative reading:** The bull images could represent a syncretistic Yahweh-Baal fusion, not pure Yahwism. The biblical polemic against them might be justified in seeing them as Canaanizing corruption.

**My assessment:** The Yahwistic identification of the Bethel/Dan bulls is well-supported. The attribute-animal substitution pattern is genuinely interesting and provides an *internal* developmental mechanism for Israelite aniconism — it wasn't imposed from outside (no "Moses brought monotheism/aniconism from Midian") but grew organically from within Canaanite/Syrian iconographic conventions. This is a much more plausible historical reconstruction than the revolutionary model.

## Claim 8: Baal, not Asherah, was the primary rival to Yahweh in Iron Age IIB — the goddess question has been overemphasized

**Authors' claim:** "Scholarly discussion concerning the religion of preexilic Israel and Judah has been dominated in recent years... by the question about whether Yahweh had a female partner. The discussion has not given adequate consideration to the fact that the dominant aspects of a symbol system show up in different geographic regions and on different media."

Hosea's polemic against Baal is "reiterated repeatedly" (Hos 2:10, 15, 18f.; 9:10; 11:2; 13:1), but "there is no explicit polemic against a female deity" — only possible "veiled allusions" (Hos 4:18; 14:9). The iconographic evidence matches this: Baal-type warrior imagery is prominent; goddess imagery is minimal. The scholarly obsession with Asherah has distorted the picture of what Iron Age IIB religion was actually about.

**Evidence presented:**
- Hosea explicitly names Baal repeatedly; never names Asherah or any goddess
- The four-winged warrior deity (illus. 210) from Hazor is a Baal-type figure — divine warrior imagery is prevalent
- In Northwest Semitic inscriptions, "Baal and Asherah are never paired" — they appear together only in deuteronomistic redaction of biblical texts
- The real religious competition in Iron Age IIB was between Yahweh and Baal(shamem), and secondarily between Yahweh and El — NOT between Yahweh and a goddess
- "The issue concerning 'Yahweh's asherah' in Samaria and Teman in the early eighth century is independent of the question about a cultic image in seventh-century Jerusalem"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The Baal-as-primary-rival thesis is well-supported by the textual and iconographic evidence. The "goddess obsession" critique of recent scholarship is pointed but justified.

**What's at stake:** If Baal was the main rival, not Asherah, then the entire "Yahweh and his wife" framework that has dominated popular and scholarly discourse (Dever's *Did God Have a Wife?*, Stavrakopoulou's *God: An Anatomy*) may be answering the wrong question. The real religious conflict in monarchic Israel was not about monotheism vs. polytheism or male god vs. goddess — it was about *which male god* would be supreme.

**Who disagrees:** Dever (whose entire popular book is built around the "wife" frame), Stavrakopoulou, and feminist scholars who emphasize goddess suppression. Also Römer, who argues for Asherah as consort.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of explicit anti-goddess polemic in Hosea could mean goddess worship was so uncontroversial it didn't need to be attacked — not that it didn't exist. And the "veiled allusions" might be more significant than Keel & Uehlinger allow.

**My assessment:** Keel & Uehlinger have a point that the Asherah question has sucked all the oxygen out of the room. But their own evidence shows the asherah WAS significant — it's the *only* mediating entity mentioned in inscriptions alongside Yahweh. It's just that its significance was as a cultic symbol, not as a goddess consort. The "Baal vs. Yahweh" frame and the "asherah as symbol" frame are complementary, not contradictory.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Iron Age IIB

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Goddess imagery minimal in IIB; absent from Hebrew seals | HIGH | New finds of goddess-heavy Hebrew seals from controlled excavations |
| 2. Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi are paratactic, not unified scenes | VERY HIGH | Nobody seriously challenges Beck's analysis |
| 3. Bes figures are apotropaic daemons, not Yahweh & Asherah | VERY HIGH | Discovery that Bes = Yahweh in some previously unknown context |
| 4. Lyre player is human, not a goddess | HIGH | Discovery of a "goddess with lyre" iconographic type |
| 5. "His asherah" is cultic symbol, not goddess consort | MEDIUM-HIGH | New Ugaritic/Hebrew evidence for divine name + possessive suffix |
| 6. Solar imagery is dominant and absorbed into Yahwism | HIGH | Evidence that solar motifs are just decorative fashion |
| 7. Bull images are Yahwistic; aniconic turn begins in IIA-IIB | MEDIUM-HIGH | Better dating showing anthropomorphic deities persist longer |
| 8. Baal is primary rival, not a goddess | MEDIUM-HIGH | Evidence of widespread goddess worship that somehow evaded the seal record |

**Strongest section:** Claims 1-5 together form a devastating cumulative case. The grammatical argument (no possessive suffix on proper names) + the iconographic argument (asherah = stylized tree) + the epigraphic argument (singular verbs, Yahweh alone blesses) + the chronological argument (pillar figurines are IIC, not IIB) → the "consort" reading is wrong. This is the systematic, evidence-based refutation that Smith's position always needed and never had.

**Weakest section:** Claim 8 (Baal as primary rival). The evidence is solid but the framing feels reactive — a pendulum swing against the goddess-obsession of recent scholarship. The question isn't "Baal OR Asherah" but "what was the shape of Israelite polytheism?" And Keel & Uehlinger's own evidence shows it was hierarchical: Yahweh at the top, with subordinated mediating entities (asherah, cherubs, Bes figures, solar powers) below him. That IS polytheism — just not the kind with a divine couple.

**Most important finding for the project:** The asherah as cultic symbol, not goddess. This is the iconographic evidence that lets us adjudicate the Smith vs. Dever debate. Smith was right about the grammar. Dever was right that the archaeological evidence matters — but wrong about what it shows for Iron Age IIB specifically. The pillar figurines ARE genuine evidence of a female divine presence in Judahite religion — but they belong to the 7th century (Iron Age IIC), not the period of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions.

## Chapter-Level Assessment

**Overall quality of argument:** HIGH. This is the book's central chapter and Keel & Uehlinger bring their full methodological arsenal to bear. The integration of iconographic, epigraphic, biblical, and grammatical evidence is exactly what the field needs and rarely gets. The restraint — refusing to identify uncertain figures, acknowledging the limits of the evidence — is methodologically exemplary.

**What shifts for the project:**
- **Smith vindicated on the grammatical point.** His reading of *'šrth* as "his asherah" (cult symbol) rather than "his Asherah" (goddess) is supported by the iconographic evidence he didn't use.
- **Dever partially vindicated, partially corrected.** The archaeological evidence IS primary and the textual scholars ARE missing half the puzzle — but Dever's specific interpretation of that evidence (Asherah as goddess consort) is wrong for Iron Age IIB. His pillar figurine evidence belongs to Iron Age IIC.
- **Solar religion is the missing piece in the Asherah debate.** Both Smith and Dever focus on the Asherah question. Keel & Uehlinger show that the real religious development of Iron Age IIB was solarization — the absorption of Egyptian solar theology into Yahwism through royal ideology. This is the convergence that Smith describes but doesn't ground in material evidence.
- **The aniconic turn begins earlier than most scholars think.** The substitution of attribute animals for anthropomorphic deities in Iron Age IIA-B provides a developmental mechanism for Israelite aniconism that doesn't depend on exilic theological innovation.
