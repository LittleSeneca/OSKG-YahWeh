# Römer, The Invention of God — Chapters 4-5

---

## Chapter 4: How Did Yhwh Become the God of Israel?

### Claim: Israel originally belonged to El, not Yhwh. Yhwh became Israel's god through a political process tied to the emergence of the monarchy.

**Römer's claim:** The name "Israel" contains the theophoric element *'ēl*, not *yāhû*. "Israel" most likely means "May El reign" or "May El impose himself as master." The group named "Israel" originally venerated El as its chief god. Yhwh was a later addition — a warrior god imported from the south who replaced El as the group's patron deity, probably during the period of Saul and David.

**Evidence presented — the name "Israel":**

- The etymology is contested. Three candidates:
  1. From *ś-r-h* ("combat"): "May El combat" — the biblical folk etymology (Gen 32:29, Hos 12:4). But this root is poorly attested in Semitic languages.
  2. From *y-š-r* ("to be just"): "El is just" — supported by the poetic name "Yeshurun" for Israel (Deut 32:15, 33:5, 26) and a Ugaritic name *y-š-r-'i-l* on a military list from the late 13th century (contemporary with Merneptah). But the vocalization speaks against this.
  3. From *ś-r-r* ("rule, govern, reign"): **"May El reign"** — Römer's preferred reading. Supported by the Masoretic revision at Hosea 12:5, which Römer argues was altered to avoid the original meaning "El imposed himself and gained the upper hand." Names like *Śĕrāyāhû* ("Yhwh reigns") show the same root with Yhwh later.

> **The key point:** Whatever the original etymology, the name "Israel" is an EL name — not a Yhwh name. "If Yhwh had been the original god, the group would be named something like *Yiśrâ-yāhû*" (paraphrasing Smith, whom Römer follows here).

**Evidence — the Merneptah Stele (c. 1210-1205):**

- The first clearly attested mention of "Israel" outside the Bible
- 3.18m granite stele celebrating Merneptah's Levantine campaign
- Key line: "Israel is destroyed, its seed is no longer"
- Israel is written with the determinative for an **ethnic group** (man + woman with three strokes for plural) — NOT a city or territory
- Israel appears alongside Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yenoam — located in the mountains of Ephraim
- "The mention of the name 'Israel' on this stele in no way presupposes an 'exodus' or any kind of emigration of this group from the land of Egypt"
- A possible EARLIER mention on a Berlin pedestal (Ramses II era) is very uncertain — the reading is speculative

> **What this tells us:** By 1200 BCE, a group called "Israel" existed in the central highlands of Canaan. They were significant enough for Pharaoh to boast about destroying them. But there's no indication they worshipped Yhwh at this point. The name suggests El worship. Yhwh came later.

**Evidence — the transition from El to Yhwh:**

- The Bible preserves the memory of this transition in the different names for God in different sources
- In Genesis, the patriarchs worship El under various titles: El Shadday, El Elyon, El Olam, El Bethel
- Exodus 6:2-3 explicitly says: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yhwh I did not make myself known to them"
- Römer reads this as an honest textual memory: the patriarchs worshipped El; Yhwh was introduced later
- The biblical editors harmonized this by claiming El and Yhwh were the same god all along — but the seams show

**The political mechanism:** Römer argues Yhwh became Israel's god through the monarchy. Samuel (an El name: "El is exalted" or "His name is El") is the transitional figure — a prophet of El at Shiloh who anoints Saul as the first king. Saul's name contains no theophoric element (it means "he who is asked for"). David's rise brings Yhwh fully into prominence. The Davidic dynasty adopted Yhwh as its patron and used his warrior profile to legitimate military expansion.

**Confidence:** HIGH that Israel originally venerated El (the name alone is strong evidence, and Exodus 6:2-3 is explicit). MEDIUM that the transition to Yhwh happened through the monarchy — plausible but impossible to prove with the limited pre-monarchic evidence.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the same claim as Smith's Chapter 1, but Römer tells it as a political story rather than a text-critical analysis. "Israel" wasn't originally Yhwh's people. Yhwh was adopted — by kings, for political reasons. The covenant between God and Israel is a royal construction, not an eternal relationship.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue Yhwh was Israel's god from the beginning. Heiser's divine council model. Conservatives who read Exodus 6:2-3 as progressive revelation. See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]].

## Chapter 5: The Entrance of Yhwh into Jerusalem

### Claim: Yhwh entered Jerusalem through David's political genius, not through divine revelation

**Römer's claim:** Jerusalem was originally an El/ Elyon city before Yhwh arrived. David made Jerusalem his capital and brought Yhwh there. The transfer of the ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) is the narrative expression of this political-theological move. Yhwh was a northern/shrine god before David made him the god of Jerusalem.

**Evidence presented:**

**Shiloh — Yhwh's first home in Israel:**
- Shiloh was the first major Yahwistic sanctuary, located in the territory of Ephraim (northern hill country)
- Archaeological confirmation: Shiloh was an important cult site in the 12th-11th centuries, destroyed by fire c. 1050 (probably by Philistines)
- The Bible preserves "strong historical memories" of Shiloh as Yhwh's home — Jeremiah 7 and 26 recall its destruction
- Samuel was a prophet at Shiloh, and he anointed Saul — the Shiloh sanctuary was the bridge between the pre-monarchic El-worship of "Israel" and the royal Yhwh-worship of the monarchy

**Saul and David — the political transition:**
- No direct extra-biblical attestation of Saul, David, or Solomon (except the Tel Dan stele, 8th century, which mentions "house of David" — confirming the dynasty existed, but telling us nothing about David himself)
- "The three kings Saul, David, and Solomon have been constructed by the editors of the Bible as paradigmatic types": Saul (rejected king → northern kingdom), David (warrior/chosen → dynasty), Solomon (builder/sage)
- BUT: "There are several traits in the narrative that cannot be pure invention." Kingdoms DID emerge in the Levant around 1000 BCE. The Philistine threat was real.
- David was originally a Philistine vassal (the biblical texts "try to give an apologetic interpretation of this fact")

**Jerusalem — the El city that became Yhwh's city:**
- Jerusalem was originally a Canaanite/Jebusite city, not an Israelite foundation
- Its original deity was probably El Elyon ("God Most High") — Genesis 14:18-20 has Melchizedek, king of Salem (Jerusalem), as "priest of El Elyon"
- David conquered Jerusalem and made it his capital — this was a political masterstroke: Jerusalem belonged to no tribe, so it could be "David's city" without favoring any tribal faction
- David brought the ark of Yhwh to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) — this was a theological masterstroke: Yhwh, the northern war god, was now installed in the old El city
- Psalm 110 (a royal psalm): "Yhwh said to my lord [the king]: 'Sit at my right hand'" — the king sits enthroned alongside Yhwh in the El city

**The two-stage synthesis:**
1. Yhwh was adopted as the patron of the Davidic dynasty (Chapter 4)
2. Yhwh was installed in Jerusalem, the old El/Elyon city (Chapter 5)

Yhwh now ruled where El had ruled. The identification of El and Yhwh — which Smith traced through textual analysis — was accomplished politically through David's unification of northern Yhwh worship with Jerusalem's El traditions.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Shiloh archaeology is solid. The Tel Dan stele confirms a Davidic dynasty existed by the 8th century. The Jerusalem-as-El-city argument is plausible but depends heavily on later biblical texts (Genesis 14, Psalm 110) that may retroject later theology. David's actual role is historically murky.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Römer is right, the God of Jerusalem — the God of the Temple, the God of the Psalms — was introduced there by a political warlord who needed a unifying deity for his new capital. The "city of God" was originally someone else's city with someone else's god. David's genius wasn't theological — it was political. He knew how to merge cults to build a kingdom.

## Cross-Cutting: Römer's Narrative Arc Through Chapter 5

1. Yhwh originates in the southern deserts (Ch. 2)
2. Yhwh moves north through Midianite/Kenite intermediaries (Ch. 3)
3. Yhwh is adopted by the group "Israel" — originally an El-worshipping people (Ch. 4)
4. Yhwh is installed in Jerusalem by David — the political masterstroke that unified northern Yhwh worship with southern El traditions (Ch. 5)

We are now at the threshold of the monarchy. Chapters 6-7 will cover Yhwh's cult in Israel and Judah. The narrative arc is remarkably clean — cleaner than Smith's more cautious approach. Römer is telling you a story. Smith was building you a case. Both are working from the same evidence.
