# Sommer, The Bodies of God — Chapters 1-3

---

## Chapter 1: Fluidity in Mesopotamia and Canaan

### Claim: The ancient Near East had a "fluidity model" — gods had multiple bodies and overlapping selves

**Sommer's claim:** "A single deity could exist simultaneously in several bodies. Further, a deity could have a fragmented or ill-defined self, for gods were not fully distinct from each other, at least not all of the time." This fluidity model was present in Mesopotamia and Canaan but ABSENT from classical Greece. This means the key distinction is not monotheism vs. polytheism — it's fluid vs. non-fluid divinity.

**Key examples:**

1. **Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh:** Appear as distinct goddesses in Assyrian treaties, are addressed as plural ("their"), receive separate cult — yet are "functionally... one and the same." Assurbanipal's hymn calls them "the Lady of Nineveh, the mother who bore me" and "the Lady of Arbela, my creator" — two mothers, both named Ishtar, distinct yet one.

2. **Marduk:** Planetary Marduk and temple Marduk were distinct manifestations. The *Enuma Elish* names Marduk with fifty names — each representing a different aspect or identity, absorbed from other gods.

3. **Baal:** At Ugarit, Baal of Saphon, Baal of Ugarit, and Baal of Lebanon were distinct local manifestations. The god Hadad was identified with Baal — distinct names, same god, but also somehow distinct.

**What "fluidity" means:**
- **Fragmentation:** Multiple local manifestations of one deity with some degree of independence
- **Overlap:** Deities can temporarily or permanently overlap with each other
- **Multiple embodiment:** A single god can be present in multiple cult statues simultaneously

**The Greek contrast:** "Greek culture provides no evidence that multiple objects could contain the presence of a particular deity at any one moment." The Greek gods had discrete bodies and discrete selves — like humans. The ANE gods were fundamentally different from humans in this respect.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the ANE evidence (texts are unambiguous). HIGH on Greek contrast (Sommer documents this).

## Chapter 2: The Fluidity Model in Ancient Israel

### Claim: J and E sources preserve the fluidity model — Yhwh had multiple bodies and a fluid self

**Sommer's claim:** The fluidity model is found in the J and E sources of the Pentateuch. In these texts, Yhwh has multiple local manifestations ("Yhwh of Samaria," "Yhwh of Teman," "Yhwh of Hebron"), and his *mal'akh* (angel/messenger) is not a separate being but a small-scale manifestation of Yhwh himself — something like an avatar.

**Evidence — Three types of fluidity in J and E:**

**1. Geographic fragmentation:**
- [[../../sources/primary-sources/kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions|Kuntillet Ajrud]]: "Yhwh of Samaria" and "Yhwh of Teman" — distinct local manifestations
- 2 Samuel 15:7: "Yhwh of Hebron" — Absalom had to go to Hebron to worship THIS Yhwh; the Jerusalem Yhwh wouldn't work. "The prince claims that he made a vow not simply to Yhwh nor to Yhwh of Jerusalem but to Yhwh of Hebron, and thus he had to leave Jerusalem to fulfill it."
- Psalm 99:2: "Yhwh at Zion"
- These recall the Mesopotamian pattern of local Ishtars and Baals

**2. The *mal'akh* as avatar:**
- In J and E texts, the *mal'akh* (translated "angel" but better "avatar") is not a messenger sent by Yhwh — it IS Yhwh in a small-scale manifestation
- Genesis 18: Yhwh appears as three men. Abraham addresses one. The narrator identifies one as Yhwh. The remaining two leave while Yhwh stays. Three bodies, one God.
- Genesis 21:17-21: "God heard the voice of the boy, and the *mal'akh* of God called to Hagar from heaven." The *mal'akh* speaks in first person as God: "I will make of him a great nation." Same being, fluid boundary.
- Exodus 3: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appears in the burning bush — then "Yhwh saw that he turned to look, and God called to him." The *mal'akh* IS Yhwh.
- Judges 6: The *mal'akh* and Yhwh alternate speaking from the same mouth

**3. Fluid overlap between Yhwh and other beings:**
- The "man" who wrestles with Jacob is identified as God (Genesis 32)
- The heavenly court where Yhwh consults with divine beings who participate in his decisions

**Sommer's key interpretive move:** When the *mal'akh* speaks as God, it's not because it's a messenger relaying God's words. It's because the *mal'akh* IS God — a fragment or avatar of God. "The distinction between the messenger and God is murky."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the textual evidence (the J and E texts are clear). MEDIUM-HIGH on Sommer's "avatar" interpretation (others read *mal'akh* as a separate messenger being — Sommer's reading is plausible but not the only possible one).

## Chapter 3: The Rejection of the Fluidity Model

### Claim: P and D sources systematically rejected the fluidity model

**Sommer's claim:** The Priestly (P) and Deuteronomistic (D) sources rejected divine fluidity. For P and D, God is transcendent and accessed only through the *kavod* (Glory) or *shem* (Name). The *kavod* and *shem* are not avatars — they are hypostases that allow God to act at a distance without being locally present. This rejection produced the "noncorporeal" God of later Judaism.

**Key evidence:**

**P's theology:**
- Genesis 1:26: Humans made in God's "image" (*ṣelem*) and "likeness" (*dĕmût*) — but this god never walks, eats, or appears in human form in P texts
- The *kavod* (Glory) is a blindingly bright fire/cloud that represents God's presence without BEING God's body
- Exodus 25-40: The Tabernacle houses the *kavod*, not God's body — a crucial distinction
- God in P is "stunningly bright, so that it had to be surrounded by dark clouds"

**D's theology:**
- God "dwells" in heaven; only his *shem* (Name) dwells in the Temple
- Deuteronomy 12:5: The place where Yhwh "causes his Name to dwell" — not where Yhwh dwells
- "The Deuteronomic editors wished to make a subtle distinction that the older texts lack: It is not God who dwells in the temple; only God's name does."

**The historical arc:**
- J and E (early): God has multiple bodies, fluid selfhood — the ANE pattern
- D (7th century): God is in heaven; only his Name is on earth
- P (exilic/post-exilic): God is transcendent; only his Glory appears
- Later Judaism: God has NO body at all

**The irony:** What later Judaism and Christianity consider "true monotheism" — a transcendent, noncorporeal God — is actually the LATER development that rejected the more ANCIENT Israelite conception. "The God of the philosophers is not the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the textual evidence of P and D's different theologies. HIGH that they represent a rejection of fluidity.

## Chapters 1-3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| ANE had fluidity model | HIGH |
| Greece lacked fluidity model | HIGH |
| J/E preserve fluidity in Israel | HIGH |
| *Mal'akh* as avatar in J/E | MEDIUM-HIGH |
| P/D reject fluidity | HIGH |
| P's *kavod* and D's *shem* are substitute hypostases | HIGH |

**The crucial implication:** Smith, Römer, and Dever trace a polytheism → monotheism evolution. Sommer shows that WITHIN monotheism, there was a FLUIDITY → ANTI-FLUIDITY evolution. J and E were monotheistic (one God) but fluid (multiple bodies). P and D were monotheistic and anti-fluid (transcendent God). The evolution wasn't polytheism to monotheism — it was fluid monotheism to non-fluid monotheism. This reframes the entire debate.
