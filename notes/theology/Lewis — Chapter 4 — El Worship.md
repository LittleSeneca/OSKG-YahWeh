# Lewis — Chapter 4: El Worship

Lewis argues that El was the original god of Israel — his name appears in "Israel" itself, and the patriarchal narratives preserve genuinely archaic El traditions even though redacted through later Yahwistic hands. The chapter surveys El in the Late Bronze Age Levant (Amarna, Ugarit, Hittite Elkunirsha), argues against the "El disappears in the Iron Age" thesis, examines each biblical El deity (El Elyon, El Roi, El Olam, El Bethel, El Shadday), and sketches a composite portrait of El as a family/clan deity — father, kinsman, benevolent protector, head of the divine council — distinct from the national, combative Yahweh.

---

## Claim 1: El was the original god of Israel — his name in "Israel" and the absence of Yahweh in early names demonstrate the priority of El worship over Yahweh worship

**Lewis's claim:** "The logical place to begin looking at ancient Israelite divinity is with El, the deity whose name is found in the designation of the eponymous ancestor 'Israel.' ... It is often argued that the absence of Yahweh in this name, together with the name of the Shechemite deity 'El, the god of (the patriarch) Israel' (Gen 33:20) and the absence of Yahweh (and Baal) in anthroponyms and toponyms in early literature, indicates the antiquity of El worship over Yahweh worship."

**Evidence:** "Israel" most likely means "El reigns supreme" or "El contends." Genesis 33:20 refers to _ʾēl ʾĕlōhê yiśrāʾēl_ — "El, the god of Israel" — which Lewis says can't be a redundant "god, the god of Israel" given the parallel with the common formula "YHWH ʾĕlōhê yiśrāʾēl." Exodus 6:3 (P) explicitly states Yahweh revealed himself to the patriarchs as El Shadday, not as Yahweh. Van der Toorn (1996b) notes the presence of El anthroponyms and toponyms before Yahweh ones.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that El worship preceded Yahweh worship in Israel. This is near-consensus in critical scholarship. The name "Israel" with its El theophoric element is the single most compelling piece of evidence.

**What's at stake:** The entire narrative of Israelite religion. If El was first, Yahweh is a latecomer who absorbed El's attributes. This inverts the biblical narrative (where Yahweh is the eternal God who revealed himself to the patriarchs) and supports a historical reconstruction where Yahweh originated separately and was later identified with El.

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars who hold to Mosaic Yahwism from the beginning. Kaufmann would reject this outright. More moderately, some scholars argue that Yahweh and El were always the same deity under different names.

**Alternative reading:** The name "Israel" could theoretically contain a generic "el" (god) rather than the proper name El. Lewis himself acknowledges the appellative/proper name ambiguity. But the cumulative case (Shechemite El, patriarchal El deities, Exodus 6:3, onomastics) makes the proper-name reading much stronger.

**My assessment:** This is Lewis's foundational claim and it's well-supported. The question is not whether El was original — it's how early Yahweh arrived and how the merger happened. That's Chapter 6's territory.

## Claim 2: The Amarna letters show no preeminence for El in Late Bronze Age Canaan — he was one deity among many, and the letters are too limited to reconstruct religious devotion

**Lewis's claim:** "There is no indication of any superiority of the deity El from this catalogue of names... the majority of those living in Canaan at this time would beg to differ, as they looked to deities other than El for personal protection... To be fair, we should not weigh the Amarna letters very heavily. They are straightforward correspondence of a fairly stereotypical nature... they have precious little comment on religion in general."

**Evidence:** Hess's catalogue shows a pluralistic environment: Egyptian (Amon, Ptah, Re, Seth), Mesopotamian (Anu, Assur, Ninurta), Kassite, Hurrian (Hebat, Tessub), Indo-Aryan, and West Semitic (Anat, Asherah, Astart, Baal, El, Milku, Yamm) deities appear in personal names. El names like Ildayyi ("El is my sufficiency") and Ilumilku ("El is king") exist alongside many non-El names. The name Jacob-el appears in toponym lists of Thutmose III, Ramses II, and Ramses III — tantalizing but too meager for reconstruction.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the Amarna evidence. HIGH that El wasn't preeminent in Late Bronze Canaan based on available sources. MEDIUM on whether this accurately reflects reality — the Amarna letters are diplomatic correspondence, not religious texts. Abence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

**What's at stake:** Whether El's prominence in Israel was a local development or inherited from broader Canaanite culture. If El wasn't dominant in Late Bronze Canaan, then Israelite El worship is a distinctive development, not a simple inheritance.

**Who disagrees:** Cross argued for an older, more widespread El cult based on Amorite onomastics and Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions. But Lewis shows those readings can't be sustained (see Claim 3).

**My assessment:** Lewis is appropriately cautious. The Amarna letters are not the right source for reconstructing Canaanite pantheon hierarchy. The real comparator is Ugarit, where ʾIlu is clearly head of the pantheon.

## Claim 3: Ugarit provides the fullest representation of El/ʾIlu in the Late Bronze Age — and the picture is of a fatherly, wise, benevolent, creative head of the pantheon who is NOT deposed by Baal

**Lewis's claim:** "The Late Bronze Age kingdom of Ugarit... has the fullest representation of the deity ʾIlu (= El) both in word and object of any ancient Near Eastern society." Critically: "Both El and Baal are called 'king' though in complementary ways: El remains the executive order of the universe and Baal is the sustainer of the cosmos. El is not deposed in an active struggle with Baal; nor is El degraded or demoted by Baal." ʾIlu is "father" (_ʾab_) to gods and humans, "Bull ʾIlu" (virility symbol), "the father of years" (_ʾab šnm_) indicating eternity, and _lṭpn ʾil d pʾid_ — "the Gracious One, the Benevolent God."

**Evidence:** Lewis walks through the full Ugaritic portrait: ʾIlu presides over the divine council; dispenses wise decrees (KTU 1.4.4.41-43); creates the gods Dawn and Dusk through sexual intercourse (KTU 1.23 — with explicit phallic imagery); magically creates an exorcist from clay (KTU 1.16.5.25-28); presides over the _marzeaḥ_ drinking banquet (KTU 1.114, where he drinks to excess and collapses in his own excrement); appears in dreams to Kirta; heals the dying king. He is NOT a _deus otiosus_ — the ribald texts prove vitality, not impotence. The deity lists consistently place ʾIlu in prominent position.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the Ugaritic evidence. HIGH that El was not deposed by Baal. This is now the consensus position — Mullen (1980), Smith, and Pitard have all argued against the older Kapelrud/Oldenburg conflict model.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite El was a diminished figure waiting to be replaced. The Ugaritic evidence shows El as fully functional, active, and supreme — just not the primary warrior. This means the Israelite merger of El and Yahweh didn't happen because El was weak; it happened because the functions were complementary.

**Who disagrees:** Van der Toorn (1992a) argued El became a _deus otiosus_ by the first millennium. Oldenburg (1969) saw a conflict between El and Baal. Lewis explicitly refutes both.

**My assessment:** Lewis is correct and his detailed treatment of KTU 1.23 (the "Dawn and Dusk" text) and KTU 1.114 (the drinking binge) is a highlight — he uses texts that seem to show El as weak or ridiculous to argue the opposite. El's very human frailties (drunkenness, lust) are signs of his vitality, not his decline.

## Claim 4: El did NOT disappear in the Iron Age — the onomastic and inscriptional evidence shows ongoing El worship across the Levant

**Lewis's claim:** "When placed alongside the El worship attested in the biblical corpus... and the presence of bull iconography in the archaeological record... one concludes along with Alberte Naccache (1996: 255) that 'our perception of a change [in El's status] might simply be due to the randomness of the available data, and that the Canaanite/'Phoenician' and Aramaic conception of El's nature had remained basically the same.'"

**Evidence:** El is regularly positioned second (after Hadad) in the 8th C BCE Sam'alian pantheon (KAI 214, 215). Lipiński: "The name ʾIl is the commonest theophorous element in the Aramaic personal names of the first millennium." Aufrecht argues El was the chief deity of the Ammonites, not Milcom. Albertz and Schmitt: "all selected Levantine onomasticons show a considerable portion of El-names" and "El was still regarded as a high god, but his activity was seen to be particularly focused on the protection of individuals."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The evidence is epigraphic and onomastic, not speculative. El persisted.

**What's at stake:** Whether the biblical patriarchal traditions preserve genuine memory or are late fictions. If El worship persisted into the Iron Age, the patriarchal El deities are more likely to be remembered traditions, not invented archaisms.

**Who disagrees:** Niehr (1990), Rendtorff, and van der Toorn have argued for El's decline. But Lewis's accumulation of epigraphic data makes this position increasingly untenable.

**My assessment:** This is an important correction to older scholarship. The persistence of El worship means the patriarchal narratives are not pure retrojection — they preserve genuine memories of pre-Yahwistic El devotion, even if heavily redacted.

## Claim 5: Deuteronomy 32:8-9, in its original form (LXX/Qumran), shows El Elyon as superior to Yahweh — who is one of the divine sons, not the high god

**Lewis's claim:** "According to the reconstruction, which restores Deuteronomy 32:8 based on the Qumran readings from 4QDeutj and the Septuagint, (El) Elyon as god most high demonstrated his supremacy by establishing national boundaries and assigning land and people to various divine beings... Yahweh starts out in the telling of this story as one of the divine sons (bĕnê ʾĕlōhîm), and yet he ends up preeminent, ruling over Elyon's people and bearing the creator's epithets."

**Evidence:** The MT reads "sons of Israel" (bĕnê yiśrāʾēl) — clearly a theological correction. 4QDeutj and LXX read "sons of God" (bĕnê ʾĕlōhîm) or "angels of God." El Elyon apportions the nations to divine beings; Yahweh receives Jacob/Israel as his portion. Lewis notes: "The poem celebrates how Israel is especially fortunate because not only were they (El) Elyon's own special people, but they were assigned to Yahweh, who is presented as the divine heir apparent." Psalm 82 tells a similar story: Yahweh condemns the unjust gods in "the council of El" (ʿădat ʾēl), and the "sons of Elyon" are stripped of immortality (Ps 82:7-8).

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the textual reconstruction (4QDeutj/Qumran evidence is decisive). HIGH on the theological interpretation — Yahweh begins as subordinate to El Elyon.

**What's at stake:** This is the smoking gun for El's original supremacy. Even the Bible itself — when the textual corrections are peeled back — preserves a tradition where Yahweh is not the Most High. Heiser reads this differently (as Yahweh = Elyon, distributing nations to the divine council), but the text as reconstructed doesn't support that reading.

**Who disagrees:** Heiser argues that Elyon IS Yahweh and the "sons of God" are created divine beings receiving administrative assignments, not independent deities. The MT's "sons of Israel" reflects an authentic tradition. The confessional approach harmonizes El and Yahweh from the start.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the Qumran/LXX reading is textually superior, the poem presents a *stage* in tradition, not necessarily historical reality. It's a theological narrative, not a historical report.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right on the text. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 is the single most important biblical passage for establishing El's original supremacy over Yahweh. Combined with Psalm 82, the pattern is clear and cannot be explained away as mere literary convention. This is the passage that Cross, Smith, and now Lewis all build their cases on.

## Claim 6: El Shadday is genuinely archaic, not a late Priestly invention — extra-biblical attestations (Ugarit, Deir ʿAlla) plus archaic biblical poetry prove its antiquity

**Lewis's claim:** "The extra-biblical attestations of Shadday together with these three biblical passages make it unwise to view P's and Job's use of Shadday as fictitious creations from a late (exilic/post-exilic) period." He then quotes Cross (1998): "The Pentateuchal tradents... were more successful in their reconstructions of early Israel than we critical historians have supposed."

**Evidence:** (1) Ugaritic KTU 1.108 attests a deity called _šd_/Shadday in Late Bronze Syria. (2) Cross and Knauf argue the name appears in a Late Bronze Egyptian name: _ś3-d-ï-ʿ-m-y_ = "Shadday is my paternal relative." (3) The 8th C BCE Deir ʿAlla texts mention the Shaddayyin (_šdyn_) meeting in divine council. (4) Three biblical passages seem archaic: Genesis 49:24-25 (El//Shadday with womb/breast imagery), Numbers 24:16 (El//Elyon//Shadday triplet), and Numbers 1:5-15 (list of names with El and Shadday theophorics but NO Yahweh or Baal names). Cross: "these lists actually reflect characteristic formations of the onomasticon of the second millennium."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Shadday is genuinely archaic. The convergence of Ugaritic, Egyptian, Deir ʿAlla, and biblical evidence is strong. MEDIUM on the Num 1:5-15 list — it could be genuinely archaic OR brilliant P archaizing.

**What's at stake:** Whether P (Exod 6:3: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday") preserves genuine historical memory or is a late theological construct. If Shadday is archaic, P is a reliable tradent, not just an inventor.

**Who disagrees:** Minimalist historians see P as a Persian/Hellenistic period creation with no access to genuine archaic traditions. The names in Num 1:5-15 would be deliberate archaisms.

**My assessment:** Lewis makes a strong case. The Deir ʿAlla Shaddayyin alone are enough to prove Shadday is not a Priestly invention — they're 8th C BCE and independent of the biblical tradition. The question of whether Shadday was associated with mountains, steppe, or destruction is genuinely unresolved (Lewis refuses to pick among the three etymologies), but the antiquity of the name is solid.

## Claim 7: Israelite El was a family/clan deity, not a national deity — his profile is fundamentally different from Yahweh's

**Lewis's claim:** "Israelite El is not portrayed as a national deity whose worship is entwined with the support of a royal hierarchy... El is not a combat deity who slays cosmic creatures... Nor is he a storm deity... His origin is never said to have been from the lands south/southeast of the Dead Sea... El narratives do not exhibit the intolerance that comes to characterize later Yahwism."

**Evidence:** El's functions cluster around family and clan: father (_ʾāb_), divine kinsman (Ammi-Shadday = "Shadday is my kinsman"), benevolent protector (Jacob-el = "May El protect"), provider of children (Genesis 49:25 — "blessings of breasts and womb"), shepherd (Genesis 48:15, 49:24), rock/refuge (ʾElî-ṣûr, Ṣûrî-ʾēl). Sacred space associated with El is small-scale: altars, wells, springs, standing stones, trees — not grand temples. Dreams and visions are El's mode of revelation (contrast Baal's storm theophany). El is associated with the divine council, wisdom, and eternal decrees.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the contrast. MEDIUM-HIGH on the full picture — some of the more specific claims (e.g., that El had no connection to war at all) may overstate the contrast, as Lewis himself acknowledges El's protective warrior side.

**What's at stake:** Whether the El/Yahweh merger was a fusion of equals or an absorption of a clan deity by a national one. Lewis's portrait implies the latter — Yahweh brought the warfare, the nation-building, the intolerance, and the centralized cult; El brought the family devotion, the benevolence, the wisdom, and the fatherhood.

**Who disagrees:** Albertz would agree on the family/national distinction. Cross would emphasize continuity more than Lewis does. Heiser would collapse the distinction entirely (El = Yahweh from the start).

**Alternative reading:** The family/national distinction may be more about the sources that happen to survive than about actual religious practice. El worshippers may have had richer, more "national" forms of devotion that simply aren't preserved. The patriarchal narratives give us El through a particular lens.

**My assessment:** Lewis's portrait of El as family deity is compelling and aligns with the Albertzian turn toward family religion. The most important insight is the negative one: El was NOT a storm god, NOT associated with the southern wilderness, NOT intolerant. All of those features belong to Yahweh's original profile, not El's. This means Yahweh brought something genuinely new to Israelite religion — not just a new name for the same god.

## Claim 8: El may have been the original god of the Exodus — but the evidence is fragmentary and the case is "tantalizing" rather than decisive

**Lewis's claim:** "What should we make of such tantalizing evidence? Its fragmentary character recommends caution. The fusion of earlier El traditions with those of Yahweh makes it likely that El's divine warrior motifs are, if one may use a linguistic term, back-formations."

**Evidence:** Numbers 23:22 and 24:8 read "El brought him out of Egypt" (_ʾēl môṣîʾām mimmiṣrāyîm_) with bull imagery suggesting invincibility in battle. Mettinger, Toews, Levine (the "El repertoire"), and Wyatt have all argued for El as the original Exodus deity. Habakkuk 3:3 mentions "Eloah marching forth from Teman" in a militaristic passage. But Lewis notes the counter-arguments: Exodus 32 (golden calf) requires textual emendation to read "El" for "these"; Psalm 106:21 is in a thoroughly Yahwistic context; Numbers 23-24 already contains Yahweh alongside El.

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The evidence exists but is too fragmentary to build a strong case on. Lewis is appropriately cautious.

**What's at stake:** Whether the Exodus was originally an El tradition later appropriated by Yahweh. If so, the foundational event of Israelite religion belongs to El, not Yahweh. This would be a major revision. If not, Yahweh brought the Exodus with him when he arrived from the south.

**Who disagrees:** Most scholars see the Exodus as a Yahweh tradition. Smith (2001b) sees Yahweh's original profile as a warrior-storm god from the south — exactly the kind of deity who would lead a military liberation. Römer likewise places Yahweh's origin in the southern wilderness.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis's suggestion that El's warrior motifs are "back-formations" — created retroactively when El and Yahweh were identified — is the most parsimonious explanation. If El had strong warrior traditions, he would have become the national deity.

**My assessment:** Lewis is right to be cautious. The El-Exodus connection is a minority position and the evidence is thin. Lewis's more important contribution is explaining WHY it's unlikely: if El was already the warrior-liberator god, what need was there for Yahweh? The sociological explanation (family deity → national deity, with different functional profiles) makes more sense.

## Chapter 4 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| El was original god of Israel | VERY HIGH | Near-consensus; strongest evidence is name "Israel" itself |
| Amarna shows no El preeminence | HIGH | Source limitation — diplomatic letters, not religious texts |
| Ugaritic ʾIlu is fullest El portrait; not deposed | VERY HIGH | Consensus position now |
| El persisted in Iron Age | VERY HIGH | Epigraphic/onomastic data is strong |
| Deut 32:8-9 shows El > Yahweh | VERY HIGH | Qumran evidence decisive on text; interpretation debatable |
| El Shadday is genuinely archaic | HIGH | Extra-biblical Shadday + archaic poetry |
| El was family deity, not national deity | HIGH | Contrast with Yahweh is well-documented |
| El as original Exodus god | LOW-MEDIUM | Fragmentary evidence; back-formation more likely |

**Strongest sections:** The treatment of Ugaritic ʾIlu (rich, detailed, corrects older misinterpretations) and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (clear textual argument with Qumran support). The El Shadday discussion, while unresolved on etymology, convincingly establishes antiquity.

**Weakest section:** The El-as-Exodus-god argument (lines 1459-1483). Lewis presents it because it's in the literature, but he clearly doesn't believe it. The section reads as dutiful coverage rather than committed argument.

**What this chapter establishes for the rest of the book:**
- El and Yahweh were originally distinct deities with different functional profiles
- El was the original god of Israel (name, patriarchal traditions)
- Yahweh arrived later from the south (to be explored in Ch 6)
- The merger happened through identification, not conflict — El wasn't defeated, he was absorbed
- The patriarchal narratives, while redacted, preserve genuine memory of pre-Yahwistic El worship

**Cross-references:** This chapter is in direct conversation with Cross's Part I ("The Religion of Canaan and the God of Israel"), which makes many of the same arguments. Lewis is more cautious than Cross on Proto-Sinaitic evidence (he rejects readings Cross relied on) and more detailed on Ugaritic material. The chapter should be read alongside Heiser's Part 2 ("The Households of God") for a contrasting approach that collapses El and Yahweh from the beginning. Smith's Part I of *Origins* covers similar divine council material but with more emphasis on the theogonic dimension. Dever has almost nothing on El as a distinct deity — his focus is almost entirely on Asherah and popular religion.
