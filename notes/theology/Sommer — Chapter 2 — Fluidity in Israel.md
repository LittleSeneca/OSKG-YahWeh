# Sommer — Chapter 2

## The Fluidity Model in Ancient Israel

---

## Claim 1: Yhwh had local manifestations — "Yhwh of Samaria," "Yhwh of Teman," "Yhwh of Hebron"

**Sommer's claim:** J and E texts preserve the same fluidity model found in Mesopotamia and Canaan. Yhwh had distinct local manifestations similar to the multiple Ishtars of Assyrian treaties. This is not polytheism — it's fluid monotheism. One God, many embodiments.

**Evidence — the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (8th century BCE):**
- Pithos inscriptions mentioning "Yhwh of Samaria" and "Yhwh of Teman"
- "The phrasing... recalls the references to Ishtars of Arbela and Nineveh, and to Baals of Saphon, Ugarit, and Lebanon that were discussed in Chapter 1"
- A bench inscription in the gate shrine also references "Yhwh of Teman"

**Evidence — biblical verses:**
- 2 Samuel 15:7: "Yhwh of Hebron" — "Absalom says to David, 'Please, I should like to go to fulfill the vow I made to Yhwh in Hebron.'"
- Sommer's exegesis: "The phrase 'the vow I made to Yhwh in Hebron' does not mean that Absalom was in Hebron when he made the vow; as Absalom states, at that time he was hundreds of miles north of there, in the Aramean principality Geshur... 'In Hebron' is part of the DEITY'S NAME: The prince claims that he made a vow not simply to Yhwh nor to Yhwh of Jerusalem but to Yhwh of Hebron, and thus he had to leave Jerusalem to fulfill it — for in Jerusalem one can pray to Yhwh generally or to Yhwh of Jerusalem, but not to Yhwh of Hebron!"
- Psalm 99:2: "Yhwh at Zion"

**The parallel to Mesopotamia is exact:** Just as treaties listed Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh as distinct beings with shared identity, so biblical texts recognize Yhwh of Samaria, Yhwh of Teman, Yhwh of Hebron, and Yhwh of Jerusalem as distinct-yet-one.

**A point of methodological honesty from Sommer:** "No single Hebrew text currently known refers to several of these manifestations together. Consequently, one cannot be sure that these terms refer to Yhwhs who were in some way independent of each other, but these texts at least allow one to raise such a possibility." He's careful not to claim more than the evidence allows.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the parallel to Mesopotamian practice (the phrasing is identical). MEDIUM that these Yhwhs were "independent" (Sommer acknowledges the evidence is thinner than for Ishtar).

## Claim 2: The *mal'akh* (angel/messenger) in J and E is an avatar of Yhwh, not a separate being

**Sommer's claim:** "In many passages, the word *mal'akh* (literally, 'messenger,' but usually translated 'angel') means a small-scale manifestation of God's own presence, and the distinction between the messenger and God is murky. This *mal'akh* is something very similar to an avatar in Indian religions... The *mal'akh* in these cases is not a being separate from Yhwh whom Yhwh sent on a mission; rather, it is a part of the deity that can act on its own."

**Evidence — four key J/E texts:**

**1. Genesis 18 (J text) — Yhwh as three men:**
- "Yhwh manifested Himself to Abraham amidst the trees of Mamre... He lifted up his eyes and saw three men coming toward him" (18:1-2)
- "The juxtaposition of these two sentences implies that Yhwh appears in the form of three men, or at least in the form of one of the three men"
- One visitor speaks prophetically in God's voice without being identified as God
- "In 18.13 the narrator stops being coy and simply refers to one of the visitors as Yhwh"
- Two visitors leave; one stays — "the one who remains with Abraham is now clearly identified as Yhwh (18:22)"
- Three men, one God. Yhwh can be in three bodies simultaneously.

**2. Genesis 21:17-21 — The *mal'akh* speaks as God in first person:**
- "God heard the voice of the boy, and the *mal'akh* of God called to Hagar from heaven... 'Do not fear, for God has heard the voice of the boy... I will make of him a great nation'"
- The *mal'akh* doesn't say "God will make him" — says "I will make him." Speaks as God.

**3. Exodus 3 (E text) — The *mal'akh* IS Yhwh:**
- "The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appeared to [Moses] in a flame of fire from amidst the bush... Yhwh saw that he turned to look, and God called to him from amidst the bush"
- No clear transition between *mal'akh* and Yhwh — they're the same presence

**4. Judges 6 — The *mal'akh* alternates with Yhwh from the same mouth:**
- "*Mal'akh* of Yhwh came... Yhwh turned to him and said..." The speaker alternates identity mid-conversation

**Sommer's interpretive conclusion:**
> "Alternatively, it is possible that Yhwh temporarily overlaps with some heavenly beings who do God's bidding (which suggests a model different from that of an avatar)." He remains open to multiple interpretive models — the key point is that the boundary between God and *mal'akh* is fluid, not fixed.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the textual evidence (the alternation between *mal'akh* and Yhwh is unambiguous). MEDIUM-HIGH on Sommer's "avatar" interpretation (the traditional "separate messenger" reading is possible but requires more interpretive work to explain the first-person divine speech).

## Claim 3: J and E are monotheistic — not polytheistic — but their monotheism is fluid

**Sommer's claim:** "I believe the documents preserved in the Hebrew Bible, when taken as a whole, are monotheistic." J and E do not depict Yhwh as one god among many — they depict Yhwh as the ONE God who has multiple bodies. The multiplicity is not of gods but of embodiments.

**The distinction matters:** If you read Genesis 18 as polytheistic (Yhwh + two subordinate gods), you get the Smith/Römer/Dever narrative: polytheism → monotheism. If you read it as Sommer does (one Yhwh with three bodies), you get fluid monotheism → anti-fluid monotheism. Same text, radically different theological framework.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is the most debatable claim in the chapter. It depends on how one defines "monotheism" — as belief in one God (Sommer's definition) or as belief that only one divine being EXISTS in any form (a stricter definition).

**What's at stake for faith:** Sommer is offering a way to accept the critical historical reconstruction WITHOUT concluding that Israel's religion was "primitive polytheism." The J and E texts are not polytheistic — they're a sophisticated form of monotheism that we've lost the categories to understand.

## Chapter 2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|-----------|
| Yhwh had local manifestations like ANE deities | MEDIUM-HIGH |
| *Mal'akh* as avatar in J/E | MEDIUM-HIGH |
| J/E are monotheistic (not polytheistic) | MEDIUM |
| The fluidity model explains otherwise puzzling biblical data | HIGH |

**The most important implication:** If Sommer is right, the entire "Israelite religion evolved from polytheism to monotheism" narrative needs to be reframed. Israel was monotheistic from early on — but its monotheism included divine fluidity. What evolved was not the NUMBER of gods but the NATURE of the one God's embodiment.
