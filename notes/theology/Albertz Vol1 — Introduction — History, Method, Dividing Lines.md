# Albertz — Introduction (§1.1–1.3): History of Research, Method, and Dividing Lines

Albertz's Introduction is a self-conscious manifesto for reviving the discipline of "history of Israelite religion" as an alternative to "Old Testament theology." He diagnoses the discipline's collapse after WWI (dialectical theology killed it), states seven demands for a new approach, argues that *Religionsgeschichte* is superior to OT theology as a comprehensive discipline, then lays out his signature methodological innovation: sociological stratification via "internal religious pluralism."

---

## §1.1 History of Research

### Claim 1: The "history of Israelite religion" as a discipline was crippled by dialectical theology after WWI, and subsequent attempts have been methodologically regressive

**Albertz's claim:** The discipline flourished in the 19th century (Gabler, de Wette, Vatke, Wellhausen, Smend) but was gutted by the post-WWI rise of Barthian "dialectical theology," which rejected historicism for revelation. The few post-WWII histories of religion (Ringgren 1963, Schmidt 1968/1982, Fohrer 1969) were "largely uninteresting resumes" that "mark more of a step back in method and system."

**Evidence presented:**
- Traces five intellectual sources for the historical approach: rationalism, Herder, Hegel (→ Vatke), comparative ANE research, and the history-of-religions school
- Vatke (1835) was the first to achieve a genuinely historical account, using Hegel's dialectic to break the "Mosaic" paradigm and produce a developmental scheme (pre-prophetic → prophetic → post-prophetic)
- Wellhausen stripped Vatke's philosophical superstructure and produced a genuinely dynamic interplay of political and religious history
- The Wellhausen school (Smend Sr, 1893) made the discipline dominant — it displaced OT theology entirely
- Then WWI: Eissfeldt (1926) tried to rescue it by proposing a clean methodological separation — history of religion = knowledge, OT theology = faith
- Post-WWII histories failed: Ringgren organized material dogmatically, Schmidt's "Faith of the Old Testament" was apologetic and ignored popular piety, Fohrer's "impulse" theory was history of ideas in disguise

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is a standard and well-documented narrative. Albertz's characterization of post-WWII histories as regressive is arguable but his factual genealogy (who wrote what when) is publicly verifiable.

**What's at stake:** If this narrative is correct, the revival of history-of-religions research is not merely filling a gap but recovering a lost methodological richness. The "history of religion vs. OT theology" dispute is not just academic turf warfare — it's about whether Israelite religion is understood historically (as a human, social, contested process) or theologically (as revelation to be appropriated).

**Who disagrees:** The entire OT theology guild! Von Rad, Eichrodt, Childs, and their successors would argue that history-of-religions approaches miss the theological *kerygma* — what the texts actually claim to *be* (revelation). Childs in particular argued that the canonical form, not the historical development behind it, is what the church receives as scripture.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that Albertz is fighting the last war. The collapse of *Religionsgeschichte* after WWI wasn't just theological fashion — it reflected genuine dissatisfaction with historicism's failure to say anything normative. And post-WWII "histories" weren't just regressive; they integrated Ugaritic and archaeological data that Wellhausen never had. Ringgren may have organized material dogmatically, but his synthesis with the Scandinavian Myth and Ritual school was genuinely innovative.

**My assessment:** Albertz is convincing on the diagnosis (the discipline collapsed, what followed was less rigorous) but less so on the prescription (that *his* new approach will fix it). The proof is in the execution. The question is whether his "internal religious pluralism" framework actually produces better history, or just a different set of blind spots.

---

### Claim 2: A new "history of Israelite religion" must satisfy seven demands derived from past failures

**Albertz's claim:** From his survey of the discipline's failures, Albertz derives seven requirements:

1. **Consistent historical construction** — no dogmatic principles of division or selection (contra Ringgren, Schmidt)
2. **Open process** leading to both Judaism and Christianity — no Christian devaluation of "Judaism" (contra de Wette's "Hebraism/Judaism" distinction)
3. **Not mere history of ideas** — must describe the interplay between political/social development and religious/cultic development
4. **Include social history** — consistently investigate the human *tradents* of religious traditions, their economic/social conditioning
5. **Restore the dialogue** — bring competing theological positions back into living conversation; the history of Israelite religion is "an ongoing discussion between various Israelite groupings"
6. **Comparative religion orientation** — fairly compare with ANE environment without apologetic interest; common features are as important as differences
7. **Re-evaluate the post-exilic period** — devote equal space to it; revise anti-Judaistic Christian prejudices

**Evidence presented:** Each demand is justified negatively by a past failure: Ringgren's dogmatic organization (demand 1), de Wette/Smend's devaluation of post-exilic "Judaism" (2), Vatke/Fohrer's abstraction into history of ideas (3), Wellhausen/Smend's neglect of social factors (4), the flattening of controversy into systematic categories (5), Schmidt's apologetic use of comparison (6), and the chronic under-treatment of the exilic/post-exilic period (7).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The demands are well-motivated by the history of research. But they are also clearly shaped by Albertz's own intellectual commitments (German social history, post-Auschwitz theology, ecumenical dialogue). Whether these are the *right* demands depends on whether his framework produces better results.

**What's at stake:** These seven demands are the programmatic charter for Albertz's entire two-volume project. If they're wrong or incomplete, the entire edifice is built on shaky ground.

**Who disagrees:** The OT theology camp would contest demand 1 (historical construction cannot be the *only* approach) and likely demand 7 on different grounds (the post-exilic period's lower source density makes equal treatment impossible). Evangelical/conservative scholars would contest demand 2's implication that the history opens equally to Judaism and Christianity.

**Alternative reading:** Albertz's demands, while well-intentioned, smuggle in a liberal Protestant theological agenda under the guise of methodological neutrality. Demand 2's insistence on "open process" and demand 6's rejection of "apologetic interest" are themselves theological positions.

**My assessment:** Sensible and well-argued. The demands for social history (4) and restored dialogue (5) are genuinely innovative and pay off in the rest of the book. The demand for comparative religion without apologetics (6) aligns with the best contemporary scholarship (Smith, Römer). The weakest is demand 7 — it's right in principle but the source disparity for the post-exilic period is a real constraint that "fairness" can't magic away.

## §1.2 Task, Method, and Hermeneutical Reflections

### Claim 3: The "history of Israelite religion" is the more meaningful comprehensive Old Testament discipline, superior to "Old Testament theology"

**Albertz's claim:** "I cannot disguise the fact that in the present situation I regard the history of religion as the more meaningful comprehensive Old Testament discipline." He gives seven reasons, pivoting on: it matches the historical structure of the OT, takes seriously that religious statements can't be separated from their background, doesn't force contradictory statements into abstraction, describes a dialogical process that mirrors contemporary ecumenical learning, sees continuity in the *people* of Israel (not ideas to be appropriated by Christians), openly dispenses with any claim to absoluteness, and facilitates interreligious dialogue.

**Evidence presented:**
- Traces the Eissfeldt compromise (1926): history of religion = knowledge, OT theology = faith → neither side is satisfied today
- Shows OT theology has drifted toward the historical: Eichrodt already located it within empirical historical scholarship; von Rad conceded "re-telling remains the most legitimate form"; Preuss returned to Eichrodt's view that OT theology "remains an undertaking with a historical orientation"
- Meanwhile, history of religion has become more theological: can't be purely objective post-Auschwitz, must pursue theological interests, must evaluate wrong decisions
- The two disciplines are converging; the question is which does the job better

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is a normative argument about disciplinary priorities, not a factual claim that can be verified. The force of the argument depends on shared presuppositions.

**What's at stake:** If Albertz is right, the entire OT theology enterprise is a category mistake — it tries to do systematically what can only be done historically. If he's wrong, his history of religion will lack the normative dimension that makes the OT matter to religious communities.

**Who disagrees:** The entire OT theology tradition. Childs would argue the canonical approach does what Albertz claims only history can do — and does it better, because it takes the text's own theological claims seriously. Von Rad would argue that "re-telling" is historical in form but theological in substance.

**Alternative reading:** Albertz's argument is clever because it makes history of religion *appear* more theological than the theologies. But this is a rhetorical move: he's defined "theological" in a way that favors his method (dialogical, non-absolutist, open to Judaism) and then claimed OT theology fails on those terms.

**My assessment:** This is the most contestable section of the Introduction. Albertz is right that OT theology has a structural problem (no agreed center, no agreed method). But his alternative — a history of religion that *evaluates* wrong decisions and pursues *theological interests* — reintroduces the very normative dimension he criticized OT theology for. The difference is that his norms emerge from the historical process itself rather than from church dogma. That's elegant but fragile: what if the historical process produced "wrong decisions" that Albertz evaluates differently than the tradents did? Whose evaluation wins? The historian's — which puts us back to Eissfeldt's problem.

## §1.3 Dividing Lines

### Claim 4: The history of Israelite religion must be organized not just chronologically but sociologically — distinguishing "official religion" from "personal piety" — because different social strata produced different religious symbolic worlds

**Albertz's claim:** His signature methodological innovation: at least two "foci of identity" existed in ancient Israel — the family (producing "personal piety") and the people/society (producing "official religion"). These are "two different strata of Israelite religion" distinct in "content, function and the degree of their institutionalization." A third intermediate level, the local/village community, can sometimes be distinguished. He calls this socially conditioned stratification "internal religious pluralism."

**Key distinctions:**
- **Official religion**: oriented on *political* experiences (especially liberation); ritualized and institutionalized; often dynamic and dysfunctional
- **Personal piety**: oriented on *family* experiences (especially parent-child relationship); primarily stabilizing and integrating; less institutionalized
- Within official religion, there is further conflict: priesthoods, royal house, prophetic opposition, Deuteronomistic reformers all compete for what counts as "official"

**Evidence presented:**
- References his own earlier monograph *Persönliche Frömmigkeit und offizielle Religion* (1978) where this model was developed
- Appeals to Luckmann's distinction between "invisible religion" and institutionalized religion
- Appeals to Mol's recognition of different "foci of identity" (individual, group, society) that can come into tension

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. This is a heuristic model, not an empirical finding. Its value depends entirely on whether it illuminates the data better than alternatives. Albertz's earlier monograph demonstrated its utility for Babylonian and Israelite material, but the model's sharp distinction between "official" and "personal" may oversimplify.

**What's at stake:** This is the load-bearing beam of Albertz's entire project. The book's structure (constantly toggling between official cult and family piety) depends on this distinction. If it's artificial, the book's organization misrepresents the data. If it's real, it explains a great deal about why biblical religion looks so different at different social levels.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who reject or heavily qualify the public/private distinction in ancient religion. Karel van der Toorn's *Family Religion in Babylonia, Syria and Israel* (1996) would complicate Albertz's model by showing how deeply family religion and official religion were interwoven. Ziony Zevit's *The Religions of Ancient Israel* (2001) emphasizes regional diversity more than social stratification.

**Alternative reading:** The "two levels" model may be too binary. Religious practice in ancient Israel may have existed on a spectrum with much more overlap between family, local, and state levels than Albertz allows. His model risks reifying "official" and "personal" as separate religions rather than different expressions of the same religion in different contexts.

**My assessment:** The model is productive — it generates specific predictions about what kinds of religious language and practice we should find in different settings. It aligns with common sense (people pray differently at home than in the state temple). But it also risks becoming a Procrustean bed: texts that don't fit either "official" or "personal" categories may get forced into one or the other. The proof will be in how well the model handles the actual biblical and archaeological evidence in the chapters that follow.

---

### Claim 5: Regional differentiation (North/South) is necessary alongside sociological, and Israel's religious distinctiveness lies not in individual elements but in the structural whole produced by its particular history

**Albertz's claim:** Beyond chronological and sociological divides, the pre-exilic period requires North/South differentiation (Jerusalem vs. Bethel, etc.), and the post-exilic period requires homeland/Diaspora differentiation. Moreover, "there are only a very few individual elements which are unique, and parallels to almost everything can be found in the environment... It is not the individual elements but the structure as a whole which makes the religion of Israel distinctive."

**Evidence presented:**
- North/South religious differences are "widely accepted" in scholarship
- The texts themselves have been "largely levelled out" by exilic reform groups and "polemically caricatured from the perspective of the South"
- In comparative perspective, Israel shared the ANE "religious heritage" — parallel prayers, sacrifices, kingship theology
- The "dynamic power within the social controversies" is what distinguishes Israel, not any single concept

**Confidence:** HIGH on the factual claim about regional differentiation (this is standard); MEDIUM on the "structure not elements" distinctiveness claim (harder to verify).

**What's at stake:** If Israel's distinctiveness is structural rather than elemental, then apologetic approaches that point to "unique" features (monotheism, covenant, aniconism) miss the point. Distinctiveness emerges from the *process* — the particular history of social and theological controversy. This aligns Albertz with the broader shift away from *sui generis* claims about Israel.

**Who disagrees:** Kaufmann and his school argued for Israel's radical distinctiveness at the elemental level (no theogony, no divine sex, no myth). Conservative scholars (Block, Hess) argue specific elements like Israel's transcendent territorial claim are genuinely distinctive.

**Alternative reading:** Albertz's "structure not elements" argument could be a sophisticated way of maintaining Israel's distinctiveness while conceding the comparative evidence. It's unfalsifiable in practice — if every parallel is explained away as "element" while distinctiveness is always "structural," the model can absorb any contrary evidence.

**My assessment:** This is a sensible middle position in the comparativist debate. It avoids both apologetic special pleading and flattening reductionism. But it also risks being a "just-so" story: Israel's distinctiveness is whatever can't be reduced to borrowed elements, and "structure" becomes a black box. I want to see how this plays out in the detailed historical reconstruction.

## Introduction Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Dialectical theology crippled the discipline, post-WWII histories were regressive | HIGH on facts, MEDIUM on evaluation | Counter-argument that post-WWII histories integrated new data (Ugarit, archaeology) that Wellhausen lacked |
| 2. Seven demands define the path forward | MEDIUM-HIGH | Whether these demands produce better history or just different blind spots |
| 3. History of religion is superior to OT theology | MEDIUM | This is a normative judgment disguised as methodology; depends on shared presuppositions |
| 4. "Internal religious pluralism" — official vs. personal piety | MEDIUM | Whether the binary oversimplifies; Zevit and van der Toorn complicate it |
| 5. Regional differentiation + structural (not elemental) distinctiveness | HIGH on differentiation, MEDIUM on structure claim | Kaufmann school; "structure" may be unfalsifiable |

**Strongest section:** §1.1, the history of research. Albertz's genealogy of the discipline's rise and fall is crisp, well-documented, and genuinely illuminating. His critique of Ringgren, Schmidt, and Fohrer is specific and fair. The seven demands that conclude the section are well-motivated by the failures catalogued.

**Weakest section:** §1.2, the argument for *Religionsgeschichte* over OT theology. It's rhetorically effective but philosophically thin. Albertz wants history of religion to be both "objective" (consistent historical method, no dogmatic selection) and "theological" (passionate concern, evaluation of wrong decisions). This tension is never resolved.

**Albertz's place in the field:** Albertz represents the German social-historical wing, distinct from the American archaeology-and-texts consensus (Cross, Smith, Dever). His model is more explicitly sociological and more attentive to internal conflict than the smoother evolutionary narratives. He's closer to the "Yahweh-alone party" model (Morton Smith, Bernhard Lang) in emphasizing factional theological struggle, but systematizes it with sociological categories. He's also notably more theologically engaged than most of his anglophone counterparts — he's doing *church* scholarship, even while insisting on historical method.

**What to watch for:** The "internal religious pluralism" model will determine whether this book succeeds or fails. If it illuminates the data (making sense of why Psalms sound different from Deuteronomy, or why household figurines don't match prophetic rhetoric), Albertz earns his place. If it becomes a mechanical grid imposed on the evidence, the book collapses into the very dogmatic systematization he criticizes in his predecessors.
