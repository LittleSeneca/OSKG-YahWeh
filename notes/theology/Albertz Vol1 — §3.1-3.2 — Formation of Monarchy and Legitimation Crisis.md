# Albertz — §3.1–3.2: Formation of the Monarchy and the Dispute Over Its Religious Legitimation

These sections mark the pivot of Albertz's narrative: the monarchy — not Canaanite syncretism, not "settlement" — was the decisive crisis for Yahweh religion. A religion born in liberation from state oppression now had to legitimate a state. The result was a theological civil war between court theologians (who imported ANE sacral kingship) and tribal opposition groups (who wielded pre-state anti-domination traditions against the monarchy). Albertz's thesis: it took over 300 years for Yahweh religion to cope with the challenge posed by the state — and the resolution only came after the state was destroyed.

---

## §3.1 The Formation of a Monarchical Territorial State

### Claim 1: The monarchy, not "settlement" or "Canaanite syncretism," was the decisive challenge to Yahweh religion

**Albertz's claim:** "It was the establishment of the kingdom and the consequent far-reaching social changes that posed the decisive challenge to the young Yahweh religion." The old model (nomadic Yahweh religion vs. Canaanite agricultural religion) is wrong. "A religion which had arisen out of the liberation from state oppression, and for almost two centuries had supported the efforts of the tribes to gain freedom in the face of claims to political hegemony... necessarily faced considerable problems as a result of the establishment of a centralistic state."

**Evidence presented:**
- Contrast with the old "settlement as crisis" model: this assumed a fundamental nomad/sedentary opposition that Albertz already rejected in §2.1
- The monarchy created conditions that directly contradicted Yahweh religion's founding character: centralized power, permanent military, taxation, forced labor, class stratification
- "It took more than three hundred years for Yahweh religion to cope with the challenge... Towards the end of the monarchy, the process of theological clarification... finally made possible a first systematic synthesis... which clearly gave the Yahweh religion of the pre-state period the upper hand over the state religion"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Albertz's reframing of the monarchy (not settlement) as the key crisis is well-argued and consistent with his model. But it's also self-reinforcing: if you define Yahweh religion as essentially anti-domination, then any state becomes a crisis by definition.

**What's at stake:** This reframing shapes the entire narrative of Part 3. Every subsequent conflict (prophetic critique, Deuteronomic reform, Josiah's reform) is interpreted as a working-out of the fundamental tension between liberation-Yahwism and state power. If the monarchy wasn't as traumatic for Yahwism as Albertz claims, the narrative loses its dramatic engine.

---

### Claim 2: Saul's monarchy was still a "chiefdom" compatible with tribal structures; David and Solomon created a genuine state by building power independent of the tribes

**Albertz's claim:** Saul's kingship was an "experiment of a charismatic 'chiefdom' close to the people" that "was still compatible with the structure of tribal organization." David, by contrast, built a mercenary army, captured Jerusalem as a neutral capital, created a bureaucracy, and imposed taxation and forced labor — transforming Israel into "a sacral monarch on the oriental pattern" under Solomon.

**Evidence presented:**
- Saul: modest residence at Gibeah, small staff (Abner as sole officer), limited professional troop of fellow tribesmen, voluntary offerings not taxes, charismatic legitimation (I Sam 11)
- David: "systematically built up a powerful troop of mercenaries" from renegades (I Sam 22:2); gained land holdings making him economically independent; captured Jebusite Jerusalem by trick (2 Sam 5:6-9); created a power base "independent of the tribes"
- Solomon: elevation was "simply a matter for the parties at court" (1 Kings 1); full sacral kingship on ANE pattern
- Population tripling in the hill country (Finkelstein's archaeology) created internal pressure for larger political organization — external Philistine threat was "only a catalyst"
- The transition from chiefdom to statehood "was by no means automatically mapped out" — African societies show segmentary societies can resist state formation

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The chiefdom-to-state transition model (Flanagan, Frick) is well-established in political anthropology. The contrast between Saul and David is textually grounded. Finkelstein's population data adds archaeological support.

**What's at stake:** If Saul's monarchy was still Yahwistically legitimate (charismatic war leader) while David's was a rupture, then the anti-monarchical polemics in the OT are not anti-monarchy per se but anti-Davidic/Solomonic state-building. The monarchy isn't intrinsically evil — it's the transformation into an ANE-style extractive state that's the problem.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more continuity between Saul and David, or who see the Saul-David contrast as a later literary (pro-Davidic) construction. "Minimalists" would question the historicity of the United Monarchy entirely.

**Alternative reading:** The chiefdom-to-state model may impose anachronistic categories on Iron Age Palestine. David's "mercenaries" could be interpreted as a standard tribal war-band, and the differences from Saul exaggerated by pro-Davidic historiography.

---

### Claim 3: The social transformations under David and Solomon — taxation, forced labor, land concentration, class stratification — created conditions that directly contradicted pre-state Yahwistic values

**Albertz's claim:** The monarchy's social effects "can hardly be overestimated." Key changes: royal taxation (tithe, provincial levies), state forced labor (mas, extended to Israelites under Solomon), royal land grants to officials (undermining family inheritance), a new class of royal officials "alien to the ideals of tribal Israelite society," reurbanization and fortification, long-distance trade creating luxury and inequality, and conscription.

**Evidence presented:**
- Solomon's 12-province system (1 Kings 4:7-19) for provisioning the court
- Forced labor (mas) initially on foreign prisoners (David), extended to Israelites by Solomon — this directly caused the northern revolt (1 Kings 12)
- Royal land grants threatened family nahala (inheritance) — cf. Ezekiel 46:16-18's utopian regulation preventing land accumulation
- The "ideal of the small farmer" (Micah 2:2: "A man and his house, a man and his inheritance") was being destroyed
- "Whereas deurbanization was the mark of the pre-state period, with the monarchy a new reurbanization began"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The social transformations are well-documented both textually and archaeologically. The contradiction with pre-state values is a matter of interpretation but hard to dispute given the texts.

**What's at stake:** This sets up the prophetic critique of the 8th century — the prophets weren't innovators but conservators, appealing to pre-state values against monarchic abuses.

## §3.2 The Dispute Over the Religious Legitimation of Kingship

### Claim 4: David's wars of conquest created a "theological vacuum" — they could not be legitimated by the old charismatic war-leader model — so court theologians imported ANE sacral kingship theology

**Albertz's claim:** "At the latest after his victory over the Philistines David's wars could no longer... be seen to be in line with the battles for liberation in the period before the state." David's wars lacked everything that characterized earlier conflicts: ongoing emergency, voluntary participation, charismatic solidarity, limited aims. "War became a means of royal power politics... that made it impossible to see him still as bringing about a saving action of Yahweh for Israel." Into this vacuum, court theologians imported "a legitimation of monarchical rule which was new for Yahweh religion, and which ran very much along the lines of the kingship theology of the Near East."

**Kingship theology's core elements:**
- **Divine sonship**: The Davidic king was adopted as Yahweh's son (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14) — "a relationship which far transcends the normal relationship between God and Israel"
- **Eternal dynasty**: Nathan's promise (2 Sam 7) guaranteed an eternal Davidic dynasty — "not just the person but the dynasty as an institution was legitimized"
- **King as mediator of blessing**: The king guaranteed shalom (peace/prosperity), fertility, justice, and cultic mediation — "all the essential aspects of the relationship between the wider group and God... run through the king and find their unity in his person"
- **Royal cultic functions**: The king performed sacrifices, gave blessings, interceded, danced before Yahweh — "a closer fusion of throne and altar" than anywhere else in the ANE

**Evidence presented:**
- The royal psalms (Ps 2, 72, 89, 110, 132) preserve the core ideology
- Nathan's oracle (2 Sam 7) as the foundational charter
- Royal cultic activity: David's dance (2 Sam 6:14ff.), Solomon's intercession and temple dedication (1 Kings 8)
- The king as "vehicle of the divine spirit" (1 Sam 16:13; 2 Sam 23:2) and "mediator of revelation" (Prov 16:10)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the existence of ANE-style kingship theology in Israel (the royal psalms are unambiguous). MEDIUM on the claim that this created a "theological vacuum" — some form of royal ideology may have developed more organically.

**What's at stake:** This is Albertz's sharpest critique of the monarchy: it wasn't just politically problematic, it was theologically corrupt. Yahweh "who had once been the symbol of liberation from state oppression" became "the guarantor of a state power which built up new mechanisms of oppression." The monarchy turned Yahwism into "ideology in the strict sense."

---

### Claim 5: Rebel movements (Absalom, Sheba, Jeroboam) used pre-state anti-domination traditions against the monarchy — Yahweh's kingship was wielded AGAINST human kingship

**Albertz's claim:** The rebel movements under David were not anti-monarchy per se but sought "a kind of constitutional monarchy... which gave the tribal groups the right to political co-operation." Their theological argument: "the institutional consolidation of political rule in the dynastic monarchy touches on the rule of Yahweh over Israel" — "the establishment of a human kingship is an attack on Yahweh's kingly rule" (1 Sam 8:7; 12:12). The resistance groups turned the kingship theology on its head: Yahweh's rule doesn't legitimate the king; it delegitimizes him.

**Evidence presented:**
- Absalom's revolt: "the elders of Israel" and "men of Israel" backed him; aimed at constitutional monarchy (2 Sam 17:4, 11, 14)
- Sheba's revolt: the slogan "We have no portion in David... Each to his tents, O Israel!" (2 Sam 20:1) "quite certainly implies a total rejection" — possibly of kingship itself
- Jeroboam's revolt: explicitly against forced labor, with appeal to Exodus tradition
- Theological polemics: Jotham's fable (Judg 9:7-15), the "law of the king" (1 Sam 8:11-17), Gideon's refusal (Judg 8:22f.)
- The twelve-tribe scheme (Gen 49; Deut 33) "can only be understood as an alternative scheme of the opposition groups" — "the unity of Israel was not to be constituted by the king but by the tribes"
- The resistance groups used "populist" Yahweh-kingship: Yahweh rules Israel directly, without royal mediation

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The texts exist and the interpretation is plausible, but dating these passages to the early monarchy specifically (rather than later Deuteronomistic retrojection) is debated.

**What's at stake:** This is the birth of "prophetic opposition" — before there were writing prophets, there were tribal resistance movements using Yahwistic anti-domination traditions. The prophetic tradition isn't an 8th-century innovation but continues a pattern established in the 10th century.

---

### Claim 6: The Succession Narrative and History of David's Rise represent "mediating positions" — humanizing the king to make him acceptable to tribal Israelite values

**Albertz's claim:** The rebel movements "compelled the monarchy to justify itself politically and theologically." The first literary works of Israel arose from this concern. The Succession Narrative (2 Sam 9-1 Kings 2) and History of David's Rise (1 Sam 16:14*-2 Sam 5:12) depict David "constrained by all kinds of human strengths and weaknesses in a way which is without parallel in the Near East." This was done "in order to bring him closer to the way of thinking among the small farmers in Israelite society."

**Evidence presented:**
- David is depicted as a tricky yet generous young man, a touching father — "all young men and fathers of families can identify with" him
- Political justification: David was innocent of Saul's downfall; Saul's own failings caused his demise
- These works mediate between sacral kingship ideology and traditional Yahwistic values by humanizing the king

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The literary observation is standard (the "Court History" is widely recognized as a masterpiece of characterization). But whether the humanization was specifically a "mediating position" responding to anti-monarchical pressure is Albertz's interpretation.

**Alternative reading:** The Succession Narrative could be political propaganda legitimating Solomon by showing David's other sons as unworthy, or could be apologetic for David's scandals (Bathsheba, Absalom). Its "humanizing" character may reflect literary artistry rather than theological mediation.

## §3.1–3.2 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Monarchy (not settlement) was the decisive challenge | MEDIUM-HIGH | Self-reinforcing if you define Yahwism as anti-domination |
| 2. Saul = chiefdom; David/Solomon = state | MEDIUM-HIGH | Historicity of the United Monarchy (minimalist challenge) |
| 3. Social transformations contradicted pre-state values | HIGH | Interpretation, not fact, is at issue |
| 4. Kingship theology imported from ANE to fill theological vacuum | HIGH (theology existed); MEDIUM (vacuum framing) | Organic development vs. conscious import |
| 5. Rebel movements used anti-domination Yahwism | MEDIUM | Dating of anti-monarchical texts; possible later retrojection |
| 6. Succession Narrative = mediating position | LOW-MEDIUM | Alternative literary explanations (propaganda, apologetic) |

**Strongest section:** The description of Davidic-Solomonic social transformations (Claim 3) and the content of kingship theology (Claim 4). Both are well-supported textually and archaeologically.

**Weakest section:** The "mediating position" interpretation of the Succession Narrative (Claim 6). This feels like Albertz forcing his model onto a text that may have quite different purposes.

**Albertz's narrative arc clarified:** Yahweh religion (liberation from state oppression) → tribal alliance (anti-domination social experiment) → monarchy (state oppression returns, but now in Israel's name). The history of Israelite religion is the story of this contradiction being worked out — through prophetic protest, reform movements, and eventually the destruction of the state itself. The monarchy sections that follow (§3.3-3.9) will trace this working-out in detail.
