# Kaufmann — Ch III — Israelite Religion

This is the positive half of Kaufmann's great contrast. Having defined paganism in Chapter II as a system unified by the metadivine realm, he now describes what makes Israelite religion fundamentally different: a God supreme over all, with no realm above or beside Him, non-mythological in essence. The chapter is Kaufmann at his most ambitious — 120 pages weaving together theology, literary analysis, and comparative religion to argue that these non-pagan features are present in the *earliest* strata of biblical literature, not late innovations.

---

## Claim 1: The basic idea is God's absolute supremacy — no metadivine realm

**Author's claim:** "The basic idea of Israelite religion is that God is supreme over all. There is no realm above or beside him to limit his absolute sovereignty. He is utterly distinct from, and other than, the world; he is subject to no laws, no compulsions, or powers that transcend him. He is, in short, non-mythological. This is the essence of Israelite religion, and that which sets it apart from all forms of paganism." (p. 60)

**Evidence presented:** The entire chapter is the evidence. Kaufmann argues this idea was "not a product of intellectual speculation, or of mystical meditation" — it was "an insight, an original intuition" that expressed itself in symbols, chiefly "the image of an omnipotent, supreme deity, holy, awful, and jealous, whose will was the highest law." The idea never received abstract systematic formulation in Israel but "working intuitively, it radically transformed the ancient mythological conceptions of Israel."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann is right about the DISTINCTIVENESS of Israelite religion's mature form. The question (as always with him) is WHEN this idea emerged. He claims it's original and intuitive — essentially a revelation. Historical scholarship sees it as a development. The textual evidence Kaufman cites for the IDEA is real; whether it's early is the battleground.

**What's at stake:** Everything Kaufmann argues in the chapter flows from this premise. If you grant that Israelite religion was monotheistic in essence from the start, then all the biblical evidence of pagan survivals become exactly that — survivals, fragments, fossils — not evidence of an earlier polytheistic stage.

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical tradition would argue that the "basic idea" Kaufmann describes is the ENDPOINT of a long development, not the starting point. Smith traces the gradual emergence of monotheistic rhetoric; Römer documents polytheistic origins. The claim that this idea was "not a product of intellectual speculation" but an "original intuition" is essentially theological, not historical.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion DID develop a unique conception of divine supremacy, and this IS its most distinctive feature. But it developed this over centuries through conflict, prophetic innovation, and historical crisis — not as an "original intuition" present from the beginning. The biblical texts themselves narrate this as a struggle, not a given.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's description of the IDEA is powerful and largely accurate as a characterization of biblical theology. The problem is his insistence that it's early, original, and popular. He's describing the finished product and claiming it's the starting point. But this is the claim the rest of the book must prove. In this chapter, he's laying out WHAT Israelite religion was — the question of WHEN it emerged is deferred to later chapters.

## Claim 2: Biblical religion lacks theogony and mythology proper — this absence is radical and unique

**Author's claim:** "The store of biblical legends lacks the fundamental myth of paganism: the theogony. All theogonic motifs are similarly absent. Israel's god has no pedigree, fathers no generations; he neither inherits nor bequeaths his authority. He does not die and is not resurrected. He has no sexual qualities or desires and shows no need of or dependence upon powers outside himself." (pp. 60-61)

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann systematically works through categories. (a) NO THEOGONY — YHWH has no origins, no parents, no children, no consort. (b) NO DIVINE BIOGRAPHY — biblical legends tell of God's dealings with men and creation, never of His private life. The rare survivals (God walking in the garden "at the cool of the day," the "mount of meeting" in Isaiah 14:13) are embedded in passages about God's relationship to creation, not His personal biography. (c) Even YHWH's battles (with Rahab, Leviathan, the dragon, the sea) are NOT theogonically significant — they involve creatures of God who rebelled, not primordial divine rivals contending for world dominion. Kaufmann explicitly contrasts this with Marduk's defeat of Tiamat: "the Bible here preserves pagan motifs, but...these materials were entirely recast." (d) The divine council passages (1 Kings 22:19ff; Isa 6; Zech 3:1) "never involve feasting and entertainment, like those of the pagan gods; they are solemn assemblies, concerned with the judgment of men, government of the world, or missions with some moral purpose." (p. 69)

He also refutes alternative explanations: it's not Semitic "poverty of imagination" (the Semites of Babylon and Canaan had rich mythologies); it's not a primitive pre-mythological stage (such a stage isn't evidenced, and Israel never developed mythology later despite syncretistic pressures); it's not the "divine king" concept (Ashur acquired a consort and pantheon through syncretism; YHWH did not).

**Confidence:** HIGH on the OBSERVATION — the Bible really does lack theogony and divine biography. This is one of Kaufmann's strongest arguments because it's a demonstrable textual fact, not an inference. MEDIUM on the EXPLANATION — that this reflects Israel's original non-mythological idea rather than later editorial censorship.

**What's at stake:** If Kaufmann is right that the absence of theogony is PRIMARY rather than the product of editorial purging, then Israelite religion was non-mythological from the start. If it's the product of censorship, then there WAS an earlier mythological stage that was systematically removed — which supports the evolutionary model.

**Who disagrees:** Smith (Origins Ch1, Ch6) would argue that the Ugaritic texts show Israel DID share mythological conceptions (the divine council, Baal's battle with Sea/Leviathan, El as father god) and that the biblical texts preserve these in fragmentary, polemically-transformed form. The absence of theogony is real, but it may reflect the particular character of Israel's God (a warrior god from the south, not originally part of the Canaanite pantheon) rather than an anti-mythological "idea."

**Alternative reading:** The absence of theogony for YHWH is explained by His origins OUTSIDE the Canaanite pantheon. Gods who enter a pantheon from outside don't have genealogies within it. YHWH was a southern deity (from Seir/Edom/Teman) who was identified with El, the father god. As El, He already HAD a mythology (the divine council, the sons of God) — but His own origins weren't narrated because He wasn't born into the Canaanite system. The theogony gap is a function of YHWH's geographic origins, not a theological revolution.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is strongest HERE. The absence of theogony in the Bible is a genuine datum that any theory must explain. The critical explanation ("it was edited out") is inadequate because — as Kaufmann notes — there are no detectable seams where theogonic material was excised. The Ugaritic texts give us a control: if the Bible preserved Canaanite mythology, we'd expect at least fragments of divine birth, sexual activity, or death/resurrection. We don't find them. This doesn't prove Kaufmann's thesis of original monotheism, but it's a serious problem for the view that Israelite religion was just Canaanite polytheism with Yahweh swapped in.

That said, Kaufmann minimizes the genuinely mythological material that IS present: Genesis 6:1-4 (sons of God mating with human women), the divine council (Psalm 82, 1 Kings 22), the conflict with Sea/Rahab/Leviathan. He assimilates ALL of these to his non-mythological framework by arguing they've been "transformed." But the transformation might be later editorial work. The Ugaritic parallels to Leviathan/Lotan and the divine council are too close to dismiss as mere "Canaanite heritage entirely recast." They suggest a stage when Israel DID share mythological conceptions.

## Claim 3: Biblical demonology is non-pagan — demons are under God's control and evil comes from sin, not a metaphysical principle

**Author's claim:** The pagan gods did not become demons in Israel. "One of the remarkable aspects of the religion of pre-exilic Israel is that it failed to transmute either its ancient pantheon or the gods of the nations into demons." (p. 63) Instead, the old gods became "the desolate haunts of dancing Se'irim who keep company with wild animals. All decisive power, divine and even demonic, has been taken from them and given to the messengers of YHWH." (p. 64) Evil proceeds from YHWH or his angels; even Satan is a member of the divine court (Job 2:1). Later Jewish demonology derives "not from a primordial evil root, but from sin" — the serpent of Eden and fallen angels, not Tiamat or Kingu. (p. 65)

**Evidence presented:** (a) No link of nomenclature between Israelite demons and pagan divinities. The names of angels (Gabriel, Michael) have no connection to the pagan pantheon. (b) Destructive agents (negef, reshef, etc.) are "messengers of evil" — members of YHWH's suite, not independent powers. Reshef, a known Canaanite god, is used in the Bible "as a bare common noun" (Job 5:7) with "no knowledge of Reshef, the mythological god." (c) The shedim and se'irim "are assigned no function in nature or in the life of men." They're "below the level of deities." (d) The "princes of the nations" in Daniel are not transformed pagan gods; "not one of their names reveals any connection with the gods mentioned in the Bible." (e) Even Rahab, the dragon, and Leviathan are not demons — they're monstrous creatures who rebelled.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the observation; MEDIUM on the inference that this proves early non-mythological religion.

**What's at stake:** This is one of Kaufmann's most original arguments. If Israelite religion was originally polytheistic and only gradually became monotheistic, we would expect the displaced gods to leave traces — as demons, as angels, as hostile powers. The standard pattern in religious history (Zoroastrianism is Kaufmann's example) is for old gods to become demons of the new faith. Israel doesn't show this pattern. This requires explanation.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars would argue that the absence is explained differently: Yahwism didn't evolve FROM Canaanite polytheism by defeating it; rather, Yahwism was always exclusive and simply didn't incorporate the Canaanite pantheon into its demonology because those gods were always "other gods" — foreign, not defeated predecessors. The Baals and Asheras were never "Israel's old gods" who became demons; they were Canaanite gods that some Israelites worshipped alongside YHWH. After the exile, they were simply abandoned, not demonized.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's argument that the gods didn't become demons is powerful — but it cuts both ways. It could mean the monotheistic idea was original. OR it could mean that Israelite monolatry was so successful at EXCLUDING other gods that they never had the status of "former gods" to be demoted to demons. The fact that the Bible depicts Israelites worshipping Baal and Asherah doesn't mean these were EVER part of the official Yahwistic pantheon in a way that would generate demonological descendants.

**My assessment:** This is genuinely insightful. The comparison with Zoroastrianism (where the daevas ARE the old Indian gods) is illuminating. Israel's demonology IS different. But Kaufmann's inference that this proves Israelite religion was non-mythological from the start overreaches. It could equally prove that Yahwism was exclusivist but NOT originally monotheistic — a monolatry that treated other gods as real but forbidden, not as demons to be integrated into a dualistic system. The evidence from Kuntillet Ajrud (YHWH and His Asherah) suggests popular religion DID pair YHWH with a consort, even if the official cult didn't. Kaufmann can't address this because the inscriptions were discovered after his death, but they're relevant to evaluating his claim.

## Claim 4: Biblical cosmogony lacks the metadivine realm and pre-existent divine stuff

**Author's claim:** Even where the Bible seems to depict creation from pre-existent matter (Genesis 1's tohu wabohu, Genesis 2's dust and rib), "the role of the tohu wabohu is quite unlike the part played by the primeval matter of pagan cosmogonies. God creates the cosmic phenomena of light, firmament, sun, moon, and host of heaven by fiat alone, with no recourse to primeval stuff." (p. 67) The notion of pre-existent stuff is "a vestigial idea which has no meaningful role in the accounts themselves." In pagan myth, by contrast, "the primordial realm is the necessary presupposition for theogony." Lacking a theogony, the Bible has no need of a pre-existent realm.

**Evidence presented:** (a) Creation by fiat (Genesis 1) — God speaks and it is. No divine substance, no sexual procreation, no emanations from the deity's body. (b) Where matter is used (dust for man, rib for woman), "considerations of etiology motivated the writer" — it explains natural connections (man returns to dust; male and female are "one flesh"), not metaphysical necessity. (c) Genesis 6:1-4 (sons of God mating with women) is perhaps "a genuine mythical fragment," but it survived because "its protagonists on both sides are creatures of God, not God himself." (d) Job 26:7 depicts God stretching out the north "upon the void" and suspending the earth "on nothingness" — "the idea of creation out of nothing is here given clear poetic expression." (e) Elsewhere, YHWH is said to have created darkness (Isa 45:7), the watery abyss (Prov 3:20), sea and dry land (Ps 95:5).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann's reading of Genesis 1 as essentially creation ex nihilo (even if not explicitly formulated) is defensible exegetically. But his dismissal of the tohu wabohu as "vestigial" is an interpretive move, not a demonstration.

**What's at stake:** Whether the Bible's creation accounts are fundamentally different from or continuous with ancient Near Eastern cosmogonies. If Kaufmann is right, the Bible's cosmology is already non-mythological even in its earliest strata.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Genesis 1 as a polemical transformation of the Enuma Elish (not an independent creation). The tohu wabohu corresponds to Tiamat; the division of waters to Marduk's splitting of Tiamat's body; the creation by word to a deliberate rejection of creation through combat. On this reading, Genesis 1 is sophisticated theological polemic — late, not early. Cross would see the Chaoskampf motif (divine warrior battling Sea) as present in Israel's earliest poetry (Exodus 15) and only later demythologized in Genesis 1.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical cosmogony IS different from Mesopotamian analogues in crucial ways — no theogony, no divine combat as the mechanism of creation. But these differences may be the product of theological development, not an original intuition. The old poetry (Exodus 15, Psalm 29, Habakkuk 3) contains combat imagery that Genesis 1 has stripped away. The direction of development is FROM mythological combat TO serene creation by fiat.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right about the DIFFERENCES but probably wrong about the SEQUENCE. Genesis 1's non-mythological character is more plausibly read as the ENDPOINT of demythologization, not its starting point. The combat mythology (YHWH vs. Sea/Rahab/Leviathan) that Kaufmann acknowledges as present in biblical poetry looks like the EARLIER stratum, with Genesis 1 representing a later, more abstract theology. This is exactly the opposite of Kaufmann's claim that the non-mythological idea is early and the mythological fragments are survivals.

## Claim 5: The biblical ban on magic and divination is fundamentally different from pagan bans — it's a blanket prohibition rooted in monotheistic logic

**Author's claim:** "The Bible bans magic under penalty of death. This ban is quite different, however, from pagan interdictions of black magic. While paganism forbade only that sorcery which was antisocial...the Bible makes no distinction between 'white' and 'black' magic." (p. 78) All pagan divinatory techniques (nahash, qesem) are banned unconditionally — not just inquiries to other gods. The Bible doesn't even recognize that pagan magic is connected with gods or spirits; it treats magic as "a form of human wisdom," an occult science "that enables man to work wonders without recourse to God, thus feeding his ambition 'to become like God.'" (p. 79)

**Evidence presented:** (a) The Egyptian magicians in Exodus work their wonders "not in the name of any god" — when they fail, they say "This is the finger of God," not "Their god is greater than ours." (b) Balaam has no contact with gods other than YHWH; YHWH is Balaam's god too. (c) Isaiah taunts Babylon's magic without referencing Babylon's gods. (d) No Israelite king (even wicked ones like Ahab) employs court magicians or diviners — they inquire of YHWH only through Urim and prophets. (e) There is no struggle between Israelite prophets and magicians — only between true and false prophets. (f) No apotropaic rites exist in Israel against magical or demonic attack. The Balaam story is telling: Balaam could have harmed Israel, but "no recourse is had to any countermagic...Israel trusts in its God." (p. 87)

On divination: The Urim and Thummim are uniquely legitimate because they're the simplest, least "scientific" oracle — they address God directly rather than interpreting natural signs. "Israelite religion did not reject divination on the basis of a clear conceptual grasp of its inner essence; here, as throughout all early Israelite creativity, a sound instinct was at work." (p. 92)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the distinctiveness of the biblical ban; MEDIUM on the claim that this proves no native Israelite magic ever existed.

**What's at stake:** Whether the biblical prohibitions reflect an ORIGINAL ideational difference or a LATER polemical recharacterization of practices that were once acceptable. If Kaufmann is right, Israelite religion was non-magical from the start.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see extensive magical practice in Israel that the Bible either polemicizes against or unconsciously preserves. The very fact that magic is banned repeatedly suggests it WAS practiced. The "magical" elements in prophetic stories (Elisha's bones reviving the dead, Moses' staff, the bronze serpent) that Kaufmann argues are "transformed" could be read as evidence of magical practice that later editors reinterpreted.

**Alternative reading:** The blanket prohibition of magic and divination IS distinctive, but it reflects a LATER stage of Israelite religion — the prophetic/deuteronomistic reform. Earlier Israelite practice was more accommodating. The evidence that magic was banned precisely because it WAS practiced is written into the prohibitions themselves. The silence about magicians at Israelite courts could reflect editorial suppression, not historical reality. Manasseh is said to have practiced divination (2 Kings 21:6) — and Manasseh reigned for 55 years.

**My assessment:** This is one of Kaufmann's strongest sections. His observation that the Bible treats pagan magic as godless — as human technique, not demonic invocation — is a genuine insight that needs explaining. The critical model (Israelite religion was originally polytheistic/magical and gradually became monotheistic) predicts that the Bible would polemicize against magic as IDOLATRY (i.e., invoking other gods). But the Bible polemicizes against it as GODLESSNESS — as human arrogance. This is a different kind of opposition than we'd expect from an evolving monolatry. Kaufmann's explanation (that Israelite religion was non-magical from the start) accounts for this better than the critical alternative. However, the archaeological evidence for magical practice (amulets, figurines, inscriptions) in Iron Age Israel complicates the picture. The religion of the TEXTS may have been non-magical while popular practice was not.

## Claim 6: Israelite prophecy differs fundamentally from pagan divination — the word of God, not ecstasy, is the primary source

**Author's claim:** In pagan prophecy, "the speech of the rapt prophet is the very utterance of a god who has taken possession of him." In Israel, "prophesying frenzy never had primary mantic value...We never hear of recording the words of ecstatics to discover their hidden divine meaning, as was done, for example, at the Delphic oracle." (p. 97) The primary revelation is the word of God that comes BEFORE the prophetic utterance: "The prophet repeats the word of God that came to him beforehand." The spirit does not cause prophecy; prophecy (the divine word) can cause manifestations of the spirit. "Frenzy never is represented as a preliminary to prophecy." (p. 100) The ideal prophet is Moses, who speaks with God "face to face, as a man speaks with his fellow" (Exod 33:11) — no ecstasy, no spirit, no vision.

**Evidence presented:** (a) Prophetic speech is reported in the past tense: "Thus said YHWH," "The word of YHWH came to me." The prophet recounts a prior revelatory experience. (b) The spirit of YHWH operates broadly (on judges, kings, Nazirites, poets) without making them prophets. The spirit gives strength, wisdom, poetic ability — but not the word of God. (c) "The primary revelation of the word of God...is not ideally bound up with either the action of the spirit or ecstasy." Abraham, Jacob, Moses, Joshua, Gideon, Samuel, Elijah, Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Zechariah, Daniel — "no mention of the spirit." (d) When the spirit acts on the seventy elders (Num 11), they "prophesy" but don't speak the word of God. (e) Ecstatic phenomena (fear, trembling, prostration, paralysis) "are described as results of the prophetic experience, rather than as preconditions to it." (f) No external stimuli (wine, vapors, dancing) are used to induce prophecy. Elisha's use of music is the sole exception, and its rarity proves the rule. (g) Symbolic dreams are assimilated to prophecy: God provides the interpretation, not the prophet's oneirocritic skill.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the distinctive Israelite conception of prophecy. LOW on the claim that this represents the earliest form rather than a developed theological ideal.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite prophecy is a unique phenomenon born from the monotheistic idea, or a local variant of ancient Near Eastern prophecy that developed distinctive features over time.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who have studied Mari and Neo-Assyrian prophecy find substantial parallels to Israelite prophecy — the messenger formula, the sense of divine commission, even the tension between "true" and "false" prophets. The distinctiveness Kaufmann identifies may be a difference in the BIBLICAL PORTRAYAL of prophecy (shaped by later prophetic literature's self-understanding) rather than a difference in the ORIGINAL PHENOMENON. The early ecstatic bands (1 Sam 10, 19) look very much like pagan ecstatic groups, and Kaufmann's attempt to separate them from "prophecy proper" feels strained.

**Alternative reading:** Early Israelite prophecy was ecstatic and closely resembled pagan prophecy. The "word-centered" model that Kaufmann identifies as primary is actually a LATER development — the classical prophets' self-understanding, read back onto earlier figures like Moses. The ecstatic "bands of prophets" are the EARLIER phenomenon, and they look a lot like Canaanite ecstatics. The non-ecstatic, word-centered ideal is the product of prophetic reflection in the 8th-6th centuries.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's analysis of the BIBLICAL CONCEPTION of prophecy is masterful — he correctly identifies that the Bible understands prophecy differently from pagan divination. But he commits the characteristic error of assuming that the Bible's theological self-understanding reflects historical origins. The fact that the Bible portrays prophecy as word-centered doesn't mean prophecy WAS word-centered from the beginning. The ecstatic bands in Samuel look like the earlier stratum; the non-ecstatic ideal looks like the later theological interpretation. Kaufmann's claim that the ecstatic phenomena are "by-products" of the word rather than its source is a theological claim, not a historical one. The Mari prophetic texts (which Kaufmann couldn't have known in detail) show that the messenger formula and the sense of divine commission were present in prophecy that was ALSO ecstatic — the two aren't mutually exclusive.

## Claim 7: The biblical cult has been fundamentally transformed — no mythological or magical basis remains

**Author's claim:** "The cult laws of the Bible have no mythological or magic background — that is the heart of the transformation. This was not the product of an artificial reworking, consciously and rationally undertaken by the priesthood of a certain age. It is the result of an organic development of Israelite religion through the course of generations." (p. 102) Sacrifice is not food for the deity; festivals are historicized, not dramatizations of divine life; impurity is a passive condition, not an active demonic force; purifications are not therapeutic or exorcistic.

**Evidence presented:** (a) SACRIFICE: "Expressions such as 'bread of God,' 'a pleasing odor'...must be regarded as petrified linguistic survivals which by biblical times had lost their original significance." Prophets use these phrases freely, proving they were innocent formulas, not living mythology. The peace offering is eaten "before" — never "with" — YHWH. The ideal is "sight of God from afar," not mystic union. (b) PURITY: Impurity is a condition, not a power. "No activity or power is ascribed to impurity in itself. The danger of mingling realms comes only from the holy, the realm of YHWH." Contact with the dead is harmless; contact with the holy is deadly. The scapegoat sends sin TO Azazel — it doesn't expel Azazel. "Of all known exorcisms having to do with demons this is unique in being conceived of not as the expulsion of a demon, but as the expulsion of something to a demon." (p. 114) (c) FESTIVALS: Passover and Tabernacles are historicized — they commemorate Exodus and Wandering, not agricultural/fertility cycles. "This accords with the nature of biblical legends to deal not with the life of YHWH, but with his relations to men." Dramatization is confined to historicized festivals because "dramatization of a cosmic event could easily have become transformed into a representation of an event in the life of God — a motif that had no place in Israel." (p. 117) (d) No spells, no incantations in the cult. Psalms are prayers, not conjurations. The priests are not healers or exorcists. (e) The sin offering (hattath) is offered AFTER recovery, not during illness — it's purificatory, not therapeutic. "Throughout the Bible healing pertains to the man of God, not the priest." (p. 107)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the transformed character of the biblical cult as PRESENTED. LOW on the claim that this reflects the earliest historical practice rather than priestly/editorial reshaping.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israel's cultus was fundamentally different from Canaanite cultus from the beginning, or whether it BECAME different through prophetic critique and priestly reform.

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological record complicates Kaufmann's picture considerably. Figurines, standing stones, and the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions suggest a popular cult that was more continuous with Canaanite practice than the biblical texts acknowledge. Even within the Bible, the golden calves at Dan and Bethel, the high places, the Asherah in the Jerusalem temple — these suggest a cult that was NOT originally as transformed as Kaufmann claims. The Priestly Code's purity system has many parallels with Hittite and Mesopotamian ritual texts, suggesting shared cultural patterns rather than radical Israelite innovation.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical cult represents a REFORMED and IDEALIZED version of Israelite practice. The transformation Kaufmann describes IS real — but it's the product of centuries of priestly systematization and prophetic critique, not an original feature. The Priestly Code (which Kaufmann dates early) is the most thoroughly systematized and demythologized stratum — which is precisely why most scholars date it late (exilic or post-exilic).

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's description of the biblical cult's non-magical character is generally accurate as a reading of the Priestly and Deuteronomic texts. But he attributes this character to "organic development" over generations, which simply pushes the question back: why did this organic development happen in Israel and nowhere else? His answer is the monotheistic idea. But the more economical explanation is that the biblical texts represent a LATE stage of Israelite religion — after the exile, after the prophetic critique, after the centralization of worship — exported backward into the foundational narratives. The fact that Kaufmann has to repeatedly argue that seemingly pagan elements (the tohu wabohu, the "bread of God," the scapegoat) are "vestigial" or "petrified" or "fossils" is telling: there are a LOT of fossils. At some point, the number of fossils suggests they're not fossils — they're the living organism, and the non-magical framework is the later overlay.

## Claim 8: The popular religion was also non-mythological — Israel's "idolatry" was superficial, not genuine polytheism

**Author's claim:** "We do find vestiges of Israel's ancient mythology imbedded in the Bible, and there was an 'idolatry' in Israel; yet the biblical God has no mythological features. It would seem that this reflects a level beyond, not before, mythology." (p. 61) The popular religion of biblical times "was non-mythological. The ancient myths disappeared from popular consciousness. Pagan influence was superficial and did not work deeply on folk creativity." (p. 67)

**Evidence presented:** (a) The gods of Israel's idolatrous cults "failed to bequeath even their names to later Jewish demons, let alone their mythological features." (b) Idols are consistently mocked as mere wood and stone — the prophets never engage with pagan theology as if it were a live option. (c) Magic, divination, and other pagan practices are always treated as foreign imports, never as native Israelite traditions. (d) Even the wicked kings don't have court magicians or diviners — with the exception of Manasseh, who is described as an aberration.

**Confidence:** LOW. This is where Kaufmann's thesis is most vulnerable to archaeological falsification.

**What's at stake:** Whether the archaeological evidence for polytheistic practice in Israel represents genuine mythological belief or superficial imitation. If Kaufmann is right, the figurines, inscriptions, and high places are "idolatry" in a degraded sense — fetishism without genuine mythological content.

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological record. Dever's "Did God Have a Wife?" documents thousands of Judean pillar figurines (likely Asherah), standing stones (massebot), and cult stands. The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions bless by "YHWH and his Asherah." The elephantine papyri show a Jewish community worshipping Anat-Yahu alongside YHWH. This isn't "superficial" paganism — it's evidence that many Israelites genuinely conceived of YHWH as having a consort, which requires a divine realm with at least two divine beings, which IS mythological thinking.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion was DIVERSE. There was an official, aniconic, exclusivist Yahwism (what became biblical religion) AND a popular religion that was more continuous with Canaanite practice. Kaufmann's claim that the popular religion was non-mythological is a misreading both of the biblical evidence (which constantly depicts the people as worshipping other gods) and the archaeological evidence. The prophets' polemic against idols as "mere wood and stone" is a rhetorical strategy, not evidence of what the idol-worshippers actually believed.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's weakest claim, and it's the one most directly falsified by archaeology. His argument that Israelites who worshipped other gods didn't REALLY believe in them as gods — that they were just going through empty motions — is unfalsifiable special pleading. It's the same move as saying "those medieval witch-burners didn't REALLY believe in witches; they were just performing a social ritual." At some point, you have to take the evidence at face value: the Israelites who set up Asherah poles, who made figurines, who blessed by "YHWH and his Asherah" — they believed in a divine realm with more than one power. Kaufmann can only maintain his thesis by defining "genuine polytheism" in a way that excludes the Israelite evidence by fiat.

---

## Claim 9: The non-pagan features are present in the earliest strata — this is not the product of later editing

**Author's claim:** This claim runs throughout the chapter. "The monotheism of these stories is, thus, not the outcome of an artificial adaptation of pagan materials. It permeates their every aspect and finds expression even in passages of artless naivete." (p. 69) "That any conscious censorship has been at work here to purge these stories of pagan features is improbable in view of the folk naivete that permeates the legends of Genesis 2-11. It was rather that the religion of YHWH instinctively excluded such motifs." (p. 68) The non-mythological features are "visible in the earliest strata, permeating even the 'magical' legends." (p. 121)

**Evidence presented:** Kaufmann's methodological argument is that the biblical texts are CANDID about embarrassing details (God walking in the garden, God's regret at creating humanity, God trying to kill Moses, the sons of God mating with women, Jacob wrestling with a divine being, ecstatic frenzy, the witch of Endor). If editors were systematically purging mythology, they did a terrible job — they left in all kinds of material that could be read mythologically. What's missing (theogony, divine sexuality, God's death and resurrection) must therefore be missing because it was never there.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the force of the argument from candor. LOW on the conclusion that the non-pagan features represent the earliest stratum.

**What's at stake:** The entire question of directionality. If the non-pagan features are early, Kaufmann is fundamentally right about Israelite religion. If they're late, the critical model stands.

**Who disagrees:** The entire documentary tradition. The argument from candor cuts both ways: the Bible's frankness about embarrassing details doesn't prove everything in it is historically reliable — it proves the editors had criteria for what to include that weren't simply "make everything look pious." The absence of theogony could reflect the fact that, by the time the traditions were written down, theogony was no longer thinkable within Israelite religion. That doesn't mean it was never there in the pre-literary stage — it means the literary tradition doesn't preserve it.

**Alternative reading:** The "candor" Kaufmann observes is genuine, but it doesn't prove earliness. It proves that Israelite religion, BY THE TIME OF ITS EARLIEST LITERARY SOURCES, was already non-mythological. But the earliest literary sources (J and E, by standard dating) are 10th-8th century BCE. The religion of Israel BEFORE those sources — during the settlement period, the period of the Judges — could have been more mythological. The archaeological evidence from that earlier period (when available) does suggest continuity with Canaanite practice.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's argument from candor is genuinely powerful, and it's one that critical scholars don't always take seriously enough. If the Bible's non-mythological character were the product of systematic editing, we'd expect the editors to have been more thorough. They left in Genesis 6:1-4, the divine council scenes, the Chaoskampf allusions, the exodus story of God trying to kill Moses — these are NOT the work of careful theological censors. The absence of theogony, divine sexuality, and divine death may indeed reflect something about the RELIGION that produced these texts, not just the EDITING.

BUT: this argument has a chronological limit. It can only push the non-mythological character back to the time of the earliest SOURCES — not to the ORIGINS of Israelite religion (whenever those were). Between whatever historical events generated Israel and the earliest written sources, there could have been centuries of oral tradition during which the non-mythological "idea" emerged. Kaufmann wants to collapse this gap: for him, the earliest sources reflect the ORIGINS. But he hasn't demonstrated that — he's assumed it.

## Claim 10: Israelite religion's transformation of pagan elements was instinctive, not the product of systematic reform

**Author's claim:** "Precisely because it never received a dogmatic formulation which could serve as a standard for the systematic reformation of the old religion, it was unable entirely to eradicate all traces of the pagan heritage." (p. 60) The transformation was "guided by instinct and intuition, not by speculation and systematic formulation." (p. 79) The idea "expressed itself rather in symbols" and "working intuitively, it radically transformed the ancient mythological conceptions of Israel." (p. 60)

**Evidence presented:** (a) Biblical religion has no systematic theology — no creed, no abstract formulation of monotheism. (b) The Bible preserves pagan fragments (Rahab, Azazel, the serpent, the sons of God) that a systematic reform would have excised. (c) The biblical view of pagan magic and divination is "distorted" — the Bible genuinely misunderstands that pagans connect magic with gods. "This can be neither accidental nor the result of a systematic reworking of the early traditions." (p. 79) If editors were systematically suppressing pagan theology, they would have polemicized against pagan gods, not ignored them. (d) The prophets freely use anthropomorphic language ("pleasing odor," "bread of God") that a systematic reformer would have purged.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the observation that biblical religion is non-systematic. MEDIUM on the inference that this proves early, instinctive monotheism rather than late, unsystematic monotheism.

**What's at stake:** Kaufmann is trying to preempt the critical argument that the non-mythological features were introduced by late priestly editors. His counter: if it were systematic editing, it would be more thorough and more theologically consistent. The messiness of the Bible — its retention of pagan fragments, its anthropomorphisms, its lack of systematic theology — is evidence of an ORGANIC, early, popular transformation, not a LATE, elite, editorial one.

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars can accept the messiness while maintaining late dating. Editing in the ancient world wasn't like modern redaction — it was often conservative, preserving traditions even when they sat uneasily with the editor's theology. The Pentateuch's combination of P (with its transcendent God) and J (with its anthropomorphic God) is evidence of conservative editing, not early monotheism.

**Alternative reading:** The non-systematic character of biblical religion reflects the fact that monotheism emerged GRADUALLY in Israel, through a process in which older materials were reinterpreted rather than discarded. The "instinct" Kaufmann describes is real — but it was the instinct of a tradition in DEVELOPMENT, not an instinct present from the beginning. The messiness is evidence of evolution, not revolution.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is making a legitimate methodological point: if you claim the non-pagan features are the result of late editorial systematization, you need to explain why the systematization was so incomplete. Why leave in the sons of God mating with women? Why leave in God trying to kill Moses? Why leave in the divine council? A genuinely systematic editor would have removed these. This is a real problem for the view that P or the Deuteronomists created Israelite monotheism through editorial activity. But it's not a problem for the view that monotheism developed gradually through a living tradition that reinterpreted rather than excised older materials. Kaufmann's "instinct" and the critical "development" may be describing the same process from different angles.

## Chapter III Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Absolute divine supremacy, no metadivine realm | MEDIUM | Idea may be late endpoint, not starting point |
| 2: Absence of theogony and mythology proper | HIGH (observation) / MEDIUM (explanation) | Ugaritic parallels to Chaoskampf and divine council; YHWH's non-Canaanite origins |
| 3: Non-pagan demonology | MEDIUM-HIGH (observation) / MEDIUM (inference) | Alternative: exclusivist monolatry, not original monotheism |
| 4: Non-mythological cosmogony | MEDIUM | Genesis 1 may be late polemic, not early stratum |
| 5: Blanket ban on magic and divination | MEDIUM-HIGH | Archaeological evidence for magical practice |
| 6: Word-centered vs. ecstatic prophecy | MEDIUM-HIGH (on biblical conception) / LOW (on historical origins) | Mari and Neo-Assyrian prophetic parallels |
| 7: Transformed cult | MEDIUM-HIGH (on presentation) / LOW (on earliness) | Archaeological evidence; late dating of P |
| 8: Popular religion was non-mythological | LOW | Kuntillet Ajrud, pillar figurines, Elephantine papyri |
| 9: Non-pagan features in earliest strata | MEDIUM | Gap between earliest sources and historical origins |
| 10: Transformation was instinctive, not systematic | MEDIUM | Gradual development explains messiness equally well |

**Strongest sections:**
1. The absence of theogony (Claim 2). This is a genuine philological datum. No amount of archaeological figurines can produce a biblical theogony that isn't there. Kaufmann is right that this requires explanation, and the "editors removed it" explanation is question-begging.
2. The distinctive character of the ban on magic (Claim 5). Kaufmann's observation that the Bible treats magic as godless technique, not idolatrous invocation, is acute and not adequately explained by the critical model.
3. The argument from candor (Claim 9). The Bible's frankness about embarrassing material is genuine evidence against thoroughgoing editorial censorship.

**Weakest sections:**
1. The claim that popular religion was non-mythological (Claim 8). This is falsified by the archaeological record. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions alone are fatal: "YHWH and his Asherah" in a blessing formula requires a divine realm with at least two powers. No amount of "the idolaters didn't really believe it" can get around this.
2. The dating of the non-mythological features to the earliest strata (Claim 9). Kaufmann conflates "present in the earliest literary sources" with "present from the origins of Israelite religion." These are different things, separated by centuries of oral tradition.
3. The claim that prophecy's word-centeredness is original (Claim 6). The ecstatic bands in 1 Samuel look very much like the earlier phenomenon; the classical prophets' word-centered self-understanding looks like a development.

**What Kaufmann gets right that critics often miss:**
- The absence of theogony IS remarkable and requires explanation beyond "editing."
- The Bible's treatment of pagan religion as fetishism rather than genuine theology IS a datum that the critical model doesn't predict well.
- The argument from candor IS a real methodological challenge to models that attribute everything to editorial activity.
- The non-systematic, "instinctive" character of biblical monotheism IS better explained by a living tradition than by a committee of editors.

**What Kaufmann misses that critics get right:**
- The archaeological evidence for polytheistic PRACTICE is overwhelming, even if the LITERARY evidence for polytheistic THEOLOGY is absent.
- The Ugaritic texts show that Israel shared mythological FRAMEWORKS (divine council, Chaoskampf, El as father god) even if it didn't share mythological NARRATIVES (theogony, divine biography).
- YHWH's non-Canaanite origins (from Seir/Edom/Teman) provide an alternative explanation for the absence of theogony that doesn't require an anti-mythological "idea."
- The earliest biblical poetry (Exodus 15, Judges 5, Habakkuk 3, Psalm 29) is more mythological than the later prose narratives — suggesting development AWAY from mythology, not an original non-mythological state.

**Ugaritic texts:** Kaufmann does engage the Ugaritic material (pp. 62, 106-107), mainly regarding the Chaoskampf. He acknowledges that "Ugaritic allusions to the defeat of Lotan, the fleeing serpent, Yam, etc. show that it is Canaanite, rather than Babylonian influence that is involved." But he argues that the Ugaritic evidence supports his case: the Ugaritic texts "do not indicate that these battles had cosmogonic significance (as did the battle of Marduk with Tiamat)," and the biblical material "does not speak of battles preceding the creation of the world." He's partially right — the Ugaritic Baal cycle is about Baal's kingship among the gods, not cosmogony. But he understates the mythological character of the material. The Baal-Yam conflict IS a theomachy — a battle between divine beings for supremacy. Kaufmann's reading of Rahab/Leviathan as "creatures" rather than divine antagonists is an interpretive move that the Ugaritic parallels make harder to sustain. If Lotan/Leviathan was a divine adversary for Baal, he was likely a divine adversary for YHWH too — at least in the early tradition.

**Kuntillet Ajrud gap:** The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions (discovered 1975-76, after Kaufmann's death in 1963) are the most significant external challenge to his thesis. They show that in the 9th-8th centuries BCE, some Israelites paired YHWH with Asherah. This isn't mere "idolatry" in Kaufmann's degraded sense — it's a blessing formula, part of everyday religious language. Kaufmann cannot be faulted for not addressing evidence discovered after his death. But any contemporary evaluation of his thesis must note that his central claim — that Israelite religion was non-mythological even at the popular level — is directly contradicted by epigraphic evidence he couldn't have known about.

**Bottom line:** Chapter III is Kaufmann's most impressive work — a sustained, erudite, and often brilliant argument for the distinctiveness of Israelite religion. He identifies real features of the biblical texts that the critical model struggles to explain: the absence of theogony, the non-demonic character of Israelite demonology, the blanket ban on magic, the word-centered model of prophecy, the non-magical cult. These are not illusions — they are genuine characteristics of the biblical literature that any theory must account for. Where Kaufmann overreaches is in claiming that these features were present "from the beginning" and were shared by the popular religion. The archaeological evidence, the Ugaritic parallels, and a more careful reading of the biblical texts' own developmental layers all suggest that Israelite religion BECAME non-mythological through a historical process — a process that was unusually thorough and resulted in a genuinely distinctive religion, but a process nonetheless. Kaufmann gives us the best description available of what that process PRODUCED. He does not give us an adequate account of how it happened or when.

---

**Methodological note:** Kaufmann describes his own method in this chapter as follows: "The new religious idea never received an abstract, systematic formulation in Israel. It expressed itself rather in symbols...Taking on popular forms, the new idea pervaded every aspect of Israelite creativity. Working intuitively, it radically transformed the ancient mythological conceptions of Israel." This is a description of how Kaufmann READS the Bible — as a unified expression of an intuitive monotheistic idea. The question is whether this is how the Bible was PRODUCED. Kaufmann's method assumes what it needs to prove: that the monotheistic idea was present from the beginning, shaping all the material. The circularity that Kaufmann identified in critical scholarship (Claim 7 of the Introduction) has its mirror image here.
