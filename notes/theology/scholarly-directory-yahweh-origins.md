# Scholarly Directory: Yahweh's Origins and the Emergence of Monotheism

A comprehensive directory of PhD-level scholars on all sides of the debate — who they are, where they teach, what they argue, and how their peers evaluate them.

---

## Position 1: The Evolutionary Consensus (Scholarly Majority)

These scholars hold that Yahweh was originally one god among many in a polytheistic Canaanite/ANE context, and that monotheism developed gradually, reaching its mature form during or after the Babylonian Exile.

### Mark S. Smith (b. 1956) — *The Central Figure*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Princeton Theological Seminary (Professor of Old Testament); formerly NYU (Chair of Bible and Ancient Near Eastern Studies) |
| **Education** | PhD, Yale (1985); studied under Frank Moore Cross at Harvard |
| **Key Works** | *The Early History of God* (1990, rev. 2002), *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), *God in Translation* (2008) |
| **Religious Affiliation** | Roman Catholic |

**Core Argument:** Israelite religion was a subset of Canaanite religion. Yahweh was a second-tier deity who absorbed El (the high god), Baal (the storm god), and Asherah (the mother goddess) through "convergence and differentiation." Monotheism was a late development, emerging from monolatry under political pressure. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 originally showed Yahweh as one of El's sons receiving Israel as his inheritance.

**Smith's institutional clout is enormous.** He trained under Cross at Harvard, taught at NYU, and now holds a chair at Princeton Seminary. His books are standard citations in virtually every academic work on the subject. Even his critics treat him as the scholar to engage.

### Thomas Römer — *Europe's Leading Voice*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Collège de France (Professor of the Hebrew Bible); University of Lausanne |
| **Key Work** | *The Invention of God* (Harvard University Press, 2015) |

**Core Argument:** Yahweh emerged in the early Iron Age somewhere in Edom or northwest Arabia as a god of the wilderness, storms, and war. He became the sole god of Israel "in fits and starts" as other gods — including the mother goddess Asherah — were gradually sidelined. It was not until the destruction of Jerusalem that Israelites came to worship Yahweh as the one god of all, creator of heaven and earth.

> **Praise:** "A masterpiece of detective work and exposition by one of the world's leading experts on the Hebrew Bible." — Robert A. Segal, *Times Higher Education*

Römer holds one of the most prestigious academic chairs in the world (Collège de France). Harvard University Press published the English translation — not a religious press, an elite academic one.

### Theodore J. Lewis — *The Monumental Reference*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Johns Hopkins University |
| **Key Work** | *The Origin and Character of God: Ancient Israelite Religion through the Lens of Divinity* (Oxford University Press, 2020) |

**Core Argument:** Lewis's book is described as a "monumental study" and "up-to-date comprehensive reference work" (Oxford UP). He explores Yahweh's historical origin through El and Yahweh traditions, how God was characterized in literature, and how God was represented in archaeology and iconography. At over 1,000 pages, this is now the definitive reference work in the field.

Oxford University Press publication and the sheer scope of the work make Lewis one of the most authoritative voices.

### John Day (b. 1948)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | University of Oxford, Lady Margaret Hall (Emeritus Professor of Old Testament Studies) |
| **Education** | PhD, Cambridge (1977) |
| **Key Work** | *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000) |

Day uses comparative mythological analysis to demonstrate that Yahweh absorbed the attributes and mythologies of Canaanite deities. His work on Asherah won the Dahood Memorial Prize. He also documented that at Elephantine (5th c. BCE), Jews worshipped Yahweh and Anat alongside Asherah and El — centuries after monotheism supposedly emerged.

### Daniel E. Fleming — *The Nuanced View*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | New York University |
| **Key Work** | *Yahweh Before Israel: Glimpses of History in a Divine Name* (Cambridge University Press, 2021) |

**Core Argument:** Fleming's approach is sharply new. He argues that Yahweh was NOT taken from outsiders (contra the standard Kenite hypothesis). Rather, the divine name is evidence for the diverse background of Israel itself. One of the Bronze Age herding peoples of the inland Levant gave its name to a deity, initially outside any relationship to Israel. This complicates the standard "imported from Midian" narrative while still supporting a non-indigenous origin.

Cambridge University Press, 2021 — among the most recent major treatments.

### William G. Dever (b. 1933)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | University of Arizona (Professor Emeritus of Near Eastern Archaeology); Lycoming College |
| **Education** | PhD, Harvard (1966) |
| **Key Works** | *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), *Has Archaeology Buried the Bible?* (2020) |

**Core Argument:** Folk religion (what ordinary Israelites actually practiced) was thoroughly polytheistic well into the monarchic period. The "official" book religion of the Deuteronomists was an elite ideal that had little purchase on the ground. Thousands of female figurines, multiple temples, and Asherah inscriptions prove widespread polytheistic practice. Self-described as "not even a theist" — approaches the Bible as a historical artifact.

### Israel Finkelstein (b. 1949)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Tel Aviv University (Professor Emeritus of Archaeology); University of Haifa |
| **Key Works** | *The Bible Unearthed* (2001, with Silberman), *The Forgotten Kingdom* (2013) |
| **Awards** | Dan David Prize (2005); Chevalier, Ordre des Arts et des Lettres (France) |

Finkelstein's "Low Chronology" upended the field — the United Monarchy of David and Solomon was far smaller than the Bible describes. His archaeological work implies Yahwism developed in two separate kingdoms with different religious trajectories. The biblical narrative of a unified, monotheistic Israel under David is not supported by the ground.

### Frank Moore Cross (1921–2012)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Harvard University (Hancock Professor of Hebrew, 1958–1992) |
| **Key Work** | *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973) |

**Core Argument:** "Yahweh" originated as a shortened form of *ˀel ḏū yahwī ṣabaˀôt* ("El who creates the hosts"), one of El's cultic names. He trained an entire generation of scholars — Mark Smith, William Dever, Richard Elliott Friedman, and Emanuel Tov were all his students. His influence on the field is hard to overstate.

### Francesca Stavrakopoulou (b. 1975)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | University of Exeter (Professor of Hebrew Bible and Ancient Religion) |
| **Key Work** | *God: An Anatomy* (2021) |
| **Religious Affiliation** | Atheist; patron of Humanists UK |

**Core Argument:** Yahweh was conceived as "a human-shaped deity who walks and talks and weeps and laughs, who eats, sleeps, feels, and breathes, and who is undeniably male." The incorporeal God of later theology was not yet present in the Bible. The process by which polytheism gave way to monotheism was "pantheon reduction" — not a revelation of the truth but a gradual winnowing of the divine realm.

### Ziony Zevit (b. 1942)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | American Jewish University (Professor of Biblical Literature) |
| **Education** | PhD, UC Berkeley (1974) |
| **Key Work** | *The Religions of Ancient Israel: A Synthesis of Parallactic Approaches* (2001) |

Zevit's synthesis of archaeological, epigraphic, and textual evidence is one of the most thorough demonstrations that ancient Israelite religion was diverse, polytheistic, and regionally varied. His "parallactic" approach examines the same data from multiple angles. He's more cautious than Smith about grand evolutionary narratives — the evidence is messier than either camp acknowledges.

### Konrad Schmid

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | University of Zurich (Professor of Hebrew Bible and Ancient Judaism) |

Schmid argues that Pentateuchal formation involves complex multi-stage redaction that doesn't fit neat evolutionary schemes. A major European voice who complicates the simple "polytheism → monotheism" story from within the critical camp.

## Position 2: Counter-Positions and Complications

These scholars challenge or substantially complicate the evolutionary consensus. Some are conservative/confessional, some are not. Some disagree with Smith fundamentally, others just nuance his model substantially.

### Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023) — *The Divine Council*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Faithlife Corporation (Logos Bible Software); Celebration Church |
| **Education** | PhD, Hebrew Bible and Semitic Languages, UW-Madison; MA, Ancient History, UPenn |
| **Key Works** | *The Unseen Realm* (2015), *Demons* (2020) |

**Core Argument:** Heiser explicitly rejects the evolutionary model. His argument rests on the Hebrew word *elohim* — he argues it's a "place of residence" term (beings of the spiritual realm), not an ontological claim. Yahweh is an *elohim*, but so are angels, demons, and the disembodied dead. The "other gods" in the Bible are real beings — created, subordinate, members of Yahweh's divine council — but NOT co-eternal rival deities. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (DSS reading) reflects this divine council structure, not polytheism. Israel was never polytheistic; it acknowledged a heavenly bureaucracy under Yahweh's absolute rule.

**Scholarly Reception:** Heiser's PhD is from a legitimate secular university (UW-Madison). His book *The Unseen Realm* has been enormously popular among evangelical laity but largely ignored by critical scholars. Heath Henning's *The Unbiblical Realm* (2023) is the first book-length critique, calling Heiser's theology "heretical" and documenting alleged alterations of source quotations in his dissertation. A 2025 Theopolis Institute symposium evaluated Heiser's views critically — Luke Stamps argued Heiser's framework "bleeds into genuine polytheism."

**Assessment:** Heiser is the most sophisticated conservative voice. He agrees with critical scholars on the textual data (Deut 32:8 originally read "sons of God") but interprets it within a framework that preserves orthodox monotheism. Whether this is legitimate scholarship or sophisticated apologetics depends on who you ask.

### Yehezkel Kaufmann (1889–1963) — *The Early Monotheism Pioneer*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Hebrew University of Jerusalem (Professor of Bible) |
| **Key Work** | *Toledot ha-emunah ha-yisreelit* (History of the Religion of Israel), 8 vols. (1937–1956) |

**Core Argument:** Israelite monotheism was NOT an evolution from polytheism. It was a *sui generis* revolution that appeared with Moses. The Hebrew Bible contains no theogony, no divine battles, no sexual activity among gods — this demonstrates a fundamental difference from every neighboring religion. Polytheistic practices were popular apostasy from an already-established monotheistic norm, not vestiges of an earlier stage.

**Kaufmann inverted the Documentary Hypothesis** — he argued the Priestly source (P) predates Deuteronomy (D), reversing the consensus. His work was enormously influential in Israeli scholarship and remains a reference point.

**Also in this tradition:** William F. Albright (Johns Hopkins), David Noel Freedman (Michigan/UCSD), John Bright (Union Theological Seminary), R.K. Harrison (Wycliffe College). All argued for early Mosaic monotheism, though this position has largely been abandoned in critical scholarship.

### Benjamin D. Sommer — *Divine Fluidity*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | Jewish Theological Seminary (Professor of Bible and Ancient Semitic Languages) |
| **Education** | PhD, University of Chicago |
| **Key Work** | *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (Cambridge University Press, 2009) |

**Core Argument:** Sommer offers a genuine third way. He argues that ANE and early Israelite thought understood gods as having "fluid selves" — able to inhabit multiple bodies simultaneously while remaining in heaven. This is NOT polytheism; it's a different understanding of how a single deity can be present in multiple locations.

He identifies three "bodies of God" in the Hebrew Bible:
1. **Fluidity model** (J and E sources): God has multiple simultaneous manifestations — the *malakh* (angel), the *kavod* (glory), the angel of the LORD who IS Yahweh yet distinct
2. **Anti-fluid model** (D and P sources): God is transcendent with one localized presence; the Temple houses God's "name," not God's body
3. **Priestly polemic:** P deliberately rejected the older fluidity model because it "bled too easily into polytheism"

**Between Kaufmann and Smith:** Early Israel was monotheistic but understood God's presence differently than later theology. What looks like "polytheistic remnants" to Smith is, in Sommer's view, a coherent theology of divine fluidity that was later suppressed but survived in the Shekhinah, Kabbalistic sefirot, and Christian Trinitarian theology.

Cambridge University Press publication gives Sommer's work serious academic weight. He's not a conservative apologist — he's a mainstream Jewish scholar offering a genuinely different reading of the same data.

### Richard Elliott Friedman — *The Levite Exodus Hypothesis*

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Institution** | University of Georgia (Ann and Jay Davis Professor of Jewish Studies) |
| **Education** | ThD, Harvard University |
| **Key Works** | *Who Wrote the Bible?* (1987), *The Exodus* (2017) |

**Core Argument:** The Exodus was historical but involved ONLY the tribe of Levi. The Levites escaped Egypt, brought Yahweh worship with them, and integrated with Israelite tribes. Monotheism came through this specific historical group with a specific experience — not a gradual evolution from Canaanite polytheism.

**Evidence:** Only Levites have Egyptian names (Moses, Phinehas, Hophni, Hur). The Song of the Sea and Song of Deborah do not mention Levites. Levites have no tribal territory. The Ark has Egyptian design parallels.

**Timing:** Monotheism crystallized in the United Monarchy (10th c. BCE). Not as early as Kaufmann's Mosaic revolution, not as late as Smith's Exilic emergence.

Friedman studied under Cross at Harvard and is a major figure in biblical scholarship. His Exodus hypothesis has gained traction and represents a middle position between Kaufmann and Smith.

### The Yahweh-Alone Party Model

**Morton Smith** (Columbia University, 1915–1991), *Palestinian Parties and Politics that Shaped the Old Testament* (1971):
- The "Yahweh-alone" party was an intolerant, zealous minority
- The syncretistic "Yahweh-plus" majority worshipped Yahweh alongside Baal, Asherah, and the Queen of Heaven
- Prophetic critique, Deuteronomic reform, and biblical redaction were driven by the Yahweh-alone party's eventual victory
- This is NOT evolution from polytheism — it's a factional political-theological struggle

**Bernhard Lang** (University of Paderborn; formerly St. Andrews), *Monotheism and the Prophetic Minority* (1983):
- Three stages: polytheistic Yahweh worship → Yahweh-alone monolatry → exclusive monotheism
- The minority prophetic movement (Elijah, Hosea) eventually won — but only after the Exile

Both Smith and Lang complicate the simple "evolution" narrative by introducing factional dynamics.

### The Minimalist School (Copenhagen and Sheffield)

## The Sub-Debates: Evidence in Detail

### 1. The Kenite/Midianite Hypothesis

**For:** Yahweh originated among the Midianites/Kenites, a nomadic group south of Canaan. Moses encountered Yahweh through his Midianite father-in-law Jethro (Exodus 18). Egyptian references to "Shasu of Yhw" place Yahweh in the southern deserts. The oldest biblical poetry (Deut 33:2, Judges 5:4-5, Habakkuk 3:3) has Yahweh "marching from Edom."

**Against:** Christian Frevel argues inscriptions mentioning "YHWH of Teman" may only denote presence there at later times. Fleming argues Yahweh was NOT imported from outsiders but the name reflects Israel's own diverse origins.

**Scholars for:** Mark Smith, John Day, Frank Moore Cross, Robert Miller II
**Scholars against/complicating:** Daniel Fleming, Christian Frevel, some minimalists

### 2. The Kuntillet Ajrud Grammar Debate

The inscription reads: *lyhwh šmrn wl'šrth* — "to Yahweh of Samaria and to his asherah"

**Goddess interpretation (majority):** The possessive suffix on *'šrth* ("his Asherah") most naturally reads as a personal name. In parallel constructions, a personal name is expected.

**Cult object interpretation (minority):** The -h ending may be a grammatical form, not a possessive. "Asherah" may refer to the wooden cult pole, not the goddess. The pithos drawings identified by some as Yahweh and Asherah are now identified by seven major scholars as the Egyptian dwarf-god Bes.

**Key scholars on each side:**
- Goddess: Mark Smith, John Day, William Dever, Saul Olyan
- Cult object: Ziony Zevit, Erhard Blum, Richard Hess
- Drawings = Bes, not Yahweh/Asherah: Smoak and Schniedewind (2019)

### 3. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 — The Text-Critical Bomb

| Source | Reading | Date |
|--------|---------|------|
| Dead Sea Scrolls (4QDeutj) | "sons of God" (*bene elohim*) | c. 1st c. BCE |
| Septuagint (LXX) | "angels of God" (*angelon theou*) | c. 3rd c. BCE |
| Masoretic Text (MT) | "sons of Israel" (*bene yisrael*) | c. 10th c. CE |

**Virtually all textual critics** — including evangelical Michael Heiser — agree that "sons of God" is the original reading. The MT scribes altered it because it was theologically embarrassing: it depicts Elyon (the Most High) parcelling out nations to his divine sons, with Yahweh receiving Israel.

**The debate:** Does this show Yahweh was originally subordinate to El (Smith), or that Yahweh IS Elyon and these "sons" are created divine beings (Heiser)?

**Key scholars:** Emanuel Tov (Hebrew University, leading textual critic), Ron Hendel (UC Berkeley), Eugene Ulrich (Notre Dame, DSS editor)

### 4. Psalm 82 — The Divine Council in Action

"God stands in the divine assembly; in the midst of the gods he judges... I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you. Nevertheless, you shall die like men.'"

**Mainstream:** Yahweh judges other gods for injustice, sentences them to death, and inherits all nations. This is a "fossil of polytheistic theology" showing the transition to monotheism.

**Heiser:** These are rebellious divine beings (members of the council), not legitimate rival deities. They are sentenced to mortality because they failed their assignments.

**Rashi (medieval Jewish commentator):** The "gods" are human judges. Jesus quotes this interpretation in John 10:34.

**Frankel (2010):** The speaking voice in vv. 6-8 is actually El — preserving an El-Yahweh distinction the text's final form obscures.

### 5. The Elephantine Papyri — Polytheistic Yahwism in the Persian Period

**The evidence:** A Jewish military colony in 5th c. BCE Egypt maintained a temple to YHW/Yahu. Documents mention Anat-Yahu (the goddess Anat paired with Yahweh). The community invoked blessings from Baal, Asherah, and other deities. The Jerusalem high priest did not object to rebuilding their temple when it was destroyed (410 BCE).

**Significance:** This is polytheistic Yahwism surviving CENTURIES after monotheism supposedly emerged in the exile. Either the exile didn't produce uniform monotheism, or Elephantine represents a pre-reform religion preserved in isolation.

**Counter:** Lemche/Thompson argue this proves the Torah was written LATER (Hellenistic period). Others argue Elephantine was an isolated, unrepresentative colony. Anat-Yahu may be a personified attribute, not a separate deity.

**Key scholar:** Karel van der Toorn (Amsterdam) — argues Elephantine "preserves an older pre-reform religion."

### 6. Ugaritic Parallels — How Direct?

**The parallels:**
- El's divine council ↔ Yahweh's divine council
- Baal as cloud-rider ↔ Yahweh as cloud-rider (Psalm 68:4)
- Baal defeats Sea/Death ↔ Yahweh defeats Sea/Death
- Asherah as El's consort ↔ Asherah associated with Yahweh
- 70 sons of El ↔ "sons of God" in Deut 32:8

**Consensus (Smith, Day):** The parallels are so extensive and specific that direct borrowing is the only plausible explanation. Psalm 29 is widely regarded as a Baal hymn with Yahweh's name substituted.

**Counter:** Heiser argues shared imagery doesn't prove shared theology. Hess argues imagery is like language — the same symbols can be applied to different deities with different meanings.

### 7. Zoroastrian Influence on Jewish Monotheism

**The argument:** Persian Zoroastrianism was dualistic (good god vs. evil god) and influenced Jewish thought during and after the exile. The sudden emergence of radical monotheism, cosmic dualism (God vs. Satan), elaborate angelology, and resurrection of the dead in post-exilic Judaism all have Zoroastrian parallels.

**Supporting scholars:** Many critical scholars see Persian influence as significant.

**Counter:** Some argue the direction of influence is unclear or that Judaism developed these ideas independently. The dating of Zoroastrian texts is notoriously difficult.

### 8. Feminist Scholarship on Asherah

| Scholar | Institution | Contribution |
|---------|------------|-------------|
| **Susan Ackerman** | Dartmouth | Women's religious practice; household religion |
| **Carol Meyers** | Duke | Household religion; women as religious agents |
| **Phyllis Bird** | Garrett-Evangelical | Feminist critique; challenges male-centered scholarship |
| **Judith Hadley** | Villanova | Detailed study of Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions |

These scholars argue that male-centered biblical scholarship has systematically downplayed evidence of goddess worship. The Asherah evidence isn't just about "polytheism vs monotheism" — it's about recovering women's religious experience that was erased by the Deuteronomistic reformers.

## Where the Field Stands: A Summary

### What virtually everyone agrees on

1. Israelite religion changed substantially over time. Nobody argues it was static.
2. The Hebrew Bible contains language acknowledging a divine council / multiple divine beings (Psalm 82, Deut 32:8-9).
3. The Masoretic Text altered Deut 32:8 from "sons of God" to "sons of Israel" for theological reasons.
4. The archaeological record shows widespread worship of a goddess (Asherah) in Israel and Judah.
5. The terms "monotheism" and "polytheism" are too blunt. Most scholars prefer more nuanced categories (monolatry, henotheism, one-deity discourse).

### What divides the field

1. **Timing:** When did exclusive Yahweh worship / monotheism emerge? Moses? United Monarchy? Exile? Persian period?
2. **Mechanism:** Was it evolution/assimilation (Smith), factional victory (Morton Smith, Lang), historical event (Friedman), or revolution (Kaufmann)?
3. **Canaanite continuity:** Was Israelite religion thoroughly Canaanite (Smith), distinctively different (Block, Kaufmann), or somewhere in between (Sommer, Zevit)?
4. **Text vs. archaeology:** Should the biblical texts be given more weight (conservatives, Sommer, Friedman) or less (Dever, Finkelstein, minimalists)?
5. **Revelation:** Can a historical argument include revelation as a factor, or must it be excluded (methodological naturalism)?

### My assessment

The evolutionary consensus (Position 1) has the weight of evidence behind it. The convergence of archaeology, epigraphy, comparative ANE studies, and textual criticism makes it very difficult to maintain that Israel was monotheistic from the beginning or even from the early monarchy.

However, the consensus is not monolithic. Scholars like Sommer and Fleming offer genuinely different readings that don't fit the simple "evolution from polytheism" narrative but also don't retreat to confessional positions. The evidence at Kuntillet Ajrud is more ambiguous than popular presentations suggest. And the Elephantine community is a genuine anomaly that neither side has fully explained.

**The strongest single argument for the consensus**: Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (original reading). That the Masoretic scribes felt the need to ALTER this text tells you everything about how theologically dangerous it was. You don't censor something unless it says something you don't want people to read.

**The strongest single argument AGAINST a simple evolutionary narrative**: The speed and completeness of the transition. Going from a full pantheon to radical monotheism ("I am the LORD, there is no other") in roughly two centuries — during national catastrophe — is genuinely remarkable and has few historical parallels. Something unusual happened, whether you call it revelation or theological genius.
