# Fleming — Chapter 1: Introduction — The Search for Yahweh's Origins

Fleming sets out a radical reframing of the entire debate over Yahweh's origins. Rather than asking "where did Israel get Yahweh from?" (the Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis, dominant since 1872), he asks: what if Yahweh and Israel were embedded in overlapping but distinct social landscapes, and the divine name is evidence for the diverse background of Israel itself, not evidence of borrowing from outsiders? His target is not the idea that Yahweh has pre-Israelite roots — he affirms that. His target is the framework of "borrowing from foreigners" and specifically the southern desert origin that has defined the Midianite Hypothesis for 150 years.

## Claim 1: Yahweh was not "taken from outsiders" — the divine name reveals Israel's diverse origins, not foreign borrowing
→ [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]] | **fleming-ybi-1.1** | Confidence: medium-high
  Fleming argues the Egyptian Yhwʒ is a Shasu people-name (not a divine name), the Song of Deborah's battle narrative has a "people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh, Judg 5:13) without reference to Israel, and the Mesha Stele shows Gad independent of Israel. The "borrowing from foreigners" framework inverts the actual relationship: Yahweh wasn't imported TO Israel; Israel was partly constructed FROM Yahweh-people.
  Contradicts: [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]], [[claim-kenite-midianite-transmission-bridge-synthesis]], [[claim-yahweh-southern-desert-war-god-origin]], [[claim-yahweh-southern-storm-warrior-origin]]

## Claim 2: The Midianite Hypothesis survived by solving a structural problem in scholarship, not by the strength of its evidence
→ [[claim-midianite-hypothesis-survived-structural-utility-yahweh-canaan-puzzle]] | **fleming-ybi-1.2** | Confidence: high
  Fleming's meta-analysis: the hypothesis persisted despite radical transformations in biblical scholarship because it solved a structural puzzle — how Yahweh could be foreign to Canaan while Israel was culturally Canaanite. El/Elohim could be local Canaanite; Yahweh needed a separate origin to explain his absence from Ugarit. The framework became functionally useful for a different intellectual project than the one that birthed it.
  Contradicts: [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]]

## Claim 3: "Little Israel" was geographically limited, excluded Judah, and expanded terminologically over time
→ [[claim-little-israel-excluded-judah-terminological-expansion]] | **fleming-ybi-1.3** | Confidence: medium
  Fleming separates "greater Israel" from "little Israel" — the latter excludes Judah and its specific location remains elusive. If "Israel" is a moving target, then asking "where did Israel get Yahweh?" is the wrong question. Yahweh could have been associated with groups that eventually became Israel without ever being "borrowed" by a unified entity.
  Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]]

## Claim 4: "People of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh) in Judges 5:13 represents a pre-Israelite identity — Israel only added in the opening hymn
→ [[claim-am-yhwh-judges-5-pre-israelite-identity]] | **fleming-ybi-1.4** | Confidence: medium-high
  All eight references to Israel in Judges 5 appear in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11), per Milstein's "revision through introduction." The core battle narrative names participants (Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, etc.) without calling them "Israel." Mark Smith (2014) affirmed the observation but reads 'am Yhwh as also belonging to the introductory revision — a key point of disagreement.
  Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]]
  Contradicts: [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]]

## Claim 5: Southern geography of old poetry (Seir, Teman, Sinai) does not establish Yahweh's origin there — it is theophany, not origin memory
→ [[claim-southern-geography-theophany-not-origin-memory]] | **fleming-ybi-1.5** | Confidence: medium
  The old poetry (Judg 5:4-5, Deut 33:2, Hab 3:3, Ps 68:8-9) describes Yahweh's theophanic movement FROM the south, not worship BY southern peoples. The Ugaritic Baal myth provides the analogy — Baal is no more "from" Sapan than Yahweh is "from" Seir. Kuntillet Ajrud's "Yahweh of Teman" is a geographical epithet, not origin evidence. The Egyptian Seir link is late (Ramses II) and absent from the earlier Soleb list.
  Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]]
  Contradicts: [[claim-kenite-midianite-hypothesis-best-explanation]], [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]]

## Claim 6: Tiele (1872), not Ghillany, was the true creator of the Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis
→ [[claim-tiele-1872-true-creator-kenite-hypothesis]] | **fleming-ybi-1.6** | Confidence: high
  Fleming's archival correction: Tiele proposed a new direction against two earlier failed alternatives (Yahweh from Egypt via Moses; Yahweh as Canaanite god). Tiele focused on Rechabites and Kenites — Midian became central only later through Moses and Jethro. The hypothesis was born in the context of emerging comparative history of religions, not confessional apologetics.
  Supports: [[claim-midianite-hypothesis-survived-structural-utility-yahweh-canaan-puzzle]]

## Claim 7: Yahweh was "embedded in nearby spaces" to early Israel — both the "foreign" and "specifically southern" become unnecessary
→ [[claim-yahweh-embedded-nearby-spaces-not-distant-southern-origin]] | **fleming-ybi-1.7** | Confidence: low-medium
  Fleming's positive alternative: Shasu were associated with the entire inland back country "from Arabia north through Jordan and Syria" (p. 32), not exclusively the deep south. Both Israel and Yahweh appear to have belonged to the same landscape (Late Bronze/Iron I transition). This is the sketchiest part of the chapter — "nearby spaces" needs Ch 2 (Egyptian evidence) and Ch 6 (Judges 5) to gain specificity.
  Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]], [[claim-little-israel-excluded-judah-terminological-expansion]]

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Yahweh not "taken from outsiders" | MEDIUM-HIGH | Yhwʒ-as-people-name reading failing |
| 2: MH survived by structural utility | HIGH | Accusation of presentism |
| 3: "Little Israel" terminological expansion | MEDIUM | Merneptah Stele showing early "Israel" |
| 4: 'am Yhwh as pre-Israelite identity | MEDIUM-HIGH | Smith's reading that 'am Yhwh is also secondary |
| 5: Southern geography ≠ southern origin | MEDIUM | Convergence of independent witnesses to the south |
| 6: Tiele as MH creator | HIGH | Historiographical dispute only |
| 7: Yahweh embedded nearby, not distant | LOW-MEDIUM | Insufficient geographical specificity |

**Strongest section:** The meta-analysis of why the Midianite Hypothesis survived (pp. 33-36). Fleming's diagnosis that the hypothesis solved a structural problem created by the Canaanite-Israel continuity thesis is original and persuasive. He's not just arguing against the hypothesis; he's explaining its resilience.

**Weakest section:** The positive proposal of "nearby spaces" (pp. 36-37). This is where the book promises more than the Introduction can deliver. The geographical argument needs Ch 2 (Egyptian evidence) and Ch 6 (Judges 5) to land before "nearby" has content.
