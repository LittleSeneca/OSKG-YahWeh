# Fleming — Chapter 1: Introduction — The Search for Yahweh's Origins

Fleming sets out a radical reframing of the entire debate over Yahweh's origins. Rather than asking "where did Israel get Yahweh from?" (the Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis, dominant since 1872), he asks: what if Yahweh and Israel were embedded in overlapping but distinct social landscapes, and the divine name is evidence for the diverse background of Israel itself, not evidence of borrowing from outsiders? His target is not the idea that Yahweh has pre-Israelite roots — he affirms that. His target is the framework of "borrowing from foreigners" and specifically the southern desert origin that has defined the Midianite Hypothesis for 150 years.

---

## Claim 1: Yahweh was not "taken from outsiders" — the divine name reveals Israel's diverse origins, not foreign borrowing

**Author's claim:** "Fleming argues in contrast that Yahweh was not taken from outsiders. Rather, this divine name is evidence for the diverse background of Israel itself" (p. 3). "It is time to reassemble all the pieces of this familiar puzzle in a fresh framework, not seeking a god foreign to Israel but one that belonged to a political landscape in which Israel played a relatively small role" (p. 27).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Egyptian Yhwʒ of Shasu-land (14th c.) is a people-name, not a divine name — a Shasu subgroup defined by kinship (p. 42)
- The Song of Deborah's battle narrative has a "people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh, Judg 5:13) without reference to Israel — Israel is added only in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11), per Milstein's "revision through introduction" (p. 27)
- The Mesha inscription (mid-9th c.) refers to "the men of Gad" occupying land "since forever" without connection to Israel (p. 27)
- Groups like Ephraim, Benjamin, and Amalek appear as participants with "the people of Yahweh" in Judg 5:14 without clear relationship to Israel (p. 27)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The exegetical observation about Judges 5 (Israel only in the frame, not the core) is strong and testable. The Mesha evidence for Gad is solid. The Egyptian Yhwʒ-as-people-name reading is Fleming's distinctive contribution (developed in Ch 2) — if it holds, the entire framework shifts. But the reconstruction depends heavily on his reading of the Egyptian material and Judges 5.

**What's at stake:** If true, the entire 150-year paradigm collapses. Yahweh worship didn't need to be "imported" from Midian or the deep south. Yahweh was already present in the inland Levantine highlands among Shasu-related groups that eventually became part of Israel. This changes how we understand Israel's formation: not a people who adopted a foreign god, but a coalition that coalesced around (or alongside) an already-present deity.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone who holds the Midianite Hypothesis — Römer (2015), Blenkinsopp (2008), Smith (2001, 2012), van der Toorn (1999), and the "Berlin hypothesis" scholars (Levin, Pfeiffer, Müller) who reject southern origins but for different reasons (late dating of all biblical texts). The Berlin school argues Yahweh was always a local highland deity; Fleming shares their skepticism of the south but rejects their late-dating methodology.

**Alternative reading:** The standard Midianite reading: the Egyptian Yhwʒ IS a place-name derived from the divine name (the region named after the god worshipped there), and Judges 5's southern geography (Seir, Edom) confirms Yahweh's southern origins independent of the prose narratives about Moses.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reframing is elegant and overdue. The "borrowing from foreigners" model has always had a colonial-era flavor — as if Israel needed to be explained by contact with exotic others rather than by the complex internal dynamics of the region. His reading of Judges 5's redaction history (Israel only in the frame) is the strongest single piece of evidence, and it doesn't depend on his Egyptian interpretation. The weakest link is how much weight the entire argument places on the Yhwʒ-as-people-name reading — if specialists reject that, the foundation cracks.

## Claim 2: The Midianite Hypothesis has survived despite massive transformations in biblical scholarship because it solved a structural problem (how Yahweh could be foreign to Canaan yet Israel could be Canaanite)

**Author's claim:** "What is remarkable is that transformations in how the Bible is understood and in how the history of Israel is reconstructed have not dislodged the common conclusion about the southern origin of Yahweh" (p. 33). The hypothesis "suited well the constraints" of the new archaeological consensus that Israel was culturally Canaanite: El/Elohim could be local Canaanite, but Yahweh needed a separate origin to explain his absence from Ugarit and other Canaanite evidence (p. 35).

**Evidence presented:**
- Dating of biblical composition has shifted dramatically later (post-586 BCE for most texts), yet the Midianite Hypothesis persists (pp. 33-34)
- Archaeological consensus since the 1970s-80s: Israelites cannot be distinguished materially from Canaanites (p. 34, citing Dever, Finkelstein, Smith)
- Yahweh's absence from Ugaritic/Canaanite pantheon lists creates a puzzle that the Midianite Hypothesis solves (p. 35)
- The framework "offers a way to leave Israel in its Canaanite environment with an essentially Canaanite culture — and religion — while partaking of contact with its nomadic neighbors" (p. 36)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is an observation about the sociology of scholarship, not about ancient history. Fleming's diagnosis of why a 19th-century hypothesis survived 20th-century transformations is acute. The Midianite Hypothesis became functionally useful for a different intellectual project than the one that birthed it.

**What's at stake:** This is a meta-level claim about how scholarship works. It suggests the persistence of the Midianite Hypothesis owes more to its structural utility (solving the Yahweh/Canaan puzzle) than to the strength of its evidence. If Fleming is right, the field has been captive to a framework that outlived its evidential basis.

**Who disagrees:** Defenders of the Midianite Hypothesis would say it persists because the evidence genuinely converges on a southern origin — not because it's structurally convenient. Römer's 2015 monograph represents the most sophisticated current defense.

**Alternative reading:** The hypothesis persists because the evidence genuinely supports it. The Egyptian Yhwʒ + Seir combination, the archaic poetry's southern geography, and the Moses-Midian connection ARE three independent lines of evidence, not a convenience.

**My assessment:** Fleming's meta-analysis is one of the chapter's strongest contributions. He's not caricaturing his opponents — he acknowledges the Midianite Hypothesis "is still the right place to start" (p. 24). But his diagnosis of why it survived is devastating: it solved a problem created by the Canaanite-Israel continuity thesis in a way that left the intellectual furniture undisturbed.

## Claim 3: "Little Israel" was geographically limited and excluded Judah; the name "Israel" expanded terminologically over time

**Author's claim:** "We begin by separating 'greater Israel' from what we call 'little Israel,' neither one to consider a fixed entity... Little Israel certainly excludes Judah, but its specific location and extent remain elusive" (p. 22). "Earliest Israel is first visible in the Bible linked to distinct kingdoms identified with Saul and David in the southern central highlands and with the town of Tirzah further north" (p. 22).

**Evidence presented:**
- Work developed with Lauren Monroe (Monroe forthcoming; Fleming forthcoming)
- Finkelstein (2019) likewise considers Israel to have grown in geographical extent
- The Song of Deborah lists participants without interest in Jerusalem or Judah (p. 26)
- This is part of a broader archaeological conversation (Near Eastern Archaeology 82/1, 2019)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The general point that "Israel" was not a fixed entity and expanded over time is well-supported archaeologically. The specific reconstruction of Saul-David + Tirzah as earliest Israel is more speculative. Monroe's work is still in progress.

**What's at stake:** If "Israel" is a moving target, then asking "where did Israel get Yahweh?" is the wrong question. Yahweh could have been associated with groups that eventually became Israel without ever being "borrowed" by "Israel" as a unified entity. This dissolves the foreign/indigenous binary.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional biblical scholarship that treats "Israel" as a unified entity from the conquest/settlement period. Also, the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE) already uses "Israel" as a people-name — though Fleming would note the determinative shows a people-group, not a territorial state.

**Alternative reading:** The Merneptah Stele already attests "Israel" as a recognizable entity in the late 13th century, so the terminological expansion argument may be overstated.

**My assessment:** The "little Israel" concept is useful heuristically but risks becoming a black box. Fleming acknowledges the location is "elusive." The key point — that "Israel" wasn't always what it became — is sound. The specific geography is less important to his argument than the principle that terminological expansion happened.

## Claim 4: The Bible's "people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh) in Judges 5:13 represents a pre-Israelite or non-Israelite identity

**Author's claim:** "The central battle narrative evokes the god first to identify the 'people of Yahweh' who will win victory and then to curse those who stay at home (Judg 5:13, 23). This 'people of Yahweh' name the alliance of groups to follow without reference to Israel, which is only linked to the battle by addition of the opening hymn in verses 2-11" (p. 40).

**Evidence presented:**
- All eight references to Israel in Judges 5 appear in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11), per Sara Milstein's (2016) "revision through introduction" framework (p. 27)
- Mark Smith (2014) affirmed and built on this observation
- Smith does not prefer Fleming's reading that the battle's participants shared identity as "the people of Yahweh" — Smith sees the 'am Yhwh as also belonging to the introductory revision (p. 27, n.11)
- The groups named (Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, Naphtali) are not called "Israel" in the core narrative

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The redaction-critical observation (Israel only in the frame) is strong and attested by multiple scholars. But whether the core narrative's participants identified as "people of Yahweh" rather than by some other collective name is more contested — Smith disagrees with Fleming on this point. The 'am Yhwh phrase in v. 13 is itself textually and interpretively disputed.

**What's at stake:** This is one of Fleming's two "principal items" of evidence (alongside the Egyptian texts). If 'am Yhwh is a pre-Israelite identity, then Yahweh defined a people before Israel did — and some of those people became Israel. This inverts the Midianite Hypothesis: Yahweh wasn't imported TO Israel; Israel was partly constructed FROM Yahweh-people.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (2014: 245-46) considers the 'am Yhwh passages part of the introductory revision, not the original battle account. Fritz (2006) similarly sees the battle as not involving Yahweh originally.

**Alternative reading:** The 'am Yhwh is an Israelite self-designation added by the same redactor who added the "Israel" frame. The original battle poem celebrated human warriors without divine involvement or collective identity labels.

**My assessment:** This is Fleming's most creative and most vulnerable claim. He's honest that Smith disagrees. The claim depends on (a) the redaction-critical separation holding up and (b) the 'am Yhwh belonging to the older layer. If Smith is right that 'am Yhwh is also secondary, this pillar weakens substantially. The whole argument is developed in Ch 6 — I'll need to see the full case there.

## Claim 5: The southern geography of the old poetry (Seir, Teman, Sinai) does not establish Yahweh's origin there — it attributes power in the wilderness without attaching worship to a population

**Author's claim:** "In my analysis, Yahweh's residence in distant southern realms and his worship as Yahweh of Teman attribute to the god a power in the southern wilderness without attaching his worship to a population there. The only indication of a population would come with the earlier Yhwʒ group in the company of Seir in the 13th century, and we will see in Chapter 2 that this geography is far from secure" (p. 36).

**Evidence presented:**
- The old poetry (Judg 5:4-5, Deut 33:2, Hab 3:3, Ps 68:8-9) describes Yahweh's theophanic movement FROM the south, not worship BY southern peoples (p. 31)
- The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (c. 800 BCE) refer to "Yahweh of Teman" — a geographical epithet, not evidence of origins (p. 36)
- The Egyptian list linking Yhwʒ to Seir is from the later Ramses II text; the earlier Amenhotep III list from Soleb lacks the Seir connection (p. 37)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Fleming is right that "coming from the south" and "originating in the south" are different claims. But the convergence of Egyptian, poetic, and inscriptional evidence ALL pointing south is hard to dismiss as coincidence. The question is whether this convergence indicates origin or simply a shared trope of divine theophany from the wilderness.

**What's at stake:** If the southern geography is a theophanic motif rather than an origin memory, the Midianite Hypothesis loses its strongest non-biblical line of evidence. The Egyptian texts become evidence for Shasu identity rather than southern geography.

**Who disagrees:** Essentially all proponents of the Midianite Hypothesis, who read the southern geography as preserving genuine memory of Yahweh's origin point. Römer (2015: Ch 2-3) gives the southern geography substantial weight. Cross (1973: 60-75) built his entire "Yahweh as epithet of El" argument around the southern league concept.

**Alternative reading:** The convergence of independent traditions (Egyptian, poetic, epigraphic) all pointing to the same southern region is unlikely to be coincidental. The most parsimonious explanation is that Yahweh genuinely originated among southern peoples and the poetry preserves this memory.

**My assessment:** Fleming is setting up a crucial distinction that many scholars blur: "comes from the south" ≠ "was first worshipped in the south." A theophany tradition could develop for theological reasons (the wilderness as locus of divine power) rather than historical ones. But the convergence problem is real: if it's all a coincidence, that's a lot of coincidence. Ch 2 and Ch 4 will need to carry this weight.

## Claim 6: Tiele (1872), not Ghillany, was the true creator of the Midianite/Kenite Hypothesis

**Author's claim:** "I found that its creator was not Friedrich Ghillany but Cornelis Tiele (1872) and that Tiele's interpretation was much less concerned to validate the accounts of Moses as founding figure than the work of many who followed" (p. 39).

**Evidence presented:**
- Tiele (1872) proposed a new direction against two earlier alternatives: Yahweh from Egypt via Moses, and Yahweh as a Canaanite god (p. 29)
- Tiele focused on Rechabites and Kenites, with Midian becoming central only later through focus on Moses and Jethro (pp. 29, 39)
- The hypothesis was born in the context of emerging Egyptology and Assyriology — Tiele was motivated by the new comparative history of religions (pp. 29-30)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is a historiographical claim, verifiable by consulting Tiele's 1872 publication.

**What's at stake:** Low for the historical argument, but significant for understanding how the hypothesis evolved. Tiele's version was more modest and less Moses-centric than what followed. The later Moses-Midian centering may have distorted the original insight.

**Who disagrees:** Those who credit Ghillany with the hypothesis.

**Alternative reading:** The priority dispute is minor; what matters is the mature form of the hypothesis, not its earliest expression.

**My assessment:** Fleming's historiographical precision is characteristic — he wants to show that the hypothesis changed substantially over time, becoming more attached to the Exodus narrative than its creator intended. This supports his larger argument that the hypothesis has been adapted to serve changing intellectual needs rather than being continuously confirmed by evidence.

## Claim 7: Yahweh was "embedded in nearby spaces" to early Israel, not distant from it — the "foreign" and "specifically southern" become unnecessary

**Author's claim:** "I will propose that the two names — Israel and Yhwʒ — and the populations associated with them were probably embedded in nearby spaces in the Levantine highlands and inland east, whatever their back-stories" (p. 36). "Rather than explain Yahweh as foreign to what became Israel, needing importation... this approach transforms the social landscape into which both Israel and Yahweh were embedded so that both the 'foreign' and the specifically southern become unnecessary" (p. 37).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Shasu were associated with the entire inland back country "from Arabia north through Jordan and Syria" (p. 32), not exclusively the deep south
- Both Israel and Yahweh appear to have belonged to the landscape of southern Palestine while Egypt was still present (Late Bronze/Iron I transition) (p. 36)
- The Mesha stele places "men of Gad" in territory later associated with Israel, without connection to Israel (p. 27)
- Neither name appears in connection with the other in the earliest evidence (p. 36)

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is the positive proposal that replaces the Midianite Hypothesis, and it's the sketchiest part of the chapter. "Nearby spaces" in the "Levantine highlands and inland east" is a big area. Much depends on the specific readings developed in later chapters.

**What's at stake:** This is Fleming's alternative framework. If it's right, the entire "origins" debate was asking the wrong question in the wrong geographical frame. Yahweh wasn't borrowed from far away; he was always nearby, among groups that eventually became Israel.

**Who disagrees:** Both the Midianite Hypothesis camp (southern origin) and the Berlin hypothesis camp (local highland origin from the start, no Shasu connection). Fleming is charting a third path: Shasu connection but NOT distant southern origin.

**Alternative reading:** The evidence for Shasu presence across the inland Levant doesn't mean Yhwʒ specifically was in the central highlands. The one geographical clue we have (Seir in the Ramses II list) points south, and all the biblical poetry points south. Fleming's "nearby" proposal requires setting aside the only geographical data we have.

**My assessment:** This is the chapter's most ambitious and least-supported claim. Fleming is essentially asking for a paradigm shift on the basis of (a) a new reading of the Egyptian evidence (Ch 2) and (b) a new reading of Judges 5 (Ch 6). The positive proposal is thin here because the book is structured to build it cumulatively. Judgment must wait for the full argument.

**Strongest section:** The meta-analysis of why the Midianite Hypothesis survived (pp. 33-36). **Weakest section:** The positive proposal of "nearby spaces" (pp. 36-37). This is where the book promises more than the Introduction can deliver. The geographical argument needs Ch 2 (Egyptian evidence) and Ch 6 (Judges 5) to land before "nearby" has content.
