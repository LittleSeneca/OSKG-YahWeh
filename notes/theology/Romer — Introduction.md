# Römer, The Invention of God — Introduction

---

## Claim 1: The God of the Bible was not always "the only God"

**Römer's claim:** "It is now beyond doubt that the god of the Bible was not always 'unique,' the one-and-only God." The Bible itself preserves traces of a polytheistic past. The journey from one god among many to THE only God is "the basic, and theologically fundamental, enigma that this book attempts to illuminate."

**Evidence presented:**
- **Judges 11:24:** Jephthah treats Chemosh as a real god on equal footing with Yhwh: "Do you not possess that which Chemosh, your god, has given into your possession? And shall we not possess that which our God has given into our possession?" Each people has its own tutelary deity. Yhwh is Israel's; Chemosh is Moab's. Neither is said to be the only god.
- **Deuteronomy's warnings:** The audience is repeatedly exhorted not to follow other gods — "without it ever being asserted that these gods did not exist or were not real." You don't warn against something you think is imaginary.
- **Exodus 6:2-3:** Yhwh reveals himself to Moses as a previously unknown god — "he himself tells Moses that this is the first time he has manifested himself under his real name." This is a trace of the historical fact that Yhwh was not always Israel's god.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The biblical polytheistic traces are unambiguous. Even conservative scholars acknowledge that the Hebrew Bible acknowledges the existence of other deities in some passages. The debate is about what this means, not whether the passages exist.

**What's at stake for faith:** This is the foundation Römer lays for the entire book. If the Bible itself admits that other gods were real to its earliest authors, then monotheism is not the original biblical position — it's a LATER development within the biblical tradition itself. This means you can't appeal to "what the Bible teaches" as a monolithic thing. The Bible teaches multiple things at different stages.

**Who disagrees:** Heiser would say these passages reflect the "divine council" — created divine beings subordinate to Yhwh, not rival gods. Traditional theologians would say Deuteronomy's warnings are about the temptation of idolatry, not an acknowledgment that other gods exist. Sommer would say Jephthah's statement reflects popular belief, not the theology of the biblical authors.

**Alternative reading:** These passages could reflect rhetorical accommodation — the biblical authors speaking in terms their polytheistic audiences would understand, without endorsing the reality of other gods. Jephthah is a character in a story, not a mouthpiece for the narrator's theology.

**My assessment:** Römer is right that the Bible preserves polytheistic traces. The cumulative weight of Judges 11:24, Deuteronomy's warnings, and Exodus 6:2-3 is hard to dismiss as mere rhetoric. But he's claiming more than the evidence strictly proves when he says "it is now beyond doubt." What's beyond doubt is that the Bible CONTAINS polytheistic-sounding passages. What's NOT beyond doubt is that the biblical authors themselves were polytheists. That depends on how you date and interpret each passage — exactly the work the rest of the book will do.

## Claim 2: Yhwh was originally a southern desert/war god who gradually became Israel's god

**Römer's claim:** Yhwh "probably had his origin somewhere in the 'South,' between the Negev and Egypt. Originally he was a god of the wilderness, of war and storms, but gradually through a series of small steps he became the god of Israel and Jerusalem." After the catastrophe of 587, he became "the one god, creator of heaven and earth, invisible and transcendent."

**Evidence presented in the Introduction:** This is a programmatic claim — Römer previews the argument the whole book will make. The specific evidence is promised for later chapters (2-3 on the southern origin, 4-5 on becoming Israel's god, 12 on becoming THE only God).

**Confidence:** Preview only — cannot assess until the evidence chapters are evaluated.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Yhwh's journey went: desert war god → national god of Israel → the only God, then monotheism is not a revelation FROM God but a human CONSTRUCTION about God. The God of Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology is the end product of a historical process. Whether that process was guided by God or was purely human is a theological question Römer doesn't answer — but he makes it impossible to claim that monotheism dropped from heaven fully formed.

---

## Claim 3: The Bible must be analyzed historically, "without preconception, just like any other document from antiquity"

**Römer's methodological claim:** "The authors of the various books of the Bible are obviously not impartial witnesses, but rather are very keen to impose on readers their vision of history and of the god of Israel." Therefore, the Bible must be subjected to the same critical analysis as any other ancient document. Textual evidence must be "compared with the archaeological, epigraphic, and iconographic facts."

**The key distinction:** Römer insists the biblical narratives are "not inventions proceeding simply from the minds of intellectuals seated in their comfortable chairs." They are "literature of tradition" — received from predecessors, freely transformed, but resting on "certain archaic kernels of fact" that preserve "traces of memory" of distant events.

**This is Römer's middle position between maximalism and minimalism:**
- AGAINST maximalists (who take the Bible at face value): The texts are ideologically shaped, late, and not objective history
- AGAINST minimalists (who say the texts tell us nothing about pre-exilic Israel): The texts preserve genuine historical memories, however distorted

> "It seems to me perfectly legitimate to reconnect to the older tradition of historical interpretation of the Bible, which was still widely cultivated at the start of the twentieth century... but which has been relatively neglected since the 1970s."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Römer's "traces of memory" methodology is defensible but hard to verify. How do you distinguish a genuine archaic kernel from a later invention that LOOKS archaic? He's more optimistic than Smith about what can be recovered from late texts.

**What's at stake for faith:** If Römer is wrong and the minimalists are right, the entire reconstruction of pre-exilic Israelite religion collapses. We can't say anything about Yhwh before the Persian period. Römer's entire book depends on his middle position being viable.

**Who disagrees:** European minimalists (Schmid, Kratz, the Copenhagen school) would say Römer is too optimistic. Conservatives would say he's too skeptical — the texts ARE reliable history. Smith occupies a position similar to Römer's but is more cautious.

**Alternative reading:** The minimalist position: the Pentateuchal narratives are Persian-period ideological constructions. Their "archaic kernels" are unfalsifiable — we can't prove they exist, and we can't prove they don't. Any historical reconstruction built on them is speculation disguised as scholarship.

**My assessment:** This is the single most important methodological claim in Römer's book, and it's also the most vulnerable. He's trying to have it both ways — the texts are ideologically shaped AND they preserve historical memory. Both things can be true, but proving the second part is extremely difficult. Track whether Römer ever acknowledges when the evidence is insufficient to distinguish memory from invention.

## Claim 4: "Invention" does not mean fabrication — it means progressive construction

**Römer's claim:** The title is provocative but Römer immediately clarifies: "When we speak, then, of 'the invention of God,' we should not imagine either that a group of Bedouins met one day and huddled around an oasis to create a god for themselves, or that some scribes, much later, invented Yahweh out of whole cloth." Rather, "invention" means "a progressive construction arising out of a particular tradition... a series of sedimentary strata gradually laid down over the course of time, which is then sometimes disrupted by historical events."

**The key metaphor:** Sedimentary geology. Layers accumulate. Sometimes earthquakes disrupt the layers and something new emerges. The conception of God was "continually revised in the light of particular, changing social and historical contexts."

**Römer calls this "collective invention":** Not one person's project, but a communal process over centuries.

**Confidence:** HIGH that this is a useful framing. It captures the gradual, cumulative nature of theological development without the pejorative connotations of "fabrication" or "fiction." It's also honest about the role of historical contingency.

**What's at stake for faith:** This framing leaves room for divine involvement (God could work through the geological processes of history) without requiring it. Whether you see God's hand in the strata or just the strata is up to you. Römer the historian doesn't answer. But he makes it clear that the strata ARE there — the development happened.

## Claim 5: The Documentary Hypothesis is obsolete; Deuteronomy dates to ~620 BCE; oldest Pentateuchal traditions to ~8th century

**Römer's claim:** "Along with most European scholars, we think it is no longer possible to accept the 'documentary theory'" — the classic J, E, D, P model. However, he retains two key dates: the first version of Deuteronomy around 620 BCE, and the assumption that "the oldest traditions in the Pentateuch (about Jacob, Moses, and later Abraham) had been written down sometime around the eighth century."

**The scroll recopying argument:** "No book, or more exactly no scroll, of the Bible was definitively formulated in one fell swoop. Papyrus and vellum are not durable materials... Their content, therefore, had to be recopied every few decades on new scrolls or sheets. Each time recopying took place, it was possible to add or suppress material, or to introduce modifications in the text."

**This is a crucial difference from Smith.** Smith retained the Documentary Hypothesis (cautiously, with quotation marks). Römer explicitly rejects it. This reflects the European vs. American divide in biblical scholarship. European scholarship has largely abandoned the classic four-source theory; American scholarship (and Smith) has been more reluctant.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the dating. Deuteronomy ~620 is widely accepted (linked to Josiah's reform). The 8th-century date for the oldest traditions is more contested. The rejection of the Documentary Hypothesis is the European consensus but not universally shared.

**What's at stake for faith:** If the Pentateuch's oldest written traditions only go back to the 8th century, and most texts are even later, then we have NO contemporary written sources for the first 400+ years of Israelite history (1200-800 BCE). Everything we think we know about early Israelite religion is reconstructed from much later texts. The uncertainty is enormous.

## Claim 6: Terminology matters — "Israel," "Judah," "Jew," and "Canaan" have specific historical meanings that must not be confused

**Römer's claim:** Using the right terms prevents retrojecting later ideas onto earlier periods.

**His definitions:**
- **"Israel"** originally meant the northern kingdom (capital Samaria) until 722. Only after Assyria destroyed it did "Israel" become a theological term for all Yhwh-worshippers. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1210) attests "Israel" as an ethnic group in Ephraim, not a kingdom.
- **"Judah"** was the southern kingdom (capital Jerusalem) until 587, then the Persian province "Yehud."
- **"Jews" / "Judaism"** cannot be spoken of before the end of the Persian period (4th century BCE). Before that: "Israelites" or "Judeans."
- **"Canaan"** is a vague term for Syria-Palestine west of the Jordan. Sometimes neutral geographic term, sometimes pejorative ("Canaanites" = indigenous population to be displaced).

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. These distinctions are widely accepted in critical scholarship and are essential for clarity.

**What's at stake for faith:** If you retroject "Judaism" onto pre-exilic Israel, you create a false continuity. The religion of the Israelite monarchy was not Judaism — it was Israelite religion, which was polytheistic in practice and included elements (Asherah, high places, figurines) that later Judaism condemned. The "Jews" didn't exist yet.

## Methodological Assessment

Römer's Introduction does three things well:

1. **States the thesis clearly:** Yhwh was not always the only God; monotheism was a historical development.
2. **Acknowledges limitations:** "The results of our investigation cannot be more than hypothetical."
3. **Defines his methodological position:** Middle ground between maximalism (the Bible is reliable history) and minimalism (the Bible tells us nothing about pre-exilic Israel).

The vulnerability is the "traces of memory" claim. Römer needs his texts to preserve genuine historical information from periods for which we have no other sources. If that claim fails, the whole book fails with it. The Introduction acknowledges but does not resolve this tension.

**Compared to Smith's Introduction:** Römer is more confident, more accessible, and more willing to tell a story. Smith is more cautious, more text-critical, and more methodologically anxious. Both are honest about uncertainty. Römer is more fun to read. Smith is harder to challenge on specifics. Reading them together is ideal — Römer gives you the narrative arc; Smith gives you the footnotes.
