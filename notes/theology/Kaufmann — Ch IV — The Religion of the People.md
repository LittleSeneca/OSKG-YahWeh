# Kaufmann — Chapter IV: The Religion of the People

This is Kaufmann's most distinctive — and most contested — chapter. He confronts the evidence that critics use to argue popular Israelite religion was polytheistic and argues that Israel's "idolatry" was never genuine mythological polytheism. The chapter answers two questions: (1) Was popular religion national, limiting YHWH to Israel? (2) Was it mythological, conceiving YHWH in pagan terms? To both: the limitation was only of *favor and cult*, not of *dominion*; and the idolatry was *vestigial fetishism*, not pagan polytheism. His most radical conclusion: Israel's religious evolution ran from monotheism → monolatry, reversing the critical sequence.

---

## Claim 1: The Bible fights cultic practice, not mythological belief — and this silence is deafening

**Author's claim:** "The biblical battle with Israel's idolatry restricts itself entirely to matters of practice, to rites and cults. There is no battle over the fundamental question: the mythological conception of deity. The significance of this fact can hardly be exaggerated." If the popular religion had genuinely been mythological, the Bible would have had to fight that mythology — and it never does.

**Evidence presented:**
- "The Bible does not fight a mythical conception of YHWH either" (p. 122)
- The Bible explicitly opposes human sacrifice (Mic 6:7; Jer 7:31), idolatrous worship forms (II Kings 17:8), the idea that God goes back on his word (Num 23:19) — but "it has not a word against mythological conceptions of YHWH"
- "What mythical vestiges are found in the Bible (e.g., the defeat of Rahab and the dragon) are part of the belief of the biblical authors. There is no myth that is regarded with hostility, as an error that must be uprooted" (pp. 122-123)
- The supposed syncretism of YHWH and Baal is only titular: "Baal means 'lord'; from the mere fact that the people used it as an epithet... what can be learned about their religious beliefs?" (p. 123)
- "Crucial is the utter lack of evidence that YHWH was given any of the mythological attributes of Baal. We do not hear, for example, that he was mated with Ashtoreth. There is no trace of a battle against mythological accretions to the popular image of YHWH" (p. 123)
- No polemic against sacrifice as food of the god — the central pagan concept (p. 125, lines 143-145)
- Prophetic rhetoric assumes the people already know God doesn't need food: "Isaiah's 'I am sated with the burnt offerings of rams' clearly assumes that its irony is self-evident to the audience" (p. 125)
- The author of Psalm 50 "could hardly have dismissed the matter so lightly" if his audience believed YHWH ate sacrifices (p. 125)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The observation itself is powerful and often overlooked. The biblical prophets really DON'T argue against mythology, theogony, or divine biography — they argue against cultic practices. This is a genuine datum that needs explanation. Where Kaufmann's confidence wobbles is in assuming this silence can only mean popular religion was already non-mythological.

**What's at stake:** The entire nature of Israelite religion. If Kaufmann is right, the prophets were calling Israel back to a monotheism it already possessed, not fighting to establish monotheism against pagan popular belief. The "battle" was about cultic purity, not theological revolution.

**Who disagrees:** The silence could be explained differently. Smith (Origins) would argue that Israelite religion DID have mythological elements (divine council, Yahweh's combat with Sea) that the Bible retained and transformed rather than fought. Dever's archaeological evidence (Did God Have a Wife?) suggests popular practice involved consort worship — which Kaufmann would have to explain as "vestigial fetishism" rather than genuine belief in a divine pair.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's silence about mythological concepts of YHWH could reflect successful editorial purging, not historical absence. The Deuteronomistic editors systematically reshaped traditions. What Kaufmann takes as evidence of monotheism's antiquity could be evidence of the editors' thoroughness. Alternatively, Smith argues the Bible DOES contain mythological elements (the divine council, combat myths) — they were simply absorbed and transformed rather than polemicized against. The dragon/Leviathan imagery IS mythological; the Bible just doesn't treat it as "other gods."

**My assessment:** Kaufmann has identified a genuinely striking feature of the biblical corpus: the prophets thunder against idolatry, but their polemic is always about PRACTICE — "don't bow down to wood and stone" — never about THEOLOGY — "Baal didn't really defeat Yamm, that's a Canaanite lie." This IS different from how, say, early Christian apologists argued against paganism. The question is whether this silence reflects (a) monotheism's priority, as Kaufmann argues, or (b) the fact that Israelite "idolatry" was genuinely polytheistic but the biblical authors polemically chose to caricature it as fetishism to avoid dignifying pagan theology with engagement. I find Kaufmann's reading more interesting than the alternatives, but not decisively proven.

## Claim 2: The creation legends prove folk monotheism — they're too naive and non-mythological to be elite literary adaptations

**Author's claim:** "The folk character of the creation legends signifies that the people among which they were formed was monotheistic" (p. 123). These stories "could not have arisen among a polytheistic people, who conceived of YHWH as one of the gods, who knew and believed in pagan myths" (p. 124).

**Evidence presented:**
- Gunkel's error: assuming these are literary adaptations of pagan materials. "In antiquity adaptation implies an acceptance of and a belief in the material adapted" (p. 123)
- "A later adapter would hardly have left so many anthropomorphisms and dangerous ambiguities in his story" (p. 123)
- The stories have a "primitive, idyllic — one might almost say provincial — character... so different from the grand pagan myths of universal forces clashing in primeval times" (pp. 123-124)
- "The homely image of the serpent who is far removed from the later symbol of cosmic evil, of a God who strolls in the garden and sews shirts for Adam and Eve point to a literary genre nearer the folk tale" (p. 124)
- "And yet with all their primitiveness there is no real myth here: no theogony, no sexual differentiation or desire in the deity, no theomachy, no magic, no demonic realm" (p. 124)
- "Too naive and crude for the prophets, these can only have been creations of the people" (p. 124); they "lack all traces of ideas peculiar to later times" so "must be early creations" (p. 124)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The observation that Genesis 2-3 lacks theogony, theomachy, and divine sexuality IS correct and significant. But Kaufmann's conclusion that the stories "could not have arisen" in a polytheistic environment assumes what he's trying to prove.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israel's creation traditions are evidence of early monotheism or later monotheistic editing of originally polytheistic material. If Kaufmann is right, Genesis 2-3 isn't demythologized myth — it was never myth in the first place.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Genesis 1-3 as Israelite transformations of Mesopotamian material (Enuma Elish, Atrahasis). The obvious parallels — primordial chaos, divine rest, the garden, the serpent, the tree of life — suggest Israel didn't invent these motifs ex nihilo. The question is whether Israel's versions represent (a) monotheistic adaptation of inherited polytheistic material (the critical view) or (b) original folk creations in a pre-existing monotheistic culture (Kaufmann's view).

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's own evidence cuts both ways. If these stories are "too naive and crude for the prophets," their monotheism may be folkloric simplicity rather than theological conviction. A folk tale about a god walking in a garden isn't monotheism — it's a simple story that hasn't yet asked the question of other gods. Kaufmann reads the absence of polytheism as evidence of monotheism, but it could equally be evidence of theological naivete.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reading of the Genesis stories as genuinely folkloric rather than literarily adapted is one of his most appealing arguments. The homely quality of Genesis 2-3 IS different from the cosmic scope of Enuma Elish. But the absence of other gods in these stories proves at most that the STORIES are non-polytheistic, not that the CULTURE that produced them was monotheistic. A polytheistic culture can produce stories focused on a single deity without implying monotheism — it just implies narrative focus.

## Claim 3: The "argument from history" — Israel's idolatry disappeared after the exile, unlike every other nation that lost independence. This only makes sense if monotheism was already popular.

**Author's claim:** "The history of religions knows no parallel to such a phenomenon" (p. 134). When other nations lost political independence, their gods were demoted but not abandoned. Israel's idolatry vanished completely — and this "has only one adequate explanation: pre-exilic Israel must have already had a monotheistic faith that was not the property of a narrow elite, but of the entire nation" (pp. 134-135).

**Evidence presented:**
- National collapse normally strengthens pagan attachment — a pagan people would interpret exile as their god's defeat by stronger gods and would "embrace these foreign gods with doubled intensity" (p. 134)
- The Jerusalem women who worshipped the Queen of Heaven explicitly offered this alternative interpretation: Judah's calamity resulted from ABANDONING her cult (Jer 44:17-18) — proving a pagan interpretation was available
- Yet despite this alternative being available, "the overwhelming bulk of the people 'returned' to their God" (p. 133)
- "Even the priests, the nobility, and the royal household... forsake their idols and cleave to YHWH forever" (p. 133)
- "The imposition of a new religion upon an entire people always involves the assistance of and promotion by the temporal power. This is the lesson of the spread of Mazdaism, Christianity, and Islam" (p. 134)
- Yet in Israel, royal reformers (Asa, Jehu, Jehoash, Hezekiah, Josiah) ALL FAILED — "Only in exile, amidst pagan surroundings, without external compulsion, with scarcely a struggle, out of an inner transformation alone, the Jewish people abandon idolatry forever!" (p. 134)
- Later Judaism expressed astonishment at this transformation in the legend of the Men of the Great Synagogue capturing and killing "the Evil Yeṣer of idolatry" (b. Yoma 69b; Sanhedrin 64a)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the historical observation; MEDIUM on the inference. The disappearance of Israelite idolatry after the exile IS a remarkable historical datum that needs explanation. Kaufmann is right that this is not how conquered peoples typically behave. But his inference that this proves POPULAR monotheism is not the only possible explanation.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite monotheism was an elite achievement gradually imposed (the critical view) or a popular possession suddenly purified (Kaufmann's view). If Kaufmann is right, the exile didn't CREATE monotheism — it merely stripped away the vestigial idolatry that had clung to an already-monotheistic people.

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus sees the exile as the crucible in which monotheism was FORGED, not merely purified. The experience of national catastrophe, interpreted through prophetic theology, produced genuine monotheism for the first time. Kaufmann's own evidence — the Queen of Heaven worshippers in Jer 44 — shows that even in Egypt, some Jews maintained polytheistic practice. And the Elephantine community (see Claim 8) complicates the picture further.

**Alternative reading:** The exile didn't extinguish polytheism in Israel because it was shallow — it extinguished it because polytheism was tied to LAND and TEMPLE. Once the temple was destroyed and the people were in exile, the infrastructure of polytheistic worship (local shrines, high places, territorial deities) was destroyed. The surviving religion was necessarily portable and aniconic — which meant monotheistic YHWH worship. The phenomenon Kaufmann observes may reflect the selective survival of a portable religion, not the pre-existing popularity of monotheism. Moreover, the very people who assimilated and adopted Babylonian gods wouldn't appear in the biblical record — the returning community was self-selected for YHWH-devotion.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's most powerful historical argument. He's identified a genuine anomaly that the evolutionary model struggles to explain: why did Israel's idolatry die so completely, so quickly, without state enforcement? The critical answer — "the exile was a theological crisis that produced monotheism" — doesn't explain why the exiles drew THAT conclusion rather than the obvious pagan one (Marduk beat YHWH). But Kaufmann's inference of POPULAR monotheism goes further than the evidence requires. An alternative: the exile didn't prove the people were monotheistic — it proved the prophets' interpretation was sufficiently persuasive to a critical mass of the elite that they could reshape the surviving community. Not "the people were monotheists" but "the monotheistic interpretation captured the surviving institutions."

## Claim 4: Universalism and monotheism — Israel's national limitation of YHWH's cult does not make it pagan

**Author's claim:** "The view that the early popular religion was polytheistic or monolatristic... finds its chief support in certain passages... that appear to restrict YHWH's domain to his land or the affairs of his people. It is assumed that only later under the influence of literary prophecy were the narrow bounds of the popular religion broken... The error of this view arises out of the failure to distinguish adequately between the various meanings of religious universalism" (pp. 127-128).

**Evidence presented:**
- Universalism has TWO meanings: (1) the deity's DOMINION is worldwide, or (2) the deity's FAVOR and SELF-REVELATION are worldwide. "Since monotheism asserts that there is but one creator and ruler of the universe, it is perforce universalistic in the first sense. But there is no inner necessity that compels it to distribute the favor of the one God equally among all men" (pp. 127-128)
- Paganism made the same distinction: "Marduk is the sun-god, and as such is cosmic; yet he is particularly the god of Babylon. Jupiter is the sky-god, but he is particularly connected with Rome" (p. 128)
- The Pentateuch and historical books "consistently represent the world as divided into two realms, Israel and the nations, with Israel alone 'the portion of YHWH'" (p. 128)
- Deuteronomy goes so far as to assert YHWH "allotted" worship of the host of heaven to the heathen (Deut 4:19) — yet this is in a monotheistic context
- Jonah: YHWH is "God of heaven, who made sea and dry land" (1:9) and rules the sea, wind, fish, plants; has solicitude for pagan Nineveh — yet Jonah flees "from the presence of YHWH" (1:3, 10), meaning from the area of divine REVELATION, not from YHWH's power
- "David's complaint that his banishment from the land of YHWH entails the worship of foreign gods is echoed in sources whose monotheistic outlook is unimpeachable" (p. 129) — see the Deuteronomist's threat of exile (Deut 4:28; 28:64) and Jeremiah (16:13)
- The priestly writings, "uniformly conceded to be monotheistic," maintain territorial limitation: "The exclusive sanctity of the land of Israel is as fundamental a part of the priestly laws as the election of the people" (p. 129)
- Josh 22: the Transjordanian tribes fear losing their "portion" in YHWH outside the land; the rest are alarmed that building an altar on "impure soil is rebellion against YHWH" (pp. 129-130)
- Psalm 137: a Levite exile, "surely no polytheist," refuses to sing YHWH's songs on alien soil (p. 130)
- Naaman (II Kings 5:15ff.): recognizes "there is no God in all the earth except in Israel," yet takes two mules' burden of earth back to Aram to worship YHWH — "His monotheism is absolute" but territorial limitation remains in the cultic sphere (p. 130)
- Jephthah's "Chemosh your god" (Judg 11:24) is diplomatic language: "Jephthah merely uses language that the king will understand" — when speaking in his own terms, "YHWH alone judges both peoples" (pp. 131-132)

**Confidence:** HIGH on the conceptual distinction; MEDIUM on the historical claim that this distinction was operative in the popular mind. Kaufmann's distinction between dominion-universalism and favor-universalism is genuinely clarifying and is supported by many biblical texts.

**What's at stake:** Whether passages that seem to limit YHWH territorially (Gen 35:2ff.; Judg 11:24; I Sam 26:19; II Kings 5:17) are evidence of early polytheism/monolatry (the critical view) or evidence of monotheism with a national cultic limitation (Kaufmann's view).

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read these passages as survivals from a genuinely polytheistic/monolatrous period, later reinterpreted by monotheistic editors. Römer, for example, reads Jephthah's statement about Chemosh as reflecting a period when Israelites genuinely acknowledged the reality of other national gods. Smith sees the territorial limitation as evidence that early YHWH worship was functionally monolatrous.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's distinction is real, but he may be reading later theological sophistication back into earlier texts. The Naaman story, Jonah, and the Deuteronomist ALL reflect relatively late theological reflection. Using them to interpret earlier passages like Judg 11:24 assumes the later theology was always present. The alternative: early Israelite religion WAS genuinely monolatrous (YHWH rules Israel, other gods rule other nations), and later monotheistic theology reinterpreted this as limitation of cult/favor rather than dominion.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann at his best — making a genuinely illuminating conceptual distinction that resolves apparent tensions in the biblical text. His reading of Jonah is particularly good: Jonah's flight "from the presence of YHWH" can't mean flight from YHWH's power (since the whole book is about YHWH's universal power), so it must mean flight from YHWH's revelation/commission. The territorial-cultic limitation is real in the texts — Kaufmann just argues it was never a limitation of YHWH's DOMINION. That said, I'm skeptical that early Israelites made this distinction as cleanly as Kaufmann does. The conceptual sophistication required to distinguish "YHWH rules everywhere but is only worshipped here" from "YHWH is OUR god, other nations have THEIR gods" is considerable. It's more likely that the texts reflect a DEVELOPMENT from the latter to the former, with the fully monotheistic version retrojected onto earlier periods.

## Claim 5: Israel's "idolatry" was vestigial fetishism, not genuine polytheism — no mythology, no temples, no priests, no pantheon

**Author's claim:** "Israelite idolatry must have been sufficiently shallow for the shock of the catastrophe to uproot it without a struggle" (p. 135). "The popular idolatry was not authentic polytheism, with mythology, temples, and priesthoods. It was vestigial idolatry, a vulgar superstition of the sort that the ignorant level of monotheistic peoples practices to this day" (p. 141).

**Evidence presented:**
- The biblical editorial framework's sweeping indictment is exaggerated: "sins of particular groups are ascribed to the entire people" (p. 136). The historiographer "could not account for what had taken place... without sin. Had there been no sin he would have had to invent it" (p. 136)
- Saul's reign is "strangely free of taint" — he extirpates mediums but there's "nothing of an extermination of Baal worship." Baal worship "has simply disappeared" (pp. 135-136)
- David's reign: "idolatry is nonexistent" (p. 136)
- During Ahab/Jezebel: the Baal worshippers all fit in ONE temple (II Kings 10:19, 21) — "the sole temple of Baal, built by Ahab in Samaria" (p. 136)
- After Jehu, "no foreign gods are known by the book of Kings to have been worshiped in the north" (p. 136)
- In Judah, Athaliah's Baal cult consists of "one temple and one priest to Baal" (p. 136)
- Manasseh's reign is the FIRST in which "we hear of a more general contamination" (p. 136)
- Jeremiah's array is challenged by the people: "'I have not become defiled, I did not go after the Baals'" (Jer 2:23, cited p. 136)
- The popular idolatry in the literary prophets is FEATURELESS: "Ephraim is not accused of serving any specified foreign god; he inquires of his wood and staff (4:12), bows down to his handiwork (14:4)... There is no clearly defined pagan cult, no specific mythology, priests, or temples" (p. 143)
- Isaiah and Micah "name no foreign deity as an object of popular worship. Idolatry is always portrayed as the service of images of silver and gold, the handiwork of men" (p. 143)
- Jeremiah's telling phrase: "'other gods' which Israel 'knew not' (7:9; 19:4; 44:3). The last is a telling phrase; the idolatrous cults are obscure, featureless, not really known to Israel" (p. 144)
- The popular idolatry was "a magical, fetishistic, non-mythological worship of images, 'gods they knew not,' imported from abroad" (p. 144)
- "The information regarding the idolatry of the earlier periods accords well with this picture. In the original stories of Judges there is scarcely a hint of real foreign gods" (p. 144)
- The editorial framework speaks of "anonymous idol-worship" — "'Baals and Ashtoreths'... These images have neither priests, prophets, nor temples. Evidently they are the figurines that have been found in such numbers in Israelite Palestine" (pp. 144-145)
- Jacob's household surrenders "foreign gods and the rings" to be buried — these are teraphim (Gen 35:2ff.), treated with "indulgent humor" by the narrator (p. 145)
- David doesn't mind his wife's teraphim (I Sam 19:13) — "neither for the writers nor for their heroes are these teraphim and 'foreign gods' real deities" (p. 145)
- "Paganism was never a creative force in Israelite culture. National poetry, narrative, prophecy, ethics, and politics were all permeated with the belief in YHWH. Sufficient proof of this is the fact that no prophets of foreign gods ever arose in Israel" (p. 146)
- "Archaeological remains fail to show a distinctive native Israelite iconography... no sculpture developed in Israel... a result of the absence of myth" (p. 147)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Kaufmann makes a genuinely impressive case from the biblical texts themselves. But he systematically avoids engaging with the archaeological evidence that complicates his picture — the thousands of Judean pillar figurines, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, the standing stones. His definition of "genuine polytheism" may exclude Israelite practice by fiat.

**What's at stake:** The entire characterization of Israelite popular religion. If Kaufmann is right, the "idolatry" the prophets condemn was a shallow, superstitious practice — like a monotheist keeping a lucky charm — not evidence of genuine belief in multiple deities. If he's wrong, Israel was genuinely polytheistic through most of the monarchy.

**Who disagrees:** Dever's archaeological work directly challenges this: thousands of Asherah figurines found in domestic contexts suggest widespread goddess worship, not marginal superstition. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions bless "Yahweh and his Asherah" — a pairing that implies consort theology, not mere fetishism. Smith (Early History of God) documents the mythological content that Israelites shared with their neighbors. Römer argues the epigraphic evidence shows genuine polytheistic belief.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that Israelite idolatry lacked the full mythological apparatus of Mesopotamian or Canaanite religion (no native theogonies, no divine biographies, no mythological epics). But this doesn't mean it wasn't polytheism — it means it was a DIFFERENT KIND of polytheism, one focused on practice rather than theology. Most ancient polytheism wasn't philosophically elaborated. People worshipped multiple deities without necessarily having creation myths about all of them. Kaufmann's requirement that "genuine polytheism" include theogony and mythology sets a bar that most actual polytheistic practice in the ancient world wouldn't meet.

**My assessment:** This is Kaufmann's signature argument, and it has genuine force. His observation that the prophetic polemic is consistently against "wood and stone," "gods they knew not," "handiwork of men" — rather than against named deities with mythologies — does suggest something different from, say, Greek philosophical critique of the Olympians. But Kaufmann's argument has a critical weakness: he defines "genuine polytheism" in a way that requires mythological elaboration, then declares that Israelite practice doesn't meet that definition. But if your neighbor keeps an Asherah figurine, prays to the Queen of Heaven for good harvests, and believes these beings can bless or curse — does it matter whether they have a theogony for them? From the perspective of religious PRACTICE, that's polytheism. Kaufmann's distinction between "vestigial fetishism" and "genuine polytheism" may be a distinction the practitioners themselves wouldn't recognize. The Kuntillet Ajrud pithos — "I bless you by Yahweh of Samaria and by his Asherah" — is the hardest evidence for Kaufmann to explain away. If Asherah is merely a fetish, why is she paired with YHWH in a blessing formula?

## Claim 6: No genuine syncretism occurred — YHWH was never merged with Baal or any other deity

**Author's claim:** "The conventional opinion that the popular religion was Canaanized and worshiped a syncretistic YHWH-Baal is groundless. Syncretism (the combining of two gods) could have arisen only out of the mutual influence of two cults. But there is no record of an independent, thriving cult of Baal in the period of the judges, nor during the monarchy before the time of Omri" (p. 141).

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal names (Eshbaal, Meribbaal, Beeliada) only show "titular" use — Baal means "lord." "From the mere fact that the people used it as an epithet... what can be learned about their religious beliefs?" (p. 123)
- The Baal worship of Ahab's time was "altogether new. Its votaries were Jezebel's Sidonian entourage. It was a special phenomenon, entirely apart from the worship of YHWH" (pp. 141-142)
- "The worshipers of Baal and YHWH are two separate groups (II Kings 10:23). Jezebel kills the prophets of YHWH... But Jezebel does not sacrifice to Baal on YHWH's altars nor erect symbols of the Baal cult in the temples of YHWH" (p. 142)
- Elijah's challenge: "If YHWH is God, follow him; but if Baal, follow him" (I Kings 18:21) — "The underlying assumption is that only one is God, and that a decision must be made as to which one it is. The audience is assumed to have a monotheistic, not a syncretistic, outlook" (p. 142)
- "The only attested instance of syncretism is to be found in Manasseh's association of pagan deities with YHWH in his own temple. But his was a royal idiosyncrasy which had no popular roots" (p. 142)
- "For genuine syncretism presupposes an essential parity between the deities to be amalgamated... But when, as in Israel, there is a belief that only one being is supreme, that he is alone of his kind, and that all other cult objects are magical or lesser beings, no genuine syncretism is possible" (p. 146)
- Hosea's supposed syncretism is imaginary: "There is no commingling of YHWH and Baal; on the contrary, the people are blamed for having forgotten YHWH and gone after other gods (Hos. 2:15; 3:1). The prophet always speaks of two separate realms" (p. 143 n.)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM on the biblical texts; LOW against the archaeological evidence. Kaufmann's reading of the Elijah narrative is genuinely interesting: the either/or logic does suggest the people aren't already blending YHWH and Baal. But the Kuntillet Ajrud evidence shows YHWH paired with Asherah in a way that looks very much like syncretism.

**What's at stake:** Whether Israelite religion was a hybrid of Canaanite and Yahwistic elements (the critical view) or a fundamentally distinct religion that occasionally suffered foreign incursions (Kaufmann's view).

**Who disagrees:** Smith (Early History of God) argues that YHWH absorbed characteristics of El, Baal, and other Canaanite deities — not by formal syncretism but by convergence and appropriation. This is a more nuanced version of the syncretism argument than the one Kaufmann attacks. Day (Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan) documents extensive borrowing. Dever's archaeological evidence of Asherah worship in Israelite contexts suggests popular syncretism regardless of prophetic opposition.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is attacking a straw man. The critical claim isn't that Israelites consciously merged YHWH and Baal into a single hybrid deity. It's that Israelite religion gradually absorbed Canaanite elements — divine council imagery from El, storm theophany from Baal, possibly a consort from Asherah — while maintaining YHWH's distinct identity. This isn't "syncretism" in Kaufmann's narrow sense (amalgamation of two gods into one), but it IS cultural and religious borrowing that complicates the picture of Israelite religion as absolutely different from paganism.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann is right that the biblical texts don't depict the kind of formal syncretism he's arguing against. The Baal cult under Ahab/Jezebel IS depicted as alien and opposed to YHWH worship, not blended with it. But Kaufmann defines syncretism too narrowly. Religious borrowing doesn't require formal amalgamation — it can happen through adoption of imagery, epithets, rituals, and divine relationships. The fact that no biblical text says "YHWH-Baal" doesn't mean there was no Canaanite influence on YHWH's characterization. And the Asherah evidence from Kuntillet Ajrud — "Yahweh and his Asherah" — looks like syncretism in a form Kaufmann doesn't adequately address: not merging YHWH with another god, but giving him a consort.

## Claim 7: The evolution ran from monotheism to monolatry — not the reverse

**Author's claim:** "Not from monolatry to monotheism, as is the conventional theory, but from monotheism to monolatry runs the path of Israel's religious evolution" (p. 147). The basic monotheistic IDEA was present from the beginning; the biblical battle was to realize its cultic CONSEQUENCES.

**Evidence presented:**
- "The biblical struggle with idolatry restricts itself entirely to the area of cult and ritual. This is decisive proof that from earliest times Israel was no longer a genuinely pagan people" (p. 147)
- "The struggle against idols, calves, pillars, divination, and magic may be said to involve not monotheism but monolatry; at issue is not belief in one God, but the exclusive worship of him" (p. 145-146)
- "The biblical writers take for granted that faith in one God is common to them and the people; they merely call on the people to consider the implications of this belief, and stop worshiping no-gods" (p. 146)
- "YHWH is never opposed to other living gods, but to other objects of worship" (p. 146)
- "The idols and 'foreign gods' are mythless cult objects" — "Such a polemic obviously presupposes a monotheistic viewpoint" (p. 146)
- The prophets wanted to "concretize and realize in practice what was still an idea" — "It was felt that without a tangible, popular concretization the idea would remain the property of but a few" (p. 147)
- "Some stages in the growth of the objection to images are still visible in the biblical record. Gideon's ephod and Micah's image show that at first there was no absolute ban on images in the cult of YHWH" (p. 147)
- The brazen serpent was legitimate for centuries until Hezekiah removed it because "the Israelites were making burnt offerings to it" (II Kings 18:4) — the cultic consequence was drawn later than the idea (pp. 147-148)
- "The basic idea was present and working on every aspect of the national culture from earliest times. It eventually created its necessary cultic symbol as well" (p. 148)
- "Only because monotheism existed long since could it emerge triumphant and even more purified after the political collapse. Only this prevented the people from accepting a pagan interpretation of the catastrophe" (p. 148)

**Confidence:** LOW. This is Kaufmann's most radical reversal of the critical consensus, and it requires him to assert what the evidence most naturally contradicts. The claim that monotheism PRECEDED monolatry in Israel is almost certainly wrong as a historical description of popular religion, even if it's true as a description of the theology of certain elite texts.

**What's at stake:** The entire historical narrative of Israelite religion. If Kaufmann is right, Wellhausen, Smith, Römer, and the entire critical tradition have the direction of development exactly backwards.

**Who disagrees:** Effectively the entire field. The archaeological record (figurines, inscriptions, high places) shows a gradual movement from polytheistic practice toward exclusive worship. The biblical texts themselves, read chronologically, show increasing monotheistic clarity. Even Tigay's onomastic study (which Kaufmann would find congenial) suggests a trajectory — not an original monotheism that later required cultic enforcement.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann has correctly identified that the biblical battle is about CULT (what you may worship) rather than THEOLOGY (what exists). But this doesn't mean theology preceded cult — it means the final editors of the Bible were already monotheists who depicted the struggle in monotheistic terms. The actual historical development almost certainly went: polytheistic practice → competing Yahwistic factions → monolatrous Yahwism → monotheistic Yahwism → cultic exclusivism. Kaufmann reverses the first and last steps.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's reversal is elegant but ultimately unconvincing as history. It requires us to believe that an entire people possessed a sophisticated theological concept (monotheism) while simultaneously practicing what looks exactly like polytheism to every outside observer — and that the "battle" was merely about getting practice to catch up to theology. This is possible in principle (people can believe one thing and do another), but as a historical description of an ancient society without philosophical schools or systematic theology, it's far less plausible than the alternative: that practice and belief developed together, with exclusive worship gradually generating monotheistic theology. Kaufmann's formulation would work better as a description of the biblical AUTHORS' perspective than of the historical reality. From the authors' standpoint, monotheism WAS original, and the battle WAS about cultic consequences — because they were writing from within an already-monotheistic framework. But that tells us about the authors, not about the people they describe.

## Claim 8: The Elephantine papyri do NOT show pagan syncretism among diaspora Jews

**Author's claim:** "The religion of the Jewish garrison of Elephantine... must not be viewed as representative of the ancient popular religion of Israel" (p. 148). Even here, the evidence doesn't show polytheism: "what is decisive is the complete absence of a mythological conception of [YHWH] even at Elephantine" (p. 149).

**Evidence presented:**
- The garrison was isolated for over a century in an alien environment by the time of the papyri: "They had become assimilated linguistically and intermarried with their neighbors" (p. 148)
- "No Israelite writing was found among them, although the pagan Ahikar romance was" (p. 148)
- "In contrast to the Babylonian colony of exiles they had no prophets among them, though they did have priests" — their religion "can therefore be used only in a most qualified way to reconstruct the popular religion of Israel in Palestine" (p. 148)
- Despite pagan god names in the onomasticon, "none of the 'gods of the nations' after whom biblical Israel strayed (Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh, etc.) are worshiped by the Elephantine Jews" (p. 148)
- The peculiar divine names (Herembethel, Anathbethel, Anathiahu, Ashambethel) "are never found in these forms in the Bible. If they were originally Israelite divinities... they can at most have been akin to the satyrs of the popular religion that had no recognized, public cult in Palestine" (pp. 148-149)
- "Only YHWH is described: he is the 'Lord' or 'God' of heaven. Only he has a temple and festivals" (p. 149)
- "Ashambethel and Anathbethel have a treasury in the temple of YHWH, but only YHWH is represented as a universal God" (p. 149)
- "The minor deities (if so they be) are given a place in his temple apparently as members of his entourage" (p. 149)
- The Elephantine Jews distinguish their priests (kahanayya) from pagan priests (kumrayya) — using biblical terminology (p. 149)
- "What is decisive is the complete absence of a mythological conception of him even at Elephantine" (p. 149)

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. Kaufmann is right that Elephantine shouldn't be taken as representative of pre-exilic popular religion — it's a diaspora community isolated for generations. But his attempt to minimize the polytheistic elements in the Elephantine evidence is strained. Anat-Bethel and Anat-Yahu don't look like "satyrs."

**What's at stake:** Whether the Elephantine papyri provide evidence that even diaspora Jews practiced something that looks very much like polytheism — which would challenge Kaufmann's claim that monotheism was deeply rooted in the popular consciousness.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who use Elephantine as evidence of continuing polytheistic tendencies in Judaism. The theophoric names combining YHWH with Anat (Anathiahu) are particularly hard to read as anything other than recognition of Anat as a deity alongside YHWH. The fact that these deities have a treasury in YHWH's temple suggests they received cult — not just casual acknowledgment.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that Elephantine is an extreme case — a garrison community isolated for over a century, without prophetic leadership, in a polytheistic environment. It may tell us more about what happens to Yahwism under diaspora conditions than about pre-exilic popular religion. BUT: the specific form of Elephantine's syncretism IS interesting. If these Jews brought polytheistic tendencies from Palestine, why don't they worship Baal, Asherah, or the Queen of Heaven — the deities the Bible says Israel pursued? The Elephantine pantheon looks different, which could support Kaufmann's claim that it's a diaspora development rather than a continuation of Palestinian popular religion.

**My assessment:** Kaufmann's dismissal of Elephantine as evidence for popular polytheism is partly justified — Elephantine is a weird case and shouldn't be treated as a window into pre-exilic Israel. But his characterization of Anat-Yahu and Anat-Bethel as analogous to "satyrs" is special pleading. "Anat-Yahu" means "Anat of YHWH" — that's a consort formulation, not a satyr. And having a treasury in YHWH's temple means these beings received cultic attention. Kaufmann's "members of his entourage" framing is doing a lot of work to make polytheistic practice sound monotheistic. That said, he's right that even at Elephantine, YHWH is supreme in a way that looks qualitatively different from the surrounding polytheism. The question is whether that difference is enough to call the religion "monotheistic" or whether "henotheistic with a supreme god and subordinate divine beings" is more accurate.

## Chapter IV Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Biblical battle is about practice, not mythology | MEDIUM-HIGH | Could be polemical strategy, not evidence of prior monotheism |
| 2: Creation legends prove folk monotheism | MEDIUM | Assumes what it's trying to prove; absence of polytheism ≠ monotheism |
| 3: Exile extinction of idolatry proves popular monotheism | MEDIUM-HIGH (observation) / MEDIUM (inference) | Survivorship bias; selective return of YHWH-loyalists |
| 4: Universalism distinction (dominion vs. favor) | HIGH (distinction) / MEDIUM (historicity) | May retroject later theology onto earlier periods |
| 5: Idolatry = vestigial fetishism, not genuine polytheism | MEDIUM | Archaeological evidence (figurines, Kuntillet Ajrud); defines polytheism too narrowly |
| 6: No genuine syncretism occurred | MEDIUM (biblical) / LOW (archaeological) | Kuntillet Ajrud "Yahweh and his Asherah" |
| 7: Evolution ran monotheism → monolatry | LOW | Counters entire archaeological and comparative record |
| 8: Elephantine doesn't show pagan syncretism | LOW-MEDIUM | Anat-Yahu formulation looks like consort theology |

**Strongest section:** The "argument from history" (Claim 3) — the disappearance of Israelite idolatry after the exile IS a genuine historical anomaly that evolutionary models struggle to explain. And the universalism distinction (Claim 4) is a genuinely helpful conceptual tool for reading the biblical texts.

**Weakest section:** The reversal of the evolutionary sequence (Claim 7). This is Kaufmann's most radical claim and the one least supported by evidence. It requires monotheism to exist as an IDEA before it exists as a PRACTICE, which is historically implausible for an ancient society.

**Cross-reference assessment:** Kaufmann's Chapter IV is the crucial bridge between his theoretical claims (Introduction) and his historical reconstruction (subsequent chapters). If he's right here, the rest of his project follows naturally. But the evidence he needs to engage — the archaeological record, the epigraphic evidence, the comparative material — is precisely what he either ignores or dismisses by definitional fiat.

**Kuntillet Ajrud — the elephant in the room:** The most damaging evidence against Kaufmann's thesis is one he never mentions: the Kuntillet Ajrud pithos inscriptions (8th century BCE) that bless "Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah" and "Yahweh of Teman and his Asherah." These are not biblical polemics — they're actual religious inscriptions from Israelites. They pair YHWH with a goddess in a blessing formula. Kaufmann would presumably classify this as "vestigial fetishism," but that stretches the term past breaking point. If people inscribe blessings invoking YHWH AND Asherah together, they're treating Asherah as a divine being who can bless — which is functionally polytheism regardless of whether they have a theogony for her.

**Methodological note — Kaufmann's definitional strategy:** Throughout this chapter, Kaufmann engages in what might be called "definitional immunology." He defines "genuine polytheism" in terms that require (1) mythology/theogony, (2) temples and priesthoods, (3) formal syncretism. Since Israelite practice doesn't meet these criteria, it isn't "genuine polytheism." But this definition is too narrow — it excludes most actual polytheistic practice in the ancient world, which was often focused on ritual and cult objects rather than elaborate mythology. Kaufmann has defined polytheism as something Christianity and Islam would recognize as paganism — a philosophically elaborated rival theology — rather than as what polytheism actually looked like in ancient West Asia: the worship of multiple divine beings through images, offerings, and local shrines. By his definition, most Canaanite villagers were probably not "genuine polytheists" either.

**The grain of truth:** Despite these weaknesses, Kaufmann is onto something real. Israelite religion WAS unusual. The biblical polemic against "wood and stone" IS different from pagan intra-pantheon polemic. Israel didn't produce native theogonies or mythological epics. The exile DID produce an extinction of idolatry that has no parallel. These facts need explanation, and Kaufmann's explanation — that some form of monotheism was genuinely early and popular — deserves engagement even if his "from the very beginning" formulation is too strong. The question is whether a more moderate version of his thesis (early distinctive monotheistic TENDENCY, gradually realized in practice) can accommodate the evidence better than either his maximalist version or the standard evolutionary model.

**The unanswered question:** Where did this monotheistic idea come from, if it was present "from earliest times"? Kaufmann has argued that the Torah literature contains it and the prophets inherited it — but he hasn't yet explained the ORIGIN of the idea itself. That's presumably for later chapters. For now, Chapter IV establishes that WHATEVER the nature of Israelite popular religion, it wasn't — by Kaufmann's definition — pagan polytheism. Whether his definition is the right one is the question that will haunt the entire book.
