# Smith — Chapter 3: The Divine Family

This is Smith's most original chapter — the one where he makes the argument previewed in the Introduction: the divine FAMILY (specifically the patrimonial household) provided polytheism with its form of "oneness." He demonstrates that the social terminology of Ugaritic administrative texts (CAT 4.360) maps directly onto the mythological presentation of the pantheon — same words for patriarch, family head, sons, daughters, servants. Then he adds the crucial twist: El's family is astral; Baal is an outsider storm-god. The structural flexibility that allowed Baal's integration into the divine family is, Smith argues, the same flexibility that eventually made monotheism possible — because the family model could absorb, redefine, and ultimately collapse distinctions.

---

## Claim 1: The four tiers of the pantheon map directly to the four levels of the divine household — the social terminology is identical across mythological and administrative texts

**Author's claim:** "The four tiers of the pantheon are analogous with different tiers of the divine household. In the top two tiers of the pantheon are the divine parents and their children; the bottom two tiers of the pantheon consist of deities working in the divine household."

**Evidence presented:**
- Tier 1 = parents: El ('adn, "father/master") and Athirat ('um, "mother," rbt, "Lady"). El is explicitly called 'adn in 1.1 IV 17 — a familial term connoting "father" (parallel with 'um in 1.24.33-34).
- Tier 2 = children: "the seventy sons of Athirat" (1.4 VI 46) — a conventional number for a large family group (Judges 9:5; 2 Kings 10:1; Emar 373.37-38). These sons have their own houses; Baal complains they have houses while he does not (1.3 IV 48).
- Tier 3 = hired craftsman: Kothar wa-Hasis, employed by divine royals for building projects — analogous to human craftsmen employed by the Ugaritic dynasty.
- Tier 4 = household workers: 'amt (female servant, 1.12 I 14-17), ml'ak (messenger, 1.2 I 22), 'nn (servant, Gpn w-'Ugr), tǵr (gate-keeper, Yarih at El's house in 1.114.11, Resheph as underworld gate-keeper in 1.78.1-3).
- Gender division in tier 4: gate-keepers and messengers are male; domestic servants are female. El's question to Athirat (1.4 IV 59-62) confirms these assumptions: "So am I a servant, Athirat a slave? ... Or is Athirat a maid-servant who makes bricks?"
- The word ml'ak in Ugaritic and biblical material refers to family messengers (Genesis 32:4-7), not just royal messengers — rooting the concept in household, not bureaucracy.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the mapping. The terminology overlap between mythological and administrative texts is convincing. MEDIUM-HIGH for the claim that tiers 3-4 specifically map to household roles rather than bureaucratic ones — this depends on the family-vs-bureaucracy debate from Ch 2.

**What's at stake:** If the pantheon IS a household, then polytheism wasn't a chaotic multiplicity of unrelated deities — it was a family. This means polytheism had its own form of coherence ("oneness") grounded in kinship, not in the singularity of a monarch. The shift to monotheism is a shift from FAMILIAL unity to MONARCHICAL unity.

**Who disagrees:** Handy (bureaucratic model). Scholars who see the family language as metaphorical overlay rather than structural principle. Those who argue that royal/administrative models were primary and family language was secondary.

**Alternative reading:** The family language could be one of several overlapping metaphors for the pantheon (family, council, court, army). Smith privileges family as THE model; the texts may not. The lack of a divine scribe (bureaucratic figure) in Ugarit is notable but doesn't prove family was the primary model.

**My assessment:** This is the core of Smith's argument and it's largely convincing. The CAT 4.360 comparison is the strongest data point — an administrative text using the same terminology as myths to describe exactly the same social structure. That's not coincidental. The family model works better than the bureaucratic model for Ugaritic data. Where I'm less convinced is whether the ancients would have recognized Smith's "family provides oneness" as their own understanding. That's Smith's interpretive move, not something the texts say.

## Claim 2: CAT 4.360 provides a direct social parallel — its terminology matches the divine household structure exactly

**Author's claim:** The administrative text CAT 4.360 uses the same terms as the myths: 'adn (patriarch), b'l (family head), bn (sons), 'aḫt (daughters), 'aḫt (sisters), 'att (women), n'rm (retainers), ḫpt (free-men). "This text follows a standard in its listing of family members: patriarchs (once called 'adn), sons called heads (b'lm), male servants... followed by daughters and women."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 4.360 lists multiple families: "The sons of B'LN of BIRY: Three heads. And their master Bull, and his four daughters. YRḪM (together) with his two sons, two heads, and three retainers and one daughter..." — same structure as mythological divine household.
- In the first family, 'adn refers to patriarch; his three married sons are b'lm ("heads"). Identical usage in myths: El is 'adn (1.1 IV 17), Baal is b'l.
- Females listed in three classes (sisters, daughters, women) — same categories in myths: Anat as Baal's sister (1.4 IV 39) and El's daughter (1.3 IV 27); Pidray, Arsay, Tallay as Baal's women (1.3 IV 40) and daughters (1.3 I 24) and brides (1.3 VI 15).
- Royal letters use b'l for social superior and 'adn for the father of the writer. The king's titulary (7.63.4) calls the king "upright lord" (b'l ṣdq).
- "In ideological terms, the king is at once the patriarchal provider and protector; he is judge and father to the society, as well as the warrior who battles on its behalf."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is a genuinely important empirical finding. The administrative and mythological texts using identical social terminology is strong evidence for the household model.

**What's at stake:** This grounds Smith's argument in documentary evidence, not just literary interpretation. If the pantheon reflects actual Ugaritic social structure, then changes in the pantheon (convergence, collapse) may reflect changes in social structure — which is exactly Smith's argument about the neo-Assyrian crisis.

**Who disagrees:** Probably few on the factual claim; the terminology overlap is clear. Disagreement would be on whether this means the divine household WAS society's self-representation or merely used convenient social language.

**Alternative reading:** The shared terminology could reflect scribal convention (administrative scribes and myth-scribes using the same vocabulary) rather than a deep structural homology. The myths might use household language because that's the available vocabulary, not because the pantheon IS a household.

**My assessment:** This is excellent evidence. The CAT 4.360 comparison is Smith's strongest empirical move in Part I. Even if one is skeptical about "the pantheon IS a household," it's undeniable that the Ugaritic texts consistently USE household language to describe divine relations. That's a fact about the texts, not an interpretation. The question is whether the language is structural or metaphorical. Smith's answer (structural) is well-supported by the systematic nature of the parallels.

## Claim 3: El's family is astral in character; Baal is an outsider because he's a storm-god, not an astral deity

**Author's claim:** "The Ugaritic texts hint that El's family was understood as astral in character, although the texts rarely stress this feature." Baal's outsider status stems from his separate paternity (bn dgn, "son of Dagan") and his different cosmic character (storm vs. stars).

**Evidence presented:**
- **For astral character of El's family:**
  - 1.10 I 3-5: "the sons of El" // "the assembly of the stars" // "the circle of those of heaven" — apparently parallel terms.
  - Shahar (Dawn) and Shalim (Dusk) are El's two sons (1.23).
  - Yarih (moon) is "the favorite of El" (n'mn ['i]lm, 1.24.25). He participates in cooking at El's house (1.114), possibly serves as El's gate-keeper.
  - Shapshu (sun) serves as El's special messenger (1.6 VI). Stars are grouped after her (1.23.54). Blessing in 1.102.26-27 parallels sun and moon with El.
  - Athtar is called "Ashtar of the stars" at Emar; Aramaic 'tršmn = "Athtar of heaven." Athtar and Athtart may represent morning and evening star (Venus).
  - Resheph possibly Mars (1.78: astronomical omen text). Paired with Yarih (1.107.40).
  - The seven astral deities identified (Shahar, Shalim, Yarih, Shapshu, Athtar, Athtart, Resheph) match "seven stars" (šb' kbkbm, 1.164.15) — "it may only be coincidental."
  - Keel and Uehlinger: lunar presentation of El on Beth-Shean cylinder seal. Brody: astral features of Athirat's iconography.
- **For Baal's outsider status:**
  - Baal's title bn dgn ("son of Dagan," 1.2 I 19; 1.5 V 23-24) — separate paternity from the rest of the divine family.
  - He can stereotypically call El "father" (1.3 V 35; 1.4 IV 47) since El is generically father of the pantheon. Ginsberg: this "may echo a stage of the tradition in which he was not a son of El."
  - Philo of Byblos provides narrative: Demarous (Baal) is a concubine's son sired by Ouranos, adopted by Dagon. The concubine's son has no legal rights; legal wife and children may expel him.
  - Baal's situation parallels Jephthah (Judges 11): son of a prostitute driven out by legal sons, becomes warrior-leader despite his status.
  - Hostility between Baal and Athirat's sons (1.4 II 24-26). Baal attacks the same seventy sons he invited to his feast (1.6 V 1). Elkunirsa myth: Baal boasts to Athirat about killing her seventy-seven/eighty-eight sons.
  - Yarih is called "brother-in-law of Baal" (1.24.25-26) — family metaphor extends to integrate outsider through marriage.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM for the astral claim — the evidence is suggestive but admits Smith himself: "one might cautiously suggest." HIGH for Baal's outsider status — the bn dgn epithet, Philo of Byblos, and the hostility texts converge.

**What's at stake:** This is the structural explanation for WHY monotheism was possible. If Baal is an outsider who achieved prominence through martial prowess and was integrated into El's family through adoption/marriage, then the divine family was structurally FLEXIBLE — it could absorb new members and redefine relationships. This flexibility is precisely what allowed the eventual collapse into monotheism: Yahweh absorbed El's identity AND Baal's functions. The astral/storm dichotomy maps onto the El/Baal dichotomy that Yahweh later unifies.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Baal as fully integrated into El's family from the beginning (reading "father" language literally). Those who deny the astral character of El's family (evidence is admittedly thin). Cross emphasizes El's identification with Yahweh very early, which would compress the timeline.

**Alternative reading:** The astral/storm distinction may be Smith's systematization of what were looser associations. Baal's outsider status could be a literary device specific to the Baal Cycle rather than a theological claim about his place in the pantheon. The bn dgn title might reflect a different tradition about Baal's origins without implying outsider status.

**My assessment:** The Baal-as-outsider argument is strong and well-supported by multiple lines of evidence. The astral-family argument is more tentative — Smith is honest about the "paucity of information." The two arguments work together: if El's family is astral and Baal is a storm-god, the outsider status has a cosmic basis, not just a narrative one. This is elegant but the astral evidence is thin enough that the argument works without it — Baal's separate paternity (bn dgn) and the Philo of Byblos narrative are enough on their own.

## Claim 4: Astral deities belonged to El's indigenous Israelite cult — their later condemnation as "foreign" rewrites history

**Author's claim:** "Some biblical scholars judge El as indigenous and Israelite whereas others view the host of heaven and astral deities as foreign and non-Israelite. Yet given the biblical acceptance of El (under the guise of his identification with Yahweh) and the condemnations of astral deities, one might argue that biblical historiography has influenced the differing scholarly assessments."

**Evidence presented:**
- Job 38:6-7: "when the morning stars sang together and all the divine beings (bĕnê 'elōhîm) shouted for joy" — morning stars parallel to sons of God. Oldenburg connects astral bodies with El.
- Isaiah 14:13: the king of Babylon attempts to exalt his throne "above the stars of El" (mimma'al lĕkôkĕbê-'ēl) — El's astral association preserved in polemic.
- Shahar continues in Israelite religion: Isaiah 14:12 alludes to "Shahar ben Helal" (fallen star). Shahar appears in Hebrew proper names. Shalem appears in proper names ('ăbîšālôm) and inscriptions from Arad, Ein Gedi, Lachish.
- 1 Kings 22:19, Zephaniah 1:5: Yahweh associated with "host of heaven." 2 Kings 21:5: Manasseh built "altars for all the host of heaven in the two courts in the house of Yahweh" — astral worship WITHIN Yahwism, not foreign import.
- "It is possible that the criticism represented by these prohibitions derived from a perceived threat of the neo-Assyrian astral cult during the Iron II period, but this fact does not diminish the indigenous character of the cultic devotion paid to the sun, moon, and stars."
- Daniel 8:9-11: the "little horn" casts down some of the host of heaven — survival of astral divine family imagery in apocalyptic.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The textual evidence for astral elements in Israelite Yahwism is solid. The claim that these were INDIGENOUS (El's family) rather than imported (Assyrian influence) is plausible but the astral character of El's family at Ugarit is itself tentative.

**What's at stake:** If astral worship was indigenous to Yahwism (inherited from El's cult), then the Deuteronomistic condemnation of astral worship is an internal Israelite purge, not resistance to foreign influence. This inverts the standard biblical narrative: the "idolatry" being condemned was Israel's OWN older tradition, not Canaanite importation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who attribute astral worship in Israel primarily to neo-Assyrian influence (the conventional view). Those who see "host of heaven" language as metaphor rather than evidence of actual astral cult.

**Alternative reading:** The "host of heaven" in 1 Kings 22:19 and similar texts could be a royal/scribal metaphor for Yahweh's court — angels as stars — without implying actual astral worship. The condemnation texts (2 Kings 21:5, Zeph 1:5) might target genuine foreign importation, not indigenous survivals.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument that astral worship was indigenous is provocative and plausible but the evidence chain has weak links: the Ugaritic astral-family evidence is tentative → the Israelite astral-survival evidence depends on reading "host of heaven" language as cultic rather than metaphoric. The strongest single data point is the proper name evidence (Shahar, Shalem in Israelite names), which suggests these deities were known and invoked, not just literary survivals.

## Claim 5: The family model provided polytheism with "conceptual unity" through its structural flexibility — outsiders could be integrated through marriage and adoption

**Author's claim:** "The concept of family provides a conceptual unity for Ugaritic divinity. It shows an elasticity that can give expression to a multitude of relations." This is the payoff for the whole chapter: the divine family was "a sort of 'mono-theism'" because it unified diverse deities through kinship.

**Evidence presented:**
- Nikkal (Mesopotamian moon-goddess) marries Yarih (CAT 1.24) — a foreign goddess integrated into the Ugaritic pantheon through the family metaphor of marriage. "Implicit in this marriage is an identification of the two as moon-deities."
- Baal integrated as "son of El" despite separate paternity (bn dgn) — the family metaphor extends to include outsiders. He is "adopted up" through his martial achievement (defeat of Yamm).
- "The language of family encompasses both divine insiders and outsiders, both the established family and the strong newcomer."
- Philo of Byblos explicitly uses family narrative to explain divine relations (concubine's son → adoption → achieved status).
- Council language points to "holders of power in council"; family language provides the relational framework.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the descriptive claim (family language IS flexible and integrative). MEDIUM for the stronger claim that this flexibility "provided conceptual unity" — Smith is interpreting, not reporting textual evidence.

**What's at stake:** THIS is Smith's answer to "why monotheism?" — the very question his book's title promises. Polytheism's family structure was flexible enough to absorb, redefine, and ultimately collapse distinctions between deities. Monotheism wasn't a revolution against polytheism's rigidity; it was an exploitation of polytheism's flexibility. The same mechanism that let Baal join El's family let Yahweh absorb both of them.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see monotheism as a radical break, not a gradual absorption. Those who see the family model as one of several organizing principles, not THE unifying concept. The claim that family "provided unity" is Smith's synthetic argument, not something the texts state.

**Alternative reading:** The family model might have been ONE way of organizing divine relations alongside others (council, cosmic realms, patronage networks). It provided flexibility, yes, but other models might have provided different kinds of coherence. Smith's focus on family might lead him to overstate its role.

**My assessment:** This is Smith's central theoretical contribution, and it's genuinely interesting. The argument has a dialectical elegance: polytheism's own internal logic (family flexibility) made monotheism possible. But it's also somewhat teleological — Smith is reading Ugaritic data through the lens of the outcome (Israelite monotheism). The family model works well for the Ugaritic data; whether it EXPLAINS monotheism's emergence is a bigger claim that needs the historical argument of Part III to test.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Four tiers map to household levels | HIGH | Alternative models (bureaucratic) fitting some data |
| 2: CAT 4.360 provides direct social parallel | VERY HIGH | Scribal convention vs. structural homology |
| 3: El's family is astral; Baal is an outsider | MEDIUM (astral) / HIGH (outsider) | Astral evidence is thin; bn dgn might not imply outsider status |
| 4: Astral worship was indigenous Israelite, not foreign | MEDIUM-HIGH | "Host of heaven" as metaphor vs. cultic reality |
| 5: Family model provided polytheism's conceptual unity | MEDIUM-HIGH | Teleological reading; family as one model among several |

**Strongest section:** Section 2 (The Royal Patriarchal Household as Model). The CAT 4.360 comparison is the best empirical evidence in the entire chapter. The systematic mapping of administrative terminology onto mythological terminology is genuinely new and important.

**Weakest section:** Section 3 (The Astral Background). The evidence is scattered and Smith's hedging ("it may only be coincidental," "one might cautiously suggest," "the textual evidence... is admittedly minimal") signals the tentativeness. The argument works without the astral claim; the astral claim adds cosmic depth but at the cost of evidentiary confidence.

**Most original argument in the book so far:** The combination of (a) divine family as structural model with (b) Baal as outsider who achieves integration through martial prowess and (c) this flexibility being the PRECONDITION for monotheistic convergence. No one else in the field connects Ugaritic social structure, divine family dynamics, and the emergence of Israelite monotheism this way. If this argument holds, it's a genuine breakthrough.

**Cross-reference:** This chapter is the theoretical heart of Part I. The Introduction previewed the claim ("the divine family was for polytheism a sort of 'mono-theism'") and now Smith has delivered the evidence. Ch 2 established the council structure; Ch 3 fills it with social content. The claim that family flexibility made monotheism possible will need Ch 7 (El-Yahweh identification) and Ch 8-10 (monotheistic rhetoric) to complete the arc. Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* sees El-Yahweh identification as early and fundamental; Smith agrees on the fact of identification but attributes it to a different mechanism (family flexibility rather than covenant innovation). Heiser's *Unseen Realm* treats the divine council as Yahweh's council from the start — Smith's argument that Yahweh was originally tier 2 under El directly challenges this. If Smith is right, Heiser's model collapses.
