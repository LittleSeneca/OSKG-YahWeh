# Smith Origins — Ch3 — The Divine Family

Smith uses the third chapter to develop the second major indigenous conceptual structure of West Semitic divinity: the divine family. This chapter extends Schloen's patrimonial household model from social theory into the mythological texts themselves. Smith argues that the Ugaritic pantheon is structured like a multi-generational patriarchal household, with El as patriarch, Athirat as matriarch, their seventy sons as the second tier, and household servants and messengers filling the bottom two tiers. The chapter concludes with a critical section on the astral character of El's family and Baal's status as an outsider to it — a distinction that explains conflict dynamics in the Baal Cycle and illuminates later Israelite adaptations of divine sonship language.

---

## Chapter Summary

Smith structures the chapter in three movements. First, he maps the four tiers of the Ugaritic pantheon (from Ch. 2) onto the structure of a patriarchal household: divine parents (El and Athirat), divine children (the seventy sons), craftsmen and specialists (Kothar), and household servants and messengers (the *ml'ak*, *'amt*, *tg'r*). He shows that Ugaritic texts like CAT 1.65 use council terminology (*mpḥrt bn 'il*) in immediate juxtaposition with family terminology (*dr bn 'il*), establishing that the council IS the family. Schloen's insight — that the divine household mirrors the human joint-family with its patriarch, married sons (each a *b'l* of his own house), and dependent workers — receives extended textual documentation. Smith traces specific vocabulary (*'adn*, *b'l*, *bn*, *'aḫt*, *'att*) across both administrative texts (CAT 4.360) and mythological texts, demonstrating systematic correspondence between human and divine social terminology.

Second, Smith develops the royal patriarchal household as the specific model for the pantheon. The monarchy represents a scaled-up patriarchal household, and the Ugaritic king is ideologically both father and warrior to society — a role mirrored by El (patriarch-judge) and Baal (warrior-protector). The royal palace at Ugarit, covering nearly 7,000 square meters, was the architectural "family of families," the apex of a social pyramid that extended down through domestic cults evidenced by widely distributed figurines. First-millennium West Semitic inscriptions (Azitawadda, Kilamuwa) continue this royal family ideology: the king claims to be "father and mother" to his people.

Third, Smith argues that El's family has an astral background — the sun (Shapshu), moon (Yarih), and stars (Shahar and Shalim, Athtar and Athtart as Venus, possibly Resheph as Mars) are members of El's household. But the textual evidence for explicit astral characterization of El and Athirat themselves is admittedly minimal. The more important payoff is contrastive: Baal, as a storm-god and "son of Dagan" (*bn dgn*), stands OUTSIDE El's astral family. Philo of Byblos's *Phoenician History* provides a later narrative explanation: Baal/Demarous was sired by Ouranos on a concubine who was then given to Dagon; Baal's status is that of a concubine's son — an outsider "adopted up" through martial achievement, comparable to Jephthah (Judges 11). Athtar, by contrast, IS a full son of El and an astral deity, but his cult declined on the coast while Baal's storm-god cult flourished — a geographical pattern (inland Athtar vs. coastal Baal) that may reflect the ecological contrast between dry farming/irrigation and rain-fed agriculture.

Smith closes by noting that Israelite religion preserved reflexes of this divine family structure: Job 38:7's "morning stars" parallel to "sons of God" (*bənê 'ĕlōhîm*), Isaiah 14:13's "stars of El" (*kôkəbê-'ēl*), and the ongoing biblical association of Yahweh with the "host of heaven" — suggesting that even after El's identification with Yahweh, the astral family persisted as part of Israel's religious heritage before eventually becoming an object of polemic.

## Claim 1: The four tiers of the Ugaritic pantheon are structurally homologous with a patriarchal household

**Author's claim:** The pantheon's four tiers (divine parents, divine children, craftsmen/specialists, servants/messengers) map directly onto the structure of a West Semitic patriarchal household. "The four tiers of the pantheon are analogous with different tiers of the divine household." El and Athirat are the royal parents at tier 1; the seventy sons are the married children (each a *b'l* of his own house) at tier 2; Kothar is the hired craftsman at tier 3; and messengers (*ml'ak*), female servants (*'amt*), and gate-keepers (*tg'r*) occupy tier 4.

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.65 uses *mpḥrt bn 'il* ("council of the sons of El") and *dr bn 'il* ("circle of the sons of El") in juxtaposition, making the council/family equation explicit (lines 2-3).
- CAT 1.40.25 repeats the same juxtaposition (and apparently in lines 8, 17, 34, 42, though broken).
- CAT 1.162.16-17 names "the circle of El and the council of Baal" (*dr 'il wpḫr b'l*) as joint recipients of sacrifice.
- The word *hmlt* (used in parallelism with *'ilm*, "the gods," in 1.2 I 18, 35) probably means "clan."
- Tier 1: El is explicitly "father of deities and humanity"; Athirat is "mother of deities and humanity" (1.4 VI 46: "seventy sons of Athirat"; Elkunirsa myth: 77/88 children).
- Tier 2: Baal's household includes three daughters (Pidray, 'Arsay, Tallay) who are also called "brides" — a household of his own (1.3 IV 48, Baal complains that Athirat's sons have houses but he does not).
- Tier 3: Kothar is hired by different divine royals for their needs, analogous to human craftsmen employed by the Ugaritic dynasty.
- Tier 4: Smith catalogs specific terms — *'amt* (female servant), *ml'ak* (messenger), *'nn* (servant), *tg'r* (gate-keeper) — with textual attestations for each. El's question to Athirat (1.4 IV 59-62) distinguishes servant from slave, male from female tasks: "So am I a servant, Athirat a slave? ... Or is Athirat a maid-servant who makes bricks?"
- Gender division: gate-keepers and messengers are male; domestic servants are female. N. Wyatt's formulation: "The image of the one family is a classic instance of systematic theology at work."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The homology is well-documented across multiple text genres (mythological, ritual, administrative). The vocabulary mapping is systematic, not anecdotal.

**What's at stake:** If the pantheon IS the divine household, then understanding polytheism requires understanding family structure. This makes polytheism intelligible as a coherent social-theological system rather than an arbitrary collection of deities. It also means the boundary between "theology" and "social structure" is porous — the gods' relationships are modeled on human relationships, not abstract metaphysical principles.

**Who disagrees:** Smith acknowledges that Schloen "barely applies his own insight to the divine family" — so Smith is doing the application work himself. Some scholars might argue that the four-tier model is too neat, that some deities don't fit cleanly into a single tier, or that the "household" metaphor is one among several (council, court, army). Anat's problematic status (daughter of El but probably not of Athirat, unattached to any male) is a case Smith himself flags as a complication.

**Alternative reading:** The family metaphor may be a literary convention for expressing hierarchy rather than a deep structural homology. The texts might use familial LANGUAGE without the pantheon being conceptually organized AS a family. The administrative text CAT 4.360 shows the template, but it doesn't prove the template generated the pantheon.

**My assessment:** Smith makes a strong case for structural homology. The convergence of ritual texts, mythological narratives, and administrative documents using the same social vocabulary is persuasive. The way he handles Anat (flagging her as a complication rather than forcing her into the model) strengthens his credibility — he's not over-harmonizing. The weakest link is tier 3: Kothar as the sole example of a "craftsman tier" might be an artifact of the limited corpus rather than a genuine structural tier. The bottom two tiers may blur together more than the model suggests.

## Claim 2: The divine family provided polytheism with its own form of "oneness" — the family WAS the conceptual unity

**Author's claim:** "The notion of the family household perhaps provided polytheism with the sort of 'oneness' that monotheists associate with monotheism." The family concept "contains a built-in flexibility to integrate deities not originally 'at home' in the main divine household" — it provides both boundedness (insiders vs. outsiders) and elasticity (outsiders can become insiders through marriage or adoption). "The concept of family provides a conceptual unity for Ugaritic divinity."

**Evidence presented:**
- The family metaphor encompasses ALL deities: parents, children, servants, messengers — no divine being stands outside the household structure.
- Outsider deities like Baal can be integrated: Baal marries into El's family (Yarih is called his "brother-in-law" in CAT 1.24.25-26), and Philo of Byblos narrates Baal's "adoption" as son of Dagon.
- The language of both council AND family allows "focus on the two main protagonists," El and Baal, without the system breaking apart — the family is capacious enough to accommodate shifting power dynamics.
- Smith explicitly connects this claim back to his Introduction claim: "The notion of the family household perhaps provided polytheism with the sort of 'oneness' that monotheists associate with monotheism. Indeed, in a society where the highest level of social association and identification was the family and not the individual, the polytheism of a divine family would have been far more intelligible than any notion of monotheism."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The evidence that family IS the dominant metaphor is strong. The claim that it provides an EQUIVALENT of monotheistic oneness is a more interpretive leap.

**What's at stake:** This is one of Smith's most provocative claims. If the divine family WAS polytheism's oneness, then monotheism didn't invent unity — it REPLACED one unity (the family) with another (the single god). This reframes the entire monotheism-vs-polytheism contrast: it's not unity vs. multiplicity but one kind of unity vs. another. It also explains why monotheistic rhetoric could emerge from within polytheism — the cognitive structure of "oneness" was already there.

**Who disagrees:** This is Smith's synthetic claim, tested across chapters 1-4. Scholars who see polytheism as inherently fragmented or chaotic would reject it. Even scholars sympathetic to Smith might argue that the family metaphor was ONE organizing principle among several (royal court, military hierarchy, cosmic order), not THE unifying concept. The elasticity Smith praises — integrating outsiders — could equally be seen as evidence that the family metaphor was too loose to provide genuine conceptual unity.

**Alternative reading:** The family metaphor may have been a convenient literary trope that ancient scribes used to tell stories about the gods, not a deep conceptual structure through which they UNDERSTOOD divinity. The fact that Baal's outsider status was never fully resolved (he remains *bn dgn* AND appeals to El as father) suggests the system was actually contested and inconsistent, not unified.

**My assessment:** Smith's claim is bold and important, but I'm not convinced this chapter fully proves it. The evidence shows that family language was pervasive and that it could stretch to include outsiders — but pervasiveness isn't the same as conceptual UNITY. A royal court metaphor could do the same work (kings, princes, courtiers, servants), and Smith acknowledges the overlap between family and royalty. I'll watch for further development in Ch. 4, where Smith promises to "explore further the presence of family language in Ugaritic myth." For now, I'd grade this as: the family was clearly the DOMINANT metaphor, and Smith has shown it, but the leap from "dominant metaphor" to "form of oneness equivalent to monotheism" needs more support.

## Claim 3: CAT 4.360 and the mythological texts share a common social vocabulary, proving the pantheon was modeled on the patriarchal household

**Author's claim:** The administrative text CAT 4.360 — which lists households with patriarchs (*'adn*), married sons called "heads" (*b'lm*), male servants (*n'rm* or *ḥpt*), daughters (*'aḫt*), and women (*'att*) — uses "an analogous use of social terminology" to the mythological texts. "The mythological evidence exhibits an analogous use of social terminology."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 4.360 lists a family: "The sons of B'LN of BIRY: three heads. And their master Bull, and his four daughters." Then: "YRH M (together) with his two sons, two heads, and three retainers and one daughter."
- In the myths, El is called *'adn* (1.1 IV 17), and in CAT 1.24.33-34 *'adn* parallels *'um* ("mother"), confirming its familial connotation of "father."
- *b'l* in the administrative text means "head of household"; in the myths, Baal is the *b'l* par excellence, head of his own divine household.
- The term *tr* ("Bull") applies both to a human patriarch in CAT 4.360 and to El as a divine title — "a marker of his premier status."
- Female categories in CAT 4.360 — sisters (*'aḫt*), daughters, women (*'att*) — map onto the myths: Anat is Baal's "sister" (1.4 IV 39) and El's "daughter" (1.3 IV 27); Pidray, 'Arsay, and Tallay are Baal's "women" AND "daughters" AND "brides."
- Proper names from Ugarit attribute fatherhood not only to El but also to Athtar, Baal, Ea, Kothar, Rapiu, and Resheph — family roles "apply to more deities in popular religious sensibility than in royal prayers, rituals, or other sanctioned Ugaritic texts."
- Similarly, proper names attribute motherhood to Athirat, Anat, Hebat, and even Athtar.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The systematic correspondence between administrative and mythological vocabulary is among the strongest evidence in the chapter.

**What's at stake:** If the same social terminology structures both human administrative records and divine myths, then the pantheon isn't just told in family language — it's CONCEPTUALIZED through the same categories that organize actual society. This makes the "family model" a deep cognitive structure, not a surface literary convention. It also suggests that changes in human social structure could drive changes in how the pantheon was understood.

**Who disagrees:** A skeptic might note that CAT 4.360 is ONE administrative text, and we can't be sure how representative it is. The Ugaritic textual corpus is fragmentary. Smith is connecting dots that may not have been connected in the minds of the texts' authors.

**Alternative reading:** The shared vocabulary may simply reflect the fact that Ugaritic (like many languages) used family terms as general status terms. Calling El *'adn* ("father/lord") doesn't necessarily mean scribes were CONSCIOUSLY modeling the pantheon on CAT 4.360-style households — it may just be the natural vocabulary for expressing hierarchy in a patriarchal society.

**My assessment:** Smith's philological demonstration is meticulous and persuasive. The CAT 4.360 comparison is one of the chapter's strongest arguments. But I note a limitation: Smith shows that BOTH human and divine realms use family terminology — but correlation isn't causation. The shared vocabulary could reflect a common cultural repertoire applied independently to different domains, rather than the pantheon being MODELED on the household. Smith would probably respond that "modeled on" is exactly what "common cultural repertoire" means in practice — the distinction is philosophical, not evidentiary.

## Claim 4: El's family has an astral background — the sun, moon, and stars are members of El's household

**Author's claim:** "The Ugaritic texts hint that El's family was understood as astral in character, although the texts rarely stress this feature." The sun (Shapshu), moon (Yarih), and stars (Shahar, Shalim, Athtar/Athtart as Venus, possibly Resheph as Mars) all belong to El's family. "One might cautiously suggest that the sun, moon, and the stars were especially associated early with El in West Semitic religion."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.10 I 3-5 parallels "the sons of El" (*bn 'il*), "the assembly of the stars" (*pḫr kbbm*), and "the circle of those of heaven" (*dr dt šmm*) — "On the face of it, the three expressions seem parallel."
- Shahar and Shalim (Dawn and Dusk) are explicitly El's sons, born in CAT 1.23.
- Yarih the moon-god is called *n'mn ['i]lm*, "the favorite of El" (1.24.25). He participates in El's household cooking (1.114) and may serve as El's door-keeper (lines 11-14, though uncertain).
- Shapshu the sun-goddess serves as El's "special messenger" (1.6 VI). Stars are grouped after her in 1.23.54.
- Athtar is called *dAš-tar MUL*, "Ashtar of the stars," at Emar; Aramaic texts attest to *'tršmn*, "Athtar of heaven." Athtart is associated with Ishtar, who has clear astral character in Mesopotamia — together they likely represent morning and evening Venus.
- Resheph may be Mars based on the astronomical omen CAT 1.78: "the Sun went down, with Resheph (≈ Mars?) as her/its gate-keeper." Resheph is paired with Yarih in 1.107.40.
- The roster adds up to seven — matching "seven stars" (*šb' kbkbm*) in 1.164.15.
- Iconographic evidence: Keel and Uehlinger argue for a lunar El on a Beth-Shean cylinder seal; Brody notes astral features in Athirat's iconography.
- Smith then catalogs Israelite reflexes: Job 38:7 ("morning stars" parallel to *bənê 'ĕlōhîm*); Isaiah 14:13 ("stars of El"); Daniel 8:9-11 (the "little horn" casting down the "host of heaven"); Shahar survives in Isaiah 14:12 (Shahar ben Helal, the fallen star); Shalem survives in proper names (*'ăbîšālôm*); Yahweh is associated with the "host of heaven" (1 Kings 22:19, Zeph 1:5, 2 Kings 21:5).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The evidence is suggestive but Smith himself concedes it's "minimal" for El and Athirat specifically. The strongest evidence is for the CHILDREN (Shahar, Shalim, Yarih, Shapshu, Athtar/Athtart) being astral. The weakest is whether El HIMSELF was astral.

**What's at stake:** If El's family is fundamentally astral, it connects West Semitic religion to the broader ANE pattern of astral divinity (Mesopotamian moon-god Sin, sun-god Shamash, Venus-Ishtar). It also explains why Baal — a storm-god, not an astral deity — is an outsider: he belongs to a different ontological domain (weather vs. heavens). This sets up a structural tension between astral El and meteorological Baal that may have theological consequences for how Israel later understood Yahweh (who combines both El and Baal features). The Israelite reflexes suggest that astral religion wasn't a late foreign import but an early indigenous feature later condemned.

**Who disagrees:** The astral interpretation of El is contested. The Beth-Shean seal is iconographic — open to multiple interpretations. Keel and Uehlinger's lunar El is suggestive but not proven. Smith's own caution ("admittedly minimal," "cautiously suggest") signals scholarly debate. Some scholars would argue that the astral character belongs to specific deities WITHIN El's family but not to El himself — that the family is mixed astral and non-astral.

**Alternative reading:** The gathering of sun, moon, and star deities under El's household may reflect a systematic theological move to subordinate ALL cosmic powers to the patriarch — not evidence that El's family was originally astral. The "family" framework may be doing political-theological work: bringing disparate cosmic deities under one patriarchal roof. The astral associations of the children don't necessarily mean El was astral — just that he was head of a household that INCLUDED astral members.

**My assessment:** Smith's caution is appropriate. The evidence is real but fragmentary. CAT 1.10 I 3-5 is the strongest text, but he admits it's "broken and not well understood." The Israelite reflexes (Job 38:7, Isa 14:13, "host of heaven" passages) provide important indirect support — they suggest the astral family concept had enough staying power to survive into the biblical period and become an object of polemic. On balance, I think Smith is right that there was an astral dimension to El's family, but I'd qualify: it was probably more prominent for the CHILDREN than for El and Athirat themselves, and the family metaphor may have been the mechanism that SUBORDINATED astral deities to El rather than evidence that El was originally astral.

## Claim 5: Baal is an outsider to El's family — his status as "son of Dagan" reflects separate paternity, not adoption

**Author's claim:** "Baal's outsider status is expressed through the family metaphor." His title *bn dgn*, "son of Dagan," "apparently points to his separate paternity from the rest of the divine family." Yet Baal also conventionally calls El "father" — a discrepancy that H. L. Ginsberg noted "may echo a stage of the tradition in which he was not a son of El." Philo of Byblos provides a later narrative rationalization: Baal/Demarous was sired by Ouranos on a concubine who was then given to Dagon — making Baal an adopted son, comparable to Jephthah (Judges 11), the son of a prostitute driven out by his father's legal sons but later elevated through martial prowess.

**Evidence presented:**
- Baal is *bn dgn* (1.2 I 19, 35, 37; 1.5 VI 24; 1.6 I 6, 52; 1.10 III 12, 14; 1.12 I 39; 1.4 II 25, I 7) and *ḥtk dgn* (1.10 III 34) — Dagan, not El, is his father in these formulas.
- Baal also appeals to El as "father" (1.3 V 35; 1.4 IV 47; cf. 1.4 I 5) — but this may be generic (El as father of ALL gods) rather than biological.
- Philo of Byblos (via Eusebius, *Praeparatio Evangelica*): Kronos (=El) wages war on Ouranos (=Heaven), captures Ouranos's pregnant concubine, and gives her to Dagon (=Dagan). She gives birth to Demarous (=Baal). Baal is thus a concubine's son "adopted" by Dagan — an outsider by birth.
- The Elkunirsa myth preserved in Hittite depicts animosity between Baal and Athirat's family. Baal boasts to Athirat: "Of thy sons I slew seventy-seven, I slew eighty-eight" — a direct conflict with El's legitimate children.
- Baal is invited to the feast of Athirat's "seventy sons" (1.4 VI 46) — but he later ATTACKS them (1.6 V 1). His relationship is ambivalent at best.
- CAT 1.24.25-26 calls Yarih the "brother-in-law of Baal" — Baal is integrated via marriage, not birth.
- Jephthah (Judges 11) provides the human parallel: son of a prostitute, driven out by legitimate sons, becomes a warrior-leader despite his origins. "Within the family, whether divine or human, the children of concubines or adoption occupy a lower status. However, the status of these children ... can change."
- Baal's rise comes through ACHIEVEMENT (defeating Yamm), not birthright — a meritocratic counterpoint to the hereditary principle.

**Confidence:** HIGH that Baal is an outsider. MEDIUM-HIGH that Philo of Byblos preserves an authentic tradition rather than a late rationalization. The Philo evidence is late (1st-2nd c. CE) and Greek-mediated, so its value as a witness to Bronze Age tradition needs defending.

**What's at stake:** Baal's outsider status explains the narrative dynamics of the Baal Cycle: why Baal needs a house "like the other gods," why he fights Yamm and Mot, why Athirat's sons are hostile. It also illuminates the political theology of Ugarit: the royal dynasty's patron god is an ACHIEVED king, not an inherited one — a theologically convenient model for a dynasty that may have had its own contested legitimacy. And for Israelite religion: if Yahweh absorbs Baal's characteristics (storm theophany, divine warrior, conflict with Sea), does he also absorb Baal's "outsider" status relative to El's family? This could explain tensions in biblical texts where Yahweh is both identified WITH El and distinguished FROM the "sons of God."

**Who disagrees:** The Philo of Byblos evidence is controversial. Some scholars treat it as a late Hellenistic synthesis with limited value for reconstructing Bronze Age myth. The "Ouranos" figure is suspiciously Greek (Ouranos = Uranus, father of Kronos) — though Smith notes the Ugaritic pair "Heaven and Earth" (*šmm w'arṣ*) as a possible West Semitic background. The Jephthah parallel is Smith's own — it's analogical, not genealogical. And the evidence that Baal is SPECIFICALLY a concubine's son (rather than just a god from a DIFFERENT pantheon) comes entirely from Philo.

**Alternative reading:** Baal's "outsider" status could reflect a historical process where a god from outside the Ugaritic pantheon (Dagan is well-attested in the Middle Euphrates, e.g., Mari, Terqa) was integrated into Ugarit's royal cult. His dual paternity (Dagan AND El) may reflect competing traditions from different cult centers — a political negotiation, not a family drama. The Philo narrative would then be a late attempt to harmonize contradictory traditions, not a survival of authentic Bronze Age myth.

**My assessment:** Smith's case is strong for the Ugaritic texts: Baal's consistent designation as *bn dgn* alongside his generic appeal to El as "father" is a genuine discrepancy begging for explanation. Philo of Byblos is a valuable witness, but Smith appropriately treats it as supplementary rather than primary. The Jephthah parallel is illuminating but shouldn't be pressed too hard — it shows the cultural plausibility of the outsider-rises narrative pattern, not direct dependence. The strongest single piece of evidence is the Elkunirsa myth's depiction of Baal killing Athirat's sons — that's contemporary (Hittite, 2nd millennium) and independent of Philo.

## Claim 6: The royal patriarchal household is the SPECIFIC model for the pantheon — the king mirrors El as father, provider, protector, and judge

**Author's claim:** "The divine family is modeled on the royal household in particular." The monarchy "represents a royal version of the patriarchal household." The king is "at once the patriarchal provider and protector; he is judge and father to the society, as well as the warrior who battles on its behalf."

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 4.360's social terminology "exhibits much of the same social terminology seen in the royal correspondence and the myths" — but the vocabulary (*'adn*, *b'l*, *'bd*) is not exclusively royal; it's used in non-royal households too.
- Ugaritic royal titulary (7.63.4) calls the king *b'l ṣdq*, "upright lord" — placing him at the head of the royal household.
- The title *tg'r mlk*, "gate-keeper of the kingdom" (7.63.6-7), "reflects the patriarchal role of protecting the 'royal household,' namely, the society, against external threats of enemies and mediating domestic conflict."
- The Ugaritic palace (almost 7,000 sq. meters) was "the 'family of families,' the top of the social pyramid."
- Domestic cults: M. Yon notes widespread distribution of figurines throughout Ugarit — evidence of religious activity in "all the inhabited areas, and not just the areas which were reserved for it."
- First-millennium West Semitic inscriptions continue the ideology: Azitawadda claims "Ba'l made me a father and a mother to the Danunites" (KAI 26 A I 2-3). Kilamuwa of Y'dy: "to one person I was a father, to another I was a mother, to another I was a brother" (KAI 24.10-11).
- Proper names: after *'il*, words for "father" and "mother" apply to more deities than any other divine role — family was the dominant religious category in popular piety.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The convergence of royal ideology, domestic cult, and mythological family structure is well-documented. The Azitawadda and Kilamuwa inscriptions are especially valuable as independent first-millennium witnesses.

**What's at stake:** If the pantheon is modeled on the ROYAL household (not just any household), then politics and theology are inseparable in West Semitic religion. The pantheon's structure legitimates the royal household, and the royal household provides the template for understanding the pantheon. This has major implications for Israel: if Davidic kingship adopts the same royal-patriarchal ideology, then Yahweh's relationship to the divine council and the king's relationship to his court are structurally parallel — each legitimates the other. The transition to monotheism might then be connected to the COLLAPSE of the monarchy: without a human king mirroring the divine king, the whole ideological structure is destabilized.

**Who disagrees:** Smith himself notes a complication: the same social terminology appears in NON-royal administrative texts (CAT 4.360). This suggests the model may be the patriarchal household GENERALLY, with the royal version as a specialized case, rather than the royal household being the primary model. The direction of influence could go either way: royal ideology could borrow from household structure, or household structure could imitate royal ideology.

**Alternative reading:** The family model may predate the monarchy. The Baal Cycle and other myths may reflect older, pre-monarchic family structures that the monarchy later appropriated. Smith's evidence for royal ideology (palace size, titulary, late inscriptions) could reflect a secondary ROYALIZATION of an originally non-royal family model.

**My assessment:** Smith is careful to acknowledge that the vocabulary is not exclusively royal. I think his actual position (as distinct from how he sometimes phrases it) is that the patriarchal household model applies at ALL levels — non-royal, royal, and divine — with the royal and divine versions being scaled-up variants of the same basic structure. This is more nuanced and more defensible than claiming the pantheon is modeled specifically on the ROYAL household. The Azitawadda and Kilamuwa inscriptions are fascinating but late — they may reflect an enduring West Semitic ideology, but they don't prove the Ugaritic pantheon was modeled on royalty rather than the household more broadly.

## Claim 7: Athtar vs. Baal reflects a historical cultic shift from inland (astral/irrigation) to coastal (storm/rainfall) religion

**Author's claim:** "The conﬂict between Baal and Athtar" reflects "the historical demise of Athtar's cult at the hands of Baal's cult." Athtar was "a major warrior deity" in inland areas but weak at coastal Ugarit. "The geographical distribution of the cults of Baal and Athtar" aligns with an ecological distinction: inland areas practiced dry farming or natural irrigation (Athtar's domain), while the coast received heavy rainfall (Baal's domain).

**Evidence presented:**
- CAT 1.2 III and 1.6 I 63 stress that Athtar is "not powerful enough to be king" — within Ugarit, he's portrayed as a weak god.
- Athtar has "no clear evidence for the cult of Athtar on the coast" outside Ugarit — absent from Amarna letters, Egyptian sources mentioning West Semitic deities, the Bible, and Philo of Byblos. The single Phoenician attestation is debatable.
- In contrast, "the cult of Baal is at home on the coast."
- Arabic usage (per W. Robertson Smith): *ba'l* refers to land "wholly dependent on rain," contrasted with irrigated land. Baba Batra 3:1 allegedly preserves this older usage.
- Ugarit's climate: "rains occur over seven or eight months and exceed 800 mm each year" — no need for dry farming or irrigation.
- Inland areas where Athtar is attested "practiced either dry farming or natural irrigation."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM for the ecological argument. MEDIUM for the geographical distribution. The evidence for Athtar's inland restriction is stronger than the ecological explanation for WHY.

**What's at stake:** This is an attempt at a materialist/ecological explanation for mythological conflict — one of Smith's few ventures into explaining WHY one god supplanted another. If correct, it suggests that theological change (Baal's rise, Athtar's decline) was driven by practical environmental factors rather than theological innovation. It also makes the Ugaritic pantheon a snapshot of a historical PROCESS: the family structure may reflect an EARLIER configuration when Athtar was among the favored sons, with Baal's ascendance representing a more recent development.

**Who disagrees:** The ecological argument is speculative. Smith himself calls it "tempting" and notes "it is impossible to ground any further speculation." The Arabic *ba'l* / irrigation distinction (from W. Robertson Smith, 19th century) may not reflect Bronze Age usage. Baba Batra 3:1 is a late rabbinic text — its value for reconstructing Bronze Age semantics is questionable. The rainfall/irrigation binary may be too neat: inland areas also received rain, and coastal areas also had wells and springs.

**Alternative reading:** Athtar's weakness at Ugarit could simply reflect the political dominance of the Baal cult at that particular city. Other coastal cities MIGHT have had Athtar cults — the argument from silence (no evidence for coastal Athtar) is just that, an argument from silence. Athtar's "weakness" in the Baal Cycle might be a literary effect of Ugarit's pro-Baal royal ideology, not a reflection of actual cultic reality elsewhere. The god may have remained powerful inland.

**My assessment:** This is the chapter's weakest claim, and Smith knows it — his language is speculative throughout ("tempting to view," "one might argue," "impossible to ground any further speculation"). I appreciate his willingness to offer an ecological hypothesis, but the evidence is thin. The geographical distribution argument (Athtar inland, Baal coastal) is the stronger part and doesn't depend on the ecological explanation. The historical process — a coastal storm-god cult displacing an inland astral-warrior cult — is plausible even without the rainfall/irrigation mechanism.

## Claim 8: Israelite religion preserved and adapted the divine family concept — the "sons of God," astral background, and polemical revision are all reflexes

**Author's claim (developed across the chapter's final pages):** Israelite texts show "a cult of El that included a minimum number of these astral deities" — the divine family structure survived in Israel, initially as part of Yahweh's identification with El and later as an object of polemic.

**Evidence presented:**
- Job 38:6-7: "When the morning stars sang together and all the divine beings (*bənê 'ĕlōhîm*) shouted for joy" — the morning stars are parallel to the sons of God, echoing the Ugaritic equation of El's sons with stars (CAT 1.10 I 3-5).
- Isaiah 14:13: The king of Babylon attempts to exalt his throne "above the stars of El" (*mimma'al ləkôkəbê-'ēl*) — an astral El survives as a polemical target.
- Daniel 8:9-11: The "little horn" (Antiochus IV) casts down "the host of heaven" — astral divine beings are still in play.
- Shahar survives: Isaiah 14:12 alludes to "Shahar ben Helal," the fallen star. Shahar also appears as an element in Hebrew proper names.
- Shalem survives: in proper names like *'ăbîšālôm* (Absalom), and Arad/Ein Gedi/Lachish inscriptions.
- Sun and moon: "Given their earlier and later attestation as deities, the sun and moon likely continued as deities at this stage as well."
- Yahweh's "host of heaven": 1 Kings 22:19 (Yahweh seated on his throne with "all the host of heaven" attending), Zephaniah 1:5 (condemning those who swear by Yahweh AND by Milcom — astral cult within Yahwism), 2 Kings 21:5 (Manasseh builds altars "for all the host of heaven in the two courts in the house of Yahweh").
- Joshua 10:12: The sun and moon as part of Yahweh's military host.
- Smith's critical observation: "Some biblical scholars judge El as indigenous and Israelite whereas others view the host of heaven and astral deities as foreign and non-Israelite. Yet given the biblical acceptance of El (under the guise of his identification with Yahweh) and the condemnations of astral deities, one might argue that biblical historiography has influenced the differing scholarly assessments of El and astral deities."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the general claim that Israelite religion preserved divine family concepts. MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific claim that the astral character was indigenous rather than imported. The polemical texts (condemning astral worship) can be read as evidence of LATE foreign influence (Assyrian astral cult) rather than EARLY indigenous practice.

**What's at stake:** If Smith is right that astral divine family religion was INDIGENOUS to Israel and later condemned, the standard scholarly narrative (astral worship as Assyrian import in the 8th-7th centuries) needs revision. It would mean the Deuteronomistic historians weren't fighting foreign influence but suppressed INDIGENOUS traditions — a more radical claim about the construction of biblical orthodoxy. It also means the divine family structure wasn't just Ugaritic background but was part of Israel's OWN religious heritage, which makes its eventual suppression by monotheistic rhetoric a more complex historical process.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date astral worship in Israel to the Neo-Assyrian period (Iron II) as a foreign import. The evidence for Manasseh's astral altars (2 Kings 21) is precisely in the context of Assyrian hegemony. The calf cult at Bethel and Dan might be indigenous, but astral worship might not be. Smith's counterargument — that the indigenousness of El implies the indigenousness of his astral family — is logical but depends on the strength of the El-astral connection, which he himself rates as "minimal."

**Alternative reading:** The biblical "host of heaven" may reflect a genuinely late development (Assyrian influence) that was retrojected into earlier periods by biblical writers or conflated by modern scholars with the older divine council. The divine council and the astral host might be DIFFERENT concepts that merged in the biblical period. Job 38:7's "morning stars" / "sons of God" parallelism might be poetic, not evidence of an ongoing cult. The proper names with *šlm* don't necessarily indicate active worship of the deity Shalem — they could be fossilized theophoric elements.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument about Israelite preservation of divine family concepts is generally persuasive, but I think he overreaches on the "indigenous astral cult" claim. The biblical evidence for astral worship clusters in the late monarchy and exilic periods, which is exactly when Assyrian and Babylonian astral religion would have been influential. Smith's key insight — that the scholarly double standard (El = indigenous, astral = foreign) might reflect biblical rhetoric rather than historical reality — is genuinely important methodologically. But the specific claim that Israel had an indigenous astral family cult of El needs more evidence than this chapter provides. The name evidence (Shahar, Shalem) is suggestive but not dispositive. I'd like to see engagement with the alternative: that Israel's divine council was originally non-astral and acquired astral features through Assyro-Babylonian influence, with earlier texts (Job 38:7) reflecting poetic imagery rather than cultic reality.

## Chapter 3 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Four tiers = patriarchal household homology | HIGH | Over-schematization; tier 3 (craftsman) may be corpus artifact |
| 2: Divine family = polytheism's "oneness" | MEDIUM | Family as ONE metaphor among several; "unity" claim too strong |
| 3: CAT 4.360 and myths share social vocabulary | HIGH | One administrative text may be unrepresentative |
| 4: El's family has astral background | MEDIUM | Minimal evidence for El/Athirat themselves; polemical texts may be late |
| 5: Baal is outsider (bn dgn, concubine's son) | HIGH | Philo of Byblos's reliability; alternative = political integration of foreign god |
| 6: Royal patriarchal household is the model | HIGH | Vocabulary not exclusively royal; household model may be primary |
| 7: Athtar vs. Baal = inland vs. coastal cult shift | LOW-MEDIUM | Ecological explanation speculative; argument from silence for coastal Athtar |
| 8: Israel preserved divine family + astral concepts | MEDIUM-HIGH | Astral cult may be late import, not indigenous survival |

**Strongest section:** Section 2 — the CAT 4.360 comparison. The philological demonstration that administrative and mythological texts share the same social vocabulary is the chapter's most original and best-supported contribution. This is where Smith does what Schloen didn't: applies the patrimonial household model to the divine texts themselves.

**Weakest section:** The Athtar/Baal ecological argument in Section 3. Smith's speculative language signals his own uncertainty, and the Arabic/rabbinic evidence for the *ba'l*/irrigation distinction is too late and too distant to carry the weight. The geographical distribution point is stronger and doesn't need the ecological overlay.

**Smith's method:** The chapter exemplifies Smith's strength as a philologically rigorous comparatist. He works inductively from specific textual evidence (CAT 1.65, 1.4, 4.360, proper names) to larger synthetic claims, and he's scrupulous about flagging uncertainty ("on the face of it," "cautiously suggest," "admittedly minimal"). Unlike some comparative work that treats Ugaritic and biblical texts as direct ancestors/descendants, Smith respects the temporal and cultural distance while arguing for shared conceptual structures.

**Unresolved tension:** The relationship between "council" and "family" as organizing metaphors. Smith treats them as complementary (Ch. 2 = council, Ch. 3 = family), but he's also said they're "coterminous." Are they genuinely two aspects of one reality, or does one dominate? The council metaphor emphasizes deliberation and hierarchy; the family metaphor emphasizes intimacy and generation. Does one do explanatory work the other doesn't? Smith gestures at this in the final paragraph — "Council in effect points to the holders of power in council" — but the synthesis may await Chapter 4.

**Cross-reference:** Smith's claim about the astral background of El's family (Claim 4) intersects with Frank Moore Cross's work on El and the divine council. Cross emphasized El as the aged patriarch, but Smith adds the astral dimension that Cross's work on the "council of the gods" and "the sons of El" implies. Römer's *The Invention of God* argues that Yahweh's identification with El occurred early, which would mean the astral family WAS part of early Israelite religion (via El), supporting Smith's "indigenous" claim. But Römer also emphasizes the LATE arrival of astral polemics (Deuteronomy 4:19, etc.), which could support the "late import" alternative reading. The tension between "early indigenous astral family" and "late Assyrian astral import" remains unresolved in both Smith and Römer.

**Forward link to Ch. 4:** Smith promises that Ch. 4 will "take up some of these relations in more detail and in the final section explores further the presence of family language in Ugaritic myth." The divine council (Ch. 2) and divine family (Ch. 3) will presumably be synthesized, and the family language's role in the coherence of Ugaritic polytheism will be more fully argued.
