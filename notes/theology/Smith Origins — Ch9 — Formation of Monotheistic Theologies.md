# Smith — Ch9: Formation of Monotheistic Theologies in Biblical Literature

Smith examines three biblical corpora where older polytheistic concepts are reconfigured into monotheistic frameworks: (1) Genesis 1 and priestly creation theology, (2) Wisdom literature (Proverbs 1–9) and Woman Wisdom, and (3) apocalyptic (Daniel 7) and the reconfiguration of the divine council. Each represents a different social location — priests, wisdom teachers/Levites, and apocalyptic visionaries. A fourth section traces the broader shift from Canaanite myth to biblical monotheism, arguing that myth wasn't rejected but transformed: old roles were transferred to Yahweh, subordinate female figures (Wisdom, Jerusalem) replaced goddesses, and priestly editors functionally censored traditions incompatible with their holiness norms.

## Claim 1: Genesis 1 creates a "monotheistic poetics" — depersonalizes cosmic enemies
→ [[claim-genesis-1-eliminates-conflict-mythology]] | **smith-obm-9.1** | Confidence: high
  Genesis 1 eliminates conflict mythology by enclosing old cosmic enemies (tehom, tanninim, sun/moon) within the created order. The key move is the enclosure strategy — enemies aren't defeated but absorbed. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-as-transformed-canaanite-myth]]. Depends on: [[claim-genesis-1-monotheistic-poetics]], [[claim-biblical-myth-exists-baal-cycle-standard]].

## Claim 2: Priestly creation transforms the old royal model — cosmos becomes sanctuary
→ [[claim-priestly-creation-transforms-royal-theology]] | **smith-obm-9.2** | Confidence: high
  Humanity replaces the king as divine image; three priestly institutions (Sabbath, calendar, kashrut) are embedded in creation itself. The cosmos-as-sanctuary reading legitimates the post-exilic priestly regime. Suppports: [[claim-monotheism-as-transformed-canaanite-myth]], [[claim-monotheism-as-exilic-rhetoric-not-religious-revolution]].

## Claim 3: Genesis 1:26 inverts Ezekiel 1:26 — humanity elevated to divine image
→ [[claim-genesis-1-26-inverts-ezekiel-1-26]] | **smith-obm-9.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  The Priestly writer reverses Ezekiel's reduction of anthropomorphism: instead of reducing God to human terms, Genesis 1 elevates humanity to divine terms. The selem/demut vocabulary also functions as implicit anti-idol polemic. Depends on: [[claim-genesis-1-eliminates-conflict-mythology]], [[claim-priestly-creation-transforms-royal-theology]].

## Claim 4: Woman Wisdom in Proverbs 1–9 takes over Asherah's roles and imagery
→ [[claim-woman-wisdom-takes-over-asherah-imagery]] | **smith-obm-9.4** | Confidence: medium
  The tree of life, female life-giver, and nurturing roles associated with Asherah are absorbed into personified Wisdom — a wisdom-scribal strategy for monotheizing goddess traditions. The 'asre/'ussar wordplay with asherah is speculative. Depends on: [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]], [[claim-ot-asherah-equals-ugaritic-athirat]].

## Claim 5: Daniel 7 preserves royal conflict mythology but removes the human king
→ [[claim-daniel-7-removes-human-king-son-of-man-community]] | **smith-obm-9.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  The "one like a son of man" represents the persecuted community, not a Davidic monarch — apocalyptic adapts royal theology for a post-monarchic world. Smith's briefest case study (barely a page). <!-- Will edge to Cross Parts II-III when extracted -->.

## Claim 6: The reduction of myth occurred in two stages driven by priestly concerns
→ [[claim-two-stage-reduction-of-myth-priestly-censorship]] | **smith-obm-9.6** | Confidence: medium-high
  Stage 1: early Israel already less anthropomorphic than Ugarit. Stage 2 (8th-6th centuries): further muting driven by monolatry, priestly censorship of sex/death, and scribalism. The priestly censorship model risks circularity. Tension with Ch6: did Israel never have death-mythology or was it censored?

## Claim 7: Woman Wisdom and personified Jerusalem functioned as goddess replacements
→ [[claim-wisdom-jerusalem-replace-goddesses]] | **smith-obm-9.7** | Confidence: low-medium
  "Counter-advertisements" to the cults of Astarte and the Queen of Heaven — goddess imagery usurped and neutered into acceptable literary personifications. Smith's most speculative claim; no direct textual evidence. Depends on: [[claim-woman-wisdom-takes-over-asherah-imagery]].

## Claim 8: Biblical monotheism IS transformed Canaanite myth — not rejected myth
→ [[claim-monotheism-as-transformed-canaanite-myth]] | **smith-obm-9.8** | Confidence: high
  The chapter's master argument: three social locations (priests, wisdom scribes, apocalyptic visionaries) produced three different transformation strategies. Monotheism is Israel's own foundational myth, not a rejection of myth. Depends on Claims 1-7. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-as-exilic-rhetoric-not-religious-revolution]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]].

**Strongest section:** The Genesis 1 analysis (Claims 1–3). Smith gives this nearly half the chapter, and it shows. The "monotheistic poetics" concept, the three priestly themes embedded in creation, and the inversion of Ezekiel 1:26 are all well-argued and textually grounded. The cosmos-as-sanctuary reading, while speculative, is supported by Psalm citations and Levenson's homology thesis.

**Weakest section:** The Wisdom and Daniel 7 case studies (Claims 4–5). Both are drastically underdeveloped — barely a page each — and read like outlines rather than fully argued sections. The Woman Wisdom/Asherah connection is more asserted than demonstrated. Daniel 7 gets the shortest treatment despite being arguably the most complex of the three corpora. Smith needed to double the length of both sections.

**The methodological problem:** Smith's social-location framework (priests, wisdom teachers, apocalyptic visionaries) is productive, but he doesn't demonstrate that the theological strategies correlate with the social locations as tightly as he claims. We know priests produced Genesis 1 — but did "wisdom teachers" produce Proverbs 1–9? Did "apocalyptic visionaries" produce Daniel 7? The social location is inferred from the content, which risks circularity.

**Tension with previous chapters:** Smith's argument here about priestly censorship of myth (Claim 6) directly echoes Ch5's argument about holiness norms censoring divine sex and death. But Ch6 argued that Israel probably never HAD a Death-confrontation mythology — it was a local Ugaritic literary production. The tension remains unresolved: is the absence of myth in the Bible due to active priestly censorship, or because the myths were never there to begin with? Smith seems to want both answers depending on the chapter.

**Cross-reference:** Cross's work on the divine council in Daniel 7 would substantially strengthen Smith's apocalyptic section. Smith mentions it only in passing but Cross's detailed analysis of the throne scene and the "son of man" as the angelic host would give the Daniel 7 case study the depth it lacks. Similarly, Romer's work on Wisdom and monotheism (cited in the task brief) isn't engaged at all — Smith's treatment predates much of that scholarship, but the note should flag the connection.

**Overall value:** Despite the uneven treatment of the three corpora, this chapter makes an essential contribution to Smith's argument. It demonstrates concretely HOW different segments of Judean society reconfigured inherited polytheistic material into monotheistic frameworks. The transformation thesis — monotheism as rewritten myth, not rejected myth — is the chapter's lasting contribution and one of the book's central arguments.
