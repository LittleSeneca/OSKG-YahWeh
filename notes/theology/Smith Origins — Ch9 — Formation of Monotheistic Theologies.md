# Smith — Ch9: Formation of Monotheistic Theologies in Biblical Literature

Smith examines three biblical corpora where older polytheistic concepts are reconfigured into monotheistic frameworks: (1) Genesis 1 and priestly creation theology, (2) Wisdom literature (Proverbs 1–9) and Woman Wisdom, and (3) apocalyptic (Daniel 7) and the reconfiguration of the divine council. Each represents a different social location — priests, wisdom teachers/Levites, and apocalyptic visionaries. A fourth section traces the broader shift from Canaanite myth to biblical monotheism, arguing that myth wasn't rejected but transformed: old roles were transferred to Yahweh, subordinate female figures (Wisdom, Jerusalem) replaced goddesses, and priestly editors functionally censored traditions incompatible with their holiness norms.

## Claim 1: Genesis 1 creates a "monotheistic poetics" — creation by divine word eliminates conflict mythology and depersonalizes the old cosmic enemies

**Author's claim:** "Genesis 1 creates a 'monotheistic poetics'... the lack of any conflict, or even any personification of the cosmic oceans or waters, is designed to heighten the picture of a powerful God who but speaks and the divine will is accomplished." The old divine enemies are "creatures like other creatures rendered in this story," enclosed within the created order and transformed by omission.

**Evidence presented:**
- The cosmic waters (*těhôm*) in Genesis 1:2 are not personified, not addressed, not fought — they simply exist as raw material for divine ordering. Contrast with the Baal Cycle where Yamm (Sea) is a personal divine antagonist defeated in combat.
- The *tannînîm* ("sea monsters") in Genesis 1:21 are mentioned only in passing as creatures God made — "not only is the conflict role eliminated in Genesis 1; even the old role of cosmic forces as domesticated has been muted by downplaying them, even depersonalizing them."
- Sun and moon are called only "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (Gen 1:16). These titles are not necessarily polemical — "great light" (*nrt rbt*) appears as a title for the Ugaritic sun-goddess Shapshu (CAT 1.161.19) — but Genesis 1 strips them of divine status: "like the sea creatures, they are located within the created order."
- The key move: "Here ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left in Genesis 1's 'monotheistic poetics.' Such a depiction drains power from any old forces of opposition and leaves God as the only power in the universe."

**Confidence:** HIGH. The contrast between Genesis 1 and the combat myths (Baal Cycle, Enuma Elish, Psalm 74, Isaiah 51) is indisputable. Smith's reading of the depersonalization strategy is well-supported.

**What's at stake:** If Genesis 1 deliberately mutes conflict mythology rather than being ignorant of it, the Priestly writer knew the old traditions and chose to reconfigure them. This makes Genesis 1 a theologically motivated act of literary transformation, not a naive or primitive account.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Genesis 1 as preserving vestiges of conflict mythology (the *těhôm* as Tiamat, the *tannînîm* as Rahab/Leviathan). Those who see Genesis 1 as independent of, rather than responding to, West Semitic combat traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The demythologization could be less polemical than Smith claims. The Priestly writer may simply have operated in a different genre (liturgical/calendrical) where cosmic combat was irrelevant, rather than actively suppressing it.

**My assessment:** Smith is right that Genesis 1 is doing something deliberate here. But "monotheistic poetics" is vague — it describes the effect (only God has power) without specifying the mechanism. The real insight is the *enclosure* strategy: the old enemies aren't defeated or banished but absorbed into the created order. That's genuinely innovative compared to the combat model.

## Claim 2: The Priestly creation theology transforms the old royal model — humanity replaces the king as divine image, and the cosmos becomes a sanctuary

**Author's claim:** "The creation of the human person in Genesis 1:26, in the 'image' and 'likeness' of God, represents a major shift from the old royal model. The idea of 'the image' of the gods was, in ancient Near East, applied to the king. It was the king who was the image of the deity." Genesis 1 democratizes this: "humanity, understood as the participant in the cosmic Sabbath, is to be the holy image of God on earth."

**Evidence presented:**
- ANE background: The king as divine image is widespread. Egyptian instruction of Meri-ka-re: "They who have issued from his body are his images" — applied to humanity generally, which Genesis 1 may echo. But the biblical passage shifts the concept from royal privilege to human vocation.
- Three priestly themes embedded in creation: (1) divine resting (*šbt*) on the seventh day anticipates the Sabbath institution (Exod 20, 31); (2) the lights mark feast-days, weeks, and years — the priestly cultic calendar (Gen 1:14); (3) the division of the universe into heavens, earth, and seas with animals assigned to each sphere foreshadows the dietary system of *kashrut*.
- Cosmos as sanctuary: "One may suggest that the cosmos in Genesis 1 was to be understood as a holy place, such as a sanctuary." The Temple in Jerusalem "mirrored the cosmos," and "biblical descriptions of creation and temple-building have influenced one another and constitute, in J. D. Levenson's view, a 'homology.'"
- Psalm 78:69: "He built his sanctuary like the high heavens, like the earth which he has founded for ever." Psalm 150:1 uses sanctuary and sky as parallel terms.
- W. Houston's argument: *kashrut* arose from priestly protection of temple purity — if so, Genesis 1's delineation of cosmic realms by animals "may evoke temple practice and in turn a vision of the world as a kind of temple."
- Historical context: "Genesis 1 reflects this change: to the royal model has been added a priestly model. The politics of creation have changed. There is still a king in this world, but it is the King of Kings... This outlook would serve Israel well in exile and beyond when responsibility for community order passed from the Davidic dynasty to the priesthood of Aaron."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the democratization of the divine image and the priestly themes embedded in creation. MEDIUM on the cosmos-as-sanctuary reading — it's suggestive but Smith acknowledges it "may lie behind Genesis 1 or may be evoked by it."

**What's at stake:** If Genesis 1 encodes priestly institutions (Sabbath, calendar, *kashrut*) into the fabric of creation itself, these are not later human inventions but built into the cosmic order by divine design. The Priestly theology legitimates the post-exilic priestly regime by making its institutions primordial.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Genesis 1 earlier (pre-exilic) and see the priestly themes as inherent to creation theology rather than reflecting exilic/post-exilic priestly ascendancy. Those who read the divine image language as ontological rather than functional/vocational.

**Alternative reading:** The cosmos-as-temple idea could work the other way — the Temple was built to model the cosmos, not the cosmos envisioned as temple. The direction of influence matters and isn't settled.

**My assessment:** The threefold embedding of priestly institutions (Sabbath, calendar, *kashrut*) is the strongest part of this argument — it's concrete and textually supported. The cosmos-as-temple idea is more speculative but the Psalm citations (78:69, 150:1) do support a genuine homology in Israelite thought. The democratization of the royal divine image is a genuinely radical theological move, well-contextualized by the shift from monarchy to priesthood.

## Claim 3: Genesis 1:26 inverts Ezekiel 1:26 — the Priestly writer reverses the prophet's reduction of anthropomorphism to magnify humanity instead

**Author's claim:** "Genesis 1:26 inverts Ezekiel 1:26, which itself represents a deliberate attempt to stress the transcendent character of Yahweh by reducing the anthropomorphic presentation of Yahweh in the heavenly divine council... Ezekiel 1:26 conveys the prophet's vision of the divine with the language of 'image' of the human person... whereas Genesis 1:26–28 presents a vision of the human person in the likeness of the divine."

**Evidence presented:**
- Ezekiel 1:26 uses *děmût kěmar'ēh 'ādām* ("an image like the appearance of a human") — the divine is reduced to human terms, a deliberately limited anthropomorphism compared to earlier prophetic visions (e.g., Isaiah 6).
- Genesis 1:26 uses the same vocabulary (*ṣelem*, "image"; *děmût*, "likeness") but reverses the direction: "Unlike Ezekiel 1:26, which reduces Yahweh to human terms in an anthropomorphic portrait... Genesis 1:26–28 magnifies the human person in divine (or perhaps mythic) terms."
- The vocabulary of *ṣelem* and *děmût* is also used for statues — "this vocabulary may represent an implicit polemic aimed against the making of images. Clearly humanity serves as the living image of the Israelite deity, and it is perhaps implicit then that images of man-made objects constitute lifeless symbols of dead gods."
- This anti-idol polemic becomes explicit in Second Isaiah (Ch10).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The verbal parallel between Ezekiel 1:26 and Genesis 1:26 is striking and the inversion reading is elegant. But Smith's claim of direct literary dependence rather than shared priestly vocabulary is harder to prove.

**What's at stake:** If Genesis 1:26 is responding to Ezekiel 1:26, the Priestly creation account is later than Ezekiel (early 6th century) and represents a further theological development. The sequence would be: Isaiah 6 (full anthropomorphic council vision) → Ezekiel 1 (qualified/reduced anthropomorphism) → Genesis 1 (humanity elevated to divine image instead). This is a plausible developmental arc.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date P earlier than Ezekiel. Those who see the parallel as coincidental or as both drawing on common priestly language rather than direct inversion.

**Alternative reading:** Both Ezekiel 1 and Genesis 1 could be independent developments from the same priestly tradition, using *ṣelem*/*děmût* language for different purposes, without one being a response to the other.

**My assessment:** The inversion reading is elegant and fits Smith's larger argument about priestly transformation of earlier traditions. But the evidence for direct dependence is thin — shared vocabulary in priestly circles is expected. The anti-idol polemic reading is stronger and well-supported by the use of *ṣelem* for statues elsewhere.

## Claim 4: Woman Wisdom in Proverbs 1–9 takes over roles and imagery previously associated with the goddess Asherah — a wisdom-scribal strategy for monotheizing goddess traditions

**Author's claim:** Proverbs 1–9 "presents a divine invitation from the female personification of Wisdom" that draws on Canaanite goddess imagery. B. Lang suggests Wisdom functions as "the divine patroness of the Israelite school system." If Proverbs is a Levitical product, "one might argue further that the female personification of Wisdom is the divine patron of the Levitical duties of teaching and writing."

**Evidence presented:**
- Scholars have long compared Wisdom to Asherah: "The 'tree of life,' which recalls the tree of the *asherah*, appears in Israelite tradition as a metaphorical expression for Wisdom (Proverbs 3:18; cf. 11:30, 15:4; Genesis 3:22; Revelation 2:7)."
- "Like the symbol of the *asherah*, Wisdom is a female figure, providing life and nurturing."
- Proverbs 3:13–18 forms a chiasm: the unit opens with *'ašrê* ("happy," v. 13) and closes with *mě'uššār* ("made happy," v. 18), and "the inside terms of the chiasm are *ḥokmâ*, 'wisdom,' and *'ēṣ-ḥayyîm*, 'a tree of life.'" Smith suggests the terms *'ašrê* and *mě'uššār* "perhaps allude to the *asherah*, the tree symbolizing life and well-being."
- This represents "a move to 'monotheize' the imagery of the Asherah in a wisdom framework."
- Proverbs' verbal links with Deuteronomy suggest Levitical authorship — and Levites were assigned teaching and writing roles. Wisdom would then be the divine patron of specifically Levitical scribal-educational functions.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The general comparison between Wisdom and Asherah has a long scholarly pedigree, but the specific verbal allusions (*'ašrê*/*'u*ššār* → *asherah*) are speculative. The Levitical connection is similarly suggestive rather than proven.

**What's at stake:** If Woman Wisdom is a monotheized Asherah, then Israelite monotheism didn't simply eliminate the goddess — it absorbed and transformed her into a subordinate, personified figure. This is a more subtle strategy than outright rejection and suggests the goddess's symbolic power was too strong to simply discard.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Woman Wisdom as an indigenous Israelite development unrelated to goddess traditions. Those who read the verbal links with Deuteronomy differently (post-exilic rather than Levitical).

**Alternative reading:** Woman Wisdom could be a hypostasis of Yahweh's own wisdom attribute, developed from within Israelite sapiential tradition, with any resemblance to Asherah being coincidental or at most a very distant echo. The tree-of-life metaphor need not imply Asherah — trees as symbols of life are nearly universal.

**My assessment:** Smith's argument here is more suggestive than demonstrative. The *'ašrê*/*'u*ššār* wordplay with *asherah* is clever but thin — it's one consonant (*š*) and the association relies on the reader making the jump. The broader pattern (female personification + tree of life + nurturing/life-giving role) is more persuasive as a cumulative case. The Levitical connection is underdeveloped and needs more evidence.

## Claim 5: Daniel 7 preserves the old royal conflict mythology but removes the human king — apocalyptic reconfigures the divine council with the "one like a son of man" representing the persecuted community

**Author's claim:** "Post-exilic apocalyptic preserved the conflictual nature of the forces in the cosmos but with the human king removed from the equation." In Daniel 7, "the human counterpart to Yahweh is no human king but a persecuted community that can take solace only in divine help. Here are the political vestiges of the old royal theology."

**Evidence presented:**
- The old pattern: cosmic divine conflict mirrors human royal conflict. In the second millennium, divine kings and human kings, cosmic enemies and human enemies were paired. This is the royal ideology Smith traced earlier in the book.
- Daniel 7 expresses the conflict in "largely cosmic terms, with the four beasts rising out of the cosmic sea." These beasts represent four world empires — the cosmic sea produces political threats.
- But the human counterpart to the divine warrior is transformed: "the human counterpart to Yahweh is no human king but a persecuted community." The "one like a son of man" who receives eternal dominion (Dan 7:13–14) represents the faithful community, not a Davidic monarch.
- "Such a portrait, though less overtly political than Daniel 7 and the apocalyptic tradition, nonetheless covertly adapts to the new order on the earthly level and adjusts its religious vision accordingly."
- Smith compares this to Genesis 1: both "magnify Judah's deity over the whole cosmos, despite what takes place in Judah's historical experience."
- Earlier examples: Isaiah 27:1 already projects the divine conflict into the eschatological future — "In that day the Lord... will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent." Apocalyptic takes this further by removing the human royal agent entirely.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the structural argument. The removal of the human king from the conflict equation is clear. MEDIUM on whether the "son of man" represents the community rather than an individual angelic figure (Michael?).

**What's at stake:** If Daniel 7 preserves the STRUCTURE of royal conflict mythology while replacing its CONTENT, apocalyptic is not a radical break from earlier Israelite religion but a creative adaptation of it. The monarch is gone, but the cosmic-political pattern endures. This supports Smith's larger argument that monotheism was achieved through transformation, not invention.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the "one like a son of man" as an individual messianic figure, an angel (Michael), or a heavenly being rather than a community symbol. Those who see Daniel 7 as borrowing from non-Israelite (especially Persian/Zoroastrian) apocalyptic patterns rather than developing Israelite royal theology.

**Alternative reading:** The "son of man" could be the archangel Michael as heavenly patron of Israel — this preserves the individual-mediator model (like the king) while transferring it to the angelic realm. Not a community symbol but an angelic replacement for the king.

**My assessment:** Smith's reading of Daniel 7 is frustratingly brief — barely a page — for a text that deserves extended treatment. The connection to royal theology is plausible but underargued. The Cross reference (divine council imagery in Daniel 7) would substantially strengthen this section; Smith gestures at it but doesn't develop it. This is the weakest of the three case studies.

## Claim 6: The reduction of myth in biblical literature occurred in two stages — an earlier stage of reduced anthropomorphism relative to Ugarit, and a later stage (8th–6th centuries) of further muting driven by priestly concerns

**Author's claim:** "Two stages of avoiding and muting anthropomorphism (and thus mythic language) are discernible, one at the outset of known Israelite texts and another perhaps in the eighth to sixth centuries."

**Evidence presented — Stage 1 (early Israel):**
- Early biblical poetry shows many points of contact with Ugaritic literature in describing the storm-god, but "Yahweh is not as personified in the oldest biblical poetry as Baal is in Ugaritic tradition."
- Baal's conflict with Yamm (CAT 1.2 IV) is "discernibly more anthropomorphic than any biblical descriptions of Yahweh's conflict against cosmic enemies." Ugaritic texts describe gods locked in hand-to-hand combat; biblical texts describe heavenly armies led by angelic warriors, with Yahweh more removed.
- Divine sex: Ugaritic texts graphically describe Baal copulating with a heifer "seventy-seven times"//"eighty-eight times" (CAT 1.5 V 19–21). The Bible "shows no example of sex or death in the case of Yahweh comparable to the sexual behavior and death in Ugaritic poetry." Even Ezekiel 16:9 (possible sexual metaphor) is "quite muted compared to divine sexual scenes attested in the Ugaritic text."
- Theriomorphism: Ugaritic deities are bulls (El) and birds (Anat). "Divine theriomorphous depictions in Israelite literature are rare and slight by comparison, surviving only in a handful of metaphors and titles."

**Evidence presented — Stage 2 (8th–6th centuries):**
- Earlier works like the Yahwist show "uninhibited anthropomorphism" (H. Bloom); later works further mute it.
- Deuteronomy 4:12, 15: Yahweh has no "form" (*těmûnâ*) — linked to the prohibition against graven images.
- Ezekiel 1 deliberately reduces the anthropomorphic presentation of the divine council compared to Isaiah 6.

**Three reasons for the reduction:**
1. **Monolatry concerns:** "The inclusion of other divine images may have been influenced by concerns about monolatry, as commentators have long claimed."
2. **Priestly censorship:** "The presentation of Yahweh generally as sexless and unrelated to the realm of death was apparently produced by a priesthood who viewed this deity as fully removed from realms of impurity, especially sex and death. Holier than the holy of holies, the deity of the priesthood would have epitomized the fullest possibilities of sacredness and separation."
3. **Scribalism:** The role of scribalism in forming Israel's religious tradition — Yahweh presented as divine scribe (Exod 31:18; 34:1; Deut 4:13; 5:22). Other sociological factors shaped which divine images survived.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the two-stage model. HIGH on the contrast between Ugaritic and Israelite anthropomorphism. MEDIUM on the priestly censorship model — it's plausible but hard to verify.

**What's at stake:** If myth was reduced rather than rejected, and the reduction was driven by specific social groups (priests, scribes) with specific agendas (holiness, monolatry), then the Bible's relative absence of myth is not evidence of Israel's theological uniqueness but of the editorial success of particular interest groups. The "demise of myth" is a sociopolitical process, not a theological achievement.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israel's reduced myth as evidence of early monotheism (Kaufmann, Albright). Those who question whether the two stages can be distinguished given the difficulty of dating biblical texts.

**Alternative reading:** The reduction could be a function of genre rather than chronology. The early poetry (Judges 5, Psalm 29) is hymnic; the Baal Cycle is narrative myth. Different genres have different conventions for anthropomorphism. The "reduction" might be an artifact of what was preserved, not what was produced.

**My assessment:** The Ugaritic-Israelite contrast on divine sexuality is Smith's strongest evidence — the difference is stark and genre alone can't explain it. But the two-stage model is sketchy: "early" texts are hard to date, and Smith acknowledges "the sources for the first stage are unfortunately inaccessible to modern students." The priestly censorship argument, while plausible, risks circularity: the priests edited out myth because they edited out myth.

## Claim 7: New mythic figures — Woman Wisdom and personified Jerusalem — functioned as replacements for goddesses, serving as "counter-advertisements" to the cults of Astarte and the Queen of Heaven

**Author's claim:** "In view of the divine language and imagery associated with both figures, perhaps both figures represent substitutions for divine female figures." G. von Rad termed Wisdom in Proverbs 9:1–9 "as a contrast [*Kontrastbild*] and a defence against" customs associated with a goddess. Wisdom and Jerusalem "may have usurped imagery associated generally with goddesses, perhaps specifically with the *asherah*... and applied it to the figure of Wisdom as a 'counter-advertisement' to the cults of Astarte and the Queen of Heaven."

**Evidence presented:**
- The presence of Astarte in Israel during the monarchy period is archaeologically and textually attested.
- The *asherah* was present in the Jerusalem Temple (2 Kgs 23:6) and widely worshipped.
- The Queen of Heaven is mentioned in Jeremiah 7:18 and 44:17–19 as a popular object of devotion.
- Both Wisdom and Jerusalem are female personifications with divine attributes — they occupy the symbolic space goddesses once filled.
- "Hence, new myths may in part represent replacements in service to monotheistic representations of divinity."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is Smith's most speculative claim in the chapter. The idea that personified Wisdom "replaces" Asherah is plausible as a functional analogy but impossible to verify. There's no smoking-gun text saying "Wisdom instead of Asherah."

**What's at stake:** If Wisdom and Jerusalem are goddess-replacements, monotheism didn't eliminate the feminine divine — it transformed it into acceptable literary personifications. This is a domestication strategy: the goddess is neutered into a metaphor. Feminist critics might see this as patriarchal erasure rather than creative transformation.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Wisdom as a genuinely new theological development unrelated to goddess polemics. Those who read the female personification of Jerusalem as rooted in the ancient Near Eastern city-goddess tradition (which predates and is independent of Asherah worship).

**Alternative reading:** Wisdom and Jerusalem could be independent developments from within Israelite traditions — personified attributes and city laments have their own literary histories. The goddess-replacement theory may overstate the polemical intent and underestimate the creative autonomy of Israelite scribes.

**My assessment:** The functional analogy is intriguing but Smith doesn't provide enough evidence to move it beyond suggestion. The von Rad citation gives the idea scholarly pedigree, but Smith needed to develop this more. It's telling that this is relegated to a few paragraphs in the final section rather than receiving its own case study. The "counter-advertisement" framing is vivid but anachronistic.

## Claim 8: Biblical monotheism represents not the rejection of Canaanite myth but its creative transformation — monotheism IS Israel's own foundational myth

**Author's claim:** "In short, biblical monotheism included transformations of Canaanite myth. Put differently, biblical monotheism constituted part of Israelite's own foundational myth comparable in some respects to Canaan's own myths."

**Evidence presented (synthesis of the chapter):**
- Three different social locations produced three different strategies:
  - **Priests** (Genesis 1): Eliminated conflict, depersonalized enemies, enclosed them within creation, envisioned cosmos as sanctuary.
  - **Wisdom scribes/Levites** (Proverbs 1–9): Transformed goddess imagery into personified Wisdom, creating a female divine patron subordinate to Yahweh.
  - **Apocalyptic visionaries** (Daniel 7): Preserved the conflict structure but removed the human king, replacing him with the persecuted community.
- "Both Ugaritic and Israelite royal and scribal-priestly groups constituted the primary literary preservers of mythic material, and they were both in a position to use ancient mythic material to suit their own ends."
- "For scribal-priestly groups, this reuse of mythic material assumed a monotheistic cast."
- The chapter's final sentence sets up Ch10: "The last chapter is reserved for the portion of the Bible that contains the most and the boldest monotheistic statements and presentation of deity in order to convince its audience of Judean victory in the era of foreign empires; that portion is Isaiah 40–55, also called 'Second Isaiah.'"

**Confidence:** HIGH on the overall thesis. The evidence across three corpora supports the claim that monotheism was achieved through transformation, not invention. The social-location framework is productive and well-illustrated.

**What's at stake:** This is the chapter's master argument. If biblical monotheism IS transformed Canaanite myth, then the traditional theological claim of Israel's radical uniqueness collapses. Israel didn't reject myth — it rewrote it with a single protagonist. This is a profound challenge to both confessional and older critical narratives of Israelite religion.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israel's rejection of myth (Kaufmann's "revolution," Albright's "Mosaic monotheism"). Those who see the three corpora as too diverse to support a unified argument.

**Alternative reading:** The transformation thesis could be overstated. Some of what Smith calls "transformation" might be independent development — Israelite scribes could have arrived at similar concepts without deliberately reworking Canaanite material. The genetic relationship between, say, Asherah and Woman Wisdom is plausible but not proven.

**My assessment:** This is the strongest synthesis in the chapter and one of the best in the whole book. The three-case-study structure is elegant, and each case illuminates a different social location and strategy. The weakness is that the three case studies receive very unequal treatment — Genesis 1 gets ~4 pages, Wisdom gets ~1 page, Daniel 7 gets barely a page — and the Wisdom and Daniel 7 sections feel rushed. Smith's heart is clearly in the Priestly material, and the other two corpora feel like afterthoughts. But the overall framework is compelling.

## Ch9 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Genesis 1's "monotheistic poetics" | HIGH | Whether depersonalization is deliberate or generic |
| 2: Priestly transformation of royal theology | HIGH | Dating of P relative to exile |
| 3: Genesis 1:26 inverts Ezekiel 1:26 | MEDIUM-HIGH | Independent use of shared priestly vocabulary |
| 4: Woman Wisdom replaces Asherah | MEDIUM | Speculative wordplay; Levitical link underdeveloped |
| 5: Daniel 7 removes human king from conflict | MEDIUM-HIGH | Son of man as individual angel vs. community |
| 6: Two-stage reduction of myth | MEDIUM-HIGH | Dating of texts; circularity of censorship model |
| 7: Wisdom/Jerusalem as goddess replacements | LOW-MEDIUM | No direct textual evidence; functional analogy only |
| 8: Monotheism as transformed myth | HIGH | Genetic vs. analogical relationship to Canaanite sources |

**Strongest section:** The Genesis 1 analysis (Claims 1–3). Smith gives this nearly half the chapter, and it shows. The "monotheistic poetics" concept, the three priestly themes embedded in creation, and the inversion of Ezekiel 1:26 are all well-argued and textually grounded. The cosmos-as-sanctuary reading, while speculative, is supported by Psalm citations and Levenson's homology thesis.

**Weakest section:** The Wisdom and Daniel 7 case studies (Claims 4–5). Both are drastically underdeveloped — barely a page each — and read like outlines rather than fully argued sections. The Woman Wisdom/Asherah connection is more asserted than demonstrated. Daniel 7 gets the shortest treatment despite being arguably the most complex of the three corpora. Smith needed to double the length of both sections.

**The methodological problem:** Smith's social-location framework (priests, wisdom teachers, apocalyptic visionaries) is productive, but he doesn't demonstrate that the theological strategies correlate with the social locations as tightly as he claims. We know priests produced Genesis 1 — but did "wisdom teachers" produce Proverbs 1–9? Did "apocalyptic visionaries" produce Daniel 7? The social location is inferred from the content, which risks circularity.

**Tension with previous chapters:** Smith's argument here about priestly censorship of myth (Claim 6) directly echoes Ch5's argument about holiness norms censoring divine sex and death. But Ch6 argued that Israel probably never HAD a Death-confrontation mythology — it was a local Ugaritic literary production. The tension remains unresolved: is the absence of myth in the Bible due to active priestly censorship, or because the myths were never there to begin with? Smith seems to want both answers depending on the chapter.

**Cross-reference:** Cross's work on the divine council in Daniel 7 would substantially strengthen Smith's apocalyptic section. Smith mentions it only in passing but Cross's detailed analysis of the throne scene and the "son of man" as the angelic host would give the Daniel 7 case study the depth it lacks. Similarly, Römer's work on Wisdom and monotheism (cited in the task brief) isn't engaged at all — Smith's treatment predates much of that scholarship, but the note should flag the connection.

**Overall value:** Despite the uneven treatment of the three corpora, this chapter makes an essential contribution to Smith's argument. It demonstrates concretely HOW different segments of Judean society reconfigured inherited polytheistic material into monotheistic frameworks. The transformation thesis — monotheism as rewritten myth, not rejected myth — is the chapter's lasting contribution and one of the book's central arguments.
