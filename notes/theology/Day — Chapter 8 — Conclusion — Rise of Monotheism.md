# Day — Chapter 8: Conclusion: The Canaanite Gods and Goddesses and the Rise of Monotheism

Day's concluding chapter synthesizes his findings into a coherent account of how monotheism emerged. His position is the scholarly mainstream: absolute monotheism was first articulated by Deutero-Isaiah in the exile, became normative in the post-exilic period, and was preceded by a minority "Yahweh-alone" monolatrous movement. He critiques both Tigay (who overestimates pre-exilic monolatry from onomastics) and Hayman (who claims monotheism wasn't achieved until the Middle Ages). He defends the historicity of Josiah's reform and argues the purged cults were Canaanite, not Assyrian. The chapter ends with a meditation on the "afterglow" of Canaanite deities in Jewish apocalyptic and demonology.

---

## Claim 1: Tigay's onomastic argument for widespread pre-exilic monolatry is methodologically flawed

**Author's claim:** Tigay's conclusion that only a small proportion of Israelites worshipped other deities — based on the 16:1 ratio of Yahwistic to pagan theophoric names in inscriptions — "need not imply that Yahweh was the only deity worshipped but is equally compatible with the idea that Yahweh was rather the most important deity worshipped" (p. 228). The net effect of several caveats "is to suggest that the worship of other gods and goddesses was more frequent than he allows" (p. 228).

**Evidence presented (four caveats):**
1. **Chief god, not only god:** A 16:1 ratio is "equally compatible with the idea that Yahweh was rather the most important deity worshipped. Those who worshipped other gods and goddesses surely still saw Yahweh as the chief god, with the other deities being regarded as subordinate members of his pantheon" (p. 228)
2. **Goddesses invisible in onomastics:** "The names of female deities hardly ever occur in Ugaritic personal names, even though we know that Asherah, Anat and Astarte were prominent goddesses at Ugarit" (p. 228). If this is true of Ugarit — where goddesses were undeniably important — then the absence of goddess names in Hebrew onomastics tells us nothing about goddess worship. Meanwhile, Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom, and thousands of pillar figurines all attest Asherah worship in the very period and region from which Tigay's epigraphic material comes
3. **Geographical and temporal limitations:** Most epigraphic names come from Judah, not the Northern Kingdom (where Samaria ostraca show Baal theophoric names). Most material is from the late monarchy — earlier centuries might show different patterns
4. **Graham Davies' review:** Tigay overestimates Yahwistic names and underestimates pagan names — the ratio may be "nearer ten to one rather than sixteen to one" (p. 229)

**Day's overall conclusion:** "Yahweh was very much the chief god in ancient Israel, and the other gods and goddesses would have been worshipped as part of his pantheon, but the frequency of their worship has been underestimated by Tigay" (p. 229)

**Confidence:** HIGH. Day's Ugaritic counter-example is devastating — if goddesses are invisible in Ugaritic onomastics despite being major deities, the argument from absence in Hebrew names collapses. The pillar figurine evidence (contemporary with Tigay's inscriptions) is an independent archaeological contradiction.

**What's at stake:** Tigay's study is the strongest empirical challenge to the consensus model of widespread pre-exilic polytheism. If Tigay is right, the OT's picture of rampant apostasy IS rhetorical exaggeration and monotheism was already the popular norm centuries before Deutero-Isaiah. Day's rebuttal preserves the consensus while acknowledging Tigay's genuine contribution.

**Who disagrees:** J.H. Tigay (*You Shall Have No Other Gods*, 1986). Mark Smith and others who accept Tigay's data more readily.

**My assessment:** Day's Ugaritic counterexample is so elegant it almost feels unfair to Tigay. The point about goddess names being absent at Ugarit — where we KNOW goddesses were worshipped from mythological texts — demonstrates that onomastics simply cannot measure goddess devotion. This is a fundamental methodological limitation of Tigay's approach that Day exploits effectively.

## Claim 2: Absolute monotheism emerged with Deutero-Isaiah in the exile, became normative in the post-exilic period, and was preceded by a minority monolatrous "Yahweh-alone" movement — NOT at the time of Moses (Albright) or the Middle Ages (Hayman)

**Author's claim:** "It is clear that there was indeed a monolatrous party already in the pre-exilic period, though it was not as dominant as Tigay supposes, and absolute monotheism was first given explicit expression by the prophet Deutero-Isaiah in the exile and became fully operative in the post-exilic period" (p. 229). The Albright/Mosaic monotheism view has been "generally rejected in recent decades" (p. 229). Hayman's medieval monotheism claim is "an extreme position" (p. 229).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Deutero-Isaiah as first explicit monotheist:** The tendency "goes with a general understanding, already maintained a century ago by J. Wellhausen, that the achievement of monotheism was a gradual process" (p. 229-230)
2. **Pre-exilic monolatry had deep roots:** The "Yahweh alone movement" (Morton Smith, Bernhard Lang) — "some scholars are willing to grant that this minority monolatrous movement may indeed go all the way back to earliest times, perhaps to Moses" (p. 230). Monolatry (worship of Yahweh alone without denying other gods' existence) preceded monotheism (denial that other gods exist)
3. **Against Hayman:** Hayman's case "hangs on the prominent position given to angels in Second Temple Judaism" — but "throughout history monotheists have not felt belief in angels to be incompatible with monotheism" (p. 229). The category error is obvious: angels are subordinate created beings in monotheistic theology, not rival deities

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the mainstream scholarly position and Day articulates it clearly. The distinction between monolatry (pre-exilic) and monotheism (exilic/post-exilic) is crucial and widely accepted.

**What's at stake:** The timing of monotheism determines whether it was always Israel's religion (conservative/confessional), a late Persian-era development (minimalist), or an exilic breakthrough (mainstream critical). Day's position occupies the scholarly center.

**Who disagrees:** Albright — Mosaic monotheism (early). Hayman — medieval monotheism (late). Minimalists — Persian/Hellenistic invention (later than Day).

**My assessment:** Day's central position is well-defended by the evidence throughout the book. The seventh-century prophets (Zephaniah, Jeremiah, the Deuteronomists) are clearly MONOLATROUS (Yahweh-alone) but not yet MONOTHEISTIC (denying other gods' existence). Deutero-Isaiah (Isa 40-55) is the first text to say "I am the LORD, there is no other" without qualification. The Albright position requires reading this back into earlier texts; the Hayman position requires ignoring it.

## Claim 3: Josiah's reform was historical, targeted Canaanite cults (not Assyrian), and was a pivotal moment in the enforcement of monolatry

**Author's claim:** "There are good grounds for believing that a religious reformation did take place in his reign" (p. 231). The purged cults were "Canaanite rather than Assyrian" (p. 232).

**Evidence presented (historicity of the reform):**
1. **Zephaniah vs. Jeremiah contrast:** Zephaniah (early Josiah) condemns syncretism of the type 2 Kings 23 describes pre-reform (Zeph 1:4-5). Jeremiah (looking back) hails Josiah as a just king (Jer 22:15-16) — which he couldn't do if Josiah tolerated syncretism. "The contrast between the words of Zephaniah and Jeremiah implies that some religious reformation must have taken place" (p. 231)
2. **Shortage of Josiah-era oracles in Jeremiah:** Apart from early chapters 2-6, there are few Josiah-era oracles — "which makes sense if Josiah had undertaken a religious reform of which Jeremiah approved" (p. 231)
3. **Cross's double redaction theory:** A first Deuteronomistic edition during Josiah's reign, with final redaction in exile. The tensions in the text (Huldah predicts peaceful death, Josiah dies in battle; "no king like him" followed immediately by blaming Manasseh for the exile) only make sense if a Josianic-era writer composed the initial account before knowing how things turned out. "No one inventing the whole story in the exile would have created such tensions" (p. 231)

**Evidence presented (Canaanite, not Assyrian cults):**
1. Josiah removed Baal, Asherah, and Molech — all well-known Canaanite deities. "Human sacrifice, associated with the god Molech (2 Kgs 23:10), is not a native Assyrian custom but is something well attested in the Canaanite religious sphere" (p. 233)
2. "Since the Canaanites also worshipped the sun and other astral deities, there is every presumption that the removal of these too (2 Kgs 23:4, 5, 11) was a response to Canaanite cults" (p. 233)
3. The moon is called *yārēaḥ* (West Semitic), not Sin (Mesopotamian) — if Assyrian, we'd expect the Mesopotamian name
4. Against Spieckermann (Asherah = code for Ishtar, Assyrian): "The Canaanite equivalent of Ishtar was not Asherah but Astarte (Ashtoreth)" (p. 233). Spieckermann wrote before Kuntillet Ajrud/Khirbet el-Qom became widely known — texts that "reveal the importance of Asherah in pre-exilic Israel"

**Confidence:** HIGH for historicity of the reform; HIGH for Canaanite identification of purged cults.

**What's at stake:** If Josiah's reform was historical, it represents the moment when monolatry was enforced from the top — when the "Yahweh-alone party" gained political power and eliminated rival cults. This is the political mechanism behind the theological development.

**Who disagrees:** Würthwein, Levin — reform not historical. Spieckermann — Assyrian cults purged, not Canaanite. Cross/Freedman — reform tied to Assyrian imperial decline (Day: Assyrian dates uncertain, and cults were Canaanite anyway).

**My assessment:** The Huldah prophecy tension (predicted peaceful death / died in battle) is the kind of internal tension that's genuinely hard to explain as fiction — why would a fabricator create a failed prophecy for a hero king? Day's point that the Chronicler (writing three centuries later) reorganized the reform chronology for theological reasons is characteristically sharp.

## Claim 4: Canaanite deities left an "afterglow" in Jewish monotheism — demoted to angels, demons, and apocalyptic imagery

**Author's claim:** Even after absolute monotheism was established, Canaanite deities "sometimes left a kind of 'afterglow'" — most marked in apocalyptic literature (p. 233).

**Evidence presented:**
1. **Seventy sons of God → seventy guardian angels:** Originally gods of El's pantheon, demoted to angel status in 1 Enoch and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (p. 233)
2. **Baal-zebul → Beelzebul/Beelzebub → Satan:** The Prince Baal became the Prince of Demons in the Gospels (p. 233)
3. **El and Baal → Ancient of Days and Son of Man:** Daniel 7 preserves the relationship of aged El and cloud-riding Baal in apocalyptic form (p. 233-234)
4. **Leviathan → seven-headed dragon of Revelation:** The chaos monster survives as Satan (Rev 12) and Rome (Rev 13) (p. 234)
5. **Baal's resurrection → resurrection of the dead:** The ultimate origin of Jewish/Christian resurrection belief (p. 234)

**Day's final summary:** "Although these images lived on, everything became transformed in the light of monotheism, and it is arguably monotheism (at first monolatry), rather than God's mighty acts in history as used to be argued, that most distinguishes the Old Testament from the religions of the other nations of the ancient Near East" (p. 234)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This "afterglow" thesis is the logical culmination of all seven preceding chapters. Each case is individually argued in its respective chapter.

**What's at stake:** Monotheism was not a clean break from polytheism but a transformation of polytheistic material. The Canaanite gods didn't disappear — they were demoted, demonized, or absorbed. The OT's distinctiveness is not its mythological content but its theological framework.

**My assessment:** Day's final sentence is his most important theological claim. He concedes that the mythological CONTENT is Canaanite while arguing that the monotheistic FRAMEWORK is Israel's distinctive contribution. This is a more sophisticated position than either "Israel was just Canaanite" or "Israel was wholly unique" — it acknowledges genuine continuity AND genuine innovation.

| Monotheism = Deutero-Isaiah (exilic), not Mosaic or medieval | HIGH | Scholarly consensus; Hayman's angel argument is a category error |

| Canaanite deities' "afterglow" in apocalyptic | HIGH | Each case argued individually in preceding chapters |

**Strongest section:** The rebuttal of Tigay. Four distinct caveats, each methodologically sound, and the Ugaritic goddess-name point is the kind of elegant comparative argument that makes Day's methodology shine.

**Weakest section:** The engagement with Hayman is too brief — Day dismisses him in a paragraph. But this reflects the weakness of Hayman's position more than any deficiency in Day's response.

**Most important contribution of the chapter (and the book):** Day's framework of "afterglow." The Canaanite deities didn't vanish — they were transformed into angels (sons of El), demons (Beelzebul, Resheph), apocalyptic imagery (Daniel 7, Revelation 12-13), and theological concepts (resurrection). Monotheism was a REORGANIZATION of the pantheon, not its abolition. The gods became subordinates; the demons became enemies; the myths became metaphors. But they never fully disappeared.
