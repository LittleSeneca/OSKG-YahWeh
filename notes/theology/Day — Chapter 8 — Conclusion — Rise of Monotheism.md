# Day — Chapter 8: Conclusion: The Canaanite Gods and Goddesses and the Rise of Monotheism

Day's concluding chapter synthesizes his findings into a coherent account of how monotheism emerged. His position is the scholarly mainstream: absolute monotheism was first articulated by Deutero-Isaiah in the exile, became normative in the post-exilic period, and was preceded by a minority "Yahweh-alone" monolatrous movement. He critiques both Tigay (who overestimates pre-exilic monolatry from onomastics) and Hayman (who claims monotheism wasn't achieved until the Middle Ages). He defends the historicity of Josiah's reform and argues the purged cults were Canaanite, not Assyrian. The chapter ends with a meditation on the "afterglow" of Canaanite deities in Jewish apocalyptic and demonology.

## Claim 1: Tigay's onomastic argument for widespread pre-exilic monolatry is methodologically flawed
→ [[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]] | **day-ygc-8.1** | Confidence: high
  Day presents four caveats against Tigay: (1) a 16:1 Yahwistic ratio is equally compatible with Yahweh-as-chief-god polytheism, (2) goddess names are invisible in Ugaritic onomastics despite major goddess worship — the same absent-goddess pattern applies to Israel, (3) geographical/temporal limits in the epigraphic sample, (4) Graham Davies recalculates the ratio closer to 10:1. The Ugaritic counter-example is the knockout: if onomastics cannot detect goddesses at Ugarit, it cannot detect them in Israel.
  Supports: [[claim-monotheism-as-exilic-rhetoric-not-religious-revolution]], [[claim-biblical-pure-yahwism-deuteronomistic-propaganda]]

## Claim 2: Absolute monotheism was first articulated by Deutero-Isaiah in the exile, preceded by a minority monolatrous movement
→ [[claim-absolute-monotheism-deutero-isaiah-exile-monolatry-preceded]] | **day-ygc-8.2** | Confidence: high
  The scholarly mainstream position: monolatry (Yahweh-alone worship without denying other gods) preceded monotheism (denial that other gods exist). Deutero-Isaiah (Isa 40-55) is the first explicit monotheist. Positions at both extremes are rejected: Albright's Mosaic monotheism (too early) and Hayman's medieval monotheism (too late).

## Claim 3: Josiah's reform was historical, targeted Canaanite cults (not Assyrian), and was a pivotal moment in the enforcement of monolatry
→ [[claim-josiah-reform-historical-canaanite-cults-not-assyrian]] | **day-ygc-8.3** | Confidence: high
  Three lines of evidence for historicity: Zephaniah-vs-Jeremiah contrast (before/after the reform), shortage of Josiah-era oracles in Jeremiah, and Cross's double redaction theory (Huldah's failed prophecy is inexplicable as fiction). The purged cults were Canaanite: Baal, Asherah, Molech, sun/astral deities use West Semitic names (not Mesopotamian), and Spieckermann's Assyrian thesis is undermined by Kuntillet Ajrud evidence.
  Supports: [[claim-deuteronomy-written-josiah-reform]], [[claim-josiah-reform-invented-exclusive-yahwism]]
  Contradicts: [[claim-josiah-reforms-endured-no-pagan-revival]]

## Claim 4: Canaanite deities left an "afterglow" in Jewish monotheism — demoted to angels, demons, and apocalyptic imagery
→ [[claim-canaanite-deities-afterglow-jewish-monotheism-angels-demons]] | **day-ygc-8.4** | Confidence: high
  Five examples of Canaanite survivals: (1) seventy sons of El → seventy guardian angels, (2) Baal-zebul → Beelzebul/Satan, (3) El and Baal → Ancient of Days and Son of Man in Daniel 7, (4) Leviathan → seven-headed dragon of Revelation, (5) Baal's resurrection → resurrection of the dead. Monotheism was a reorganization of the pantheon, not its abolition — the gods became subordinates, demons, enemies, and metaphors.

## Chapter 8 Overall Assessment

**Strongest section:** The rebuttal of Tigay. Four distinct caveats, each methodologically sound, and the Ugaritic goddess-name point is the kind of elegant comparative argument that makes Day's methodology shine.

**Weakest section:** The engagement with Hayman is too brief — Day dismisses him in a paragraph. But this reflects the weakness of Hayman's position more than any deficiency in Day's response.

**Most important contribution of the chapter (and the book):** Day's framework of "afterglow." The Canaanite deities didn't vanish — they were transformed into angels (sons of El), demons (Beelzebul, Resheph), apocalyptic imagery (Daniel 7, Revelation 12-13), and theological concepts (resurrection). Monotheism was a REORGANIZATION of the pantheon, not its abolition. The gods became subordinates; the demons became enemies; the myths became metaphors. But they never fully disappeared.
