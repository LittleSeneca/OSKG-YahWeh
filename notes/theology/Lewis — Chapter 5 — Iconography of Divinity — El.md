# Lewis — Chapter 5: The Iconography of Divinity — El

Lewis surveys ancient Near Eastern divine image practices (materials, artisans, rituals of quickening, procession, destruction) and then examines the iconographic evidence for El/ʾIlu in Ugarit and Israel. The central finding: Iron Age Israel is exceptional in the ancient Near East for its *lack* of male divine images. While Ugarit has clear enthroned ʾIlu figurines and bull imagery, Israelite El's iconography is almost entirely aniconic — attested through standing stones (_masseboth_) rather than anthropomorphic or theriomorphic statues. The famous bronze figurines from Israel are Late Bronze Age heirlooms, not Iron Age productions.

---

## Claim 1: Iron Age Israel is iconographically exceptional — there are virtually no provenanced Iron Age male divine images, and this must be explained by the aniconic tradition

**Lewis's claim:** "In contrast, Iron Age Israel, with its lack of physical representations of male divinity, is exceptional. ... In contrast to the absence of concrete representations, literary texts freely employ anthropomorphic language for El and Yahweh." He quotes Uehlinger: "On the whole, the notion of 'Ancient Israel' as an 'aniconic nation' is erroneous... The prohibition [of cultic images] presupposes the knowledge and practice of iconolatry in at least some circles of Judahite society."

**Evidence:** All the bronze figurines discussed (Hazor, Beth-Shemesh, Megiddo, Beth-Shean, Tel Kinneret, Nablus/"Shechem") are Late Bronze Age types. Hendel (1997): these bronzes "are Late Bronze Age types that represent... 'the end of the Canaanite tradition in the region to be occupied by the kingdoms of Judah and Israel.' These types were no longer produced after the eleventh and tenth centuries BCE." Dever's famous 1983 statement: "no representations of a male deity in terra cotta, metal, or stone have ever been found in clear Iron Age contexts." Even the Gezer stick figure, often cited as the exception, comes from destruction debris with intrusive Persian material — its tenth-century date is questionable. The Hazor bronze Ahlström called "Yahweh" is from a LB hoard.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the absence of Iron Age male divine figurines. HIGH that this reflects aniconism. The fact that female figurines (Judean pillar figurines) ARE abundant in Iron Age contexts makes the absence of male ones all the more striking.

**What's at stake:** The aniconic tradition is often treated as a late theological development (Deuteronomic, prophetic). The archaeological evidence suggests it was operational much earlier — affecting actual cultic practice, not just elite theology. This complicates the standard narrative that aniconism emerged FROM monotheism.

**Who disagrees:** Niehr and others argue for a cult statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple. Mettinger argues that masseboth ARE divine images, so Israel wasn't aniconic in a strict sense. Uehlinger says the prohibition proves iconolatry existed — you don't ban what nobody does.

**Alternative reading:** The absence could be due to preservation bias — wood and clay images may not survive. The biblical image-ban texts describe precisely the kinds of images we DON'T find (gold, silver, wood), suggesting they existed but were destroyed or didn't preserve.

**My assessment:** Lewis's treatment of this is excellent. He doesn't claim absolute aniconism — he documents the masseboth as possible exceptions. But the contrast with Ugarit (where multiple clear ʾIlu images exist) and with female figurines (abundant in Iron Age Israel) is telling. Something was different about how Israel represented its male deity.

## Claim 2: The Ugaritic enthroned ʾIlu figurines and bull imagery provide a clear iconographic template — but this template was NOT continued in Israel

**Lewis's claim:** Four Ugaritic artifacts (Figs. 5.16, 5.18, 5.19, 5.20) "seem to represent the deity ʾIlu (a consensus among scholars) because their physical representations coincide with the description of ʾIlu we find in Ugaritic literature (bearded, aged, bull [= horned], benevolent, enthroned with footstool, etc.)." The bronze bull from the South City (Fig. 5.32), found alongside the ʾIlu and Baal figurines, is the best candidate for a theriomorphic ʾIlu image.

**Evidence:** The typological distinction: striding smiting figures = warrior/storm gods (Baal); enthroned benedictory figures = El. ʾIlu's textual epithets ("Bull ʾIlu," "father of years," "Gracious One") align with the iconography. One figurine (5.16) was found in a residential area — not a temple — underscoring family religion. The bull image (5.32) has "strongly emphasized sexual organs" matching ʾIlu's ribald texts (KTU 1.23, 1.4.4.38-39). Cornelius: "the title 'bull' is the prerogative of ʾIlu."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Ugaritic identification. HIGH that this template wasn't continued in Israel.

**What's at stake:** If Israel knew about ʾIlu/El iconography from Ugaritic/Canaanite predecessors but chose NOT to replicate it, the aniconic tradition is a deliberate rejection, not ignorance. This supports Uehlinger's point: the prohibition exists because the practice existed.

**Who disagrees:** Ornan identifies the large Hazor bronze as Baal, not El, based on the "tree and horned animals" crown motif. Lewis argues El is equally associated with agricultural bounty (KTU 1.23).

**My assessment:** The Ugaritic ʾIlu imagery is well-established and non-controversial. Lewis's inference that Israel deliberately departed from this template is plausible but inferential — we can't prove intent from absence.

## Claim 3: Masseboth (standing stones) are the most likely physical representations of El in Iron Age Israel — and the Arad single stela is the clearest example of a massebah functioning as a divine symbol in a temple niche

**Lewis's claim:** "The etymological origin of cultic stones known as betyls or baetyls (from bēt-ʾel, 'the house of God/El') predisposes us to look for ways in which sacred stones were envisioned as physical representations of the deity El." The Arad stela "occupied the place where the deity resided and was manifest" (Bloch-Smith). Masseboth are associated with El in biblical tradition at Bethel (Gen 28:18, 22; 31:13; 35:14) and at Shechem (El Berith temple).

**Evidence:** Six sites with masseboth: Shechem (huge 6.5 ft stela next to Temple 1, likely El Berith temple), Bull Site (large worked stone with bronze bull), Hazor Area B (curved basalt stone as focal point, with bronze figurine in buried jar), Hazor Area A and M (multiple installations, some LB heirlooms), Arad (single red-painted limestone stela in temple niche), Tel Dan (multiple installations in gate complex), Khirbet Ataruz (1.5m stone in cultic niche with bull-decorated jar). Biblical texts associate masseboth with El (Gen 28, 31, 35), Baal (2 Kgs 3:2; 10:26-27), and Yahweh (Isa 19:19-20; Hos 3:4; 10:1-2).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH that some masseboth represented El. MEDIUM on specific site identifications. The evidence is circumstantial — no inscription accompanies any massebah identifying the deity. The Arad stela is the clearest case of a massebah as divine symbol (it's in the holy of holies niche), but whether it represented Yahweh, El, or Yahweh-El is unknown.

**What's at stake:** If masseboth represent El worship, then El devotion persisted into the Iron Age in material form — not just in textual traditions. The small scale of masseboth sites (Bull Site, "ruin cults" at Hazor) aligns with El's profile as a family/clan deity rather than a national one.

**Who disagrees:** Mettinger overstates the case: "cultic masseboth must be regarded as aniconic representations of the divine." Lewis pushes back: standing stones had multiple functions (tombstones, boundary markers, commemorative markers, tribal markers, surrogate heirs) — not all are divine symbols. Even cultic masseboth may mark a theophanic encounter rather than the deity's ongoing presence.

**Alternative reading:** The masseboth could represent ancestor cults (Korpel), or they could be generic cult foci not tied to a specific deity. The word "betyl" connects to El etymologically but may have become a generic term.

**My assessment:** Lewis's treatment is admirably cautious. He resists the temptation to claim every massebah as El. The cumulative picture — El's biblical association with stones, the etymological connection (betyl = bêt-ʾel), the small-scale clan-oriented cult sites — is suggestive but not probative. This is iconographic evidence at its limit: we can see that SOMETHING was worshipped at these sites, but we can't see WHO.

## Claim 4: Jeroboam I's bull images at Bethel and Dan were likely El images — part of a strategy to "out-archaize" David by appealing to venerable El traditions

**Lewis's claim:** "Jeroboam I's cult was devoted to the deity El as he wooed the people away from the Jerusalemite worship of Yahweh to his version of a venerated El worship, especially at Bethel, an old El sanctuary. ... With this strategy Jeroboam I was attempting to 'out-archaize' David, who was so skillful in his use of the Ark of the Covenant, a venerated symbol of divine presence from the days of the tribal league (Cross 1973: 74)."

**Evidence:** Bethel and Dan were old cult sites. Jeroboam rebuilt Shechem and Penuel (1 Kgs 12:25), both associated with El worship. The Deuteronomist's polemic equates Jeroboam's bulls with Aaron's golden calf — which is explicitly called ʾĕlōhîm ("gods"/"God"). The word _ʿēgel_ ("calf/bull") may be a pejorative Deuteronomistic substitution for _šôr_ ("bull"). Had Jeroboam introduced Baal worship, "tradition should have preserved that fact, in vivid invective." Knoppers: "Like the authors of Exodus 32, the Deuteronomist implicitly concedes the antiquity and appeal of Jeroboam's cultus."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Lewis himself admits "all interpreters are forced into a good deal of speculation." The Deuteronomist's polemical language is our only source, and it's designed to discredit.

**What's at stake:** Whether northern Israelite religion was fundamentally El-oriented rather than Yahweh-oriented. If Jeroboam's bulls were El images, northern Yahwism may have been much thinner than the Bible suggests — the northern kingdom may have primarily worshipped El, with Yahweh as a secondary or merged figure.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the bulls as Yahweh images (the majority view), or as Baal images, or as pedestals for an invisible deity rather than divine images themselves. The Exodus 32 connection could argue for Yahweh — the golden calf is made in response to Moses' delay and is introduced with "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up from Egypt."

**Alternative reading:** Jeroboam may have been syncretizing — using bull imagery (an El symbol) but directing worship to Yahweh. The "these are your gods" language in Exodus 32 may be polemical exaggeration — Jeroboam may have intended the bulls as pedestals, not as images of the deity himself.

**My assessment:** Lewis leans toward the El interpretation but is appropriately tentative. This is one of those questions where the data won't settle the debate. What's valuable is Lewis's contextualization: Jeroboam's actions make more sense as an appeal to archaic northern El traditions than as a Yahwistic innovation. The "out-archaize" framing (from Cross) is elegant.

## Claim 5: The Beth Shean cylinder seal does NOT provide "independent confirmation" of El and his asherah — Keel and Uehlinger overread the evidence

**Lewis's claim:** "The difficulties in interpreting the evidence from this cylinder seal provide enough reasonable doubt that such a certain verdict cannot be rendered." He objects that the "servant with fan or brush" motif "never appears in front of a god in contemporary glyptic art" but does appear before kings — suggesting the seated figure is human, not divine. The "armed man" behind the figure is unexplained. The asherah identification relies on a "conflation" of separate motifs.

**Evidence:** Lewis notes that Keel and Uehlinger themselves admit the servant-with-fan appears in Neo-Assyrian scenes before "a standing or seated king" — making their conclusion that the scene "portrays a local deity" inconsistent. He adds: "while ʾIlu and ʾAthiratu are attested as a pair at Late Bronze Age Ugarit, our Iron Age Judean sources (i.e., epigraphic material and the biblical tradition) suggest the pairing of Baal and/or Yahweh with the asherah, not El."

**Confidence:** HIGH that Keel and Uehlinger overread this seal. MEDIUM on what it actually depicts.

**What's at stake:** Whether we have ANY clear Iron Age iconographic evidence pairing El with Asherah. If the Beth Shean seal doesn't show this, then the Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions ("Yahweh and his asherah") remain our only epigraphic evidence for divine pairing — and that's Yahweh/Asherah, not El/Asherah.

**Who disagrees:** Keel and Uehlinger (1998) explicitly state the seal provides "completely independent confirmation" of the "Yahweh... and his asherah" interpretation. They use it as evidence that El-aspects "advance into the foreground of the Palestinian religious symbol system during Iron Age IIC."

**My assessment:** Lewis's critique is convincing. Keel and Uehlinger are the leading authorities, but Lewis catches them in a contradiction: the iconographic comparanda they cite point toward a KING, not a god. This is exactly the kind of careful iconographic critique Lewis's methodology chapter promised.

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Iron Age Israel lacks male divine images — exceptional | VERY HIGH | Preservation bias; wood/clay images don't survive |
| Ugaritic ʾIlu iconography clear; not continued in Israel | HIGH | Israel may have had wooden images that didn't preserve |
| Masseboth as likely El representations | MEDIUM-HIGH | No inscriptional identification; multiple functions |
| Jeroboam's bulls = El images | MEDIUM | Purely textual/polemical source; speculation |
| Beth Shean seal doesn't show El + asherah | HIGH | Keel/Uehlinger are leading authorities |

**Strongest sections:** The masseboth survey (Shechem through Khirbet Ataruz) is a tour de force of archaeological synthesis. The critique of Keel and Uehlinger's Beth Shean seal interpretation is methodologically sharp. The distinction between LB heirlooms and actual Iron Age productions is an important corrective that many scholars miss.

**Weakest section:** The ancient Near Eastern divine image survey (Section II) is comprehensive but reads more like a catalogue than an argument. It's valuable as reference material but doesn't advance many evaluable claims.

**What this chapter establishes for the rest of the book:**
- Israel was genuinely aniconic with respect to male deity images — this isn't just biblical rhetoric
- El was worshipped through masseboth, not statues — aligning with his profile as a clan/family deity with small-scale cult
- The aniconic tradition complicates the study of Israelite religion: texts freely use anthropomorphic language while material culture refuses it
- The Ugaritic template (enthroned, bearded, horned, benedictory El) was known but not replicated in Iron Age Israel

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the iconographic companion to Ch. 4 (textual El worship). Dever covers similar archaeological ground but with more focus on female figurines and popular religion. Stavrakopoulou's bodily approach (feet, genitals, torso) is the inverse of Lewis's approach — she argues for radical anthropomorphism in texts where Lewis documents aniconism in artifacts. The tension between these two approaches is productive: Israelites wrote about God's body but refused to depict it.
