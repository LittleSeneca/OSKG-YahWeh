# Lewis — Chapter 5: The Iconography of Divinity — El

Lewis surveys ancient Near Eastern divine image practices (materials, artisans, rituals of quickening, procession, destruction) and then examines the iconographic evidence for El/ʾIlu in Ugarit and Israel. The central finding: Iron Age Israel is exceptional in the ancient Near East for its *lack* of male divine images. While Ugarit has clear enthroned ʾIlu figurines and bull imagery, Israelite El's iconography is almost entirely aniconic — attested through standing stones (*masseboth*) rather than anthropomorphic or theriomorphic statues. The famous bronze figurines from Israel are Late Bronze Age heirlooms, not Iron Age productions.

## Claim 1: Iron Age Israel lacks male divine images — aniconic exceptionalism
→ [[claim-iron-age-israel-lacks-male-divine-images-aniconic]] | **lewis-ocg-5.1** | Confidence: very-high
  Virtually no provenanced Iron Age male divine images exist. All bronze figurines from Israel are Late Bronze Age types, not produced after 11th-10th C BCE. Female figurines ARE abundant in Iron Age contexts, making the male absence striking.
  Depends on: [[claim-epigraphy-rewritten-history-israelite-religion]]. ## Claim 2: Ugaritic ʾIlu iconography is clear — but NOT continued in Israel
→ [[claim-ugaritic-ilu-iconography-template-not-continued-israel]] | **lewis-ocg-5.2** | Confidence: high
  Enthroned benedictory figurines (bearded, aged, horned, footstool) match textual ʾIlu epithets. Bull imagery with emphasized sexual organs aligns with ribald texts. This template was known but deliberately not replicated.
  Depends on: [[claim-ugaritic-ilu-fullest-el-portrait-not-deposed-baal]], [[claim-iron-age-israel-lacks-male-divine-images-aniconic]].

## Claim 3: Masseboth (standing stones) are likely El representations
→ [[claim-masseboth-standing-stones-el-representations-arad]] | **lewis-ocg-5.3** | Confidence: medium-high
  Etymology (betyl = bêt-ʾel, "house of God/El") connects sacred stones to El. Seven sites surveyed: Shechem, Bull Site, Hazor, Arad, Tel Dan, Khirbet Ataruz. Arad stela in holy of holies niche is clearest case. But no inscription identifies any massebah with El.
  Depends on: [[claim-israelite-el-family-clan-deity-not-national]], [[claim-iron-age-israel-lacks-male-divine-images-aniconic]]. Supports: [[claim-el-did-not-disappear-iron-age-onomastic-epigraphic]], [[claim-jeroboam-bulls-bethel-dan-el-images-out-archaize]].

## Claim 4: Jeroboam I's bulls at Bethel and Dan were likely El images
→ [[claim-jeroboam-bulls-bethel-dan-el-images-out-archaize]] | **lewis-ocg-5.4** | Confidence: medium
  Strategy to "out-archaize" David by appealing to venerable northern El traditions at old El sanctuaries. Deuteronomist's polemic equates bulls with golden calf (called ʾĕlōhîm). "All interpreters are forced into a good deal of speculation."
  Depends on: [[claim-el-was-original-god-israel-name-and-absence-yahweh]], [[claim-masseboth-standing-stones-el-representations-arad]]. Contradicts: [[claim-golden-calves-legitimate-yhwh-symbols]], [[claim-golden-calves-yhwh-worship-not-pagan-idolatry]].

## Claim 5: Beth Shean cylinder seal does NOT show El and his asherah
→ [[claim-beth-shean-cylinder-seal-not-el-asherah-keel-overread]] | **lewis-ocg-5.5** | Confidence: high
  Keel and Uehlinger overread the evidence. Servant-with-fan motif appears before kings, not gods in contemporary glyptic. "Armed man" unexplained. Iron Age sources pair asherah with Baal/Yahweh, not El.
  Depends on: [[claim-ugaritic-ilu-fullest-el-portrait-not-deposed-baal]], [[claim-iron-age-israel-lacks-male-divine-images-aniconic]]. Supports: [[claim-asherah-el-consort-transferred-to-yahweh]], [[claim-yhwh-consort-asherah-goddess-baal-link-deuteronomistic-synthesis]].

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

**Strongest sections:** The masseboth survey (Shechem through Khirbet Ataruz) is a tour de force of archaeological synthesis. The critique of Keel and Uehlinger's Beth Shean seal interpretation is methodologically sharp. The distinction between LB heirlooms and actual Iron Age productions is an important corrective that many scholars miss.

**Weakest section:** The ancient Near Eastern divine image survey (Section II) is comprehensive but reads more like a catalogue than an argument. It's valuable as reference material but doesn't advance many evaluable claims.

**What this chapter establishes for the rest of the book:**
- Israel was genuinely aniconic with respect to male deity images — this isn't just biblical rhetoric
- El was worshipped through masseboth, not statues — aligning with his profile as a clan/family deity with small-scale cult
- The aniconic tradition complicates the study of Israelite religion: texts freely use anthropomorphic language while material culture refuses it
- The Ugaritic template (enthroned, bearded, horned, benedictory El) was known but not replicated in Iron Age Israel

**Cross-references:** This chapter is the iconographic companion to Ch. 4 (textual El worship). Dever covers similar archaeological ground but with more focus on female figurines and popular religion. Stavrakopoulou's bodily approach (feet, genitals, torso) is the inverse of Lewis's approach — she argues for radical anthropomorphism in texts where Lewis documents aniconism in artifacts. The tension between these two approaches is productive: Israelites wrote about God's body but refused to depict it.
