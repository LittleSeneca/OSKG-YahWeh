# Fleming — Chapter 6: The People of Yahweh

This is Fleming's positive argument: the Bible preserves traces of a pre-Israelite identity called "the people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh). The Song of Deborah's battle account (Judg 5:12-23) knows an alliance of highland groups who fight together without any reference to "Israel" — their only collective name is 'am Yhwh. The name "Israel" is imposed only by the introductory hymn (vv. 2-11), a monarchic-era addition. Exodus 15:16 independently attests the same pattern: "your people, Yahweh" — a people defined solely by its god. These biblical "people of Yahweh" correspond structurally to the Egyptian Yhwʒ of Shasu-land: both are coalitions of kinship-based groups identified by the name Yhw(h). Fleming proposes that a Shasu people named Yhwʒ eventually came to understand their divine patron as sharing their name — the people-name became a god-name. This is "Yahweh before Israel" in its fullest sense.

---

## Claim 1: The 'am Yhwh in Judges 5:13 is the collective name of the battle alliance — it is NOT "Israel" and predates the Israel identification

**Author's claim:** "Deborah and Barak lead the 'am Yhwh, the only name offered the gathered whole in verses 12-23. It is only by uniting in alliance that a collective entity under this name can be said to fight" (p. 223). "All eight references to Israel in the Song of Deborah appear in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11) and thus reinterpret the battle by what Sara Milstein (2016) terms 'revision through introduction'" (Ch 1, p. 27, reaffirmed here).

**Evidence presented:**
- The hymn (Judg 5:2-11) uses "Israel" eight times and presents a unified collective acting as one body — in sharp contrast to the battle account's picture of independent groups deciding separately whether to fight (p. 220)
- The battle account (vv. 12-22/23) lists ten individual peoples — Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, Reuben, Gilead, Dan, Asher, Naphtali — each named separately, each making independent decisions about participation (vv. 14-18)
- The hymn presents Israel as a far-flung nation reaching from the Jezreel Valley north and east — a geography that suits the Omride kingdom of the 9th century, not any pre-monarchic reality (p. 220)
- The Mesha inscription (mid-9th c.) associates Israel's intrusion east of the Jordan with Omri "as if a new thing," while the "men of Gad" had been there "since forever" (p. 221)
- Gad is notably absent from the list of participants in Judg 5:14-18 — perhaps they were never part of this particular alliance (p. 221)
- The hymn's vision of Israel "suits — perhaps only — the northern kingdom of the 9th and 8th centuries" (p. 221)
- "The very effort to forge this identity, without undermining the tradition of decentralized decision-making, suggests that the finished Song invited what might still be recent incorporation of these peoples into a single Israel" (p. 221)
- "Under Yahweh as 'god of Israel,' the united Israel will gain the respect of 'kings' (v. 3)... an outside audience of individual nations like Israel itself" (p. 221)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The redaction-critical observation about Israel in the hymn vs. the battle account is strong and shared by multiple scholars (Milstein 2016, Smith 2014 acknowledges it even while disagreeing about 'am Yhwh). But the claim that 'am Yhwh specifically is the original collective name depends on the phrase being original to the battle account — and Smith (2014) considers it part of the secondary introduction.

**What's at stake:** This is Fleming's centerpiece. If 'am Yhwh is the pre-Israelite identity of the coalition, then Yahweh defined a people before Israel did. The implication is that the kingdom of Israel was built partly from Yahweh-people who preceded it.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (2014: 245-46) considers 'am Yhwh in both verses 11 and 13 to belong to the introductory revision, not the original battle account. Smith sees the original battle poem as not involving Yahweh at all in its earliest form.

**Alternative reading:** 'Am Yhwh is simply a poetic synonym for "Israel" — another way of referring to the same people. The alternation between "Israel" and "people of Yahweh" is stylistic variation, not evidence of different compositional layers.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is elegant and explains features of the text that are otherwise puzzling (why two different collective terms? why does the hymn's Israel look so different from the battle account's alliance?). But Smith's objection is serious: if 'am Yhwh is also secondary, the entire construction collapses. The battle account in Smith's reading had no collective name at all — just a list of allies and non-allies. Fleming needs to establish the relative antiquity of 'am Yhwh more firmly than he does here.

## Claim 2: The ten 'am Yhwh occurrences in the Bible show a pattern — the distinct ones present 'am Yhwh as a political entity separate from Israel

**Author's claim:** The ten texts cluster in three groups: "In five (probably six) of these, 'the people of Yahweh' defines the worshipping congregation, and the expression is never independent of Israel." The other three or four "attach 'the people of Yahweh' to Israel, with the latter always in the second position" (p. 213).

**Evidence presented:**
- Five congregational uses (Num 11:29, 17:6; 1 Sam 2:24; 2 Sam 1:12, 6:21; 2 Kgs 9:6 in part) all identify Yahweh's people AS Israel — standard usage, probably late
- The three "political" texts are the interesting ones:
  - **2 Sam 1:12**: David mourns "for Saul and for Jonathan his son and for the people of Yahweh and for the house of Israel" — four separate entities, with 'am Yhwh distinct from "the house of Israel." The Greek replaces 'am Yhwh with "the people of Judah," showing the phrase was puzzling to ancient translators (p. 214)
  - **2 Sam 6:21**: Textually doubtful — MT has "the people of Yahweh, over Israel" but LXX lacks 'am Yhwh entirely (p. 215)
  - **2 Kgs 9:6**: Jehu is anointed "king over the people of Yahweh, over Israel" — but the phrase is absent from Elisha's original instructions in v. 3 and may be a secondary addition (p. 216)
- **Judg 5:11, 13**: The two occurrences that straddle the hymn/battle boundary — v. 11 in the introduction, v. 13 in the call to battle (p. 216)
- "The very rarity of the 'am Yhwh phrase, along with the contrast of these uses to the worshipping congregation displayed in the other five texts, suggests that we are not dealing with the stereotyping hand of writers with large-scale composition in view" (p. 213)
- The three texts with 'am Yhwh as political entity all concern moments of leadership transition in Israel: Saul's death (2 Sam 1), David's establishment (2 Sam 6), Jehu's anointing (2 Kgs 9) — "all three defined by leadership of Israel without reference to Judah" (p. 213)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The pattern is real and interesting. The observation about the Greek translation in 2 Sam 1:12 is particularly telling — the translator found 'am Yhwh puzzling enough to "correct" it. But the small sample size (3-4 texts) makes pattern detection inherently fragile.

**What's at stake:** If 'am Yhwh was a genuinely distinct entity in earlier tradition, later biblical writers had to reconcile it with "Israel." The pattern of 'am Yhwh being paired with Israel (always second) suggests the writers knew these were originally separate and were harmonizing them.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who reads 'am Yhwh as simple stylistic variation for "Israel."

**Alternative reading:** The separate mention of 'am Yhwh and "house of Israel" in 2 Sam 1:12 could be poetic parallelism (A and B, B and A), not evidence of distinct entities.

**My assessment:** The 2 Sam 1:12 text is genuinely striking and hard to explain as mere parallelism — four items connected by repeated 'al ("for"), not two pairs. Fleming's pattern analysis is careful and fair. The small sample size is a real limitation, but the convergence of three leadership-transition texts all treating 'am Yhwh as something distinct from (though paired with) Israel is hard to dismiss as coincidence.

## Claim 3: Exodus 15:16 ("your people, Yahweh") independently attests a people defined solely by its god — the same pattern as 'am Yhwh

**Author's claim:** "Sandwiched between the echoing definition as a people bought by its god we find a name by direct address that would only transform 'the people of Yahweh' into hymnic form: 'your people, Yahweh'" (p. 231). "Once again we encounter a unified entity, here a group that celebrates its existence as independent from those around it, not by any proper name but only by association with Yahweh as its god" (p. 231).

**Evidence presented:**
- Exodus 15 never names the rescued people as "Israel" — the only collective identifications are "the people that you redeemed" (v. 13), "your people, Yahweh" (v. 16), and "the people that you purchased" (v. 16) (p. 231)
- The Song of the Sea is probably a Jerusalem composition, not from the northern kingdom — language shared with Isaiah 40-55 and Psalms (pp. 231-232)
- The poem describes entry into a land between Philistia, Edom, Moab, and Canaan — the southern highlands — without reference to Egypt as point of departure! (p. 232)
- "The beneficiaries of this victory have never been in Egypt" — they enter from outside, passing between the four named peoples (p. 232)
- The prose of Exodus 14 is probably a reinterpretation of the poem, not its source — the poem's imagery (chariots thrown into the sea, sinking) clashes with the prose narrative (walls of water, shaken loose) (p. 232)
- The poem was likely composed independent of the exodus narrative and later incorporated into it

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The observation that Exodus 15 never names Israel is undeniable and important. The claim that it's independent of the exodus narrative is more contested — European scholarship largely dates it later than the prose. But Fleming's point about the absence of "Israel" stands regardless of dating.

**What's at stake:** If Exodus 15 independently attests a "people of Yahweh" without reference to Israel, it confirms the pattern from Judges 5. Two separate poems, from different regions (north and south), both know a people defined by Yahweh rather than by a proper name.

**Who disagrees:** Schmid (2012a) and European scholarship generally treat Exodus 15 as post-Priestly and dependent on the prose — no independent tradition. If the poem is late, its "your people, Yahweh" language may be derived from standard biblical usage, not from an old tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The poem assumes the readers know this is Israel — the name is simply omitted for poetic reasons, not because a different entity is in view.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading of Exodus 15 as independent of the exodus narrative is plausible but not forced — the geographical mismatch (never in Egypt) is genuinely striking. The absence of "Israel" is a real datum. But whether this reflects an old tradition or a poetic choice is hard to determine.

## Claim 4: The Yhwʒ people of Shasu-land and the 'am Yhwh of Judges 5 share the same structural pattern — coalitions of kinship-based groups identified by the name Yhw(h)

**Author's claim:** "I underscore the social continuity between the Shasu people and the 'people of Yahweh' in the Song of Deborah. What the Egyptians call 'Shasu-land' is their interpretation of a coalition consisting of the listed names, which I understand as 'families' or 'tribes' (mhwt)... Such alliance of collectives resembles markedly what is depicted in the Song of Deborah" (p. 250).

**Evidence presented:**
- Shasu-land = Egyptian term for a coalition of 4-5 named groups (Trbr, Yhwʒ, Smt, Pyspys) encountered in a single military confrontation (Ch 2)
- The 'am Yhwh in Judg 5 = an alliance of ~10 named groups (Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, etc.) fighting together
- Both are coalitions of independent groups uniting for war against an external enemy
- Both are identified by the name Yhw(h) — the Shasu subgroup Yhwʒ and the biblical 'am Yhwh
- The Egyptian mhwt ("families/tribes") matches the kinship-based organization visible in the Song of Deborah's list of peoples
- "The Song's definition of the alliance as the 'am Yhwh, which could be rendered the 'Yahweh-people' as well as 'the people of Yahweh,' identifies deity with human community so closely that without the curse of verse 23 and the larger biblical text we could imagine Yhwh to represent a social-political entity, a 'people'" (p. 250)
- The geographies are compatible once Seir is removed from the Soleb list: the Shasu inhabited the inland back country wrapping around the Levantine highlands, which is where Judg 5's peoples lived (p. 249)
- Both are dated to periods before the kingdom of Israel: Yhwʒ to c. 1400 BCE, the battle account's political landscape to before the 9th century Omrides

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is the most speculative claim in the book. The structural parallel is real, but the temporal gap (600+ years between Yhwʒ and the composition of Judg 5) makes direct continuity impossible to demonstrate.

**What's at stake:** If the parallel holds, Fleming has identified a genuine historical continuity: the name Yhw(h) designates a coalition of kinship-based groups in both the 14th-century Egyptian record and the pre-monarchic Israelite tradition. This would be the strongest evidence yet for "Yahweh before Israel."

**Who disagrees:** Anyone skeptical of identifying Yhwʒ with 'am Yhwh — the names are similar but the contexts are vastly different (Egyptian military list vs. Hebrew poetry).

**Alternative reading:** The similarity is coincidental. Lots of ancient peoples formed military alliances. The name Yhwʒ/ Yahweh appearing in both contexts proves nothing about structural continuity.

**My assessment:** Fleming is honest about the speculative nature of this claim. The structural parallel is intriguing but can't bear much weight. The temporal gap is vast, and we have no evidence for what happened to the Yhwʒ people between 1400 and 900 BCE. The parallel is suggestive, not probative.

## Claim 5: The Mesha inscription's Yahweh sanctuary at Nebo may predate Omride rule — Yahweh east of the Jordan before Israel

**Author's claim:** "Mesha never claims that Yahweh was introduced to Nebo only with Omri and Israel, and sacred sites tend to persist in time. It is possible that the sanctuary for Yahweh at Nebo preceded Omri's arrival" (p. 207). "If the Yahweh shrine at Nebo went back to time before Omri, then it could reflect worship that was not tied to Israel. In such a reconstruction, Yahweh at Nebo would also be 'before Israel'" (p. 208).

**Evidence presented:**
- The Mesha inscription distinguishes between Omri's recent intrusion and the long-established "men of Gad" who occupied Atarot "since forever" (line 10)
- Mesha captures Nebo "from Israel" (line 14) and drags "the vessels of Yahweh" before Kamosh (lines 17-18)
- But Mesha never says the Yahweh shrine was NEW or established by Israel — the text simply notes its presence at Nebo when Israel controlled the town
- "Sacred sites tend to persist in time" — the shrine could predate Omri
- In Num 32:34, the Gadites are associated with the same territory — a biblical tradition independent of the Mesha text

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is an argument from silence: Mesha doesn't say Omri built the shrine, so maybe it was older. But Mesha also doesn't say it was older. The text is simply silent on the question.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh was worshipped east of the Jordan before Omri, the god's presence in the inland region predates Israelite political control. This would support Fleming's larger thesis.

**Who disagrees:** The standard reading: Yahweh came to Nebo WITH Israel. Mesha mentions Yahweh in the context of Israel's defeat, and the shrine vessels are taken because Israel is defeated.

**Alternative reading:** The Yahweh shrine at Nebo was an Israelite foundation, established when Omri took the town. Mesha's desecration of it is specifically aimed at humiliating Israel and its god.

**My assessment:** This is a weak argument that Fleming presents cautiously. It's possible but impossible to prove. The "sacred sites persist" principle is true in general but doesn't tell us anything about this specific site.

## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: 'am Yhwh as pre-Israelite identity | MEDIUM | Smith's reading that 'am Yhwh is also secondary |
| 2: Pattern in ten 'am Yhwh texts | MEDIUM | Small sample size, alternative parallelism reading |
| 3: Exod 15:16 as independent attestation | MEDIUM | European late-dating of Exodus 15 |
| 4: Yhwʒ / 'am Yhwh structural parallel | LOW-MEDIUM | 600-year gap, coincidence |
| 5: Nebo shrine predates Omri | LOW-MEDIUM | Argument from silence |

**Strongest section:** The analysis of Judges 5's compositional layers (pp. 219-224). This is careful, well-argued, and genuinely illuminating about how the Song was constructed. The contrast between the hymn's unified Israel and the battle account's coalition of independent groups is real and important, regardless of whether 'am Yhwh is the original collective name.

**Weakest section:** The Yhwʒ / 'am Yhwh parallel (pp. 248-250). Fleming knows it's speculative and presents it as such. The structural similarity is real but the historical connection is unprovable.

**Overall:** This is the chapter where Fleming's thesis either succeeds or fails. The 'am Yhwh argument is the positive alternative to the Midianite Hypothesis. It's creative, textually grounded, and fits the evidence better than the southern-origin model in several respects. But it depends heavily on a specific reading of Judges 5 that Mark Smith — Fleming's most important dialogue partner — rejects. The gap between "suggestive" and "demonstrated" remains wide.
