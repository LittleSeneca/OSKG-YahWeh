# Fleming — Chapter 6: The People of Yahweh

This is Fleming's positive argument and the constructive center of the book. Having dismantled the Midianite Hypothesis (Ch 3-4) and established Yhwʒ as a Shasu people-name (Ch 2), he now asks: does the Bible preserve any trace of a people called "Yahweh"? The answer is yes — and it's hiding in plain sight. The phrase 'am Yhwh ("the people of Yahweh") occurs only ten times in the entire Bible, but those ten occurrences cluster into two distinct groups: five late congregational uses, and five that present 'am Yhwh as a genuine political entity. The most important is Judg 5:13, where the alliance fighting the "kings of Canaan" is identified not as "Israel" but as "the people of Yahweh" — and the name Israel only appears in the secondary introductory hymn (vv. 2-11). Exodus 15:16 independently attests the same pattern from a different region (Jerusalem/southern highlands): "your people, Yahweh." Two separate poems, from north and south, both know a people defined by Yahweh without reference to Israel. The chapter concludes by arguing that the biblical 'am Yhwh and the Egyptian Yhwʒ of Shasu-land share the same structural pattern: coalitions of kinship-based groups united for war, identified by the name Yhw(h). This is the link that connects the 14th-century BCE Shasu people to the first-millennium god.

---

## Claim 1: The 'am Yhwh in Judges 5:13 is the collective name of the pre-Israelite alliance — Israel is imposed only by the secondary hymn

**Author's claim:** "In this older poem, they fought as 'the people of Yahweh' (v. 13), and if Yahweh had any other role it was to curse those who failed to join (v. 23)" (p. 243). "Without the anachronistic recasting as Israel in the introductory hymn, the people of Yahweh named in verse 13 are 'before Israel' in the sense that Israel is in no way identified with them" (p. 244).

**Evidence presented:**
- All eight references to Israel in Judges 5 appear in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11) — per Sara Milstein's (2016) "revision through introduction" (p. 211)
- The hymn presents Israel as a unified collective acting as one body — this is NOT the image of the battle account, where individual groups make independent decisions (p. 220)
- The battle account (vv. 12-22/23) lists ten peoples by name, each deciding separately whether to fight: six join (Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, Naphtali), four decline (Reuben, Gilead, Dan, Asher) — none is called "Israel" (pp. 223-224)
- "Deborah and Barak serve to lead them into battle, jointly and without title; otherwise the group leadership is always plural" (p. 244) — no king, no permanent political structure
- The enemy is equally an alliance: "the kings of Canaan" (v. 19) — city-based rulers united for war, like the Mari and Amarna patterns of coalition (p. 224)
- "The named groups are never classified as 'tribes,' and there is no effort to organize them as comparable units of some larger whole" (p. 223) — the only category word is 'ām, applied fluidly
- The end of verse 11 ("Then the people of Yahweh went down to the gates") "shows itself a seam that binds the opening hymn in verses 2-11a to the core battle account of verses 12-22/23" (p. 217)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The redaction-critical observation is strong and shared. But the claim that 'am Yhwh specifically is the OLDER collective name depends on verse 13 being original to the battle account, which Mark Smith (2014: 245-46) disputes — he considers 'am Yhwh part of the secondary introduction.

**What's at stake:** This is Fleming's centerpiece. If 'am Yhwh is a pre-Israelite identity, then Yahweh defined a people before Israel did, and the kingdom of Israel was partly constructed from Yahweh-people who preceded it.

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (2014: 245-46) considers 'am Yhwh in both v. 11 and v. 13 to belong to the introductory revision — no 'am Yhwh in the original battle poem at all.

**Alternative reading:** 'Am Yhwh is simply poetic variation for "Israel" — both terms refer to the same entity, and the alternation is stylistic.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is elegant and explains features otherwise puzzling. But Smith's objection is serious. If 'am Yhwh is secondary, Fleming's entire positive case loses its oldest anchor. The question turns on whether the phrase belongs to the received battle poem or was added by the hymnic redactor — a question the text alone may not be able to answer definitively.

## Claim 2: The ten 'am Yhwh texts fall into two distinct clusters — congregational (late) and political (possibly old), with the political ones presenting 'am Yhwh as a genuine entity separate from Israel

**Author's claim:** "Five of these are concerned entirely with the people as the possession of Yahweh, serving him, supplied by him, and ruled by him. These are broadly later than and secondary to the others, conceptually derivative and theological" (p. 211). The remaining five "all join 'the people of Yahweh' to Israel, with the latter always in the second position" but present 'am Yhwh as something with its own political character (p. 213).

**Evidence presented:**
- **Five congregational texts**: Ezek 36:20 (Yahweh's name defamed when his people go into exile); Zeph 2:10 (enemies scorn "the people of Yahweh of Hosts"); Num 11:29 (Moses wishes all Yahweh's people were prophets); Num 17:6 (rebels accuse Moses of killing Yahweh's people); 1 Sam 2:24 (Eli hears bad reports about Yahweh's people). All are generally considered late, post-monarchic, and treat 'am Yhwh as the worshipping congregation identified WITH Israel (pp. 211-212)
- **Five political texts**: 2 Sam 1:12; 2 Sam 6:21; 2 Kgs 9:6; Judg 5:11; Judg 5:13. These "all join 'the people of Yahweh' to Israel, with the latter always in the second position" (p. 213) — as if the two are paired but not identical
- **2 Sam 1:12** is the most striking: David mourns "for Saul and for Jonathan his son and for the people of Yahweh and for the house of Israel" — FOUR separate entities, connected by repeated 'al ("for"). The Greek translator replaced 'am Yhwh with "the people of Judah," showing the phrase was puzzling even in antiquity (p. 214)
- **2 Sam 6:21** is textually doubtful — MT has "the people of Yahweh, over Israel" but LXX lacks 'am Yhwh entirely (p. 215)
- **2 Kgs 9:6**: Jehu anointed "king over the people of Yahweh, over Israel" — but the phrase is absent from Elisha's original instructions in v. 3, suggesting it's a secondary addition that nevertheless preserves an older conception (pp. 215-216)
- The three non-Judges political texts all cluster around leadership transitions: Saul's death (2 Sam 1), David's establishment (2 Sam 6), Jehu's anointing (2 Kgs 9) — "all three defined by leadership of Israel without reference to Judah" (p. 213)
- "The very rarity of the 'am Yhwh phrase, along with the contrast of these uses to the worshipping congregation displayed in the other five texts, suggests that we are not dealing with the stereotyping hand of writers with large-scale composition in view" (p. 213)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The pattern is real — five congregational texts do have a distinctly different feel from the five political ones. But the small sample (10 total, 5 in each category) makes definitive conclusions fragile. The 2 Sam 1:12 text, with its four separate entities, is the strongest single piece of evidence.

**What's at stake:** If 'am Yhwh is systematically distinguished from Israel in older texts, then the equation of Yahweh's people with Israel is a secondary development — precisely what Fleming's thesis requires.

**Who disagrees:** Those who read 'am Yhwh as simple stylistic variation. If 'am Yhwh = Israel in all cases, there's no pattern to detect.

**Alternative reading:** The separate mention of 'am Yhwh and "house of Israel" in 2 Sam 1:12 is poetic parallelism, not evidence of distinct entities. Many biblical texts pair synonyms for rhetorical effect.

**My assessment:** The 2 Sam 1:12 text resists the parallelism explanation — four items connected by repeated 'al, not two pairs. The Greek translator's "correction" shows the phrase was genuinely puzzling. Fleming's pattern detection is careful. But 10 occurrences is a tiny sample for statistical reasoning.

## Claim 3: The 'am Yhwh in Judges 5 is an alliance for mutual defense — "Yahweh is defined by the people" — in contrast to Exodus 15 where "the people are defined by Yahweh"

**Author's claim:** "In Judg 5:12-22/23, Yahweh is defined by the people. He is not a king; he is not a warrior; and he is not a storm god. Indeed, the function of Yahweh as deity is to bind the allies who fight under his name" (p. 243).

**Evidence presented:**
- In Judges 5's battle account, Yahweh does NOT fight: "only 'the stars fought from heaven' (Judg 5:20)" (p. 243)
- Yahweh's only active role is to curse those who fail to join the alliance (v. 23, if original to the battle account)
- The alliance has no permanent structure — it's activated only for military need: "we are to imagine no regular ritual observance or lasting league, as Martin Noth proposed for his amphictyony" (p. 225)
- No sacred location or ritual observance is mentioned — "so far as no sacred place of assembly is named, we are left to imagine that such was chosen based on the geography of the crisis" (p. 225)
- Contrast with Exodus 15: there, Yahweh IS a storm god and king — he defeats Pharaoh by driving waves with his winds, he rules as divine king, the people's identity flows FROM him (p. 242)
- The contrast is expressed as: "In the Song of the Sea, the people are defined by Yahweh, while in the Song of Deborah, Yahweh is defined by a people" (p. 242)
- The political landscape of the 'am Yhwh alliance is pre-monarchic: "allies for mutual defense, in this case against a corresponding alliance called 'the kings of Canaan'" (p. 243)
- The geography straddles the Jordan and the Jezreel Valley, roughly matching the later northern kingdom's extent — "the congruent space shows how later kings were able to extend their realms by following traditional lines of alliance" (p. 225)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The contrast between the two poems is real and illuminating — Fleming has identified a genuine difference in how Yahweh relates to people in each text. But whether this difference tracks a historical development (older = people-defines-god, newer = god-defines-people) or simply reflects different genres/regions is harder to establish.

**What's at stake:** This contrast supports Fleming's developmental model: early Yahweh was defined by the people who bore his name; later, under monarchy, Yahweh became the god who defines and creates his people.

**Who disagrees:** Smith considers Yahweh absent from the original battle poem, so there's no "Yahweh defined by the people" in the oldest layer.

**Alternative reading:** The difference between the two poems reflects different regional traditions (northern alliance vs. southern/Jerusalem royal theology), not chronological development.

**My assessment:** The contrast is genuine and important regardless of dating. Fleming has identified two different modes of relationship between deity and people in the Bible's oldest poetry. Whether one is older than the other, or both coexisted in different regions, the typological distinction is valuable.

## Claim 4: The "people of Kemosh" ('am kĕmôš) in Numbers 21:29 provides a Moabite parallel — another people defined by a deity, using the same grammatical construction

**Author's claim:** "In what Levine (2000: 123-25) calls the 'Heshbon Ballad' of Num 21:27-30... a poetic fragment recalling Heshbon's defeat sets in parallel 'Moab' and 'the people of Kemosh' ('am kĕmôš, v. 29)" (p. 211). "'The people of Kemosh' therefore appear an old traditional category, with another named god entering the Bible without complaint of illegitimacy or idolatry, caught up in memory of military defeat" (p. 211).

**Evidence presented:**
- Num 21:28-29a presents Sihon and Heshbon belonging to Moab before the text is aligned with Amorite conquest — "neither this nor any other biblical text explains who defeated them or when" (p. 211)
- Jeremiah 48:45-46 preserves a variant of the same text in an oracle against Moab
- The 'am kĕmôš construction is grammatically identical to 'am Yhwh
- Kamosh appears in the 9th-century Mesha inscription as Moab's god — and Mesha devotes slaughter "to Kamosh and to Moab" (line 12), precisely pairing god and people
- "'The people of Yahweh' should likewise be old and traditional, not a later monarchic or post-monarchic construction" (p. 211)
- The broader South Arabian evidence (developed in Ch 7) confirms this pattern: "descendants of 'Amm" / "descendants of Almaqah" = people defined by divine descent

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The 'am kĕmôš parallel is a genuinely important piece of evidence that has been underutilized in Yahweh-origins scholarship. The grammatical identity and the Mesha inscription's parallel pairing ("to Kamosh and to Moab") make a strong case.

**What's at stake:** If 'am kĕmôš is an old traditional category in Moab, and 'am Yhwh uses the identical construction in Israel, the pattern is regional, not uniquely Israelite. This supports Fleming's argument that 'am Yhwh represents a genuine political-religious type, not a late theological coinage.

**Who disagrees:** Those who date the Heshbon Ballad late — if it's post-exilic, it could be imitating Israelite usage rather than representing independent Moabite tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The 'am kĕmôš phrase could be an Israelite/Judahite coinage applied to Moab — not evidence of actual Moabite usage.

**My assessment:** The combination of the Heshbon Ballad with the Mesha inscription's "to Kamosh and to Moab" makes the alternative reading unlikely. Two independent sources (biblical poem + Moabite inscription) both pair Kamosh with his people in parallel. The pattern is real.

## Claim 5: Exodus 15:16 independently attests "your people, Yahweh" — a people defined solely by its god, without any proper name

**Author's claim:** "Sandwiched between the echoing definition as a people bought by its god we find a name by direct address that would only transform 'the people of Yahweh' into hymnic form: 'your people, Yahweh'" (p. 231). "Once again we encounter a unified entity, here a group that celebrates its existence as independent from those around it, not by any proper name but only by association with Yahweh as its god" (p. 231).

**Evidence presented:**
- Exodus 15 never names the rescued people as "Israel" — the only identifications are "the people that you redeemed" (v. 13), "your people, Yahweh" (v. 16), and "the people that you purchased" (v. 16) (p. 231)
- The poem was likely composed at Jerusalem but preserves genuinely old tradition: the Song lacks the detailed political geography of Judges 5 and shares significant vocabulary with Psalms and Isaiah, yet its core content is independent of all other biblical writing (pp. 235-237)
- The poem divides into two parts: Pharaoh's defeat at sea (1b-12) and the people's journey to Yahweh's highland home (13-18). Neither part depends on the prose exodus narrative (p. 233)
- Egypt is defeated by Yahweh acting as storm god — but "Yahweh does not defeat the sea like Baal at Ugarit or Marduk in the Babylonian creation myth... The sea becomes Yahweh's weapon" (p. 235) — this is a unique conception
- The people travel to Yahweh's dwelling in "the highlands" (hār), described as nāweh ("pasture") — language associated with shepherds and grazing flocks, not a temple or city (p. 236)
- "Nothing in the poem portrays a single mass in procession, a notion that we only bring to the text from the prose of an exodus out of Egypt" (p. 236)
- The poem is "biblically old, independent of other composition in its main ideas" (p. 237) — probably pre-8th century
- The Song presents an origins story: "A people identified by its worship of Yahweh in a space inhabited by Yahweh comes to share this land and enjoy security from its neighbors after Pharaoh's defeat" (p. 239)
- Crucially, "the beneficiaries of this victory have never been in Egypt" (p. 232) — they enter the land from outside, passing between Philistia, Edom, Moab, and Canaan

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Fleming's reading of Exodus 15 as independent of the prose is well-argued and the textual observations (no Israel, never in Egypt, pastoralist language) are important. But the dating remains contested — European scholarship largely dates the poem post-exilic. The claim that it's "biblically old" would need more defense against the Schmid/Pfeiffer position.

**What's at stake:** If Exodus 15 independently attests a "people of Yahweh" without Israel, from a DIFFERENT region (the southern highlands / Jerusalem sphere) than Judges 5 (the north), then the 'am Yhwh identity was widespread, not a local northern peculiarity. Two separate poems from two separate regions both know a people defined by Yahweh without the name Israel.

**Who disagrees:** Schmid (2012a) and European scholarship treat the Song as post-Priestly and dependent on the prose. Pfeiffer (2017) considers it late. If the poem is late, "your people, Yahweh" may be standard biblical usage, not evidence of old tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The Song assumes the reader knows this is Israel — the name is simply omitted for poetic/hymnic reasons.

**My assessment:** Fleming's reading is textually careful and the observations are sharp. The absence of "Israel" in a poem that later tradition embedded in the Exodus narrative is genuinely striking — if a late writer were composing to fit the prose, why omit the name? The pastoralist language (nāweh, "pasture") is also hard to explain as late theological coinage. This is a stronger argument than alternative readings acknowledge.

## Claim 6: The Yhwʒ people of Shasu-land and the 'am Yhwh of Judges 5 share the same structural pattern — both are coalitions of kinship-based groups identified by the name Yhw(h), united for war

**Author's claim:** "If by far the oldest attestation of the name attests an inland pastoralist people, not a god, how could a people give its name to the deity who became the focus of worship in the two kingdoms of Israel and Judah?" (p. 242). The link is structural: both Yhwʒ and 'am Yhwh name coalitions of independent groups united for mutual defense.

**Evidence presented:**
- Yhwʒ of Shasu-land: one of 4-5 named subgroups within "Shasu-land," encountered by Egypt in a single military confrontation, organized by kinship (mhwt = "families/tribes") (Ch 2)
- 'Am Yhwh of Judges 5: an alliance of ~10 named groups, each making independent decisions about military participation, with no permanent political structure
- Both are coalitions of collectives — groups defined by kinship rather than by cities or kings — uniting against external enemies
- Both are identified by the name Yhw(h): the Shasu subgroup Yhwʒ and the biblical alliance 'am Yhwh
- The Egyptian mhwt category for Shasu subdivisions matches the kinship-based organization visible in the Song of Deborah's list of peoples
- "As an alliance, this people of Yahweh consists of collectives on all levels in a mode that in other contexts would be taken to indicate kinship-based social organization of the sort represented also by the 'families' or 'tribes' (mhwt) of the Shasu in Egyptian conception" (p. 244)
- "The conceptual distance between 'Yahweh-people' and 'people of Yahweh' is not great, and with this use, the Song of Deborah brings us closest to a trace of how one of the ancient Shasu peoples could have given its name to a god" (p. 218)
- The geography is compatible: the Shasu inhabited the inland back country wrapping around the Levantine highlands, which is where Judg 5's peoples lived (p. 249)
- "Both would represent large entities gathered to fight an external foe... Nothing makes the comparison intrinsically out of scale" (p. 229)

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. The structural parallel is real and genuinely suggestive, but the temporal gap (600+ years between Yhwʒ c. 1400 and the composition of Judg 5's battle account, whatever its exact date) makes direct continuity impossible to demonstrate. Fleming acknowledges this: "we confront a chronological gap that resembles in some ways the one presented by Israel in the Merenptah stele" (p. 226).

**What's at stake:** If the parallel holds, Fleming has identified a genuine historical continuity connecting 14th-century Shasu to first-millennium Israelite tradition. This is the strongest possible evidence for his "Yahweh before Israel" thesis.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone skeptical of identifying Yhwʒ with 'am Yhwh across a 600-year gap with no intermediate evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The similarity is typological (many ancient peoples formed military alliances) rather than genealogical. Yhwʒ and 'am Yhwh happen to share the same name, but the structural similarity is coincidental.

**My assessment:** Fleming is honest about the speculative nature of this claim. The structural parallel is intriguing and has not been previously identified. But the evidentiary gap is vast, and the argument depends on accepting multiple prior conclusions (Ch 2: Yhwʒ as people-name; Ch 4: Seir absent from Soleb; Ch 6: 'am Yhwh as pre-Israelite). Each link in the chain is individually plausible but collectively fragile.

## Claim 7: The Song of the Sea (Exodus 15) is an origins story recalling Egypt's withdrawal from Canaan, not an exodus from Egypt

**Author's claim:** "The Song of the Sea is an origins story. A people identified by its worship of Yahweh in a space inhabited by Yahweh comes to share this land and enjoy security from its neighbors after Pharaoh's defeat" (p. 239). Following Hendel, "the exodus preserves a 'cultural memory' of Egypt's domination of Canaan during the dynasties of the New Kingdom" (p. 239).

**Evidence presented:**
- The people in the poem are never in Egypt — they enter the land from outside, passing between four awestruck peoples (Philistia, Edom, Moab, Canaan)
- Pharaoh's army is defeated at "the Sea of Reeds" (Yam Suph) without reference to any human enemy present — "Egypt's army is put out of action without reference to any human enemy, so we cannot picture any military conflict except in prospect" (p. 239)
- "The four peoples named in verses 14b-15 are all groups that have territorial associations, effectively enclosing a particular space to be occupied by the human protagonists" (p. 239) — they also benefit from Egypt's defeat
- Hendel's "cultural memory" thesis: the poem recalls Egypt's New Kingdom domination of the Levant, not a literal escape from Egypt
- The poem's geography — Peleset (Philistia), Edom, Moab, and Canaan — frames the southern highlands, suiting the core territory of Judah, not northern Israel
- The poem was embedded in the Moses-exodus narrative secondarily — "the separate story of a 'sea' episode originated in the poem" (p. 239)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The observation that the poem's people are never in Egypt is textually solid. The "cultural memory" reading is plausible and has scholarly support (Hendel). But the transition from "Egyptian withdrawal from Canaan" to "exodus from Egypt" is a major reinterpretation, and the poem's original setting remains uncertain.

**What's at stake:** If the Song of the Sea originally described Egyptian withdrawal from Canaan (not Israelite escape from Egypt), then the entire exodus narrative is a secondary development, and the poem's "your people, Yahweh" refers to highland populations who benefited from Egypt's departure — not to escapees from Egypt.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional readings that treat the poem as celebrating the exodus from Egypt. Also, European scholars who date the poem post-exilic and dependent on the prose — if the prose came first, the poem can't preserve older tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The poem always described an exodus from Egypt — the absence of explicit Egypt-as-starting-point is poetic compression, not evidence of a different original setting.

**My assessment:** This is one of Fleming's most creative arguments and deserves more attention. The observation that "the beneficiaries have never been in Egypt" is undeniable on a plain reading of the text. Whether this reflects an older tradition or a poetic choice is debatable, but Fleming has shifted the burden of proof.

## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: 'am Yhwh as pre-Israelite identity in Judg 5 | MEDIUM | Smith's reading that 'am Yhwh is secondary |
| 2: Two-cluster pattern in ten texts | MEDIUM | Small sample, parallelism alternative |
| 3: Yahweh defined by people vs. people by Yahweh | MEDIUM | Whether difference is chronological or regional |
| 4: 'am kĕmôš as Moabite parallel | MEDIUM-HIGH | Dating of Heshbon Ballad |
| 5: Exod 15:16 as independent attestation | MEDIUM | European late-dating of Exodus 15 |
| 6: Yhwʒ / 'am Yhwh structural parallel | LOW-MEDIUM | 600-year gap, chain of dependent arguments |
| 7: Song of Sea as Canaan-memory, not exodus | MEDIUM | Poetic compression alternative |

**Strongest section:** The contrast between Exodus 15 and Judges 5 — "Yahweh defined by the people" vs. "the people defined by Yahweh" (pp. 241-245). This is genuinely illuminating and identifies a structural difference in how deity relates to community that has implications beyond the Yahweh-origins question.

**Weakest section:** The Yhwʒ/'am Yhwh parallel (pp. 245-250) is the most ambitious claim but also the most speculative. Fleming acknowledges the chronological gap honestly but the argument requires accepting too many prior conclusions simultaneously.

**Overall:** This is the chapter where Fleming's thesis succeeds or fails. The 'am Yhwh argument is creative, textually grounded, and fits the evidence better than the Midianite Hypothesis in several respects. But it depends heavily on a specific reading of Judges 5's redaction history that Fleming's most important dialogue partner (Smith) rejects. The gap between "suggestive" and "demonstrated" remains wide — but Fleming has opened up a genuinely new line of inquiry that the field will need to engage.

**Cross-reference to primary sources:** [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]] on the Mesha Stele (Section 5) is directly relevant — the Mesha inscription's pairing of Kamosh and Moab provides the epigraphic parallel to the biblical 'am Yhwh / 'am kĕmôš pattern.
