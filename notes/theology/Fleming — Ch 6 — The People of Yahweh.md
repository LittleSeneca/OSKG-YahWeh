# Fleming — Chapter 6: The People of Yahweh

This is Fleming's positive argument and the constructive center of the book. Having dismantled the Midianite Hypothesis (Ch 3-4) and established Yhwʒ as a Shasu people-name (Ch 2), he now asks: does the Bible preserve any trace of a people called "Yahweh"? The answer is yes — and it's hiding in plain sight. The phrase 'am Yhwh ("the people of Yahweh") occurs only ten times in the entire Bible, but those ten occurrences cluster into two distinct groups: five late congregational uses, and five that present 'am Yhwh as a genuine political entity. The most important is Judg 5:13, where the alliance fighting the "kings of Canaan" is identified not as "Israel" but as "the people of Yahweh" — and the name Israel only appears in the secondary introductory hymn (vv. 2-11). Exodus 15:16 independently attests the same pattern from a different region (Jerusalem/southern highlands): "your people, Yahweh." Two separate poems, from north and south, both know a people defined by Yahweh without reference to Israel. The chapter concludes by arguing that the biblical 'am Yhwh and the Egyptian Yhwʒ of Shasu-land share the same structural pattern: coalitions of kinship-based groups united for war, identified by the name Yhw(h). This is the link that connects the 14th-century BCE Shasu people to the first-millennium god.

## Claim 1: The 'am Yhwh in Judges 5:13 is the collective name of the pre-Israelite alliance — Israel is imposed only by the secondary hymn
→ [[claim-judges-5-am-yhwh-pre-israelite-alliance-israel-secondary-hymn]] | **fleming-ybi-6.1** | Confidence: medium
  All eight Israel references appear in the hymnic frame (vv. 2-11); the battle account (vv. 12-23) names ten peoples individually without "Israel." The 'am Yhwh is the alliance's collective identity. Depends on: [[claim-am-yhwh-judges-5-pre-israelite-identity]]. Challenged by: Smith reads 'am Yhwh as also secondary.

## Claim 2: The ten 'am Yhwh texts fall into two distinct clusters — congregational (late) and political (possibly old), with the political ones presenting 'am Yhwh as a genuine entity separate from Israel
→ [[claim-ten-am-yhwh-texts-two-clusters-congregational-political]] | **fleming-ybi-6.2** | Confidence: medium
  Five late congregational texts (Ezek 36:20, Zeph 2:10, etc.) vs. five political texts (2 Sam 1:12, Judg 5:11,13, etc.). 2 Sam 1:12 lists four separate entities: Saul, Jonathan, 'am Yhwh, and house of Israel. The Greek translator was puzzled and "corrected" it.

## Claim 3: The 'am Yhwh in Judges 5 is an alliance for mutual defense — "Yahweh is defined by the people" — in contrast to Exodus 15 where "the people are defined by Yahweh"
→ [[claim-judges-5-yahweh-defined-by-people-exodus-15-people-by-yahweh]] | **fleming-ybi-6.3** | Confidence: medium
  In Judg 5, Yahweh doesn't fight — he only curses those who fail to join. In Exod 15, Yahweh IS a storm god and king. Two different modes of divine-human relationship. Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-originally-storm-god-warrior-secondary-traits]].

## Claim 4: The "people of Kemosh" ('am kĕmôš) in Numbers 21:29 provides a Moabite parallel — another people defined by a deity, using the same grammatical construction
→ [[claim-am-kemosh-num-21-29-moabite-parallel-am-yhwh]] | **fleming-ybi-6.4** | Confidence: medium-high
  Num 21:29 parallels 'Moab' and 'the people of Kemosh.' The Mesha stele (9th c.) confirms: slaughter devoted "to Kamosh and to Moab." The pattern is regional, not uniquely Israelite. Supports: [[claim-south-arabian-political-gods-best-analogy-early-yahweh]].

## Claim 5: Exodus 15:16 independently attests "your people, Yahweh" — a people defined solely by its god, without any proper name
→ [[claim-exodus-15-16-your-people-yahweh-independent-attestation]] | **fleming-ybi-6.5** | Confidence: medium
  Exodus 15 never names "Israel." The people enter the land from outside, passing between Philistia, Edom, Moab, and Canaan. The poem's pastoralist language (nāweh, "pasture") resists late-dating theories. From the southern highlands/Jerusalem sphere, complementing Judges 5's northern setting.

## Claim 6: The Yhwʒ people of Shasu-land and the 'am Yhwh of Judges 5 share the same structural pattern — both are coalitions of kinship-based groups identified by the name Yhw(h), united for war
→ [[claim-yhw3-shasu-am-yhwh-structural-parallel-coalitions-war]] | **fleming-ybi-6.6** | Confidence: low-medium
  Both are coalitions of collectives united against external enemies; both identified by Yhw(h); both organized by kinship. The 600-year gap makes direct continuity impossible to demonstrate. Depends on accepting multiple prior conclusions (Ch 2, Ch 4, Ch 6). Supports: [[claim-yahweh-not-taken-from-outsiders-divine-name-israel-diverse-origins]].

## Claim 7: The Song of the Sea (Exodus 15) is an origins story recalling Egypt's withdrawal from Canaan, not an exodus from Egypt
→ [[claim-song-of-sea-egypt-withdrawal-canaan-not-exodus-from-egypt]] | **fleming-ybi-6.7** | Confidence: medium
  Following Hendel: "cultural memory" of Egypt's New Kingdom domination of Canaan. The people in the poem have never been in Egypt — they enter the land from outside. The poem was embedded in the Moses narrative secondarily. Supports: [[claim-exodus-3-6-schematic-bridge-texts-not-historical-memory]].

## Chapter 6 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: 'am Yhwh as pre-Israelite identity in Judg 5 | MEDIUM | Smith's reading that 'am Yhwh is secondary |
| 2: Two-cluster pattern in ten texts | MEDIUM | Small sample, parallelism alternative |
| 3: Yahweh defined by people vs. people by Yahweh | MEDIUM | Whether difference is chronological or regional |
| 4: 'am kĕmôš as Moabite parallel | MEDIUM-HIGH | Dating of Heshbon Ballad |
| 5: Exod 15:16 as independent attestation | MEDIUM | European late-dating of Exodus 15 |
| 6: Yhwʒ / 'am Yhwh structural parallel | LOW-MEDIUM | 600-year gap, chain of dependent arguments |
| 7: Song of Sea as Canaan-memory, not exodus | MEDIUM | Poetic compression alternative |

**Strongest section:** The contrast between Exodus 15 and Judges 5 — "Yahweh defined by the people" vs. "the people defined by Yahweh" (pp. 241-245). This is genuinely illuminating and identifies a structural difference in how deity relates to community that has implications beyond the Yahweh-origins question.

**Weakest section:** The Yhwʒ/'am Yhwh parallel (pp. 245-250) is the most ambitious claim but also the most speculative. Fleming acknowledges the chronological gap honestly but the argument requires accepting too many prior conclusions simultaneously.

**Overall:** This is the chapter where Fleming's thesis succeeds or fails. The 'am Yhwh argument is creative, textually grounded, and fits the evidence better than the Midianite Hypothesis in several respects. But it depends heavily on a specific reading of Judges 5's redaction history that Fleming's most important dialogue partner (Smith) rejects. The gap between "suggestive" and "demonstrated" remains wide — but Fleming has opened up a genuinely new line of inquiry that the field will need to engage.

**Cross-reference to primary sources:** [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]] on the Mesha Stele (Section 5) is directly relevant — the Mesha inscription's pairing of Kamosh and Moab provides the epigraphic parallel to the biblical 'am Yhwh / 'am kĕmôš pattern.
