# Smith — Ch8: The Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric in Ancient Judah

This is the central chapter of the entire book — and its most radical thesis. Smith argues that Israelite monotheism was not an ontological discovery, not a philosophical position, and not a "new stage of religion." It was a *rhetoric of exclusivity* that emerged in the late monarchy and exile to reinforce Israel's exclusive relationship with Yahweh. Monotheistic statements (like "there is no god besides me" in Deutero-Isaiah) function as inner-community discourse — they use the language of Yahweh's exceptional divine status to absolutize his claim on Israel and express ultimate fidelity. Monotheism serves monolatry; it does not supersede it.

The chapter has five sections: (1) defining monotheism, (2) the relative infrequency and rhetorical purpose of monotheistic statements, (3) the prior context of Judean discourse about divinity, (4) the national god and royal ideology, and (5) the social and historical context of monotheism.

---

## Claim 1: Biblical monotheistic statements are overwhelmingly exilic or later — the Albright/Mosaic monotheism model is untenable

**Author's claim:** "Most of the references to monotheism derive from the exilic period or later." The debate between Albright (early monotheism) and Meek (late development) "solved nothing" because neither side addressed the constructive points of the other — and neither "attempted to situate the issues in terms of Judah's larger social structure."

**Evidence presented:**
- Albright claimed the First Commandment ("no other gods before me") demonstrated Mosaic monotheism based on Punic `'l(t) pn-` ("besides"). Smith: "Albright had a point" about the translation, but "whether it demonstrated monotheism was in fact problematic."
- Albright dodged the key question: *if* Israel was monotheistic from the start, "why did its monotheistic faith appear in clearer, less ambiguous forms in the seventh and sixth centuries?"
- Even if 1 Samuel 2:2 and Psalm 82 date to the late monarchy, "on the whole the late monarchy and exile seem to represent the general period for the emergence of monotheistic rhetoric."
- Halpern: "Had Second Isaiah, Cyrus' Judahite spin-doctor, not had Jeremiah's (and Deuteronomy's) voice crying on his own arrival from the steppe, his explicit monotheistic claims would have fallen on deaf ears, and probably set them ringing to boot."
- The First Commandment is explicitly monolatrous (cultic exclusion), NOT monotheistic. "Monotheistic exclusivity is not simply a matter of cultic observance...It extends further to an understanding of deities in the cosmos (no other gods, period)."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The chronological distribution is clear — the concentration in Second Isaiah (exilic, ca. 540 BCE) is overwhelming. Even Smith's dating caveats don't undermine the basic point.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism emerges in the late monarchy/exile, the Moses-at-Sinai narrative is retrojection. The religion of Iron I-II Israel was polytheistic or at most monolatrous. The "monotheistic revolution" is a sixth-century phenomenon, not a Mosaic one.

**Who disagrees:** Albright school (Mosaic monotheism). Conservative biblicists who date Deuteronomy and Isaiah earlier.

**Alternative reading:** Some monotheistic expressions COULD be earlier but were edited — absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. But Smith's point is that even the surviving texts cluster late.

**My assessment:** The Albright-Meek debate recap is a bit of a straw man at this point (2001), but it sets up Smith's methodological contrast nicely. The Halpern quote is excellent — it foregrounds the question of *audience* and *intelligibility* that Smith builds his whole argument around.

## Claim 2: Defining monotheism requires excluding other gods' EXISTENCE, not just their worship — and the Shema doesn't qualify

**Author's claim:** The common inclusion of the Shema (Deut 6:4) and First Commandment as monotheistic is incorrect. True monotheism requires denying the reality of other gods, not just prohibiting their worship. "Therefore, in discussing monotheism, one must exclude the reality of other gods."

**Evidence presented — two categories of genuine monotheistic statements:**

**Category 1 — Exclusivity formulas** (`*lĕbadd-`, "alone"; `*zûlat-`, "besides"):
- Deut 4:35: "Yahweh alone is God, there is none beside Him." (a "sermon" = rhetoric of persuasion)
- 2 Kings 19:15, 19 ∥ Isa 37:16, 20: "You alone are God of all the kingdoms of the earth"
- Neh 9:6: "You alone are Yahweh."
- Ps 86:10: "You are God alone."
- 2 Sam 7:22 ∥ 1 Chron 17:20: "there is no god except You"

Smith notes: "All but the first of these statements belong to prayers. Monotheism here belongs to the rhetoric of praise." — this is a crucial point: monotheism is *performative language*, not abstract theology.

**Category 2 — Denial of reality** (`'ên`, "there is not"; `'ĕlîlîm`, "nothings"; `mētîm`, "dead"):
- Deut 4:39: "Yahweh is god in heaven above and on earth below; there is no other"
- 1 Sam 2:2: "There is none beside You"
- Jer 16:19-20: "Shall a man make gods for himself / And they are no gods?"
- Ps 96:5 ∥ 1 Chron 16:26: "all the gods of the nations are nothings" (clever pun: `'ĕlōhîm` → `'ĕlîlîm`)
- Ps 82:7: "like a mortal you shall die" (language of *divine death*, old polytheistic order passing)

**What's excluded:**
- **The Shema (Deut 6:4):** Tigay: "This is not a declaration of monotheism, meaning that there is only one God." It declares exclusivity of relationship (Yahweh alone is Israel's God), not ontological exclusivity. Von Rad read it as anti-Baal polemic. Zech 14:9 (Hellenistic?) may envision only one deity, but doesn't mean Deut 6:4 did.
- **The First Commandment (Ex 20:3 / Deut 5:7):** "no other gods before me" — prohibits worship, doesn't deny existence. Zebah may be monolatry, not monotheism.
- **Deut 32:12, 15b-21, 37-39:** condemns veneration of other deities without commenting on existence.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The definitional rigor is necessary and Smith applies it carefully. The exclusion of the Shema is particularly important given its centrality in later Judaism.

**What's at stake:** If the Shema isn't monotheistic, the central confession of Judaism was originally monolatrous. Monotheism is a later interpretive overlay — even on the most famous "monotheistic" text.

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish interpreters who read `'eḥād` as "alone/only." NJPS and NRSV both translate "alone."

**Alternative reading:** The Shema COULD be monotheistic depending on the semantic range of `'eḥād` — but Smith (with Tigay and von Rad) makes a strong case that it's about exclusivity of relationship, not ontology.

**My assessment:** The definitional section is one of the chapter's most important contributions. By establishing clear criteria and excluding borderline cases, Smith forces the debate onto unambiguous ground. The pun on `'ĕlōhîm`/`'ĕlîlîm` in Ps 96:5 is a particularly nice touch — monotheism operates through *wordplay*, not philosophical argument.

## Claim 3: Monotheistic statements are RHETORICAL, not descriptive — they serve inner-community discourse, not a new "stage" of religion

**Author's claim:** This is the chapter's core thesis: "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms. As rhetoric, monotheism reinforced Israel's exclusive relationship with its deity. Monotheism is a kind of inner community discourse establishing a distance from outsiders."

**Evidence presented:**
- "Because of the post-biblical importance of monotheism, the relative rarity of its expression in the Bible is quite striking." The few instances are "spread over the whole of the so-called Deuteronomistic History" — they are NOT concentrated in a "monotheistic revolution" moment.
- "Israelites continued to worship deities other than Yahweh both before and possibly after the exile." The ruling priestly groups maintained "a Yahwistic monolatry expressed in its rhetoric of monotheism, but such a historical conclusion does not justify claims for an entirely 'monotheistic culture.'"
- "Because of the relative rarity of monotheistic claims and the ongoing presence of polytheism in ancient Israel, no one can confirm a clear evolution from monarchic monolatry...to a new stage of religion called monotheism."
- Monotheism "uses the language of Yahweh's exceptional divine status beyond and in all reality ('there are no other deities but the Lord') to absolutize Yahweh's claim on Israel and to express Israel's ultimate fidelity to Yahweh."
- Seitz quote (which Smith fully endorses): "This is not a sublime monotheism capable of differentiation from a more concrete henotheism — rather it is henotheism of a particularly potent stripe. The other elohim that continue to claim allegiance from humanity have detachable names and detachable existences — to the degree that YHWH insists that they do not exist at all."
- Monotheistic statements "attempted to persuade Judeans still unconvinced of this perspective." They may represent "the efforts of a minority of 'monotheists' to persuade a majority of Judeans who held Yahweh as the head of a larger group of divinities."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is Smith's finest conceptual move in the entire book. The distinction between "new stage of religion" and "rhetoric serving monolatry" fundamentally reframes the entire debate.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism is rhetoric, not a new stage, then:
1. The "evolution" model (polytheism → henotheism → monotheism) is wrong
2. Monotheism and monolatry coexisted — monotheism is what monolatry SOUNDS LIKE when pushed to rhetorical extremity
3. The question shifts from "when did Israelites become monotheists?" to "under what conditions did Judeans START USING monotheistic rhetoric?" — a much more productive question

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who sees monotheism as a genuine theological breakthrough or a new conceptual stage (evolutionary models). Scholars who take the monotheistic statements at face value as describing Israelite belief.

**Alternative reading:** The fact that monotheistic language is RARE doesn't mean it's "just rhetoric" — minority positions can be genuine innovations. And "rhetoric" implies insincerity in a way Smith may not intend.

**My assessment:** This is the best version of Smith's argument. The Seitz quote is the pithiest formulation: monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe." Smith is NOT saying monotheism is fake — he's saying it's a *way of talking* that serves and reinforces an exclusive relationship, not a *way of thinking* that replaces an older worldview. The minority-persuasion model (monotheists trying to convince a polytheistic majority) is also productive — it makes the rhetoric *functional*, not decorative.

## Claim 4: The prior context was a "national theology" where Yahweh was one son of Elyon among many — not the presider god

**Author's claim:** Before monotheistic rhetoric emerged, Israel operated with a "national theology" where each nation had its own patron god, Yahweh was Israel's, and a higher god (Elyon/El) presided over the arrangement. "In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god but one of his sons."

**Evidence presented:**

**Psalm 82** — the key text:
- "God (`'ĕlōhîm`) stands in the assembly of El / Among the divinities He pronounces judgment" (Ps 82:1)
- The gods are "sons of Elyon" (v. 6) — Elyon is a title of El (cf. Gen 14:18-20)
- They are condemned to die "like humans" (v. 7)
- A prophetic voice calls on Yahweh to "arise" and "judge the world" and "inherit all the nations" (v. 8) — Yahweh is to *arrogate* the inheritance of all other gods
- "Psalm 82 preserves a tradition that casts the god of Israel not in the role of the presiding god of the pantheon but as one of his sons."

**Deuteronomy 32:8-9** (LXX + DSS version, not MT):
- "When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance...He fixed the boundaries for peoples, according to the number of the divine sons: For Yahweh's portion is his people, Jacob His own inheritance."
- MT changed "divine sons" (`bĕnê 'ĕlōhîm`) to "sons of Israel" (`bĕnê yiśrā'ēl`) — "reflects a discomfort with this polytheistic theology"
- "Even if this text is quite mute about the god who presides over the whole arrangement, it does maintain a place for such a god who is not Yahweh."

**The 70-nation framework:**
- The notion of ~70 divine sons matches "the Ugaritic motif of the seventy sons of El and Athirat (CAT 1.4 VI 46)"
- "This older picture...presupposes the model of roughly equal national gods for all of the seventy nations of the world"

**Survival mechanism:**
- These texts survived only because they could be conformed to later monotheism: Elyon in Ps 82 could be read as Yahweh; MT "corrected" Deut 32:8
- "Analyzed not in terms of the later monotheism but in terms of the earlier national situation, these two passages offer an important witness to the old monarchic period theology of the national god."
- The surviving evidence is "only the tip of the iceberg" given later editing

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Deut 32:8-9 reading (LXX/DSS) is now widely accepted in scholarship. Psalm 82 is unambiguous.

**What's at stake:** If Yahweh was originally a second-tier god — a son of El/Elyon — then Israelite monotheism involved not just demoting other gods but *promoting* Yahweh to the top tier and then absorbing El's identity. This is the culmination of the "convergence" argument Smith has been building since Part I. The tripartite development is: (1) El is the high god, Yahweh is a national patron among many → (2) Yahweh absorbs El's identity and titles → (3) monotheistic rhetoric declares the other gods don't exist.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date these texts late and read them as already monotheistic. Those who question whether "Elyon" is El or just an epithet of Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** Psalm 82 could be read as Yahweh already presiding and judging — the "assembly of El" could mean "divine assembly" generically. But the "sons of Elyon" in v. 6 makes the El identification strong.

**My assessment:** This is where Smith ties the whole book together. The national theology model explains WHY monotheistic rhetoric was needed — the old model (each nation has its own god, presided over by El) became unintelligible when the nations collapsed. If Assyria and Babylon conquered Israel, the national god model failed. Monotheism was the theological response to imperial crisis. The tip-of-the-iceberg metaphor is also important: what survived was what could be reinterpreted monotheistically.

## Claim 5: Royal ideology provided the matrix for monotheism — the king as divine, the parallel between cosmic and human realms

**Author's claim:** "The royal theology and religious practice served as a basic matrix to support the national god." Three overlapping types of royal theology drew on West Semitic myth: (a) action parallel between Yahweh and the king, (b) metaphorical granting of divine power to the king in the language of the Baal-Yamm conflict myth, and (c) possibly the king as `'ĕlōhîm` (divine).

**Evidence presented:**

**(a) Action parallel — divine and human kings mirror each other:**
- Royal psalms present Yahweh and his "anointed" ruling together from Jerusalem (Ps 2), with Zion as cosmic center (Pss 46, 48, 87)
- "In contrast, all the other kings and their nations, ideally, are to submit to the authority of Yahweh and his human regent on earth."
- 1 Sam 25:28: the king fights "Yahweh's wars"
- Ps 18 ∥ 2 Sam 22: Yahweh described in Baal's storm-battle terms (vv. 8-19), enabling the king to conquer (vv. 29-45)
- Habakkuk 3: Yahweh's storm-theophany tramples enemy-nations to save the "anointed" (v. 13); includes Resheph (v. 5) as attendant destructive divinity
- "This poem bears a further importance, as it illustrates divine powers subservient to Yahweh, the warrior-king, in a context supportive of the monarchy."

**(b) The king receives Baal's power:**
- Ps 89:26 — "I will set his hand on Sea and his right hand on Rivers." The human king's power extends to the cosmic enemy of Baal. "It crosses the boundaries of this parallelism by making the king into a figure of his patron storm-god."
- Nur-Sin letter from Aleppo to Zimri-Lim of Mari: Adad (Baal) says "when you ascended the throne of your father, I gave you the weapon with which I slew Sea." — the same motif appears at Mari centuries before Israel.
- Egyptian texts (Tutmoses III stele) dress the king in Baal's storm imagery.
- This is not merely figurative: "the human king was perhaps thought to 'participate in' the power of the divine king."

**(c) The king as `'ĕlōhîm` (divine):**
- Ps 45:7: "your throne, O divine one (`'ĕlōhîm`), (is) forever and ever." The versions generally support this reading.
- Isaiah 9:5: the "messianic" figure is called `'ēl gibbôr` ("warrior-god") and `'ăbî 'ad` ("eternal father") — titles drawn from Yahweh's own titles (Isa 10:21; Deut 32:6)
- Holladay's counter-argument (these are theophoric throne-names, not divine attributions) fails: `*gibbôr` is not productive in Israelite names
- Zech 12:8: "the house of David shall be like God" — "muted reflex" of older high royal theology
- Ezek 28:14 may polemicize against the notion of the divine king
- Whitley's comparison of Ps 89 and Ps 45:7-8 shows the `'ĕlōhîm` of Ps 45:7 IS the Davidic figure
- Porter: 2 Sam 14:17 (David as "angel of God" knowing good and evil) parallels Gen 3:22, placing David among the `'ĕlōhîm`

**Key concession:** "The use of `'ĕlōhîm` for the monarch may represent not an ontological claim but a description intended to heighten the power of the king by rhetorically raising him to divine status." Whitelam: "It needs to be asked to what extent the audience or audiences of royal rituals would have drawn such careful distinctions in the use of language."

**The hērem-warfare connection to Anat:**
- The West Semitic conflict myth also underlies Israelite `hērem` (ban) warfare
- Ugaritic Anat conducts `hērem` warfare: "Destroy under the ban for two days" (CAT 1.13)
- 1 Kings 20:42: Ahab is condemned for NOT executing `hērem` on Ben-hadad
- "As the major deployers of warfare, Iron Age monarchs were well-served by the mythos of hērem-warfare. Like other mythological concepts...descriptions of hērem-warfare impute the desire for warfare to the deity whom the king serves."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the royal theology framework; MEDIUM on the king-as-`'ĕlōhîm` reading (Smith himself concedes it's "debatable but plausible").

**What's at stake:** If the king was called `'ĕlōhîm`, divinity was on a spectrum — not a binary. The same rhetorical heightening that made the king "divine" would later make Yahweh "the only god." Monotheistic rhetoric may be the same kind of move applied to a different object. The royal ideology that exalted the king ALSO exalted the national god — when the monarchy collapsed, the cosmic exaltation remained but the earthly parallel vanished, creating the conditions for monotheism.

**Who disagrees:** Emerton and Mosca on Ps 45:7 (lack of clear parallels to king as `'ĕlōhîm`). Holladay on Isa 9:5 (theophoric names). Anyone who reads Ps 45:7 as "Your throne is God's throne forever."

**Alternative reading:** Even if the king wasn't literally called `'ĕlōhîm`, the broader royal framework (king as Yahweh's earthly parallel, empowered by divine weapons) is independently attested and doesn't depend on the Ps 45:7 debate.

**My assessment:** This is the longest and most technical section of the chapter, and the arguments are somewhat esoteric for the larger thesis. But the PAYOFF is important: it shows that the conceptual TOOLKIT for monotheism (absolute language, cosmic scope) was already present in royal ideology. Monotheism didn't invent new concepts — it repurposed old ones under new conditions. The hērem-Anat connection is a bonus insight — it shows how deeply royal warfare drew on West Semitic divine mythology.

## Claim 6: The social context — erosion of the lineage system made the divine FAMILY metaphor unintelligible

**Author's claim:** "A culture with a diminished lineage system, one less embedded in traditional family patrimonies due to societal changes in the eighth through sixth centuries, might be more predisposed both to hold to individual human accountability for behavior and to see an individual deity accountable for the cosmos."

**Evidence presented:**
- At Ugarit and early Israel, "the strongest form of social identity...was the family." The divine council was conceptualized AS a divine family — "conceptual religious unity was expressed most strongly in the identification of the divine council as a divine family, a single family of deities whose connectedness is marked by their familial relations."
- By the seventh century, "the lineage system of the family had perhaps eroded, thanks to a variety of factors, including the deleterious effects of royal power on traditional patriarchal authority, the purchase of family lands by a growing upper class, and the devastating effects of warfare on the countryside."
- The post-exilic `bêt 'ābôt` ("fathers' house" — a new term) vs. the older `bêt 'āb` may witness the decline of traditional family structure.
- Prophetic shift to individual accountability in the sixth century: Jer 31:29-30, Ezek 18 (cf. 33:12-20), Deut 24:16 — children no longer punished for sins of fathers.
- "Later Israelite monotheism was denuded of the divine family, perhaps reflecting Israel's weakening family lineages and patrimonies."
- Smith views "individual accountability at the human and divine levels as concomitant developments."

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is Smith's most speculative argument in the chapter. The correlation is plausible but the causal mechanism is underdemonstrated.

**What's at stake:** If the social structure IS the matrix for theological concepts, then theology is not autonomous — changes in social organization drive changes in concepts of divinity. Monotheism isn't a "discovery" but a reflection of social atomization.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who sees theology as independent of social structure. Scholars who think the divine family metaphor persisted long after the lineage system weakened.

**Alternative reading:** The divine family metaphor could have weakened for purely theological reasons (convergence, priestly editing) without any causal connection to social structure. Correlation ≠ causation.

**My assessment:** Smith is doing Durkheim here — social structure as the model for cosmic structure. It's an elegant hypothesis but he acknowledges it's a "working hypothesis." The individual accountability texts (Ezek 18, Jer 31) are real and date to the right period, but whether they reflect family erosion or a theological innovation (or both) is hard to prove. The argument works better as a description of what HAPPENED (the divine family faded) than as an explanation of WHY it happened.

## Claim 7: The historical context — imperial crisis made national theology obsolete and demanded a super-national god

**Author's claim:** "The rise of the Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian empires issued in a serious religious reflection on Yahweh's power over the nations. The loss of identity as a nation changed Israel's understanding of the national god. Looming empires made the model of a national god obsolete. Moreover, the rise of supra-national empires suggested the model of the super-national god."

**Evidence presented:**
- Tigay: "The need to emphasize the monotheistic idea in this period was probably due to the increased exposure of Israel to the triumphant Assyrian and Babylonian empires, which attributed their victories, including victories over Israel, to their gods."
- The traditional theology (Deut 32:8-9) affirmed that all nations had their own patron-gods, with Yahweh as Israel's — but this model PRESUMED rough equality among nations.
- "First in the face of the great empires and then in exile, Israel stands at the bottom of its political power, and it exalts its deity inversely as ruler of the whole universe."
- The reduction of Judean kingship (especially after Josiah) changed the "parallel or mirroring worldview" of the royal psalms.
- Mesopotamian precedents: "the figures of Assur and Marduk assumed such proportions, the super-gods whose patronage of empires matches their manifestation as the sum-total of all the other deities." Mesopotamian authors were "exploring the nature of all divinity in relation to a single major god" (as discussed in Ch5).
- "The timing of the emergence of Israelite monotheism in the late Iron Age and exilic period fits Karl Jaspers's the 'Axial Age'...'the separation between transcendent and mundane spheres of reality.'"
- Smith links this to the inverse correlation: "As Judah's situation on the mundane level deteriorated in history, the cosmic status of its deity soared in its literature."

**Against the Persian/Zoroastrian model:**
- Some scholars locate monotheism in the Persian period, crediting Zoroastrian dualism as a model.
- Smith's reservations: (1) Zoroastrian dualism (Ahura Mazda vs. Angra Mainyu) "does not truly resemble biblical monotheism" — a principle of evil only appears in latest biblical works. (2) Biblical monotheistic language "appears to represent...developments of older language exalting the national god." (3) Proponents of the Persian model "rarely, if ever, address these issues."
- Concession: Persian tradition may have "reinforced monotheistic rhetoric" or influenced some presentations (Oppenheim: the Persian king's "eyes and ears" as model for "the satan" in Job 1-2). "Yet it was not Judean monotheism's main progenitor."

**The two-corollary synthesis:**
- Factor 1 (social): Weakening family lineages → divine family metaphor erodes → individual deity becomes intelligible
- Factor 2 (historical): Rise of empires → national god model obsolete → super-national god emerges
- Together: "These two features constitute the larger landscape of monotheistic discourse and help to explain its intelligibility in this time period."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the imperial crisis argument; MEDIUM on the Axial Age framing; HIGH on the rejection of Persian primacy.

**What's at stake:** If monotheism is a response to imperial crisis (Assyria/Babylon), then it's POLITICAL theology — a way of asserting Yahweh's sovereignty when Israel had no political sovereignty. The Axial Age framing situates Israelite monotheism within a broader world-historical pattern without making it derivative.

**Who disagrees:** Persian-period advocates (who date the key texts later). Scholars who see monotheism as a purely internal Israelite development. Those who credit Zoroastrian influence more heavily.

**Alternative reading:** The Mesopotamian precedent (Assur, Marduk as sum-total of all gods) might be the REAL model — Israelite monotheism as imitation of imperial theology rather than reaction against it. Smith notes this but doesn't develop it fully here (it's in Ch5).

**My assessment:** This is the strongest of Smith's explanatory sections. The inverse correlation (political power ↓ → cosmic status ↑) is a genuinely elegant insight, and the Mesopotamian parallels (Marduk in Enuma Elish, Assur) show that Israel wasn't unique — imperial theology tended toward monotheism-like formulations across the ANE. The rejection of Persian primacy is well-argued: the Zoroastrian model doesn't fit the actual FORM of Israelite monotheistic statements, which are clearly developments of older Yahwistic rhetoric. The "reinforced but not progenitor" position is sensible.

## Cross-Reference Notes

- **Tigay's onomastic data:** Tigay showed that personal names from the late monarchy are overwhelmingly Yahwistic — suggesting practical monolatry was widespread. Smith's argument here is compatible but shifts focus: even if MOST Judeans were functionally monolatrous, monotheistic RHETORIC was a minority discourse trying to push monolatry into an absolute key. Tigay provides the demographic baseline; Smith provides the rhetorical superstructure.

- **Römer on the "invention" of monotheism:** Thomas Römer's *The Invention of God* (2015) develops a similar argument about the Persian period as the crucible for monotheism. Smith's 2001 position is earlier and more focused on the late monarchy/exile. The key difference: Smith sees the transition happening in the late 7th-6th centuries (late monarchy + exile), while Römer extends it into the Persian period. Smith's critique of Persian primacy (section 5) anticipates this debate. The two positions can be partially reconciled: Smith is right that the RHETORIC emerges in the exile; Römer may be right that the INSTITUTIONALIZATION of monotheism as orthodoxy is Persian-period.

- **Connection to Ch9:** Smith explicitly transitions: "The expression of monotheism also extended to monotheistic descriptions and narratives; these are explored in the next chapter." Ch8 covers monotheistic DECLARATIONS; Ch9 covers monotheistic THEOLOGIES (priestly creation, Wisdom, apocalyptic).

- **Connection to Ch4-5:** Smith's earlier arguments about "convergence" (multiple deities' traits collapsing into Yahweh) and "differentiation" (Yahweh separated from other deities) are the conceptual preconditions for the monotheistic rhetoric described here. Monotheistic statements are the LINGUISTIC endpoint of a long process of conceptual convergence.

**Strongest section:** Section 2 (rhetorical purpose) — this is Smith's master argument and the conceptual heart of the book. The claim that monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe" (Seitz) reframes the entire debate.

**Second strongest:** Section 3 (prior context / national theology) — the Deut 32:8-9 + Ps 82 evidence for Yahweh as a second-tier son of El is compelling, and the "tip of the iceberg" metaphor for surviving evidence is methodologically important.

**Weakest section:** Section 5's social-structural argument (family erosion → divine family erodes). It's a Durkheimian correlation without demonstrated causation. Smith himself calls it a "working hypothesis" and a "corollary," not a demonstrated cause.

**The book's central chapter — why this chapter matters:**
Ch8 is where Smith delivers the thesis that the rest of the book has been building toward and that Ch9-10 will elaborate. Every prior chapter (divine council, family, convergence, traits, Baal, El-Yahweh fusion) establishes the polytheistic BACKGROUND. This chapter argues that what emerged from that background was NOT a new religion but a new RHETORIC. The argument has three layers:

1. **Descriptive:** Monotheistic statements are rare, late, and concentrated in crisis literature (Second Isaiah)
2. **Functional:** They serve as inner-community discourse reinforcing exclusive Yahwism, not as philosophical claims about the cosmos
3. **Explanatory:** They became intelligible because of changing social structure (weakening families) and historical circumstances (imperial crisis)

The most radical implication: if monotheism is rhetoric serving monolatry, then the standard evolutionary model (paganism → polytheism → henotheism → monotheism as progressively "higher" stages) collapses. Monotheism is not "more advanced" than monolatry — it's monolatry under extreme pressure, speaking in absolute language. This is Smith's deepest challenge to the field.

**Unresolved tension:** If monotheism is "just" rhetoric, why did it STICK? Why did post-exilic Judaism institutionalize it as orthodoxy? Smith gestures toward this (priestly groups, Persian period) but defers it to later chapters. The gap between "rhetoric that serves monolatry" and "belief that there is only one God" needs more examination than this chapter provides. Is there a point where the rhetoric BECOMES the belief? Smith's model implies the answer is no — monotheism always remains a way of TALKING about an exclusive relationship. But the later tradition (Philo, rabbinic Judaism, Christianity, Islam) clearly took it as ontology. When and how did the rhetoric harden into doctrine? That's a question for beyond this chapter — and possibly beyond this book.
