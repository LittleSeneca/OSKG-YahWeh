# Smith — Ch8: The Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric in Ancient Judah

This is the central chapter of the entire book — and its most radical thesis. Smith argues that Israelite monotheism was not an ontological discovery, not a philosophical position, and not a "new stage of religion." It was a *rhetoric of exclusivity* that emerged in the late monarchy and exile to reinforce Israel's exclusive relationship with Yahweh. Monotheistic statements (like "there is no god besides me" in Deutero-Isaiah) function as inner-community discourse — they use the language of Yahweh's exceptional divine status to absolutize his claim on Israel and express ultimate fidelity. Monotheism serves monolatry; it does not supersede it.

The chapter has five sections: (1) defining monotheism, (2) the relative infrequency and rhetorical purpose of monotheistic statements, (3) the prior context of Judean discourse about divinity, (4) the national god and royal ideology, and (5) the social and historical context of monotheism.

## Claim 1: Monotheistic statements are exilic or later
→ [[claim-monotheistic-statements-exilic-late-monarchy]] | **smith-obm-8.1** | Confidence: very-high
  "Most of the references to monotheism derive from the exilic period or later." The Albright/Mosaic monotheism model is untenable — the First Commandment is monolatrous, not monotheistic. Even if some passages date to the late monarchy, the concentration in Second Isaiah (ca. 540 BCE) is overwhelming.
  Supports: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-neo-assyrian-crisis-drove-theological-shift]]. Contradicts: Albright's Mosaic monotheism.

---

## Claim 2: True monotheism requires denying other gods' EXISTENCE
→ [[claim-true-monotheism-denies-other-gods-existence]] | **smith-obm-8.2** | Confidence: high
  The Shema (Deut 6:4) and First Commandment are monolatrous, not monotheistic — they prohibit worship, not deny existence. True monotheistic statements use exclusivity formulas ('alone,' 'besides') and denial-of-reality language ('nothings,' 'dead'). The pun on 'ĕlōhîm/'ĕlîlîm in Ps 96:5 shows monotheism operating through wordplay, not philosophical argument.
  Supports: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-monotheistic-statements-exilic-late-monarchy]].

---

## Claim 3: Monotheism is RHETORIC, not a new stage of religion
→ [[claim-monotheism-rhetoric-inner-community-discourse]] | **smith-obm-8.3** | Confidence: high
  This is the chapter's core thesis: "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms." Monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe" (Seitz) — a minority discourse persuading a polytheistic majority. The evolutionary model (polytheism → henotheism → monotheism) is wrong.
  Depends on: [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]] (intro.9 preview). Supports: [[claim-monotheistic-convergence-valued-divine-traits]]. Contradicts: evolutionary models, Axial Age theories.

## Claim 4: The prior context was "national theology" — Yahweh as son of Elyon
→ [[claim-yahweh-second-tier-son-of-elyon-national-theology]] | **smith-obm-8.4** | Confidence: high
  "In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god but one of his sons." Psalm 82 and the LXX/DSS version of Deut 32:8-9 preserve a theology where Elyon/El presides and Yahweh is one of ~70 divine sons, each assigned a nation. The MT "corrected" this. The tripartite development: (1) El high, Yahweh patron → (2) Yahweh absorbs El → (3) monotheistic rhetoric denies other gods exist.
  Depends on: [[claim-deut-32-8-9-sons-of-el]], [[claim-yahweh-originally-tier-two-el-presided]], [[claim-three-stage-convergence-el-yahweh]], [[claim-divine-council-seventy-sons-of-el]]. Supports: [[claim-yahweh-el-originally-distinct-deities]].

## Claim 5: Royal ideology was the matrix for monotheism
→ [[claim-royal-ideology-matrix-for-monotheism]] | **smith-obm-8.5** | Confidence: medium-high
  Three types of royal theology provided the conceptual toolkit: (a) action parallel between Yahweh and king, (b) the king receiving Baal's conflict-myth power (Ps 89:26; Mari parallel), (c) possibly the king as 'ĕlōhîm (Ps 45:7; Isa 9:5). When the monarchy collapsed, the cosmic exaltation remained but the earthly parallel vanished — creating conditions for monotheism. The hērem-warfare tradition connects to Ugaritic Anat (CAT 1.13).
  Depends on: [[claim-yahweh-absorbed-baal-storm-warrior-imagery]], [[claim-monarchy-constructive-role-monotheism-development]], [[claim-anat-martial-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]]. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-rhetoric-inner-community-discourse]].

## Claim 6: Lineage erosion made the divine family unintelligible
→ [[claim-lineage-erosion-made-divine-family-unintelligible]] | **smith-obm-8.6** | Confidence: low-medium
  "A culture with a diminished lineage system... might be more predisposed both to hold to individual human accountability for behavior and to see an individual deity accountable for the cosmos." The divine family metaphor eroded as Israel's traditional family patrimonies weakened (royal power, land consolidation, warfare). Smith calls this a "working hypothesis."
  Depends on: [[claim-lineage-erosion-drove-monotheism-individual-accountability]], [[claim-family-model-polytheism-conceptual-unity]], [[claim-family-model-better-than-bureaucratic-pantheon]].

---

## Claim 7: Imperial crisis demanded a super-national god
→ [[claim-imperial-crisis-demanded-super-national-god]] | **smith-obm-8.7** | Confidence: high
  "As Judah's situation on the mundane level deteriorated in history, the cosmic status of its deity soared in its literature." The Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian empires made the national-god model obsolete. Mesopotamian precedents (Assur, Marduk as sum-total of all gods) show the pattern wasn't unique. Persian/Zoroastrian influence "reinforced but was not progenitor" — the rhetoric developed from older Yahwistic language.
  Depends on: [[claim-neo-assyrian-crisis-drove-theological-shift]], [[claim-yahweh-second-tier-son-of-elyon-national-theology]], [[claim-royal-ideology-matrix-for-monotheism]]. Supports: [[claim-monotheism-rhetoric-inner-community-discourse]], [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]], [[claim-four-stage-model-monotheism-development]]. Contradicts: Persian/Zoroastrian primacy models.

## Cross-Reference Notes

- **Tigay's onomastic data:** Tigay showed that personal names from the late monarchy are overwhelmingly Yahwistic — suggesting practical monolatry was widespread. Smith's argument here is compatible but shifts focus: even if MOST Judeans were functionally monolatrous, monotheistic RHETORIC was a minority discourse trying to push monolatry into an absolute key. Tigay provides the demographic baseline; Smith provides the rhetorical superstructure.

- **Römer on the "invention" of monotheism:** Thomas Römer's *The Invention of God* (2015) develops a similar argument about the Persian period as the crucible for monotheism. Smith's 2001 position is earlier and more focused on the late monarchy/exile. The key difference: Smith sees the transition happening in the late 7th-6th centuries (late monarchy + exile), while Römer extends it into the Persian period. Smith's critique of Persian primacy (section 5) anticipates this debate. The two positions can be partially reconciled: Smith is right that the RHETORIC emerges in the exile; Römer may be right that the INSTITUTIONALIZATION of monotheism as orthodoxy is Persian-period.

- **Connection to Ch9:** Smith explicitly transitions: "The expression of monotheism also extended to monotheistic descriptions and narratives; these are explored in the next chapter." Ch8 covers monotheistic DECLARATIONS; Ch9 covers monotheistic THEOLOGIES (priestly creation, Wisdom, apocalyptic).

- **Connection to Ch4-5:** Smith's earlier arguments about "convergence" (multiple deities' traits collapsing into Yahweh) and "differentiation" (Yahweh separated from other deities) are the conceptual preconditions for the monotheistic rhetoric described here. Monotheistic statements are the LINGUISTIC endpoint of a long process of conceptual convergence.

**Strongest section:** Section 2 (rhetorical purpose) — this is Smith's master argument and the conceptual heart of the book. The claim that monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe" (Seitz) reframes the entire debate.

**Second strongest:** Section 3 (prior context / national theology) — the Deut 32:8-9 + Ps 82 evidence for Yahweh as a second-tier son of El is compelling, and the "tip of the iceberg" metaphor for surviving evidence is methodologically important.

**Weakest section:** Section 5's social-structural argument (family erosion → divine family erodes). It's a Durkheimian correlation without demonstrated causation. Smith himself calls it a "working hypothesis" and a "corollary," not a demonstrated cause.

**The book's central chapter — why this chapter matters:**
Ch8 is where Smith delivers the thesis that the rest of the book has been building toward and that Ch9-10 will elaborate. Every prior chapter (divine council, family, convergence, traits, Baal, El-Yahweh fusion) establishes the polytheistic BACKGROUND. This chapter argues that what emerged from that background was NOT a new religion but a new RHETORIC. The argument has three layers:

1. **Descriptive:** Monotheistic statements are rare, late, and concentrated in crisis literature (Second Isaiah)
2. **Functional:** They serve as inner-community discourse reinforcing exclusive Yahwism, not as philosophical claims about the cosmos
3. **Explanatory:** They became intelligible because of changing social structure (weakening families) and historical circumstances (imperial crisis)

The most radical implication: if monotheism is rhetoric serving monolatry, then the standard evolutionary model (paganism → polytheism → henotheism → monotheism as progressively "higher" stages) collapses. Monotheism is not "more advanced" than monolatry — it's monolatry under extreme pressure, speaking in absolute language. This is Smith's deepest challenge to the field.

**Unresolved tension:** If monotheism is "just" rhetoric, why did it STICK? Why did post-exilic Judaism institutionalize it as orthodoxy? Smith gestures toward this (priestly groups, Persian period) but defers it to later chapters. The gap between "rhetoric that serves monolatry" and "belief that there is only one God" needs more examination than this chapter provides. Is there a point where the rhetoric BECOMES the belief? Smith's model implies the answer is no — monotheism always remains a way of TALKING about an exclusive relationship. But the later tradition (Philo, rabbinic Judaism, Christianity, Islam) clearly took it as ontology. When and how did the rhetoric harden into doctrine? That's a question for beyond this chapter — and possibly beyond this book.
