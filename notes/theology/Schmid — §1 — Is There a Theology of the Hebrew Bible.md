# Schmid — §1: Is There a Theology of the Hebrew Bible?

Schmid's introduction grounds his entire project: theology of the Hebrew Bible must be historical-descriptive, not normative-kerygmatic. He argues that the Hebrew Bible's character as "updating literature" (Fortschreibung) and its internal systemizations justify theological inquiry, while its nature as elite scribal literature rules out any claim that it reflects "lived religion."

## Claim 4: Normative "theology of the OT" is a historically recent and theologically suspect development
→ [[claim-normative-ot-theology-recent-suspect-development]] | **schmid-ht-1.4** | Confidence: medium-high
  Normative OT theology's roots lie in the Enlightenment and neo-orthodoxy. Canonicity originates in liturgical use, not normative content declaration — freeing Schmid to do critical theology without defending normative claims. Supports: [[claim-historical-theology-descriptive-reconstructive-not-normative]].

## Claim 5: The Hebrew Bible is elite scribal literature — not a window into folk religion
→ [[claim-hebrew-bible-elite-scribal-literature-not-folk-religion]] | **schmid-ht-1.5** | Confidence: high
  Schmid's strongest empirical claim. The HB was produced by a small circle of literate elites; it does NOT document what ordinary Israelites believed. Devastating for using biblical texts as evidence for pre-exilic polytheism. Supports: [[claim-biblical-texts-late-elitist-theologically-biased]], [[claim-bible-ideal-religion-never-existed-israelites]]. Contradicts: [[claim-dever-folk-polytheism-outlasted-monarchy-monotheism-book-religion]].

## Claim 6: The Persian period is MORE important for literary formation — not less
→ [[claim-persian-period-more-important-literary-formation]] | **schmid-ht-1.6** | Confidence: medium-high

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Theology necessary, not just history of religion | HIGH | If canonical shape is accidental rather than intentional |
| Plato analogy for HB unity | MEDIUM | Obvious disanalogy: multiple authors vs. one |
| Historical and theological exegesis need each other | HIGH | The dichotomy may be functional, not dysfunctional |
| Normative theology is recent and suspect | MEDIUM-HIGH | Is itself a normative theological claim |
| HB as elite scribal literature | HIGH | Counter-examples of folk religion preserved in texts |
| Persian period more important than preexilic | MEDIUM-HIGH | Requires positive evidence, not just refutation of counter |
| "Neither Christian nor Jewish" | LOW-MEDIUM | Ideological critique of "neutrality" claims |

**Strongest section:** §1.2 on the HB as elite scribal literature. This is empirically grounded, clearly argued, and directly relevant to the Truth project's core questions. Schmid's counter to the "absence of Persian-period Hebrew inscriptions" argument is particularly sharp.

**Weakest section:** The Plato analogy and the "neither Christian nor Jewish" framing. Both are rhetorical moves that overclaim what the evidence supports. The Plato analogy flatters Schmid's project (treating the HB as a single-author corpus) and the neutrality claim obscures his Protestant location.

**Cross-reference implications for the Truth project:** Schmid's elite-literature thesis is the single biggest challenge to using biblical texts as evidence for pre-exilic polytheism. If the texts are Persian-period creations by a small Jerusalem scribal elite, they tell us what those scribes THOUGHT about Israel's past — not what that past actually WAS. This makes Dever's archaeological evidence MORE important, not less (contra what one might expect from a "late dater"). The texts become secondary; the artifacts become primary. Schmid is the scholar who forces this conclusion most forcefully. See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]] for the full implications of the Schmid challenge to the American consensus.
