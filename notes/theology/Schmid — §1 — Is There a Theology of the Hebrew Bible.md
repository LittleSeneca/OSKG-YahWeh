# Schmid — §1: Is There a Theology of the Hebrew Bible?

Schmid's introduction grounds his entire project: theology of the Hebrew Bible must be historical-descriptive, not normative-kerygmatic. He argues that the Hebrew Bible's character as "updating literature" (Fortschreibung) and its internal systemizations justify theological inquiry, while its nature as elite scribal literature rules out any claim that it reflects "lived religion."

---

## Claim 1: Traditional OT theology is impossible — but so is replacing it with mere "religious history"

**Schmid's claim:** The traditional project of OT theology "arose as compensation for the loss of the unity between Scripture and Christian doctrine. Today, such an approach has become impossible and inadvisable. The texts of the Hebrew Bible do not allow for a particular 'biblical' synthesis with regard to doctrine; they are too divergent both historically and conceptually." But "the premature calls...for both the dismissal of the discipline of 'Old Testament theology' as well as the suggestion to replace it with the 'religious history of ancient Israel' prove inadequate."

**Evidence presented:**

- The texts are historically and conceptually divergent — no single "biblical" synthesis is possible
- TWO reasons why theology remains necessary:
  1. The canon itself contains "different systemizations and organizations of material, which partially overlap and partially compete with one another. These organizations and systemizations put the theological designs of the biblical authors and redactors on display."
  2. The literary nature of the HB as Fortschreibung (updating) literature — "texts and the books of the Hebrew Bible only reached their current forms over a period of time through inner-biblical commentary and expansion" — constitutes "a unique body of literature that genuinely and prominently includes elements of theological reflection"
- Biblical texts themselves acknowledge being updating literature (Jer 36:32) and theologically reflective literature (Deut 1:5)

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is Schmid's core methodological insight and it's well-grounded. The two observations — canon as organized theological statement, and Fortschreibung as inherently theological — are empirical. They don't depend on any particular dating scheme.

**What's at stake:** If Schmid is right that theology is necessary because the canon IS a theological construct, then purely historical or archaeological approaches are insufficient. Conversely, if Schmid overstates the theological intentionality of the canonical shape, his whole project overreaches. This claim is the justification for the entire book.

**Who disagrees:** The "religious history" camp (broadly the history-of-religions school) would say that describing Israelite religion historically IS sufficient. Schmid's response: that approach misses what makes the HB unique — its character as deliberately constructed updating literature with theological intent.

**Alternative reading:** One could accept Schmid's two observations but argue they justify literary/redactional history, not theology. The "theological designs" of redactors are still historical data — "theology" might claim more than the evidence warrants.

**My assessment:** This is the strongest foundation for a critical theology I've seen. Schmid doesn't claim the texts reveal God — he claims they reveal theological thinking by ancient scribes. That's falsifiable, historical, and genuinely interesting. It's also the move that lets him escape the Barthian trap (theology must be normative) AND the historicist trap (theology is just religion).

## Claim 2: The Hebrew Bible is comparable to Plato's works — internally diverse but investigable as a coherent thought-world

**Schmid's claim:** "A theology of the Hebrew Bible is as important and necessary as is an account of Plato's philosophy. Plato's work also displays diversity because it was written at different times and addresses various topics. However, while continuing to respect its internal diversity, one can investigate the trajectories, developments, and types of logic involved in Plato's philosophy."

**Evidence presented:**

- Like Plato, the HB can be investigated without subsuming it into reception history, though the reception dimension is present
- "One can investigate the diverse (but not endlessly diverse) theology of the Hebrew Bible"
- The HB "even demonstrates overarching structural elements in its literary presentation, which indicate a certain degree of textual unity" (unlike Plato, Schmid claims)
- The texts speak of God "in manifold ways," which justifies calling it "theology"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Plato analogy is rhetorically effective but question-begging. Plato was one author with a developing thought; the HB is dozens of authors over centuries. The "overarching structural elements" claim is doing a lot of work — Schmid will need to demonstrate these in Parts E-H.

**What's at stake:** The Plato analogy is Schmid's warrant for treating the HB as having a THEOLOGY (singular) rather than just theologies (plural). If the analogy fails, the book should be titled "Theologies of the Hebrew Bible" — which Schmid admits would be "in principle" defensible.

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who emphasizes the HB's polyphony. Schmid himself acknowledges that the plural title "would provide a different emphasis" and "the unifying literary elements would then receive less value."

**Alternative reading:** The HB is more like a library than a single author's corpus. Treating it as a "thought-world" risks imposing unity where there is only proximity.

**My assessment:** The Plato analogy is the weakest structural move in the introduction. It's doing too much work with too little justification. Schmid's better argument is his empirical one: the texts ARE interconnected through Fortschreibung, and those interconnections display theological logic. That's a stronger basis than a dubious analogy with a single-author philosophical corpus.

## Claim 3: Historical and theological exegesis are mutually dependent — separating them produces bad history AND bad theology

**Schmid's claim:** "Historical-critical exegesis renders itself historically implausible when it devalues the theological dimension of the biblical texts; theological interpretation of biblical texts deteriorates into Docetism when it prioritizes the theological 'y-coefficient' before the historical 'x-coefficient' of textual interpretation."

**Evidence presented:**

- The two approaches "exist strangely separate from one another" in current scholarship
- Historical scholars "view [theological approaches] as subjective, confessionally determined, and unscholarly"
- Theological interpreters "show little concern for the results of historical exegesis, which are viewed as arbitrary, artificial, and irrelevant"
- "The charges of arbitrariness raised by both sides have some basis in reality"

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is a diagnosis of the field's dysfunction, not an empirical claim. The mutual suspicion between historical and theological approaches is well-documented.

**What's at stake:** Schmid's project aims to heal this divide by doing theology that is rigorously historical AND history that takes theological content seriously. If he succeeds, he's solved a structural problem in the discipline. If he fails, he's produced work that satisfies neither camp.

**Who disagrees:** Hard historicists would say theological content is just more historical data — it doesn't require a separate "theological" approach. Hard theologians would say Schmid's historical approach is just history of religion dressed as theology.

**Alternative reading:** The separation is functional and appropriate. Historical and theological inquiry serve different purposes and communities. Trying to unite them produces confused scholarship that serves neither purpose well.

**My assessment:** Schmid's diagnosis is correct, and his proposed solution — letting historical conclusions drive theological description — is the right direction. The acid test is whether his "theology" produces insights that purely historical inquiry wouldn't. If Schmid's theology just redescribes the history in theological vocabulary, he's failed. If it illuminates patterns, logics, and meanings that historical description misses, he's succeeded.

## Claim 4: Normative "theology of the OT" is a historically recent and theologically suspect development

**Schmid's claim:** "In terms of the history of scholarship, the notion of 'Old Testament theology' as an explicitly normative discipline is comparatively new. Its roots lie in the Enlightenment, and it first gained significant ground...under the influence of neo-orthodox theology at the beginning of the twentieth century."

**Evidence presented:**

- "Premature demands for normativity with regard to the interpretation of the Bible should...be met with healthy skepticism, especially in Protestant theology"
- "Protestantism reads the Bible as gospel and not as demand. Reading the Bible as gospel means...letting its content and declarations address the audience and, when appropriate, win them over, even if this may not be accorded canonical authority"
- "It would be theologically (and also historically) incorrect to base its ability to persuade on its canonicity, rather than on the nature of its content"
- Canonicity originates in liturgical use — "the prestige of the canonical books refers to their recognition as writings worthy of liturgical use and not as specific content that is now declared normative"

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The historical claim (normative OT theology is recent) is well-supported by the history of scholarship Schmid details in Part B. The theological claim (that normativity arises from content, not canonicity) is a Protestant theological judgment, not a historical finding.

**What's at stake:** This is Schmid's liberation move. If canonicity is a reception-history phenomenon, not an intrinsic property of the texts, then a historical-descriptive theology is legitimate. The texts' authority rests on their persuasive power, not on a declaration. This frees Schmid to do critical theology without needing to defend normative claims.

**Who disagrees:** Catholic and Orthodox traditions that locate authority in the Church's declaration of canonicity. Conservative Protestants who hold to verbal inspiration. Barthians who locate authority in the Word's self-attestation. All would reject Schmid's functional theory of canonicity.

**Alternative reading:** Schmid's functional theory of canonicity is itself a normative theological claim — it's a Protestant (specifically liberal Protestant) theology of Scripture. Calling it "historical" obscures its confessional character.

**My assessment:** This is elegant theology — but it IS theology, not just historical description. Schmid is making a normative claim about how canonicity works while claiming not to do normative theology. He's right that his approach is more historically defensible than inspiration theories, but he should be more honest that he's doing Protestant theology here, not neutral description.

## Claim 5: The Hebrew Bible is elite scribal literature — not a window into folk religion

**Schmid's claim:** "The Hebrew Bible does not provide an unmediated window into the lived experience of folk religion, as many scholars supposed in the heyday of form-critical studies. The Hebrew Bible...bears witness to a literary religion, which, as such, was never lived, but instead gave rise to an extraordinarily strong reception history." (Citing Stephen Geller, *Sacred Enigmas*, 1996.)

**Evidence presented:**

- "The texts of the Hebrew Bible were produced and read within the comparably small circle of individuals who were able to read and write quite proficiently within a widely illiterate society"
- Comparative evidence from Greece and Egypt supports this
- Writing in Israel only became pronounced in the 9th c. BCE, in Judah in the 8th c. BCE (Finkelstein and Sass 2017)
- The HB's character as "agglutinative (growing by supplementation) interpretive literature" requires that books existed as single copies — "It is hardly imaginable that the process of literary updating, consisting of multiple steps...could have taken place if numerous copies of the books were in circulation"
- Deut 17:18 assumes the king's copy is the sole additional copy
- 2 Macc 2:15 shows "not even the Jewish community of Alexandria possessed a complete Bible in the second century BCE"
- The Jerusalem temple likely housed a model exemplar used as basis for new augmented copies
- "The copying of an Isaiah scroll in the rabbinic period equaled about six months' income for a scribe"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The elite-literature thesis is now mainstream in Hebrew Bible scholarship (van der Toorn, Carr, Schniedewind). The material evidence (inscription distribution, production costs, single-copy model) is strong.

**What's at stake:** This claim is DEVASTATING for the standard evolutionary model of Israelite religion. If the HB is elite scribal literature from Jerusalem (Persian period onward), it does NOT document what ordinary Israelites believed. It documents what a small group of Jerusalem scribes WANTED TO BELIEVE about Israel's past. This is the opening Schmid needs for his late-dating argument — and it's a claim that the American consensus (Smith, Dever) has not fully absorbed.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that form criticism can recover oral traditions and folk religion behind the texts. Scholars who argue (contra Schmid) that the texts preserve genuine pre-exilic material (Cross, Freedman, early poetry school). Scholars who think the "elite literature" claim overstates the literacy gap.

**Alternative reading:** Even elite literature can preserve genuine traditions. Scribes don't invent from nothing — they work with inherited material. The question is HOW MUCH filtering has occurred, not WHETHER filtering has occurred.

**My assessment:** This is Schmid's strongest empirical claim and the one with the most direct relevance to the Truth project. If the HB is elite literature from the Persian period, then Smith's use of Genesis-Judges as evidence for pre-exilic polytheism is methodologically indefensible. The texts may describe polytheism — but they're descriptions by monotheists of what they IMAGINE their polytheistic past looked like. That's a radically different kind of evidence.

## Claim 6: The Persian period is MORE important for literary formation — not less

**Schmid's claim:** Some American and Israeli scholars argue that "due to the absence of Hebrew inscriptions from the Persian period one should primarily place the formation of the Hebrew Bible in the preexilic period." Schmid counters: this "goes against historical probability. Persian period inscriptions were written in the lingua franca of the time, Aramaic. Furthermore, their number is significantly larger than the number of Hebrew inscriptions. In principle, the number of Persian period inscriptions, if anything, confirms the importance of the Persian period for the formation of the literature of the Hebrew Bible rather than constituting an argument against it."

**Evidence presented:**

- Persian period inscriptions exist — they're just in Aramaic, not Hebrew
- Hebrew was a literary/religious language in the Persian period, not an administrative one
- The absence of Hebrew inscriptions in the Persian period is exactly what you'd expect if Hebrew had become a scribal language used for literary production, not administration
- The argument from absence of Hebrew inscriptions is therefore invalid

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Schmid's counter-argument is logical and lexically sound. But the claim that Persian period literary formation is "more important" requires additional evidence — it can't rest solely on refuting the counter-argument. Schmid's fuller case would need to be built through the rest of the book (especially §11, §18, §§21-28).

**What's at stake:** This is the chronological foundation for Schmid's entire project. If the Persian period is the primary period of literary formation, then the HB's theological content is Persian-period theology, not pre-exilic religion. The entire evolutionary model (polytheism → monolatry → monotheism) might describe the PERSIAN PERIOD's theological imagination, not pre-exilic religious reality.

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date substantial portions of the HB to the monarchy (Cross, Freedman, many American scholars). The "early poetry" school that dates Exod 15, Judg 5, Deut 33, Hab 3 to the pre-monarchic or early monarchic period.

**Alternative reading:** The Persian period is important for the FINAL FORM of the texts, but the texts preserve earlier traditions. Schmid's claim that Persian-period scribes "constructed" Israel's past rather than "preserved" it is a judgment about scribal practice, not a necessary inference from dating.

**My assessment:** This is where Schmid is most controversial — and most important for the Truth project. If he's right, the entire consensus edifice is built on sand. But "right" here has degrees: even if substantial material IS Persian-period, the question is whether ANY pre-exilic kernel survives. Schmid tends toward maximal skepticism; Römer, Smith, and Fleming each find more pre-exilic material than Schmid does. The truth probably lies somewhere between Schmid's maximalism and the American school's optimism.

## Claim 7: The Hebrew Bible's theology is "neither a Christian nor a Jewish" undertaking — but stands in relation to both

**Schmid's claim:** "The theology of the Hebrew Bible and, at the same time, the promotion of the theology of the Hebrew Bible as historical description means that the results of such a project can neither be a Christian theology of the Old Testament, nor — with qualifications — a Jewish theology of the Hebrew Bible." However, "there is some difference in content between what a historically descriptive approach...will reconstruct...and what is to be expected in Christian and Jewish tradition. However, it will not be completely alien, as these traditions have developed from, among other sources, the Hebrew Bible."

**Evidence presented:**

- "The Hebrew Bible is older than Christianity, and this is also the case for the relationship of the Hebrew Bible to Judaism with regard to the preexilic texts"
- Both traditions developed FROM the HB, so historical description should not be entirely foreign to either
- The differences and proximities will be addressed in Part I at the book's end

**Confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM. This is the same claim from the Preface, slightly elaborated. The "older than" argument is historically true but hermeneutically naive — it assumes that "older" means "independent of," when in fact the HB AS A CANON is a product of both Jewish and Christian reception decisions.

**What's at stake:** Same as in the Preface. Schmid wants to claim a neutral academic space. Whether that space exists is debatable.

**Who disagrees:** See Preface note. Levenson, Sommer, and others who argue "Hebrew Bible" is a Christian construct.

**My assessment:** Repeating my assessment from the Preface: this is Schmid's most vulnerable claim. He's doing Protestant historical-critical scholarship and calling it neutral. That doesn't invalidate his results — but pretending he's neither Christian nor Jewish is less honest than admitting his location and arguing for its methodological advantages.

## Chapter 1 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Theology necessary, not just history of religion | HIGH | If canonical shape is accidental rather than intentional |
| Plato analogy for HB unity | MEDIUM | Obvious disanalogy: multiple authors vs. one |
| Historical and theological exegesis need each other | HIGH | The dichotomy may be functional, not dysfunctional |
| Normative theology is recent and suspect | MEDIUM-HIGH | Is itself a normative theological claim |
| HB as elite scribal literature | HIGH | Counter-examples of folk religion preserved in texts |
| Persian period more important than preexilic | MEDIUM-HIGH | Requires positive evidence, not just refutation of counter |
| "Neither Christian nor Jewish" | LOW-MEDIUM | Ideological critique of "neutrality" claims |

**Strongest section:** §1.2 on the HB as elite scribal literature. This is empirically grounded, clearly argued, and directly relevant to the Truth project's core questions. Schmid's counter to the "absence of Persian-period Hebrew inscriptions" argument is particularly sharp.

**Weakest section:** The Plato analogy and the "neither Christian nor Jewish" framing. Both are rhetorical moves that overclaim what the evidence supports. The Plato analogy flatters Schmid's project (treating the HB as a single-author corpus) and the neutrality claim obscures his Protestant location.

**Cross-reference implications for the Truth project:** Schmid's elite-literature thesis is the single biggest challenge to using biblical texts as evidence for pre-exilic polytheism. If the texts are Persian-period creations by a small Jerusalem scribal elite, they tell us what those scribes THOUGHT about Israel's past — not what that past actually WAS. This makes Dever's archaeological evidence MORE important, not less (contra what one might expect from a "late dater"). The texts become secondary; the artifacts become primary. Schmid is the scholar who forces this conclusion most forcefully. See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]] for the full implications of the Schmid challenge to the American consensus.
