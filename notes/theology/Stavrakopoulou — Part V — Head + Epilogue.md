# Stavrakopoulou — Part V: Head + Epilogue (Chapters 16-21)

---

## Chapter 16: Face to Face

### Claim: God's face could be seen — sometimes fatally, sometimes safely

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** "God's face is the most sought-after and the most dangerous part of his body." The tension in the Hebrew Bible is not between "God has a face" and "God doesn't have a face," but between "you can see it" and "seeing it will kill you."

**Evidence:**
- Moses sees God "face to face" (Exodus 33:11; Numbers 12:8; Deuteronomy 34:10)
- Isaiah 6:1-5: Isaiah sees God's face and expects to die — purged by the coal instead
- Jacob: "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life is preserved" (Genesis 32:30)
- The Aaronic blessing: "Yahweh make his face shine upon you" (Numbers 6:25)
- "Seek my face" (*baqqĕšû pānay*) is a liturgical call to worship (Psalm 27:8)

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. The face texts are among the most explicit in the Bible.

## Chapters 17-19: Headstrong Beauty, Profile, Sense and Sensitivity

### Claim: God's head had hair, a beard, eyes, ears, nose, and mouth — all functioning sense organs

**Stavrakopoulou's comprehensive catalogue:**

- **Hair:** God's hair is white as wool (Daniel 7:9) — the Ancient of Days with snowy hair
- **Beard:** Implied by the Ancient of Days imagery and ANE divine iconography
- **Eyes:** God's eyes "roam throughout the earth" (2 Chronicles 16:9). He sees everything because he HAS eyes
- **Ears:** "Incline your ear, Yahweh, and hear" (2 Kings 19:16). Prayers are directed at divine ears
- **Nose:** God's "nostrils" (*'ap*) flare in anger. "Smoke went up from his nostrils" (2 Samuel 22:9). The word for anger (*'ap*) literally means "nose"
- **Mouth:** God's mouth speaks, eats, drinks — "a sword came out of his mouth" (Revelation 1:16)

**The sensory God:** "God is not an abstract mind thinking pure thoughts. He is a sensory being who sees, hears, smells, tastes, and touches. His senses are more powerful than human senses — he can see across the earth, hear prayers from any distance — but they are not categorically different from human senses. He perceives the world the way humans do: through sense organs."

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH on the sense organ catalogue. Every body part is textually attested.

## Chapter 20: Gasp and Gulp

### Claim: God breathed, ate, drank, and spoke — his mouth was fully functional

**Stavrakopoulou's claim:** God's mouth was not just for speaking commands. He BREATHED into Adam. He ATE meals with Abraham. He DRANK wine (implied by the sacrificial libations). His mouth was a functioning biological organ.

**Evidence:**
- Genesis 2:7: "Yahweh God formed man of dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life"
- Genesis 18:8: Abraham serves the three visitors (one of whom is Yahweh) "curds and milk and the calf" — and they EAT
- Psalm 50:12-13: "If I were hungry, I would not tell you... Do I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?" — the question implies other deities DO expect to be fed this way; Yahweh's transcendence of this is a LATER development

**Confidence:** HIGH on breathing and eating. MEDIUM on drinking (implied but less explicit).

## Epilogue: Chapter 21 — An Autopsy

### Claim: God's body was killed — by theology

**Stavrakopoulou's final claim:** "The God of the Bible was an embodied deity. He walked, ate, fought, and loved. He had a body you could see, hear, and touch. But over centuries, Jewish and Christian intellectuals, shaped by Greek philosophy, killed that body. They replaced the living, breathing God of the Bible with an immaterial abstraction."

**The autopsy metaphor:** The book has been performing an anatomical dissection of God's body, part by part. The epilogue is the autopsy report: cause of death = philosophical abstraction. Time of death = gradual, over the first millennium CE. The body was never recovered — it was explained away.

**Stavrakopoulou's closing argument:** "The real God of the Bible is a stranger to modern Judaism and Christianity. Worshippers have been praying to a ghost."

**Confidence:** HIGH on the historical development (God was historicized into abstraction). MEDIUM on the moral valence — whether the corporeal God is "better" or "more authentic" than the abstract one is a value judgment.

## Stavrakopoulou: Book-Level Assessment

| Strength | Weakness |
|----------|----------|
| **Overwhelming textual evidence** — the body-part catalogue is irrefutable | **Rhetorical overreach** — occasionally claims more than the evidence supports (God defecating) |
| **Material culture integration** — 'Ain Dara, standing stones, seals | **Tone** — deliberately provocative; may alienate readers who would otherwise be persuaded |
| **Comparative ANE grounding** — situates Israelite embodiment in wider context | **Single-body model** — doesn't engage Sommer's more nuanced fluidity model |
| **Readable, engaging, accessible** — written for a general audience | **Atheist polemic** — her conclusion that God was "killed" is more editorial than analytical |

**Stavrakopoulou's contribution to the project:** She provides the most visceral, sensory, material account of divine embodiment. Where Sommer provides the philosophical framework (fluidity vs. non-fluidity), Stavrakopoulou provides the body. She doesn't argue about whether God had multiple bodies or one. She argues that God HAD a body — and that recovering that body requires reading the Bible without the filters of later theology. Her book is the strongest possible case for divine corporeality. Whether you accept her conclusion that the embodied God was later "killed" is a separate question from whether she's right about the texts. On the texts, she's overwhelmingly right.
