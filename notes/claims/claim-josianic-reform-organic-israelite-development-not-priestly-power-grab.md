# kaufmann-ri-8.9: Josianic reform was an organic religious development, not a priestly power grab

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"The Deuteronomic idea must perforce be regarded as one of the intuitive symbols through which Israelite religion sought expression. It is not a means to some didactic or material end, but an end in itself." Centralization "was no guarantor of a pure cult" — pagan influence radiated from the royal centers, not the rural altars. Nor did it serve priestly interests — rural priests lost their livelihoods, and Jerusalem priests had their monopoly challenged. The reform "aimed at concentrating all cultic activity in the one chosen temple" based on "the idea of divine election" — Israelite sanctity "is a creation of the will of God; it originates always in a historical election, a revelation of God's word."

## Evidence

- Earlier reforms (Asa, Jehoshaphat, Jehu, Jehoiada) targeted paganism and "idolatry" — Josiah's reform went further, centralizing even legitimate YHWH worship
- "Nothing in the doctrines of literary prophecy implied centralization of the cult, nor is it a logical inference from the monotheistic idea"
- "Centralization was, therefore, no guarantor of a pure cult" — Manasseh "converted the Solomonic temple itself into a heathen pantheon"
- Rural priests "were, of course, hit hardest" by centralization. The Deuteronomic law "assured them the right to serve at the central sanctuary (Deut. 18:6 f.), but in fact it was impossible for so many priests to find employment at one temple"
- Jerusalem priests also lost — the law "encroached upon the monopoly of the Jerusalem priests"
- "The immediate and most far reaching result of centralization was to empty the daily religious life of the people at large of all priestly influence"
- The idea traces to the "tent" tradition of the Aaronic priesthood — a portable sanctuary unconnected with any sacred site
- Deuteronomy 12 forbids worship at "every place" — "The ban is not grounded on the fact that these were pagan cult sites... but on the view that this very manner of worship was pagan: 'You shall not do so for YHWH your God' (Deut. 12:4)"
- "The Deuteronomic temple of the future is diametrically opposed to the pagan temple whose sanctity is 'prehistoric,' mythological. It is an eschatological temple"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's critique of the 'priestly power grab' theory is effective, but his alternative — that it was an 'intuitive symbol' of Israelite religion — is vague and under-argued.

## Stakes

Whether the Deuteronomic reform was (a) a pious reform based on religious principle, (b) a Jerusalem priestly power grab, or (c) an Assyrian-influenced political consolidation. Kaufmann argues for (a) while carefully distinguishing it from (b).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholarship has offered several explanations Kaufmann rejects. The "priestly power grab" theory argues centralization concentrated cultic revenue in Jerusalem. The "Assyrian influence" theory sees Deuteronomic centralization as paralleling Assyrian imperial cult policies. Many scholars see the reform as a complex event with multiple motivations — religious, political, and economic — not reducible to any single factor.

**Alternative reading:** The Deuteronomic reform was driven by a coalition of Jerusalem priests, scribes, and court officials who had multiple motivations: genuine religious conviction, political consolidation in the wake of the northern kingdom's fall, and material self-interest. Kaufmann's point that centralization HURT rural priests and limited Jerusalem priestly monopoly is well-taken and complicates simple "power grab" theories.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's critique of the "priestly power grab" theory is effective and valuable. His observation that centralization cost priests their livelihoods and emptied daily life of priestly influence is a genuine contribution that any adequate theory of the reform must account for. However, his positive explanation — that Deuteronomy's centralization was an "intuitive symbol" expressing Israelite ideas of historical election — is less persuasive. It's not wrong, but it doesn't explain WHY this particular "intuitive symbol" emerged in the late 8th-7th centuries specifically. Kaufmann's anti-materialist hermeneutic prevents him from considering the political and social factors that made centralization conceivable at that particular historical moment.
