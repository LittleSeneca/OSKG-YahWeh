# keel-ggi-10.3: There was never a distinctive "Yahweh iconography" — Yahweh was depicted THROUGH the imagery of other deities, not with his own unique visual vocabulary

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch X — Summary and Conclusion]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter X

## The Claim

"We do not believe that there was an actual Yahweh iconography in Israel and Judah. This does not mean that Israelite and Judean women and men would not have recognized an appropriate portrayal of their god in one or another of the pictures presented here."

## Evidence

- Throughout the book, they have identified solar motifs (winged disk, scarab, falcon), celestial motifs (four-winged figures, stars), and royal motifs (cartouche, lion, cherub throne) as the visual vocabulary of Yahwism
- But none of these are UNIQUE to Yahweh — they are borrowed from Egyptian, Phoenician, and Syrian repertoires
- The four-winged Baal figure becomes interchangeable with the four-winged scarab (sun god symbol) — the SAME image could represent either Baal or the sun god, and in Israel either could represent Yahweh
- "Yahweh's 'essence,' described by an assortment of widely varying images that have been brought together, was quite open to expansion"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is the logical conclusion of their entire survey. If Yahweh had a distinctive iconography, they would have found it. They did not.

## Stakes

This is a different way of saying what Smith calls "convergence" and what Romer describes as Yahweh's "absorption" of other gods' traits. The iconographic evidence shows that Yahweh did not REPLACE other gods' imagery — he ABSORBED it. The same solar disk that represented Re in Egypt and Baalshamem in Phoenicia represented Yahweh in Israel. The visual vocabulary was shared; only the name was different.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who think they CAN identify Yahweh-specific iconography — e.g., the "Yahweh and his Asherah" reading of the Kuntillet Ajrud pithos (rejected in Ch VII), or attempts to identify specific seal motifs as Yahwistic.

**Alternative reading:** The lack of distinctive Yahweh iconography could reflect an aniconic tendency rather than absorption — Yahweh was deliberately NOT depicted, and the solar/royal motifs were generic divine symbols, not Yahweh-specific ones. Keel & Uehlinger would partially agree with this — they see the aniconic trend as real — but they would say it coexisted with the absorption of other deities' imagery.

## Assessment

This is one of the book's most important conclusions. It means the question "what did Yahweh look like?" is the wrong question. The right question is "which existing divine images did Yahweh worshippers use to represent THEIR god?" The answer: the sun, the winged protective powers, the royal lion, the blessing tree. Yahweh looked like whatever the "Most High God" looked like in the contemporary Levantine symbol system.
