# smith-obm-3.5: The family model provided polytheism with conceptual unity through structural flexibility

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch3 — The Divine Family]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 3

## The Claim

"The concept of family provides a conceptual unity for Ugaritic divinity. It shows an elasticity that can give expression to a multitude of relations." This is the payoff for the whole chapter: the divine family was "a sort of 'mono-theism'" because it unified diverse deities through kinship.

## Evidence

- Nikkal (Mesopotamian moon-goddess) marries Yarih (CAT 1.24) — a foreign goddess integrated into the Ugaritic pantheon through the family metaphor of marriage. "Implicit in this marriage is an identification of the two as moon-deities."
- Baal integrated as "son of El" despite separate paternity (bn dgn) — the family metaphor extends to include outsiders. He is "adopted up" through his martial achievement (defeat of Yamm).
- "The language of family encompasses both divine insiders and outsiders, both the established family and the strong newcomer."
- Philo of Byblos explicitly uses family narrative to explain divine relations (concubine's son → adoption → achieved status).
- Council language points to "holders of power in council"; family language provides the relational framework.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Smith's textually-grounded observations about family flexibility are strong; the claim that this "provided conceptual unity" is his synthetic interpretation.

## Stakes

THIS is Smith's answer to "why monotheism?" — the very question his book's title promises. Polytheism's family structure was flexible enough to absorb, redefine, and ultimately collapse distinctions between deities. Monotheism wasn't a revolution against polytheism's rigidity; it was an exploitation of polytheism's flexibility. The same mechanism that let Baal join El's family let Yahweh absorb both of them.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see monotheism as a radical break, not a gradual absorption. Those who see the family model as one of several organizing principles, not THE unifying concept.

**Alternative reading:** The family model might have been ONE way of organizing divine relations alongside others (council, cosmic realms, patronage networks). It provided flexibility, yes, but other models might have provided different kinds of coherence. Smith's focus on family might lead him to overstate its role.

## Assessment

This is Smith's central theoretical contribution, and it's genuinely interesting. The argument has a dialectical elegance: polytheism's own internal logic (family flexibility) made monotheism possible. But it's also somewhat teleological — Smith is reading Ugaritic data through the lens of the outcome (Israelite monotheism). The family model works well for the Ugaritic data; whether it EXPLAINS monotheism's emergence is a bigger claim that needs the historical argument of Part III to test.
