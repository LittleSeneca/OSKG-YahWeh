# albertz-hir-3.1-3.2.4: David's wars created a theological vacuum filled by imported ANE sacral kingship

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.1-3.2 — Formation of Monarchy and Legitimation Crisis]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.2

## The Claim

"At the latest after his victory over the Philistines David's wars could no longer... be seen to be in line with the battles for liberation in the period before the state." David's wars lacked everything that characterized earlier conflicts: ongoing emergency, voluntary participation, charismatic solidarity, limited aims. "War became a means of royal power politics... that made it impossible to see him still as bringing about a saving action of Yahweh for Israel." Into this vacuum, court theologians imported "a legitimation of monarchical rule which was new for Yahweh religion, and which ran very much along the lines of the kingship theology of the Near East."

### Kingship theology's core elements:
- **Divine sonship**: The Davidic king was adopted as Yahweh's son (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14) — "a relationship which far transcends the normal relationship between God and Israel"
- **Eternal dynasty**: Nathan's promise (2 Sam 7) guaranteed an eternal Davidic dynasty — "not just the person but the dynasty as an institution was legitimized"
- **King as mediator of blessing**: The king guaranteed shalom (peace/prosperity), fertility, justice, and cultic mediation — "all the essential aspects of the relationship between the wider group and God... run through the king and find their unity in his person"
- **Royal cultic functions**: The king performed sacrifices, gave blessings, interceded, danced before Yahweh — "a closer fusion of throne and altar" than anywhere else in the ANE

## Evidence

- The royal psalms (Ps 2, 72, 89, 110, 132) preserve the core ideology
- Nathan's oracle (2 Sam 7) as the foundational charter
- Royal cultic activity: David's dance (2 Sam 6:14ff.), Solomon's intercession and temple dedication (1 Kings 8)
- The king as "vehicle of the divine spirit" (1 Sam 16:13; 2 Sam 23:2) and "mediator of revelation" (Prov 16:10)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the existence of ANE-style kingship theology in Israel (the royal psalms are unambiguous). MEDIUM on the claim that this created a "theological vacuum" — some form of royal ideology may have developed more organically.

## Stakes

This is Albertz's sharpest critique of the monarchy: it wasn't just politically problematic, it was theologically corrupt. Yahweh "who had once been the symbol of liberation from state oppression" became "the guarantor of a state power which built up new mechanisms of oppression." The monarchy turned Yahwism into "ideology in the strict sense."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see royal ideology as a legitimate development within Yahwism rather than a foreign import. The royal psalms could represent organic theological creativity rather than ANE borrowing.

**Alternative reading:** Divine sonship and eternal dynasty may have developed from pre-state charismatic leadership patterns (the spirit-endowed judge becomes the spirit-endowed king) rather than being imported wholesale from ANE models.

## Assessment

The existence of ANE-style kingship theology in the royal psalms is unambiguous and important. Whether this represents a "theological vacuum" filled by conscious importation or a more organic development is harder to determine. Albertz's framing as "ideology in the strict sense" is his sharpest normative judgment — and it drives the entire narrative of Part 3.
