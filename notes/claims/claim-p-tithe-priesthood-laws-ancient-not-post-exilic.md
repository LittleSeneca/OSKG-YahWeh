# kaufmann-ri-5.5: P's Tithe and Priesthood Laws Are Ancient

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch V — The Sources]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch V

## The Claim

P's tithe laws "are ancient" and the Levitical tithe of Numbers 18 "was, by the beginning of Second Temple times, an obsolete law, a relic of another age." The priests/Levites distinction is pre-exilic, traceable to tribal origins — not a post-exilic invention.

## Evidence

**Tithes:**
- **Three independent tithe laws** exist (Lev 27:30-33; Num 18:21-32; Deut 14:22-27), not two as harmonizers claim. "There are no grounds for combining and reducing the three tithes to two."
- **The animal tithe of Lev 27 is ancient.** "Tithing animals, far from being a sign of lateness, is a genuine mark of antiquity, being rooted in the early pastoral age." The Second Temple "knew of no priestly tithe of animals."
- **The Levitical tithe (Num 18) was votive, not annual.** Nothing in Num 18:21ff specifies an annual levy. It was "a votive offering which is to be given to the Levites." The annual interpretation is a later midrashic development.
- **Post-exilic reality contradicts P.** If priests composed P in the post-exilic period, "is it credible that the priestly authorities of later times bestowed this most lucrative of all sacred gifts upon lowly temple servants [Levites]?" Thousands of priests were impoverished while the tithe went to a handful of Levites.
- **No post-exilic tithe law was ever added.** Despite the need, "no explicit law providing for an obligatory priestly tithe made its way into the Torah." This proves the Torah was fixed and unalterable by post-exilic times.

**Priests and Levites:**
- **The degradation theory is implausible.** Neither D (Deut 18:6-8) nor II Kings 23 indicates rural priests were demoted. "II Kings 23:9 speaks of a personal penalty that applied only to those who actually served at the high places."
- **Levites existed as a class in the Exile (Ezra 2:40; 8:15ff).** "They cannot, then, have come into being as a result of the centralization of worship, or in accordance with Ezekiel's demand." Their existence "requires not merely a pre-exilic, but a pre-Josianic origin for the class."
- **The vast majority of rural priests were NOT demoted.** The 20,000+ returning priests "could not have stemmed from the family of Zadok; the vast majority of them must have been descendants of rural priests."
- **P presents Levite status as an honor, not a degradation.** Contrast Ezekiel, for whom Levitical status is "a punishment and a disgrace." P portrays Levi's consecration as a reward for loyalty at the golden calf (Exod 32:26-29).
- **Ezekiel was ignored.** His plan to replace Nethinim with Levites "was also disregarded."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's most impressive argument in the chapter. The tithe evidence is genuinely hard to explain on the assumption that P was composed by post-exilic priests for their own benefit. The priest/Levite argument is more nuanced than most critics acknowledge. However, what the data supports is that P preserves genuinely old priestly traditions — not that P was composed before D. Ancient traditions can be preserved in a late document.

## Stakes

The tithe/priesthood argument is Wellhausen's strongest evidence for P's lateness. If Kaufmann can overturn it, Wellhausen's castle loses its foundation stone.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition reads the same data oppositely. The three tithe laws are evidence of DEVELOPMENT: from votive to annual, from local consumption to centralized consumption, from priestly portion to Levitical portion. The contradictions show successive stages, not parallel origins. P's complex sacrificial system (sin offerings, guilt offerings, graded purification) shows a systematization absent from earlier sources.

**Alternative reading:** P wasn't composed as a practical legal manual — it was composed as an idealized charter. The Levitical tithe in Num 18 may preserve the memory of an earlier practice even if the text was composed late. The priest/Levite distinction may have pre-exilic ROOTS while receiving its full literary EXPRESSION in the exile.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's most impressive argument in the chapter. The tithe evidence is genuinely hard to explain on the assumption that P was composed by post-exilic priests for their own benefit. But Kaufmann's conclusion ("therefore P is the oldest source") goes beyond his evidence. What his data supports is: (1) P preserves genuinely old priestly traditions; (2) the simple post-exilic-composition model has problems. It does NOT support "P was composed before D." Kaufmann consistently conflates "this tradition is old" with "this document is old."
