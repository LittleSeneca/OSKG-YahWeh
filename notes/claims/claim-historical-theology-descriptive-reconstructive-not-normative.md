# schmid-ht-preface.3: Theology of the Hebrew Bible should be descriptive and reconstructive, not normative

**Source:** [[Schmid — Preface]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), Preface

## The Claim

"'Theology of the Hebrew Bible' should essentially be carried out as a reconstructive (and not a constructive) undertaking." Schmid understands theology as "a descriptive project" — "Where does the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament allow for the recognition of reflexive and synthesizing efforts that could be described at least rudimentarily as theology?"

## Evidence

- The German original lacked the qualifier "historical" because German "Theologie" can denote a historical approach without kerygmatic overtones
- He cites parallels: de Pury and Knauf (1995) on "kerygmatic or descriptive?", Carl Steuernagel (1925), Dalit Rom-Shiloni (2016) on a "Jewish descriptive approach"
- He acknowledges the alternative: "under certain presuppositions and by means of certain definitions it is quite possible to pursue 'theology' of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament as a 'normative' undertaking"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The claim itself is a methodological choice, not an empirical claim. It's coherent and well-defended. The question is whether a merely "descriptive" project can still count as "theology" in any meaningful sense — Schmid anticipates this objection.

## Stakes

This is the central methodological commitment of the entire book. If Schmid is wrong that descriptive theology is a legitimate form of theology, then his project is misnamed — it's history of Israelite religion, not theology. If he's right, he's carved out a space for theology that takes critical scholarship seriously without being captured by dogmatic commitments.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Bernd Janowski argues that theology "concerns not only the genesis of the Hebrew Bible but also its application." Traditional systematic theologians would say theology MUST be normative. Barthians would reject a merely historical approach entirely.

**Alternative reading:** The descriptive/normative distinction may be false. All description involves selection and interpretation. Schmid's "descriptive" theology will inevitably reflect normative judgments about what counts as "theological." The alternative is to make those normative commitments explicit rather than hiding behind "description."

## Assessment

Schmid's approach is intellectually honest and methodologically appropriate for a critical scholar. But he's right that some will call this "not real theology." The key question is whether his "descriptive" theology can generate insights that matter beyond the historical — he'll need to show that understanding what the texts MEANT theologically has implications for what they might MEAN theologically. If his project is purely antiquarian, the critics have a point.
