# albertz-hir-5.2: Post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy — it was a Persian province with limited self-administration borrowing from pre-state tribal structures

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 5 — Post-Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"At least for the Persian period there is no question of a dominant priestly rule. The priestly college was only one governing body alongside the council of elders, and while the temple was an important unifying symbol, temple power and the temple economy were not decisive factors." The community structure "clearly borrows from models from the period before the state."

## Evidence

- The Persian governor (pehah) headed the province, not the high priest — governors could be Jewish or Persian
- Two governing bodies: a "council of elders" (horim/seganim, lay nobility) and a "priestly college" under the high priest
- Below them: the popular assembly (qahal), summoned occasionally — but it "deteriorated into a purely executive organ" as richer families dominated
- The population was organized into artificial "clans" (bet 'abot) — "an attempt at an artificial restoration of the social structure of the pre-state period"
- We see the realization of "constitutional monarchy" ideals that reform groups had advocated since Absalom's rebellion
- The high priest assumed royal cultic functions (anointing, turban, breastplate) — filling the king's role in the cult — but was himself under intensified holiness restrictions
- The Persian religious policy was tolerant (supporting local cults) but the taxation policy was brutal — driving the wedge between rich and poor

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The institutional structure is clearly attested in Ezra-Nehemiah, but the "pre-state tribal structures" claim is more interpretive.

## Stakes

Calling post-exilic Judah a "theocracy" distorts our understanding of power dynamics. The priests didn't rule — they were one power bloc among others, and a Persian governor sat above them. This matters for understanding the conflicts in Ezra-Nehemiah and the later Hasmonean period.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Josephus (who coined "theocracy"), and scholars who follow him. Blenkinsopp's "temple community" model. Weinberg's "citizen-temple community" model, which Albertz finds more appropriate but still needing refinement.

**Alternative reading:** Even if not formally a theocracy, the temple and priesthood exercised disproportionate influence because they controlled the central symbol of identity. De facto theocracy may be a better description than de jure.

## Assessment

Albertz's correction is important and well-evidenced from the Nehemiah memorandum. The dual governing body (elders + priests) is clearly attested. However, the high priest DID accumulate power over time, and by the Hellenistic period the "theocracy" label becomes more accurate. Albertz is right for the Persian period but the trajectory matters.
