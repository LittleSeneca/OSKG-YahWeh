# romer-inv-2-3.2: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is the best explanation for how Yhwh moved from the southern deserts to Israel

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin and Moses]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapters 2-3

## The Claim

Moses' connection to Midian (his father-in-law Jethro was a Midianite priest) preserves a historical memory of how Yhwh worship was transmitted to proto-Israel. The Midianites/Kenites were the bridge between the southern Yhwh of the Shasu and the Israelite Yhwh. The hypothesis "provides the best explanation currently available of the relevant literary and archaeological data" (citing Blenkinsopp) — but it is elegant, plausible, and underdetermined.

## Evidence

**On Moses — a constructed figure with historical kernels:**
- "Moses is a figure of tradition, of whom, however, there are no historical traces" (quoting Assmann)
- But his NAME is Egyptian: derived from Egyptian *msj* ("to engender, beget"). The Hebrew rendering uses *sin* rather than *samek*, suggesting an early transcription — "the name Moses takes us perhaps back to the second millennium."
- Two possible Egyptian historical parallels: Beya (Canaanite chancellor under Seti II) and Ben-Ozen (Semite mediator between Shasu laborers and Egyptian overseers). Neither is a perfect match.
- Moses is "a 'construct' in which memory traces of various different historical events and figures are combined."

**On the Midianites — who were they?**
- The name "Midian" may mean "extended territory" — long valleys, suitable for nomads
- 1 Kings 11 mentions Midian as a country between Edom and Paran, accessible from the south
- The Midianites were a nomadic or semi-nomadic group in northwestern Arabia/southern Transjordan
- They appear in the biblical narrative as both allies and enemies of Israel
- Archaeological evidence for Midian is sparse — they were nomads who left few permanent settlements

**The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis chain:**
1. Moses flees Egypt, marries Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, "priest of Midian" (Exodus 2-3)
2. Jethro already worships Yhwh and offers sacrifice to him (Exodus 18) — Yhwh was ALREADY worshipped by Midianites before Moses encountered him
3. The Kenites (a Midianite subgroup) appear as metalworkers associated with Israel (Judges 1:16, 4:11)
4. Hypothesis: Yhwh was a Midianite/Kenite god → Moses encountered him through his Midianite father-in-law → Moses brought Yhwh worship to the Israelites

**Romer's assessment of the hypothesis:**
- The biblical narrative preserves a "historical recollection of the importance of the Midianites and of some kind of close contact between them and Moses"
- BUT: the biblical narratives about Moses and Midian are "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance" — they're literary constructions, not straightforward history

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The hypothesis elegantly connects the Soleb evidence (Chapter 2) with the Moses narrative (Chapter 3). But it depends entirely on the biblical text preserving a genuine memory, which Romer himself acknowledges is "very difficult to reconstruct." The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is plausible, elegant, and underdetermined by the evidence.

## Stakes

If this is correct, Moses didn't receive a revelation from a previously unknown God. He encountered a god his Midianite father-in-law already worshipped. Yhwh wasn't revealed to Israel through Moses — Moses brought Yhwh TO Israel from Midian. "Revelation" becomes "cultural transmission."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critics of the Kenite hypothesis (Tebes, 2021, for archaeological reasons). Scholars who see the Jethro narrative as a late literary construct with no historical value. Scholars who argue Yhwh was indigenous to Canaan.

**Alternative reading:** The Jethro/Midian connection is a LITERARY device — the biblical author uses Midian to explain how a foreign god (Yhwh) became Israel's god. The story preserves the memory of foreign origin while domesticating it ("Moses' father-in-law was a Midianite priest, therefore Yhwh is in the family"). The story explains a problem, not a history.

## Assessment

This is where Romer's "traces of memory" methodology is most contested. The Kenite hypothesis is elegant but fragile. If Schmid is right that the Pentateuch is Persian-period, then the Jethro narrative tells us nothing about the 13th century BCE. Romer's willingness to use these texts as historical sources is what distinguishes him from the minimalist camp — but it's also his greatest vulnerability.
