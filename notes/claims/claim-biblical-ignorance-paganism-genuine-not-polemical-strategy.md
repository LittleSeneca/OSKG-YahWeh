# kaufmann-ri-1.3: The biblical ignorance of paganism is genuine — not polemical strategy

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"These phenomena go too deep and are too pervasive to be explained merely as monotheistic reworking." "Nothing can make plausible the suppression of a polemic against polytheistic beliefs, had such a polemic been in existence." Kaufmann argues that if the biblical writers knew pagan mythology, they would have used it — monotheism had no reason to suppress mythological material entirely. Later Judaism and Christianity "saw no harm in stories of God's battles with rebellious angels"; they could have treated pagan gods as demons or evil spirits. The total absence of such engagement indicates genuine ignorance, not editorial suppression.

## Evidence

1. **Monotheism didn't require total silence:** "While monotheism could not acknowledge the divinity of the pagan gods, it need not have denied them legendary roles. We have seen that occasionally the Bible does allow them the status of demons; these might have been permitted to play the part of evil spirits or enemies of YHWH."
2. **Later Jewish and Christian polemics DID engage:** "Greek thinkers in their attacks upon the popular religion gave due attention to its fetishistic aspect, but they did not permit this to distract them from combating the popular myths. Nor did the later Jewish and Christian polemics rest content with the fetishistic argument only."
3. **Polemical strategy would have exploited mythological material:** "If our author had but dipped into the treasury of Babylonian myths, what a mine of material he would have found for his satires: gods who are born and die, who procreate, who eat, drink, and sleep, who make war on their mother." That Second Isaiah never uses this material suggests he didn't know it.
4. **The writers attribute their own viewpoint to pagans:** "It is abundantly clear that the writers naively attribute their own viewpoint to the idolaters." When Sennacherib boasts of defeating the gods of the nations, the biblical writer has him saying "he cast them into the fire" — no hint that Sennacherib ascribes his triumphs to Ashur.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's third argument (why didn't Second Isaiah use mythological material?) is his strongest — the satiric potential of Babylonian mythology really IS obvious. But his other arguments are weaker than they appear. Arguments from silence about what polemicists "should have done" are methodologically fragile.

## Stakes

The entire explanatory framework. If the ignorance is genuine, Israelite religion was non-mythological from the start. If it's editorial/polemical, the evolutionary model stands — the Bible's silence is a product of successful monotheistic suppression of Israel's own pagan past.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus treats biblical silence about pagan mythology as a product of editorial activity. The Deuteronomistic editors systematically reshaped older traditions. Smith documents precisely the mythological categories that biblical texts share with Ugaritic literature but are often demythologized or polemically transformed. The presence of mythological *fragments* (Leviathan, Rahab, the divine council) suggests the material WAS known but was being suppressed.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical writers knew pagan mythology but made a deliberate theological choice to avoid dignifying it through engagement. Mocking Marduk by name would have acknowledged Marduk as a meaningful entity — better to reduce all pagan gods to the undifferentiated category of "wood and stone." The fetishism argument is *more* effective at undermining pagan religion than engaging with mythology would be.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's strongest point is the contrast with later Jewish and Christian polemics — they DID engage with pagan mythology (Philo, Justin Martyr), while the Hebrew Bible does not. This is a real historical difference. However, Kaufmann's argument from silence is methodologically fragile. The biblical authors might have known pagan mythology and chosen not to use it for reasons: (a) naming pagan gods might have been considered ritually dangerous; (b) the fetishism argument might have been more effective with their actual audience; (c) the biblical writers might have been engaging with the *popular* face of paganism (image worship) rather than its *elite* face (mythology) because that's what most Israelites encountered.
