# smith-obm-3.3: El's family is astral; Baal is an outsider storm-god with separate paternity

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch3 — The Divine Family]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 3

## The Claim

"The Ugaritic texts hint that El's family was understood as astral in character, although the texts rarely stress this feature." Baal's outsider status stems from his separate paternity (bn dgn, "son of Dagan") and his different cosmic character (storm vs. stars).

## Evidence

**For astral character of El's family:**
- 1.10 I 3-5: "the sons of El" // "the assembly of the stars" // "the circle of those of heaven" — apparently parallel terms.
- Shahar (Dawn) and Shalim (Dusk) are El's two sons (1.23).
- Yarih (moon) is "the favorite of El" (n'mn ['i]lm, 1.24.25). He participates in cooking at El's house (1.114), possibly serves as El's gate-keeper.
- Shapshu (sun) serves as El's special messenger (1.6 VI). Stars are grouped after her (1.23.54). Blessing in 1.102.26-27 parallels sun and moon with El.
- Athtar is called "Ashtar of the stars" at Emar; Aramaic 'tršmn = "Athtar of heaven." Athtar and Athtart may represent morning and evening star (Venus).
- Resheph possibly Mars (1.78: astronomical omen text). Paired with Yarih (1.107.40).
- The seven astral deities identified (Shahar, Shalim, Yarih, Shapshu, Athtar, Athtart, Resheph) match "seven stars" (šb' kbkbm, 1.164.15) — "it may only be coincidental."
- Keel and Uehlinger: lunar presentation of El on Beth-Shean cylinder seal. Brody: astral features of Athirat's iconography.

**For Baal's outsider status:**
- Baal's title bn dgn ("son of Dagan," 1.2 I 19; 1.5 V 23-24) — separate paternity from the rest of the divine family.
- He can stereotypically call El "father" (1.3 V 35; 1.4 IV 47) since El is generically father of the pantheon. Ginsberg: this "may echo a stage of the tradition in which he was not a son of El."
- Philo of Byblos provides narrative: Demarous (Baal) is a concubine's son sired by Ouranos, adopted by Dagon. The concubine's son has no legal rights; legal wife and children may expel him.
- Baal's situation parallels Jephthah (Judges 11): son of a prostitute driven out by legal sons, becomes warrior-leader despite his status.
- Hostility between Baal and Athirat's sons (1.4 II 24-26). Baal attacks the same seventy sons he invited to his feast (1.6 V 1). Elkunirsa myth: Baal boasts to Athirat about killing her seventy-seven/eighty-eight sons.
- Yarih is called "brother-in-law of Baal" (1.24.25-26) — family metaphor extends to integrate outsider through marriage.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The astral evidence is suggestive but Smith himself hedges; Baal's outsider status has converging evidence from multiple sources.

## Stakes

This is the structural explanation for WHY monotheism was possible. If Baal is an outsider who achieved prominence through martial prowess and was integrated into El's family through adoption/marriage, then the divine family was structurally FLEXIBLE — it could absorb new members and redefine relationships. This flexibility is precisely what allowed the eventual collapse into monotheism: Yahweh absorbed El's identity AND Baal's functions. The astral/storm dichotomy maps onto the El/Baal dichotomy that Yahweh later unifies.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Baal as fully integrated into El's family from the beginning (reading "father" language literally). Those who deny the astral character of El's family (evidence is admittedly thin). Cross emphasizes El's identification with Yahweh very early, which would compress the timeline.

**Alternative reading:** The astral/storm distinction may be Smith's systematization of what were looser associations. Baal's outsider status could be a literary device specific to the Baal Cycle rather than a theological claim about his place in the pantheon. The bn dgn title might reflect a different tradition about Baal's origins without implying outsider status.

## Assessment

The Baal-as-outsider argument is strong and well-supported by multiple lines of evidence. The astral-family argument is more tentative — Smith is honest about the "paucity of information." The two arguments work together: if El's family is astral and Baal is a storm-god, the outsider status has a cosmic basis, not just a narrative one. This is elegant but the astral evidence is thin enough that the argument works without it — Baal's separate paternity (bn dgn) and the Philo of Byblos narrative are enough on their own.
