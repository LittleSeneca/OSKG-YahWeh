# heiser-ur-25.1: *Ḥērem* was cosmic warfare — Yahweh reclaiming the nations from corrupt *elohim*

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 5 — Conquest and Failure]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 25

## The Claim

The conquest was Yahweh reclaiming the nations from the corrupt *sons of God* to whom they'd been assigned at Babel. The *ḥērem* (devotion to destruction) was a ritual act of cosmic warfare, not ethnic cleansing. The destruction was directed at the spiritual powers behind the nations, not the people per se.

## Evidence

- The language of "driving out" in Deuteronomy and Joshua uses cosmic-warfare terminology
- The "hornet" Yahweh sends ahead of Israel (Exodus 23:28; Deuteronomy 7:20; Joshua 24:12) is a metaphor for supernatural intervention
- The ban on plunder (*ḥērem*) marks the conquered goods as belonging to Yahweh alone — like firstfruits, but total
- Heiser's framing: "The conquest of Canaan is one of the most troubling portions of the Old Testament. In the effort to protect God's goodness, many Christians have sought to deny that God ordered the killing of noncombatants or to suggest that the conquest never really happened. These approaches either dismiss the biblical text or take the edge off its straightforward meaning. In my judgment, we need not fear the text."

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** Heiser's framework is coherent within his larger system, but it requires reading the conquest narratives through the lens of the Deut 32 worldview. The texts themselves don't say "we're killing giants, not humans." They say "devote to destruction men and women, young and old" (Joshua 6:21). Heiser tries to restrict this to Nephilim bloodlines, but the text doesn't cooperate.

## Stakes

This is where Heiser's framework faces its hardest test. If his reading of the conquest fails, it doesn't necessarily fail his divine council thesis — but it shows the limits of using the Deut 32 worldview as a universal hermeneutical key.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars who see the conquest as theological fiction — the *ḥērem* never happened historically, it was Deuteronomistic propaganda. Ethicists who argue that even IF the conquest was cosmic warfare, the killing of non-combatants is morally indefensible regardless of the framework.

**Alternative reading:** The conquest narratives are Deuteronomistic theology written centuries later to justify Israel's claim to the land. *Ḥērem* was a literary-theological construct, not a historical practice. The moral problem is dissolved by acknowledging the events never occurred.

## Assessment

Heiser's weakest argument in Part 5. The Deut 32 worldview is doing too much work. The attempt to solve the ethical problem of the conquest through cosmic warfare is ambitious but strained. The texts resist the framework — *ḥērem* commands target whole populations, not just giant clans. And even if Heiser were right about the cosmic framework, it doesn't resolve the moral problem: the text still describes killing non-combatants.
