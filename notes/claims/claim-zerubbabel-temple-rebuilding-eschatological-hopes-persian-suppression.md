# albertz-hir-5.3: Temple rebuilding sparked Zerubbabel hopes that Persians crushed — marginalizing prophecy

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 5 — Post-Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"The building of the temple... sparked off glowing expectations of a revolution in world history and a national restoration which attached themselves to Zerubbabel of the house of David. The situation seemed so dangerous to the Persians that the satrap Tatnai paid a personal visit to Jerusalem... While he allowed the rebuilding... this was probably only on condition that Zerubbabel was withdrawn and that the dangerous prophetic movements were stopped."

## Evidence

- Haggai and Zechariah's oracles explicitly link Zerubbabel to Davidic restoration hopes (Hag 2.20-23; Zech 4.6-10; 6.9-15)
- The Persian satrap Tatnai's investigation (Ezra 5.3-17) indicates official alarm
- Zerubbabel disappears from the record after the temple's completion — Albertz infers Persian-ordered withdrawal
- The prophetic movements were "stopped or controlled effectively"
- Haggai and Zechariah's prophecies were "eschatologized" — their unfulfilled promises were reinterpreted for a distant future
- Ezra 4.6, 4.7-23 indicate ongoing suspicion and occasional violent suppression of nationalist-rebellious activity
- Nehemiah himself had to fend off prophets who wanted to proclaim him king (Neh 6.6-14)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The basic sequence (Zerubbabel → hopes → Persian intervention → Zerubbabel's disappearance) is well-supported, but the claim that Zerubbabel was "withdrawn" by Persian order is inference from silence.

## Stakes

This episode established a pattern that shaped all of post-exilic religion: eschatological prophecy was politically dangerous and had to be marginalized. The pro-Persian coalition (priests concerned with cult autonomy + lay leaders seeking self-administration) learned that nationalist restoration hopes threatened the fragile arrangement with the empire.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Zerubbabel's disappearance as natural (death, retirement) rather than forced. Those who date Haggai/Zechariah differently or read their oracles as less politically charged.

**Cross-reference:** Romer (Invention of God, Ch 12) discusses the "end of prophecy" and the marginalization of prophetic voices in the post-exilic period. Albertz gives this a sharper political edge.

## Assessment

Albertz's reconstruction makes excellent sense of the narrative gaps and tensions. The pattern of Persian tolerance combined with ruthless suppression of political-nationalist threats fits what we know of Achaemenid imperial practice. The "eschatologizing" of unfulfilled prophecy is a crucial concept for understanding the development of apocalyptic literature.
