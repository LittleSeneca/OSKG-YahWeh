# kaufmann-ri-9.6: Wisdom morality is the earlier, universal stream — Torah transformed it nationally

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"The moral conceptions of the Bible have been crystallized in two parallel but distinct traditions: Torah and prophecy on the one hand, and the wisdom literature on the other." Torah morality is prophetic, national, grounded in covenant — "the source of morality is the will of God." Wisdom morality is sapiential, individual, universal — "its source is not prophetic revelation, but understanding and discernment." Wisdom literature "exhibits hardly a trace of the influence of either [Torah or prophecy]; it runs as a parallel and independent stream of thought." Far from being late, "individual and universal morality was the earlier, and the societal-national morality the offspring of a later, inner Israelite development."

## Evidence

- Proverbs contains all of the Decalogue except the ban on idolatry and Sabbath — but frames them as prudent counsel, not divine command
- Proverbs has no covenant concept, no historical/national motivation for morality
- "In striking contrast with the Torah, the moral rules of Proverbs are never provided with a historical or national motive"
- The cultic and social-moral aspects of Sabbath are equally ignored in Proverbs
- "Despite its solicitude for depressed classes, Proverbs lacks the great command of loving the stranger — in fact, there is no word at all about gerim"
- Proverbs' morality is utilitarian: "Morality is the way to success"
- Wisdom speaks of torah and commandment — but they are "the instruction and commands of father, mother, sage, or of wisdom personified. There is nothing about a revealed Torah, concerning which a covenant has been made"
- Torah and wisdom were only identified later (Ben-Sira onward)
- Biblical wisdom belongs to the common sapiential legacy of the ancient Near East — "Hardly anything in biblical wisdom (apart from its monotheistic viewpoint) cannot be paralleled in the literatures of Egypt and Mesopotamia"
- "The absence of a distinctively Israelite element in wisdom morality indicates the antiquity of the style of that literature"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The literary observation that wisdom lacks Torah's national-covenantal framework is genuine, but the chronological inference (wisdom = earlier) is unprovable and probably wrong.

## Stakes

Kaufmann's chronological inversion. If national covenant morality is the LATER development, the prophets are not innovators but culminators of a long process — and the earlier "international" wisdom morality proves Israel was always ethically sophisticated. But if national covenant morality is EARLIER, wisdom literature's universalism is a late achievement — supporting the critical narrative of progressive universalization.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus dates Proverbs' final form to the Persian or Hellenistic period. The identification of Wisdom with Torah (Ben-Sira) is seen as the culmination of a process, not a late innovation. Kaufmann's dating of Proverbs as pre-exilic is possible for individual sayings, but the collection as a whole shows signs of late composition.

**Alternative reading:** Wisdom literature's universalism is not evidence of primitivity but of a deliberate international literary persona. The sages adopted the voice of "everyman" wisdom, suppressing national particularities as a genre convention. This doesn't tell us about chronological priority — it tells us about genre. Torah and wisdom are parallel streams in Israelite thought, but we can't date their relative priority from their literary form alone.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's literary observation is genuinely valuable — the contrast between Torah's national-covenantal morality and wisdom's individual-universal morality is real and important. But his chronological inference is unprovable and probably wrong. Both streams likely existed concurrently throughout Israel's history, with Torah representing the distinctively Israelite contribution and wisdom representing Israel's participation in the international sapiential tradition. Neither is "earlier" — they're different genres with different social locations.
