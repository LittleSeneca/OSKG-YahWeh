# keel-ggi-7.3: The Hurvat Qitmit Edomite shrine preserved a full "Canaanite" religion with god and goddess side-by-side into the 6th century, raising unresolved questions about the shared roots of Qaus, Yahweh of Teman, and the "Lord of the Ostriches"

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VII — Iron Age III]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VII, §221

## The Claim

The Edomite shrine at Hurvat Qitmit (eastern Negev, ~25 km east of Beer-sheba, ~10 km south of Arad), dated to the early 6th century (late Iron Age IIC / early Iron Age III), provides the most vivid archaeological counterpoint to contemporary developments in Judah. "One could imagine oneself transported at this site right back into a long-past era of 'Canaanite' religion, with god and goddess visibly standing side-by-side as partners." The shrine includes: a massebah likely representing Qaus; a three-horned goddess statue with a raised dagger — an *aggressive* goddess, not a passive consort; naked goddess figurines; a stylized tree possibly representing Asherah; ostrich and pig figurines. The authors raise the open question: "Do 'Yahweh of Teman,' Qaus, and the 'Lord of the Ostriches' all have their roots finally in one and the same figure?"

## Evidence

- **Architecture** (§221): Two buildings — Building A (three long parallel rooms opening to a courtyard) and Building B (square, tower-like with benches/podia and altar). Comparable structures at Kadesh Barnea, Idumean Lachish, and Nabatean temples suggest the three-part *adyton* "is most likely a characteristic feature of Edomite-Idumean sacral architecture."
- **Qaus inscription** (§221): A vessel fragment from Building B bears the incised inscription `...]blqwshp[...` — the element *qws* reproduces the Edomite national god Qaus. A massebah in Building B "very likely represents the god Qaus."
- **Warrior deity and three-horned goddess** (§221): Fragments from the courtyard include a hand holding a dagger, a dagger, and an arm holding a weapon — probably from a warrior deity statue (Qaus? or his female partner?). A three-horned head from a goddess statue (illus. 375) parallels Bronze Age metal statuettes of a goddess with three-horned helmet holding a dagger — "her right hand is raised to strike." This is an *aggressive* goddess, not a passive consort.
- **Naked goddess presenting breasts** (§221): A statuette "without horned helmet or any attributes of a deity" (illus. 377) — the same type as the Bronze/Iron Age nude female figurines.
- **Stylized tree** (§221): A terra-cotta in the shape of a stylized tree (illus. 376) raises the question "whether this might rather be an Edomite variation of Asherah."
- **Music and worshipers** (§221): A double-pipe player (illus. 378) and small anthropomorphic terra-cottas that "ought not be identified as deities, but are probably human male and female worshipers."
- **Ostrich and pig figurines** (§221): Numerous ostrich images recall the "Lord of the Ostriches." An image of a pig (illus. 379) is significant — pig offerings are mentioned scornfully in Isa 65:4; 66:3, 17; the dietary prohibitions of Lev 11:7 and Deut 14:8 "probably would have been formulated in exilic or early postexilic times," so a pig votive in 6th-century Edom is the kind of practice those prohibitions were targeting.
- **The shared-roots question** (§221): "Do 'Yahweh of Teman,' Qaus, and the 'Lord of the Ostriches' all have their roots finally in one and the same figure? If the answer is 'yes,' for how long were they understood to be identical to one another or at least related?" The authors connect this to Job 39:13–16 (Yahweh as Lord of the Ostriches) and Job's home in northwestern Arabia.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the archaeological description (published by Beit-Arieh and Beck). SPECULATIVE on the shared-roots question — the authors flag it as an open question, not a conclusion.

## Stakes

If Qaus, Yahweh of Teman, and the Lord of the Ostriches share a common root in the southern steppe/desert, then the Edomite religion preserved at Hurvat Qitmit might represent a *sibling* tradition to early Yahwism, not a foreign one. The aggressive three-horned goddess is also significant — she looks more like the Bronze Age warrior goddess (Anat?) than the maternal Isis of the Phoenician coast. Different regions preserved different aspects of the "Canaanite" heritage.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The identification of the massebah with Qaus is plausible but not certain — the inscription is fragmentary. Some scholars might read the three-horned goddess as Anat rather than a local Edomite deity. The shared-roots hypothesis is acknowledged as speculative by the authors themselves.

**Alternative reading:** Hurvat Qitmit could represent an imported Edomite cult imposed on the region after Edomite expansion into the Negev following Judah's collapse, rather than an indigenous survival of "Canaanite" religion. The architectural parallels with Kadesh Barnea and Nabatean temples support this.

## Assessment

The most arresting claim here is that Yahweh, Qaus, and the Lord of the Ostriches may have a common origin. This would mean that the god who became the sole deity of monotheistic Judaism once had *brother* gods in the same southern pantheon — and one of those brothers (Qaus) continued to be worshipped with a goddess partner centuries after Judah had begun purging goddess imagery. The Edomite-Judean break might have been as much a religious schism between sibling traditions as a political conflict.
