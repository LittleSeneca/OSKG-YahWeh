# smith-obm-7.4: Deuteronomy 32:8-9 preserves an older polytheistic theology — Yahweh as son of Elyon

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch7 — El Yahweh and the Original God of Israel]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 7: El, Yahweh, and the Original God of Israel

## The Claim

The Septuagint and Dead Sea Scroll (4QDeutj) versions of Deuteronomy 32:8-9 "cast Yahweh in the role of one of the divine sons, understood as fathered by El, called Elyon in the first line." The MT changed "sons of El" to "sons of Israel" — reflecting discomfort with the polytheistic implications. This single passage provides a snapshot of an early Israelite theology in which Yahweh was NOT the supreme god — El/Elyon was. It's as close to a smoking gun as we get in Hebrew Bible studies.

## Evidence

### The reconstructed text

> When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance,
> When He divided up humanity,
> He fixed the boundaries for peoples,
> According to the number of the divine sons:
> For Yahweh's portion is his people,
> Jacob His own inheritance.

### Text-critical data

| Source | Reading |
|--------|---------|
| MT | bĕnê yiśrā'ēl ("sons of Israel") |
| LXX | ἀγγέλων θεοῦ ("angels of God") |
| 4QDeutj | bny 'lwhm / bny 'l ("sons of God" / "sons of El") |

- The MT reading makes no sense in context: how would the number of Israel's sons determine the boundaries of ALL peoples?
- The LXX/4Q reading aligns with a known ANE concept: the high god assigns nations to lesser gods
- "This passage...presents a cosmic order in which each deity received its own nation. Israel was the nation which Yahweh received, yet El was the head of this pantheon and Yahweh only one of its members."
- "This reading points to an old phase of Israel's religion when El held a pre-eminent position apart from the status of Yahweh."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is one of the most important text-critical discoveries for understanding early Israelite religion. The MT change is a classic example of theological correction. The LXX/4Q reading is clearly superior on text-critical grounds, and the theology it preserves aligns with what we know of West Semitic pantheon structures.

## Stakes

This single passage provides a snapshot of an early Israelite theology in which Yahweh was NOT the supreme god — El/Elyon was. If this reading is correct, Israelite monotheism was not an original feature but an innovation that required suppressing older traditions. This passage is the keystone of Smith's argument that early Israel knew a distinction between El and Yahweh.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Evangelical/conservative scholars who defend the MT reading. Those who argue the LXX translator misunderstood the Hebrew (though 4QDeutj now makes this harder to argue). Scholars who read the passage as poetic parallelism where Elyon and Yahweh are the same deity.

**Alternative reading:** The passage could be read with Elyon = Yahweh from the start — i.e., "the Most High" IS Yahweh, and the "divine sons" are his subordinate angels. The MT change would then be a clarification, not a correction. But this reading struggles with the structure: if Elyon = Yahweh, why does the text say Elyon allotted nations and Yahweh received Israel as HIS portion? If they're the same, the structure is redundant.

## Assessment

Smith's reading is the consensus position in critical scholarship and for good reason. The text-critical evidence (4QDeutj) combined with the contextual logic (how do "sons of Israel" determine the boundaries of all peoples?) makes the MT reading indefensible as original. This passage is the keystone of Smith's argument that early Israel knew a distinction between El and Yahweh.
