# sommer-bog-intro.1: The God of the Hebrew Bible has a body — this is the standard notion, not the exception

**Source:** [[Sommer — Introduction]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Introduction

## The Claim

"The god of the Hebrew Bible has a body. This must be stated at the outset, because so many people, including many scholars, assume otherwise. The evidence for this simple thesis is overwhelming, so much so that asserting the carnal nature of the biblical God should not occasion surprise."

Sommer's radically reframing thesis: "What I propose to show in this book is that the startling or bizarre idea in the Hebrew Bible is something else entirely: not that God has a body — that is the standard notion of ancient Israelite theology — but rather that God has many bodies located in sundry places in the world that God created."

Sommer defines "a body" as: "Something located in a particular place at a particular time, whatever its shape or substance."

He distinguishes two types of divine embodiment:

| Type | Description | Biblical Examples |
|------|-------------|------------------|
| Non-material anthropomorphism | God has a form but not material substance — a body of energy/light, not matter | P texts, Kaufmann's view |
| Material anthropomorphism | God's body has the same shape AND substance as human bodies | J texts — God walking, eating, talking face-to-face |

## Evidence

- Genesis 1:26: "Let us make humanity in our form, according to our shape" — God and heavenly creatures HAVE bodies, and human bodies share their shape
- Genesis 2:7: God blows life-giving breath into Adam — action implying a mouth
- Genesis 3:8: Adam hears God "going for a stroll in the Garden of Eden at the breezy time of the day" — a being with legs
- Genesis 11:5: God "comes down" to look at the tower
- Genesis 18: God walks to Abraham's tent and converses
- Exodus 33:11: "Moses would speak with God face to face, as a man speaks with his friend" — not metaphor, face to face
- On Exodus 33:20 ("No one can see Me and live"): "In fact this text does not claim that God has no body for us to see; the point is rather that seeing God's body will lead immediately to death. (Similarly, the statement, 'One cannot touch a high-voltage wire and live,' does not mean that there is no such thing as a high-voltage wire; on the contrary, high-voltage wires are dismayingly, dangerously real. So is the embodied deity of the Hebrew Bible.)"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The biblical texts are unambiguous. Sommer's reading is straightforward — it's the denial of divine embodiment that requires interpretive gymnastics.

## Stakes

The "spiritual," invisible, transcendent God of later Judaism and Christianity — the God who "has no body, no form" of Maimonides' Thirteen Principles — is not the God of the Hebrew Bible. The biblical God walked, talked, ate, could be seen (sometimes fatally), and had a back that Moses glimpsed. The disembodied God is a later philosophical development, not a biblical idea.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Gerstenberger (refers to Yhwh as "the invisible God"), Scarry (maintains God "has no body"), Eichrodt (dismisses anthropomorphism as "Canaanite influence"), Maimonides (Thirteen Principles: God "has no body, no form")

**Alternative reading:** The anthropomorphic language is metaphor or accommodation to human limitations, not a theological claim about God's actual nature.

## Assessment

Sommer's opening claim is deliberately provocative but textually well-grounded. The evidence from Genesis and Exodus is straightforward. The real question is not whether the biblical God is embodied — Sommer demonstrates that clearly — but what KIND of embodiment the biblical texts assume, and whether the later theological tradition's rejection of divine corporeality represents an improvement or a loss.
