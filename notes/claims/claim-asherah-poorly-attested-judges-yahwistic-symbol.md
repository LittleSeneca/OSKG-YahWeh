# smith-ehg-1.4: Asherah as a goddess is poorly attested in the Judges period; the asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol.

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2nd ed., 2002), Chapter 1

## The Claim

The asherah (wooden pole/tree symbol) was present in early Israelite religion, but it probably functioned as a symbol within Yahweh's cult, not as a representation of a separate goddess. The goddess Asherah may have been known earlier (Genesis 49:25 possibly preserves her epithets), but by the Judges period, her cult had merged into Yahweh's.

## Evidence

- Asherah does NOT appear as a theophoric element in Hebrew names
- Judges 6 focuses on Baal, not Asherah — only the asherah pole is mentioned
- Genesis 49:25 may preserve goddess epithets (*šādayim wārāḥam*) paired with El's epithets, suggesting an early stage when a goddess was invoked alongside El — but this language was not later applied to Yahweh
- The Taanach cult stand (10th c.) shows a tree (asherah), a bull deity, and a goddess figure (probably Astarte, not Asherah)

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** This is Smith at his most cautious — and possibly his most wrong. The majority of scholars disagree with him. He himself concedes "I may be wrong." Flag as: Smith's conclusion here is a judgment call based on how much weight you give to different pieces of ambiguous evidence.

## Stakes

See Foreword/Preface note — same assessment. If Asherah WAS a goddess and Yahweh's consort, the biblical God had a wife. Smith resists this conclusion even though it would strengthen his larger argument.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone. See the full list in the Foreword/Preface note.

**Alternative reading:** The asherah pole WAS the cult symbol of the goddess Asherah. Her absence from theophoric names doesn't prove absence from worship (per Smith's own argument against Tigay on onomastics!). The Taanach cult stand's goddess figure MAY be Asherah, not Astarte — the identification is debated.

## Assessment

Smith is probably wrong here — the majority interpretation (Asherah was a goddess) has better cumulative evidence. But his caution is methodologically instructive. He's not willing to claim more than the evidence supports, even when the claim would help his case. That's a mark of intellectual honesty worth noting.
