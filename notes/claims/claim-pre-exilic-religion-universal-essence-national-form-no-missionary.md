# kaufmann-ri-ep.4: Pre-exilic religion was universal in essence but national in form

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Epilogue and Prospect]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Epilogue

## The Claim

During the pre-exilic period, "the religion of Israel, universal in essence, had embodied itself exclusively in national forms." There was "no battle against the paganism of the gentiles." Idolatry was a sin for ISRAEL, not for the nations. The nations' worship of their gods was "considered legitimate" on their own soil.

## Evidence

- The early religion doesn't address gentiles or have missionary tendencies
- The ger (resident alien) assimilated to Israelite culture by settling in the land — not through conversion
- Naaman is the one pre-exilic convert, and even his conversion involves no polemic against idolatry — only acknowledgment of YHWH's power
- Isaiah first envisioned the end of idolatry; Habakkuk and Jeremiah first declared it sinful for nations
- The pre-exilic period produced "but one religious convert, Naaman"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's description of pre-exilic religion as non-missionary is historically accurate. The innovation of Second Temple Judaism was precisely the missionary impulse.

## Stakes

Whether monotheism's universalization was a natural unfolding of its internal logic or a radical innovation forced by historical circumstances (exile, diaspora).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars see universalistic strands earlier (Deutero-Isaiah's servant songs, the "light to the nations" motif). But these are exilic, which actually supports Kaufmann's point.

**Alternative reading:** The universalistic potential was always there in the idea of YHWH as creator of all — but it took exile to activate it. Kaufmann essentially agrees with this.

## Assessment

This is one of Kaufmann's less controversial claims, and it's largely correct. The interesting question is whether the national form of pre-exilic religion COMPROMISED its monotheistic essence — Kaufmann says no, the essence was always universal even when the form was national.
