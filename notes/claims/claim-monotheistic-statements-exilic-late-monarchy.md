# smith-obm-8.1: Biblical monotheistic statements are overwhelmingly exilic or later; the Albright/Mosaic monotheism model is untenable

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch8 — Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 8

## The Claim

"Most of the references to monotheism derive from the exilic period or later." The debate between Albright (early monotheism) and Meek (late development) "solved nothing" because neither side addressed the constructive points of the other — and neither "attempted to situate the issues in terms of Judah's larger social structure."

## Evidence

- Albright claimed the First Commandment ("no other gods before me") demonstrated Mosaic monotheism based on Punic `'l(t) pn-` ("besides"). Smith: "Albright had a point" about the translation, but "whether it demonstrated monotheism was in fact problematic."
- Albright dodged the key question: *if* Israel was monotheistic from the start, "why did its monotheistic faith appear in clearer, less ambiguous forms in the seventh and sixth centuries?"
- Even if 1 Samuel 2:2 and Psalm 82 date to the late monarchy, "on the whole the late monarchy and exile seem to represent the general period for the emergence of monotheistic rhetoric."
- Halpern: "Had Second Isaiah, Cyrus' Judahite spin-doctor, not had Jeremiah's (and Deuteronomy's) voice crying on his own arrival from the steppe, his explicit monotheistic claims would have fallen on deaf ears, and probably set them ringing to boot."
- The First Commandment is explicitly monolatrous (cultic exclusion), NOT monotheistic. "Monotheistic exclusivity is not simply a matter of cultic observance...It extends further to an understanding of deities in the cosmos (no other gods, period)."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The chronological distribution is clear — the concentration in Second Isaiah (exilic, ca. 540 BCE) is overwhelming. Even Smith's dating caveats don't undermine the basic point.

## Stakes

If monotheism emerges in the late monarchy/exile, the Moses-at-Sinai narrative is retrojection. The religion of Iron I-II Israel was polytheistic or at most monolatrous. The "monotheistic revolution" is a sixth-century phenomenon, not a Mosaic one.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Albright school (Mosaic monotheism). Conservative biblicists who date Deuteronomy and Isaiah earlier.

**Alternative reading:** Some monotheistic expressions COULD be earlier but were edited — absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. But Smith's point is that even the surviving texts cluster late.

## Assessment

The Albright-Meek debate recap is a bit of a straw man at this point (2001), but it sets up Smith's methodological contrast nicely. The Halpern quote is excellent — it foregrounds the question of *audience* and *intelligibility* that Smith builds his whole argument around.
