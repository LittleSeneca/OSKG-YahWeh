# albertz-hir-4.12: The Ezekiel school created the separation of cult from state, the priest-Levite distinction, and a radically sacralized temple

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 4 — Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"Here for the first time in the history of Israelite religion a priestly group quite voluntarily and with its own distinctive voice engaged in discussion about reform. These people, the sons and grandsons of priests who had been deported and had lapsed from royal service, no longer wanted to remain the lackeys of a state cult which had met its end."

## Evidence

**Architectural separation:** The temple vision (Ezek 40-43) imagines a sacred precinct protected by three walls, each with massive gate complexes (26m x 13m) — larger than the temple hall itself. These gates give the priesthood "complete control in all circumstances over access to the sacred temple precinct."

**Priest-Levite distinction:** The group reserved the inner court and altar service for Zadokite priests (40.46b; 43.19; 44.15f.; 48.11) and assigned "service of the house" (doorkeeping, slaughtering lay sacrifices) to the Levites — who were excluded from holy things (44.13). Albertz defends Wellhausen's classic hypothesis: the Levites of Ezek 44 are the demoted descendants of the country priests put out of work by Josiah's reform. The Zadokites "used the term 'Levite' to describe all non-Zadokide priests who had been associated with the Jerusalem temple as a result of the centralization of the cult under Josiah."

**Detachment from the state:**
- Architecturally: the temple is separate from the palace (43.7b-8 — proximity was a profanation)
- Legally: the temple is built by the people, consecrated by Yahweh's glory, not by royal legitimation; the ark is never mentioned
- Economically: sacrifices are provided by the people through taxation, not by the king. A later revision (45.16-17) inserts the king as intermediate authority, but only as mediator of the people's offerings
- The king (nasi') retains some functions but is clearly subordinate to the priests in the cultic sphere

**Laity exclusion:** The strict separation of inner and outer courts "achieved [priestly independence] at the high price of an almost complete exclusion of the laity from the cult."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Wellhausenian priest-Levite reconstruction is still controversial. The cult-state separation and the general program of the Ezekiel school are clearer.

## Stakes

This is the blueprint for post-exilic theocracy. The priest-Levite distinction becomes structural in Second Temple Judaism. The separation of cult from monarchy is a genuine innovation — previously the king was the supreme cultic patron. The tension between priestly independence and lay exclusion will shape post-exilic conflicts.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Gunneweg (Leviten und Priester) rejects Wellhausen's identification of Ezek 44 Levites with displaced country priests. Duke argues the distinction pre-existed Ezekiel and the chapter restores ancestral rights rather than introducing degradation. The literary unity and dating of Ezek 40-48 is contested.

**Alternative reading:** The Ezekiel program was utopian speculation that had limited influence on actual historical development. The priest-Levite distinction emerged pragmatically in the Second Temple period, not from a reform blueprint.
