# albertz-hir-6.5: Apocalyptic emerged from marginalized groups as a "theology of resistance" — three-stage development

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 6 — Hellenistic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 6

## The Claim

The late prophetic and apocalyptic literature represents "oppositional eschatological interpretation of history" that developed in marginalized groups. It progressed through three stages: (1) Zech 9-14's oppositional eschatology sparked by Alexander's conquests, (2) Isa 24-27's eschatological assurance from lower-class circles, and (3) Daniel's apocalyptic instructions for resistance under Antiochus IV.

## Evidence

**Zech 9-14 (Deutero-Zechariah, late 4th century):**
- Sparked by Alexander's conquest (332) — Zech 9.1-8 reflects the destruction of Phoenician and Philistine cities
- The group developed an "oppositional eschatological interpretation of history" — Yahweh himself would come as warrior-king to defeat the nations
- But critically, the "day of Yahweh" would first be judgment on Israel: Jerusalem conquered, half the population deported (14.2), two-thirds exterminated (13.7f.) — "the onslaught of the nations on Zion would certainly not be stopped by God at the beginning, as the traditional Zion theology wanted to make people believe"
- This prepared the way for "the apocalyptic notion of the 'terrors of the end-time'"

**Isa 24-27 ("Isaiah Apocalypse," early 3rd century):**
- Arises from lower-class circles — using the terminology of the social conflict (saddiq/rasa', 'ebyon/'aris)
- The unnamed destroyed "city" is not a specific historical city but "urban life generally" — universalized judgment
- The group's distress was threefold: political domination by foreign and native lords (26.13), theological distress that Yahweh's judgment tarried (26.8-10), and impotence — "they could not win any victories on earth or bring down their over-powerful opponents" (26.16-18)
- The parallel between "the wicked" and "the inhabitants of the earth" generalizes the class conflict to cosmic dimensions
- Hope centers on Yahweh's universal kingship, the resurrection of the dead (26.19 — one of the earliest clear statements), and the eschatological banquet for all peoples (25.6-8)

**Daniel (Maccabean period, 167-164 BCE):**
- Written during the persecution of Antiochus IV Epiphanes
- The Hasidim (hasidaioi) — identified by Albertz (following Kampen) as an influential scribal group, not a lower-class pietistic conventicle — were the tradents
- Daniel combines: wisdom tradition (court tales), prophetic eschatology (the four-kingdom scheme), Torah piety (Daniel's prayer practices), and apocalyptic vision (the Son of Man)
- The apocalyptic theology of history divides time into periods determined by God, culminating in the establishment of God's eternal kingdom
- It serves as "instructions for resistance" — calling for faithful endurance under persecution, promising resurrection and vindication

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The specific sociological location of each text is inherently uncertain. The dating and sociological contexts are speculative.

## Stakes

Albertz traces apocalyptic not to foreign influence (Persian Zoroastrianism) or to the "failure of prophecy" but to the concrete social experience of marginalized groups within Judaean society. Apocalyptic is a theology FROM BELOW — it gives voice to those crushed by imperial and aristocratic power. This has political implications for how we read apocalyptic texts today.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Hanson (The Dawn of Apocalyptic) locates apocalyptic origins earlier, in the early post-exilic period, and in conflicts between visionary and hierocratic groups. Ploger sees the Hasidim as a pietistic conventicle, not scribal elites. Scholars who emphasize Persian/Zoroastrian influence on Jewish apocalyptic. Those who read Daniel as wisdom literature rather than resistance literature.

**Cross-reference:** Smith (Origins, Part III) discusses apocalyptic as one trajectory of Israelite monotheism — where the denial of other gods becomes cosmic conflict between good and evil.

## Assessment

Albertz's three-stage model (Deutero-Zechariah → Isaiah Apocalypse → Daniel) provides a coherent developmental narrative. His emphasis on the social dimension — apocalyptic as the voice of the marginalized — is important and often overlooked in favor of literary or comparative approaches. However, the sociological identifications are thin: we really don't know who wrote these texts. The scribal identification of the Hasidim (following Kampen) is suggestive but needs more evidence. The claim that Isa 24-27 comes from "lower-class circles" rests heavily on the presence of poor/righteous terminology that could be conventional.
