# romer-inv-5.3: Saul, David, and Solomon are paradigmatic constructs

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 5 — Entrance into Jerusalem]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 5

## The Claim

The biblical portrayal of the first three kings is heavily shaped by the Deuteronomistic editors' theological program. Saul is the rejected king (northern kingdom), David is the warrior/chosen one (dynasty), Solomon is the builder/sage. But "the passage from Iron Age I to Iron Age II (about 1000) coincides with the origin of kingdoms in the Levant (Moab, Ammon, the Aramaean kingdoms). The fact that an Israelite 'kingdom' arose in an area under strong influence from the Philistines is certainly an element of the stories that has some historical basis."

## Evidence

- **No extra-biblical attestation of individuals:** Saul, David, and Solomon are unattested outside the Bible as individuals. The Tel Dan stele mentions "house of David" (dynasty), not David himself.
- **David as Philistine vassal:** David was originally a Philistine vassal — "even though the biblical texts try to give an apologetic interpretation of this fact."
- **Saul's territory:** Saul operated in the Philistine sphere of influence.
- **Samuel as transitional figure:** Samuel (whose name is El-based: "El is exalted" or "His name is El") is the prophet who anoints Saul (whose name contains no theophoric element). Samuel bridges the pre-monarchic El-worshipping "Israel" and the royal Yhwh-worshipping monarchy.
- **Divergent traditions preserved:** The biblical accounts of Saul's rise are "very divergent" (1 Samuel 8-12) — evidence of multiple, conflicting traditions being preserved rather than harmonized.
- **Paradigmatic typology:** "The three kings Saul, David, and Solomon have been constructed by the editors of the Bible as paradigmatic types."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that the biblical portraits are theologically shaped (the Deuteronomistic framework is well-established in scholarship). MEDIUM that a historical David existed (the Tel Dan stele confirms the dynasty; dynasties normally have founders). LOW on specific details of David's reign.

## Stakes

The founder of the Messianic line, the ancestor of Jesus, the man after God's own heart — may have been a Philistine vassal who seized a Canaanite city and installed his patron deity there for political reasons. The biblical portrayal of David as the ideal king is theology, not biography.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Maximalist historians who accept the biblical portrait of David as substantially historical. Scholars who argue the Tel Dan stele confirms David as an individual (the "house of David" must have a founder named David).

**Alternative reading:** The biblical accounts, while theologically shaped, preserve genuine historical memory of Saul, David, and Solomon. The Tel Dan stele confirms David's historicity as a dynastic founder. The Deuteronomistic editing shaped but did not invent the narratives.

## Assessment

Römer is appropriately cautious here. He doesn't deny that a historical David existed — he notes traits in the narrative that "cannot be pure invention." But he insists on distinguishing between the historical figure (about whom we know almost nothing) and the theological construct (which is what the Bible gives us). The Tel Dan stele confirms the DYNASTY; it tells us nothing about the MAN. This is the responsible historical position.
