# kaufmann-ri-8.3: Solomon's foreign worship was a private court defection, not a popular paganization

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

According to the biblical narrator, I Kings 11:4 divides the history of the monarchy into two — from Saul to Solomon's last days there was "no national sin of idolatry," after which sin continued to the fall. "This view, however, is a dogmatic necessity for the biblical historians and cannot be taken at face value." The private chapels Solomon built for his pagan wives "ultimately brought about his own involvement in their cults." But "this was not a public, aggressive pagan influx, as later in the days of Jezebel." Kaufmann explicitly concedes: "There can be no doubt that the religious condition of the bulk of the people after Solomon's reign did not change substantially from what it was before. Vestigial idolatry existed among the vulgar, both before and after."

## Evidence

- I Kings 11:4 states Solomon's heart was turned after other gods in his old age — this was "private defection of the court"
- Ashtoreth, Milcom, and Chemosh had altars built "on the soil of Israel — and at the hands of an Israelite king" for the first time
- But "the Bible does not know a popular cult of any foreign god" in this period
- "The post-Solomonic age cannot, therefore, be regarded as a period of the paganization of the national religion"

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann concedes 'vestigial idolatry existed among the vulgar, both before and after' — this concession sits awkwardly with his thesis and the archaeological evidence of persistent polytheistic practice.

## Stakes

Kaufmann's model of Israelite religion as fundamentally non-pagan throughout the monarchy. He needs to explain why the Bible says Solomon built high places for foreign gods without conceding that this reflected or produced popular polytheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars point out that Solomon is credited with building temples for Chemosh (Moab), Milcom (Ammon), and Astarte (Sidon) — I Kings 11:5-7. If a king could do this openly without provoking a popular backlash, this suggests the religious atmosphere was more pluralistic than Kaufmann allows. The archaeological record — figurines, cult stands, inscriptions — suggests polytheistic practice was widespread at all levels.

**Alternative reading:** The Solomonic period was one of religious pluralism — Yahweh worship alongside the cults of other deities — that the Deuteronomistic historians later framed as "Solomon's sin" to explain the kingdom's division.

## Assessment

One of the weaker sections of the chapter. Kaufmann's admission that "vestigial idolatry existed among the vulgar, both before and after" is a significant concession that sits awkwardly with his broader thesis. What exactly distinguishes "vestigial idolatry" that has always existed from the "idolatry" that the Bible claims began with Solomon? The distinction seems driven by Kaufmann's need to protect his thesis rather than by the evidence.
