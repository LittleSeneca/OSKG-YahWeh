# fleming-ybi-2.1: The Egyptian hieroglyphic writing y-h-w3 is phonologically the same name as the Hebrew Tetragrammaton YHWH

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 2 — Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 2

## The Claim

"In agreement with a significant majority of scholars, I conclude that the first question demands with high likelihood an affirmative response: the names are truly the same" (Fleming, p. 43). The hieroglyphic writing y-h-w3 is "clearly syllabic": <y> (double reed leaf) always renders /ya/, <h> is a single consonant without vowel marker, <w3> is almost certainly /wa/ — per Thomas Schneider.

## Evidence

- The hieroglyphic writing y-h-w3 is "clearly syllabic": <y> (double reed leaf) always renders /ya/, <h> is a single consonant without vowel marker, <w3> is almost certainly /wa/ — per Thomas Schneider (p. 59, n.a)
- Final <w3> could also represent /we/; in either case the name ends in an open vowel
- The match is "not particularly strong" only to skeptics (Goedicke 1994, Ahlström 1986), while a long list accepts it: Weippert (1974), Astour (1979), Redford (1992: 272-73), van der Toorn (1999: 911-12), Schneider (2007), Adrom and Müller (2017)
- The occurrence as "a major identity among the Shasu people" supports the connection

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The phonological match is precise and the scholarly consensus is overwhelming. Even Adrom and Müller (2017), who are cautious Egyptologists without stake in the Yahweh debate, accept the reading without objection.

## Stakes

If the match is rejected, the earliest evidence for the name Yahweh disappears entirely, and the entire historical investigation loses its oldest anchor. Fleming's argument doesn't strictly require the match (he could argue from the poetic/biblical evidence alone), but it gives his reconstruction a 14th-century BCE starting point.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Goedicke (1994), Ahlström (1986: 57-60). Mark Smith (2001a: 25) calls the evidence "not particularly strong."

**Alternative reading:** The phonetic similarity could be coincidental — a Shasu subgroup name that happens to resemble the later divine name but has no historical connection.

## Assessment

The phonological case is strong, and the burden of proof is on skeptics. The consonants y-h-w are too distinctive for coincidence, and the Egyptian syllabic orthography maps cleanly. Fleming's argument doesn't depend on certainty here — he acknowledges it remains a probability — but the rest of the book is built on the assumption that the match is real.
