# keel-ggi-4.2: The "Lord of the Ostriches" as Dominant Indigenous Male Deity in Iron IIA Glyptic

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part IV — Iron Age IIA]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part IV

## The Claim

The "Lord of the Ostriches" appears as the most common indigenous anthropomorphic figure in Iron IIA glyptic art. Unlike the Egyptian-influenced solar figures on Philistine scarabs, this figure belongs to the local steppe/desert tradition — and may represent an early iconographic expression of Yahweh. The ostrich motif develops from an anthropomorphic "Lord" (center, frontal, raised arms) to the ostrich alone, without the human/divine figure — the same substitution pattern seen in goddess → tree abstraction.

## Evidence

- "The ostrich, whose relation to humans is not always easy to figure out and that is tamed only with great difficulty, represents not only a deserted, dangerous and sinister world but also a numinous power that commands respect and honor because it can survive mysteriously at the edge of habitable land"
- The motif develops from an anthropomorphic "Lord" to the ostrich alone — "the likelihood that this substitution did occur is shown, for example, on two scaraboids made at the same workshop in Lachish. One shows the traditional constellation while the other shows two ostriches... without any human figure"
- Ostrich + caprid combinations without any divine figure appear on 10th-9th century scaraboids
- The steppe/desert habitat of the ostrich connects to early Yahweh traditions associated with Seir, Paran, Teman, and Midian (cf. Deut 33:2; Judg 5:4-5; Hab 3:3)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The steppe associations are suggestive but indirect. There is no inscription identifying the "Lord of the Ostriches" as Yahweh specifically.

## Stakes

If this figure IS an early Yahweh, it suggests Yahweh's earliest iconography was not borrowed from Baal or El but was indigenous — a steppe/desert deity associated with the ostrich, a creature of the wilderness. This aligns with the "Yahweh from the south" (Midianite/Kenite) hypothesis.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Yahweh as originally a Canaanite deity assimilated from El or Baal. Also those who argue Yahweh was aniconic from the beginning.

**Alternative reading:** The "Lord of the Ostriches" could represent any number of local numinous powers, not necessarily Yahweh. The ostrich motif could be decorative rather than theological.

## Assessment

The substitution pattern (anthropomorphic lord → ostrich alone) is the same mechanism we see for goddess → tree. It is part of the broader aniconic trend. Whether specifically Yahweh or not, it shows that even male deity figures were being abstracted into attribute animals during Iron IIA.
