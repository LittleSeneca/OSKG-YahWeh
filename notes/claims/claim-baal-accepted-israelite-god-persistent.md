# smith-ehg-2.1: Baal worship was a persistent, accepted feature of Israelite religion from the Judges period through the fall of Judah

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 2 — Yahweh and Baal]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2002), Chapter 2: Yahweh and Baal

## The Claim

Baal worship was a persistent feature of Israelite religion for centuries, not a brief northern apostasy under Ahab and Jezebel. The ninth-century conflict was an innovation driven by political tensions with Phoenicia, not a return to original purity. "There is no evidence that prior to the ninth century Baal was considered a major threat to the cult of Yahweh."

Smith partially rehabilitates Ahab: the biblical portrait is "a considerable degree of negative typecasting." Ahab's program "perhaps did not initially include the eradication of the cult of Yahweh." The Elijah conflict was a clash of THREE visions: (1) Ahab/Jezebel elevating Baal alongside Yahweh, (2) Elijah's Yahweh-alone, (3) the traditionalist coexistence.

## Evidence

- **Baal-names in Saul's Yahwistic family** (covered in Ch. 1): a Yahwistic royal family using Baal theophoric names suggests Baal was not considered alien
- **Samaria ostraca** (9th-8th c.): 5 Baal names vs. 9 Yahweh names — a high ratio for the capital city
- **Ahab's ambiguous apostasy:** He built a Baal temple but "was not quite the apostate... his sons bear Yahwistic names. He consults Yahwistic prophets. He repents before Elijah."
- **Jehu's reform was incomplete:** Kuntillet Ajrud texts mention Baal AND Yahweh together, with Hebrew vowel letters (not Phoenician script — these are Israelite texts, not imported)
- **Hosea** (8th c.) still polemicizes against Baal; his oracle in 2:23-24 directly parallels Baal's speech to Anat in the Ugaritic Baal cycle (KTU 1.3 III)
- **Reform cycle:** Hezekiah tried to eliminate Baal → Manasseh restored him → Josiah purged him again
- **Jeremiah** condemns Baal worship in the final days of Judah
- "On the whole Baal was an accepted Israelite god... this god remained popular through the end of the southern kingdom."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The cumulative evidence from the Judges period through Jeremiah is overwhelming; the persistence of Baal AFTER multiple reform attempts proves popular demand, not just royal sponsorship.

## Stakes

If Baal was always part of Israelite religion, the biblical narrative of a pure Yahwism corrupted by foreign queens is false. The Elijah stories are not about restoring lost purity — they're about INNOVATING a new exclusivity. Elijah wasn't a conservative; he was a radical.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue the biblical portrait of Ahab as apostate is historically reliable. The Baal-names could reflect the problem the Bible describes (syncretism), not evidence there was no problem.

**Alternative reading:** Ahab really WAS an apostate who tried to replace Yahweh with Baal. The Yahwistic names of his sons reflect court convention, not personal theology. Jehu's reform really DID suppress Baal worship, and its later resurgence represents backsliding, not continuity. This requires trusting the biblical narrative more than Smith does.

## Assessment

Smith's strongest evidence is the persistence of Baal AFTER multiple reform attempts. If Hezekiah purged Baal and Josiah purged him again, Baal kept coming back — which suggests popular demand, not just royal sponsorship. The cumulative timeline from Judges through Jeremiah is persuasive regardless of how you read Ahab. The question isn't whether Baal was worshipped (he was) but whether this represents "indigenous Israelite religion" (Smith) or "persistent syncretistic corruption" (traditional). That's a framing question, not an evidence question.
