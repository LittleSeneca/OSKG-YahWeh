# sommer-bog-5-6.3: The Trinity is fluid monotheism, not polytheism

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapters 5-6 + Appendix]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 6

## The Claim

"The doctrine of the Trinity represents a particular version of the theology of divine fluidity." Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: three persons, one God. Three bodies (the Son's incarnation; perhaps the Father's in some traditions), one divine self. From the ANE perspective, the Trinity is perfectly intelligible as fluid monotheism — one deity with multiple manifestations that are distinct yet are the same being.

This has significant implications for Jewish-Christian dialogue. Judaism has historically perceived the Trinity as polytheistic because Judaism internalized anti-fluid monotheism so deeply that ANY multiplicity in God looks like polytheism. But if the ANE fluidity model is the proper context, the Trinity is not polytheism — it's one legitimate monotheistic development of the biblical inheritance, while rabbinic anti-fluid monotheism is another. Both are monotheistic; they just developed different possibilities within the fluidity/anti-fluidity tension.

> "The Trinity is not polytheism. It is a legitimate monotheistic development of the ANE fluidity model. Judaism rejected this model in its mainstream; Christianity embraced it. Both are monotheistic — they just developed different possibilities within the biblical inheritance."

## Evidence

- **ANE parallel:** Mesopotamian Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh were distinct goddesses receiving separate cult, yet were "functionally one and the same" — the Trinity's three-persons-one-God structure has an ANE precedent
- **Multiple manifestations:** ANE gods had multiple local manifestations with partial independence; the Trinity's persons have distinct roles/identities while being the one God
- **Embodiment:** The incarnation (Son has a body) is a specific case of the ANE pattern of divine embodiment — the difference is that Christianity has ONE incarnation rather than multiple cult statues
- **Jewish perception:** Jews perceive the Trinity as polytheistic because rabbinic Judaism's anti-fluid framework cannot process multiplicity within unity — but from the ANE fluidity framework, the Trinity is monotheistic
- **Christianity as fluidity's heir:** Christianity embraced the fluidity model that mainstream Judaism rejected — the incarnation is the ultimate expression of divine embodiment

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The structural parallel is clear, but this is a theological claim, not a historical one; Christians have their own theological language for the Trinity (persons, substance, relations) that does not map perfectly onto Sommer's fluidity categories.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, the Jewish-Christian debate over monotheism is based on a category mistake. Jews accuse Christians of polytheism because Jews measure Christianity against anti-fluid monotheism. Christians defend the Trinity using Greek philosophical categories (ousia, hypostasis) that neither Jews nor the biblical writers used. The ANE fluidity model provides a third framework in which both traditions are monotheistic — they simply resolved the fluidity/anti-fluidity tension differently. This doesn't settle the theological debate, but it reframes it in historically more accurate terms.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Jewish theologians who maintain that the Trinity IS polytheism regardless of historical context. Christian theologians who insist the Trinity is sui generis and cannot be explained by ANE parallels. Historians who argue that Christian Trinitarianism developed from Greek philosophical categories, not from ANE/Semitic fluidity patterns.

**Alternative reading:** The Trinity emerged from Hellenistic Jewish speculation about divine intermediaries (Logos, Wisdom, Spirit), combined with the Christian experience of Jesus as divine. The ANE fluidity model is an interesting parallel but not the genealogy of the doctrine.

## Assessment

This is Sommer's most provocative claim and the one most vulnerable to critique. The structural parallel between ANE fluidity and the Trinity is real, but Sommer's argument conflates structural analogy with historical genealogy. The Trinity may LOOK like fluid monotheism, but that doesn't mean it developed FROM fluid monotheism. Christian theologians will push back on having their central doctrine explained by Mesopotamian cultic practice. Jewish theologians will push back on having their monotheism-polytheism distinction relativized. Sommer's claim is valuable not because it settles the debate but because it exposes how much of the Jewish-Christian dispute over monotheism rests on unexamined assumptions about what "monotheism" means.
