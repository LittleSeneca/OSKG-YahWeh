# day-ygc-8.4: Canaanite deities left an "afterglow" in Jewish monotheism as angels, demons, and apocalyptic imagery

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 8 — Conclusion — Rise of Monotheism]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 8

## The Claim

Even after absolute monotheism was established, Canaanite deities "sometimes left a kind of 'afterglow'" — most marked in apocalyptic literature (p. 233). The final summary: "Although these images lived on, everything became transformed in the light of monotheism, and it is arguably monotheism (at first monolatry), rather than God's mighty acts in history as used to be argued, that most distinguishes the Old Testament from the religions of the other nations of the ancient Near East" (p. 234).

## Evidence

1. **Seventy sons of God → seventy guardian angels:** Originally gods of El's pantheon, demoted to angel status in 1 Enoch and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (p. 233)
2. **Baal-zebul → Beelzebul/Beelzebub → Satan:** The Prince Baal became the Prince of Demons in the Gospels (p. 233)
3. **El and Baal → Ancient of Days and Son of Man:** Daniel 7 preserves the relationship of aged El and cloud-riding Baal in apocalyptic form (p. 233-234)
4. **Leviathan → seven-headed dragon of Revelation:** The chaos monster survives as Satan (Rev 12) and Rome (Rev 13) (p. 234)
5. **Baal's resurrection → resurrection of the dead:** The ultimate origin of Jewish/Christian resurrection belief (p. 234)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This "afterglow" thesis is the logical culmination of all seven preceding chapters. Each case is individually argued in its respective chapter.

## Stakes

Monotheism was not a clean break from polytheism but a transformation of polytheistic material. The Canaanite gods didn't disappear — they were demoted, demonized, or absorbed. The OT's distinctiveness is not its mythological content but its theological framework.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israel's radical distinctiveness (Kaufmann school) would reject the continuity thesis. Scholars who see the apocalyptic material as late and marginal would downplay its significance for understanding Israelite religion proper.

**Alternative reading:** The apocalyptic "survivals" are too late and too marginal to represent genuine continuity. They reflect the creative reuse of biblical motifs, not the survival of Canaanite religious consciousness.

## Assessment

Day's final sentence is his most important theological claim. He concedes that the mythological CONTENT is Canaanite while arguing that the monotheistic FRAMEWORK is Israel's distinctive contribution. This is a more sophisticated position than either "Israel was just Canaanite" or "Israel was wholly unique" — it acknowledges genuine continuity AND genuine innovation.
