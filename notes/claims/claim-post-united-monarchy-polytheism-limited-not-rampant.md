# tigay-nog-1.3: Post-United Monarchy polytheism was limited, not rampant

**Source:** [[Tigay — Chapter I — The Onomastic Evidence]] — Jeffrey H. Tigay, *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986), Chapter I

## The Claim

"After the United Monarchy, and perhaps even earlier, the evidence currently available makes it very difficult to suppose that many Israelites worshipped gods other than YHWH." This is the central conclusion Tigay draws from the combined biblical and epigraphic onomastic evidence. Polytheism existed, but it was a minority phenomenon, not the dominant religious posture of Israelite society.

## Evidence

- Biblical onomasticon: 89% Yahwistic names; pagan names drop from 14% (Judges/United Monarchy) to 3% (Divided Monarchy)
- Epigraphic onomasticon: even MORE Yahwistic than the Bible, with only a handful of plausibly pagan names across all pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions
- The ba'al name revisions prove scribal sensitivity to pagan theophorics AND that the remaining names reflect genuine naming practices — not systematic censorship
- The evidence converges: both the edited biblical text and the unedited inscriptions point to overwhelming YHWH-devotion

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Well-supported by Tigay's onomastic data, but depends on how representative the inscriptions are of the full population — women, rural peasants, and non-literate Israelites are underrepresented in the epigraphic record.

## Stakes

This is the direct empirical challenge to the Smith/Römer/Dever consensus that pre-exilic Israel was pervasively polytheistic. If Tigay is right, the consensus narrative of "monotheism as late elite invention" must be qualified: the populace was already overwhelmingly YHWH-devoted (even if not exclusively monotheistic) long before the exile.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-tigay-onomastics-does-not-disprove-polytheism]] (Smith), [[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]] (Day), [[claim-dever-folk-polytheism-outlasted-monarchy-monotheism-book-religion]] (Dever)

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data could be right about ONOMASTICS without being right about RELIGION. The inscriptions come disproportionately from administrative/military contexts — the very circles most likely to be Yahwistic. Women and rural peasants are underrepresented. The figurines (Dever's evidence) are a different dataset from the names (Tigay's evidence). Both could be right: the names show what people SAID (YHWH-devotion); the figurines show what people DID (goddess worship). One shows the public face; the other shows the private practice.

## Assessment

Tigay's conclusion is well-supported by HIS data. The question is whether his data (names and inscriptions) captures the FULL picture of Israelite religion, or only the official/elite/literate dimension. Dever's figurines tell a different story. They're not contradictory — they're complementary. One shows the public face. The other shows the private practice.
