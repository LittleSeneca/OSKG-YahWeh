# keel-ggi-p2.5: Beth-Shean shows extreme Egyptian state religion — Amun, pharaoh, exclusion of goddesses from monuments

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

At Beth-Shean, "the indigenous tradition survives in the later strata only on inexpensive terra-cotta images, contrasting with the monumental art and image-bearing artifacts made with precious materials" which are "purely Egyptian, both in style and theme." On the Ramesses II monuments: "No goddess is to be seen on any of these royal monuments. It is equally remarkable how much the royal god Amun is emphasized at the time of Ramesses II."

## Evidence

- **Strategic location:** Controls eastern entrance of Jezreel Valley at crossroads of major routes — "this important strategic location was probably what made Beth-Shean an important Egyptian center from the fifteenth century on."
- **Mekal stele:** Enthroned "Mekal, the god of Beth-Shean" and Egyptian architect Amen-em-Opet — "fashioned in a pure Egyptian style."
- **Goddess in costly vs. cheap media:** Gold pendants and stelae show goddess in Egyptian style — clothed, holding was-scepter and ankh, wearing atef-crown. Identified as "Anat, Mistress of Heaven, Mistress of the Gods" on one stele. But terra-cotta goddesses continue in indigenous Canaanite form: naked, holding breasts or children. "The nakedness of the goddess on the terra-cottas is a typically Canaanite feature. The Canaanite nursing goddess is an indigenous form, not just a local adaptation of an Egyptian theme."
- **Cultic stands with goddess imagery:** From 12th century south temple — goddess in doorway holding doves, with snake below; fragmentary piece with naked seated figure, doves, lions, and snakes. "Doves and lions continue to be made as terra-cotta figures."
- **Anat as warrior goddess:** Cylinder seal from Bethel shows Anat holding lance, facing Baal-Seth. Jerusalem-purchased scarab shows Anat on horseback, wearing atef-crown, swinging scimitar over fallen enemy.
- **Royal stelae of Sety I and Ramesses II:** Sety I stele (2.45 m) — pharaoh worshiping Re-harakhty. Ramesses II stele (2.67 m) — Amun presenting scimitar of victory to pharaoh.
- **Ramesses II cylinder seal:** Asiatic god (possibly Reshef-Mekal) presents scimitar to pharaoh — rare fusion of Egyptian royal ideology with local deity.
- **Amun dominance:** During Eighteenth Dynasty, 22 of 38 double-sided plaques (58%) name Amun. In Nineteenth Dynasty, Amun appears anthropomorphically, as ram-headed god, or ram sphinx. Clay loaf of bread stamped repeatedly with *Ymn-R'* suggests "a particularly strong Amun cult in Beth-Shean."
- **Official loyalty monuments:** Megiddo plaque shows vizier revering Amun; Megiddo ivory shows official kneeling before Amun; door lintels show officials revering king; nearly complete seated statue of Ramesses III found at Beth-Shean.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Beth-Shean is the most extensively excavated Egyptian administrative center in Canaan, and the monumental evidence is unambiguous. Medium on the Anat identification — Helck (1962) argued against identifying unarmed goddess on stele as Anat.

## Stakes

Beth-Shean is a laboratory for what Egyptian colonialism did to indigenous religion. The split between monumental (Egyptian, male-dominated) and terra-cotta (indigenous, goddess-inclusive) religious expression is starkest here. If this split was typical of Egyptian-controlled cities, then the "official religion" of LB Canaan was essentially the Egyptian state cult — Amun, Re-harakhty, Ptah, the divine pharaoh — while indigenous gods and goddesses survived in domestic, private, and cheaper forms. This is the archaeological precedent for Dever's argument that "book religion" and "folk religion" coexisted throughout the monarchy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Helck (1962) disputes Anat identification on stele. Thompson (1970) studied Mekal as local city god. Rowe's original reports interpreted finds differently.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of goddesses from royal monuments could reflect Egyptian royal ideology (always male-focused) rather than deliberate suppression. The pharaoh's relationship with Amun and Re-harakhty was a theological-political program that didn't necessarily displace local cults — it just didn't depict them on royal monuments. The Anat stele and goddess cultic stands *do* exist — goddess worship wasn't suppressed, it just wasn't "state religion."

## Assessment

Keel & Uehlinger's argument for a split between official and private religion is strongest at Beth-Shean. The contrast between the monumental stelae (all male gods and pharaoh) and the terra-cotta cultic stands (goddess with doves, lions, snakes) is so stark that it can't be accidental. The key takeaway: the gender of divinity in official/public religion was already heavily masculine in the LB — well before Yahwism's emergence. The Hebrew Bible's male monotheism didn't invent the suppression of goddesses; it continued a LB trajectory.
