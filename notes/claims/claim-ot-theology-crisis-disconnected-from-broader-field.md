# schmid-ht-preface.1: OT theology as a discipline is in crisis because it has lost integration with the broader field

**Source:** [[Schmid — Preface]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), Preface

## The Claim

The subdiscipline of OT theology maintains an "almost extra-territorial existence" — it is "not really integrated into the communal discourse of Hebrew Bible studies." While introductory studies, the history of Israel, and the history of Israelite religion have undergone "enormous upheavals in the past forty years," theology has "remained comparatively diffuse."

## Evidence

- The major theologies have been published by scholars at progressively older ages (Eichrodt at 43 in 1933 → Jeremias at 76 in 2015)
- Von Rad's 1957 work set such a high standard that it produced a "ten years' pause in the stream of OT theologies"
- Important drafts have been published in recent years, but unlike the close interaction in non-theological subdisciplines, theology has not integrated new conclusions

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The aging curve of authors is an empirical observation. The disconnect between theology and other subdisciplines is widely acknowledged in the field — Schmid's diagnosis of a "mechanization of biblical studies" when theological questions are neglected is a pointed but fair critique of German-language scholarship.

## Stakes

If Schmid is right that theology has become disconnected from critical scholarship, then theologies written without engagement with recent literary-historical findings (especially late dating) are methodologically obsolete. This sets up his entire project: a theology that takes the new literary history seriously.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue that OT theology has recently "blossomed" — Hermann Spieckermann, Friedhelm Hartenstein. Schmid acknowledges their work but says a "blossoming" requires "rootedness in the topsoil of Hebrew Bible studies."

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that theology's separateness is a feature, not a bug — it pursues different questions than history or literary criticism. The "lack of integration" is appropriate to a discipline with different aims.

## Assessment

Schmid's diagnosis rings true. The late-dating revolution in European scholarship has massive implications for theology that most theologies have not yet absorbed. Schmid's project — letting the new literary history drive theological conclusions — is the right response.
