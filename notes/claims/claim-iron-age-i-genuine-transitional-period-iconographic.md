# keel-ggi-5.1: Iron Age I is a genuine transitional period — the Late Bronze urban system collapsed, but the iconographic repertoire shows both continuity with Egyptian traditions and radical innovation

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

The period is "a typical transitional period, marked by overlapping trends." At Hazor, "the settlement located in the lower city ceased to exist and from that point on people lived almost exclusively up on the tell." At Megiddo, the Late Bronze city was destroyed by the mid-twelfth century, and Str. VIB preserves "a small and poorly built village." At Lachish, "a gap in settlements followed, lasting some two hundred years."

Yet iconographically, "despite all the efforts to distance themselves from Egypt and their desire to be emancipated from this colonial power that had ruled in the previous age, the inhabitants in the southern coastal plain continue to use Egyptian-style glyptic art themes." Egyptian traditions survived — but specific Egyptian markers (the ram sphinx, the falcon-head of Horus, the uraeus and crown on the archer) disappeared. The result is a hybrid: Egyptian-derived motifs stripped of their most identifiably Egyptian features and adapted into a local repertoire.

## Evidence

- Settlement archaeology: Hazor Str. XII-XI (semi-nomadic villages), Megiddo Str. VIB (poor village) → VIA (Philistine center) → VB (poor again), Lachish (200-year gap), Beth-Shean (no true Iron I village stratum)
- The Megiddo hoard (Str. VI A, 11th century): 32 seal amulets found in a painted vessel with seven handles, 25 identifiable — "the name 'Amun' dominates, by far, all other motifs," appearing on 36% of the pieces
- Continuity of Egyptian themes (Amun cryptography, pharaoh-as-lion, chariot/archer motifs) alongside disappearance of specifically Egyptian iconographic markers
- New local production: black calcite conoids, crude limestone seals — indigenous manufacture replacing Egyptian imports

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The archaeological evidence for settlement disruption is independently established. The iconographic pattern of Egyptian continuity-with-stripping is documented across multiple site strata and seal types.

## Stakes

This establishes that Iron Age I is not a "dark age" devoid of religious evidence but a period with its own coherent iconographic system — one that both inherits from the Late Bronze Age and transforms that inheritance in ways that will shape Iron Age II. The "gap" is in urban architecture, not in religious imagery.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat Iron Age I as archaeologically invisible for religious history (a common tendency given the paucity of temple architecture). Keel and Uehlinger's seal-amulet approach fills precisely this gap.

**Alternative reading:** The disappearance of Egyptian-specific markers could reflect loss of technical skill by local seal-cutters rather than deliberate theological choice. The "adaptation" may be accidental degradation.

## Assessment

The Megiddo hoard alone — 32 seal amulets from a single stratified context, with Amun cryptography dominating — is a powerful data point. The authors are right that Iron Age I has been underrated as a period of religious change because the evidence is glyptic rather than architectural.
