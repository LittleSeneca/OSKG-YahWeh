# lewis-ocg-3.4: Albertz Revolutionized Onomastics — Personal Names Reflect Personal Piety, Not State Religion

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 3 — Methodology]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Albertz "argued that [questions about pantheon reduction and Yahweh's dominance] were sorely misguided, as they largely ignored the social context of personal names." Instead, "the verbs and nouns in the large majority of personal names were expressions of personal piety. Rather than reflecting matters of state and priestly cult, the petitions, thanksgivings, hopes for salvation, confessions, and concerns about the birth process that are found in names had more to do with personal religion and life cycle events."

## Evidence

- Lewis cites Albertz's three defining works (1978, 1994, 2012 with Schmitt)
- Tigay's cautions: onomastic habits change slowly, so names can persist after devotion shifts; political pressure can produce Yahwistic names for polytheistic families (e.g., Ahab's children)
- Pardee's genre-specific analysis: different divine names appear in different text types — mythological vs. ritual vs. onomastic
- "Asherah" appears only once as a theophoric element in Ugaritic personal names despite a vibrant cult attested in other genres — demonstrating that onomastic frequency does not map directly to cultic importance
- The verbs and nouns in theophoric names cluster around birth, protection, thanksgiving, and petition — life-cycle concerns, not state theology

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High that Albertz's approach is methodologically superior to simple distribution-counting; medium-high on how much can actually be recovered — the cautions from Tigay and Pardee are significant.

## Stakes

If Albertz is right, onomastics tell us about family religion, not about what percentage of Israelites were Yahwists. The long-standing debate about whether Israelites were mostly polytheistic or mostly Yahwistic (Tigay vs. Dever, essentially) changes shape: names answer a different question than the one most scholars have been asking.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Tigay (who Lewis respects and cites) would say onomastics CAN be used for distribution analysis if properly controlled. Hess has used onomastics to argue for widespread Yahweh worship. The debate is about controls, not about whether names contain religious information.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's approach — using onomastics with proper controls — can still yield distribution data. The question is what control framework to use, not whether onomastics are useful at all.

## Assessment

This is genuinely important. Lewis's treatment of onomastics is more sophisticated than most comparable works (Dever's, for instance, uses onomastics more crudely). The Albertz-Pardee-Tigay triangulation is the right framework.
