# fleming-ybi-4.4: The old poetry is probably monarchic (9th-8th centuries BCE), not pre-monarchic

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 4 — The Old Poetry]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"It is important to recognize the possibility, or I would say likelihood, that all of the texts in view were composed to serve monarchic interests" (Fleming, p. 179). "In this early first-millennium vision, Yahweh is a god who fights for Israel as a people under royal leadership. This poetry represents a tradition of monarchy" (p. 180).

## Evidence

- The "revision through introduction" in Judges 5 (the hymn, vv. 2-11) transforms a battle alliance into "Israel" — "most easily understood as an effort serving kings" (Stahl 2020, cited p. 180)
- Deuteronomy 33 presents "the tribes of Israel as one" gathered around divine kingship ("there was a king in Jeshurun," v. 5) — monarchic vocabulary (p. 156)
- Habakkuk 3 references "your anointed" (māšîaḥ, v. 13) — a king (p. 165)
- Psalm 68 is broadly considered a Jerusalem composition from the monarchic period
- The Mesha inscription (9th c.) shows the same pattern: Kamosh fights for Moab as Yahweh fights for Israel — national gods under royal patronage
- Even Pfeiffer (2017), who radically down-dates most old poetry, considers the Song of Deborah core to be 9th/8th century (p. 141, n.24)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The monarchic dating for the texts as we have them is well-supported. But whether the southern theophany motif itself is a monarchic invention or an older tradition appropriated by monarchic writers is harder to settle. Fleming acknowledges: "We cannot discern the origin of this poetic motif... it is important to recognize the possibility... that all of the texts in view were composed to serve monarchic interests" (p. 179).

## Stakes

If the poetry is monarchic, Yahweh's southern associations were promoted by kings for ideological reasons — projecting royal power into the wilderness, claiming the mysterious divine warrior for the state. This undermines the idea that the poetry preserves pre-monarchic "memory" of Yahweh's origins.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Albright, Cross, Freedman, and their students treated the poetry as pre-monarchic (12th-11th centuries). Smith (2014) defends substantial antiquity while acknowledging the need for caution.

**Alternative reading:** The archaic linguistic features of Judges 5 (long hair for battle, "routes," "village militia" — all in the introductory hymn per Smith 2014: 219) suggest pre-monarchic composition, even if the texts were later adapted for monarchic use.

## Edges

**Supports:**
- [[claim-southern-geography-theophany-not-origin-memory]] — if the poetry is monarchic, the southern geography was promoted by kings as royal ideology, not preserved from pre-monarchic memory
- [[claim-old-poetry-theophanic-movement-not-yahweh-origin-ugaritic-analogy]] — the Ugaritic analogy gains force if the poetry is monarchic: Israelite kings adopted the West Semitic divine-warrior motif the same way they adopted royal ideology
- [[claim-four-biblical-poems-preserve-southern-origin-tradition]] — if the poetry is monarchic royal ideology, it cannot simultaneously preserve pre-monarchic memory of Yhwh's southern origin; Römer's reading requires the poetry to be both old (pre-monarchic origin memory) and adaptable (monarchic royal ideology)

## Assessment

Fleming shifts the burden of proof. The poetry as we have it looks monarchic. If you want to claim it preserves pre-monarchic tradition, you need to demonstrate that, not just assume it. The archaic features could be deliberate archaism or genuinely old vocabulary preserved in later compositions.
