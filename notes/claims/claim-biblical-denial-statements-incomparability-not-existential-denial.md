# heiser-ur-4.3: Biblical denial statements are incomparability claims, not existential denials

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 2 — The Households of God]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 4

## The Claim

When Deut 4:35 says "there is none besides him," it means no other *elohim* COMPARES to Yahweh — not that they don't EXIST. The same Hebrew idiom is used of Babylon (Isaiah 47:8: "there is none besides me") and Nineveh (Zephaniah 2:15: same phrase), and those are clearly incomparability claims, not existential denials — other cities obviously existed.

## Evidence

**The city analogy:**
- Isaiah 47:8: Babylon says "there is none besides me" — is this a denial that other cities exist? "That would be absurd."
- Zephaniah 2:15: Nineveh says the same thing — same Hebrew idiom, same incomparability meaning

**The logical argument:**
- Psalm 97:9 extols Yahweh's superiority ABOVE all *elohim* — "How is it coherent to say that verses extolling the superiority of Yahweh above all *elohim* are really telling us Yahweh is greater than beings that don't exist? To my ear, it mocks God to say, 'You're greater than something that doesn't exist.' So is my dog."

**Deuteronomy's own testimony:**
- Deut 4:35 says there's no god besides Yahweh
- Deut 4:19-20 acknowledges other gods were "allotted" to the nations
- Deut 32:17 refers to *elohim* that Paul (1 Cor 10:20) took as literally existing
- "Is Scripture filled with contradictions? No."

**Interpretive resolution:** Read the denial statements as incomparability and all passages cohere. Read them as existential denials and the Bible contradicts itself.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The analogy to Babylon and Nineveh's self-descriptions is strong — the same Hebrew idiom is used for cities, which obviously don't mean other cities don't exist. The interpretive payoff (coherence across Deuteronomy) is significant.

## Stakes

If denial statements are incomparability, the entire "monotheism emerged in the exile" timeline collapses. Deutero-Isaiah's "no god besides me" is not a new theological breakthrough — it's the same incomparability language that predates the exile. The biblical writers always acknowledged other *elohim* existed; they simply denied any of them compared to Yahweh. "Monotheism" defined as "only one divine being exists" is not a biblical category.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Deutero-Isaiah as the first consistent monotheism (Römer, Smith, Albertz, Day). They read "there is no god besides me" as a genuine existential denial — a theological innovation that emerged in response to the exile. Kaufmann would agree with Heiser that monotheism was early, but for different reasons — Kaufmann reads the denial statements as existential, not incomparability.

**Alternative reading:** Deutero-Isaiah represents a genuine theological breakthrough from monolatry (other gods exist but only Yahweh is worshipped) to monotheism (other gods don't exist). The denial statements in Deutero-Isaiah ARE existential, and they mark a development beyond earlier incomparability rhetoric.

## Assessment

This is Heiser's strongest chapter. The city analogy is genuinely clever — if "there is none besides me" means "I'm the only city that exists" when Babylon says it, the phrase is nonsense. The interpretive payoff is that Deuteronomy, which contains both denial statements AND acknowledgment of other gods, becomes coherent rather than contradictory. The question is whether the denial statements EVOLVED from incomparability (early) to existential denial (late) — if so, Heiser is right about the early texts but wrong to apply the same reading to Deutero-Isaiah.
