# albertz-hir-3.3-3.4.1: The monarchy created a new type of cult — the royal state sanctuary fusing throne and altar

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.3-3.4 — State Cults of Jerusalem and Bethel]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.3

## The Claim

The monarchy was "an important turning point for the history of the main cult." Royal sanctuaries emerged that were "the property and the private chapel of the king" while claiming to be the public sanctuary of the kingdom. This "set in motion a centralization of the main Israelite cult which later... was to issue in the Deuteronomic requirement of the abolition of all local cults outside Jerusalem." It led to a "close institutional fusion of political power and the cult."

## Evidence

- Amos 7:13 calls Bethel "house of the kingdom" (bêt mamlākâ) and "sanctuary of the king" (miqdāš hammelek)
- State financing transformed the cult: the temple had unprecedented buildings, vessels, personnel, and festival scale — "could only be financed and organized by a system of state taxation"
- The Jerusalem temple and palace formed a single complex (1 Kings 7:12; Ezek 43:8)
- Priests were "royal officials" and sometimes members of the royal family (2 Sam 8:17-18)
- The king exercised priestly functions: sacrifice, blessing, intercession

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The fusion of throne and altar in the Jerusalem temple is well-documented both textually and archaeologically.

## Stakes

If the royal state sanctuary was the monarchy's innovation, then Deuteronomy's centralization — often seen as a radical reform — was actually the logical culmination of a process the monarchy itself began. The Deuteronomists completed what the monarchy started.

## Disagreement

<!-- Few scholars dispute the fusion of throne and altar; debates center on its theological significance -->

**Alternative reading:** The royal sanctuary may have been less novel than Albertz claims — pre-state sanctuaries like Shiloh may have had some centralized functions, and the distinction between "royal" and "public" sanctuary may not have been as sharp as Amos 7:13 suggests.

## Assessment

High confidence on the factual claim, but Albertz's narrative use of it (the monarchy began a centralization process that would eventually destroy all local cults) is analytically sharp. The irony he identifies — the state created the institution that would later be used to justify the state's theological monopoly — is genuinely important.
