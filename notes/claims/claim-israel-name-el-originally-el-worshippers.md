# romer-inv-4.1: The name "Israel" is an El name — the group originally venerated El

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 4 — How Yhwh Became Israel's God]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"Israel" contains the theophoric element *'el* — the proper name of the Canaanite god El or a generic term for "god." Whatever the original etymology, it is an EL name, not a Yhwh name. "If Yhwh had been the original god, the group would be named something like *Yisra-yahu*" — but it isn't. Israel originally belonged to El. Yhwh was adopted later.

## Evidence

**Three proposed etymologies:**

| Etymology | Meaning | Evidence | Romer's Assessment |
|-----------|---------|----------|-------------------|
| From *s-r-h* ("combat") | "May El combat" | The biblical folk etymology (Gen 32:29, Hos 12:4). Jacob "struggled with God." | **Unlikely.** This root is attested in Hebrew only in these two texts, and poorly in other Semitic languages. |
| From *y-s-r* ("to be just") | "El is just" | Poetic name Yeshurun for Israel (Deut 32:15; 33:5, 26). A Ugaritic name *y-s-r-'i-l* on a military charioteer list from the late 13th century — contemporary with Merneptah. | **Possible but problematic.** "A name on a list in Ugarit need not necessarily be in any way connected with the biblical Israel." Hebrew vocalization favors a preformative conjugation. |
| From *s-r-r* ("rule, govern, reign") | **"May El reign" / "May El impose himself as master"** | **Romer's preferred reading.** Supported by a Masoretic revision at Hos 12:5 that he argues conceals the original meaning. Names like *Serayahu* ("Yhwh reigns") use the same root with Yhwh. | **Most plausible.** The root fits El's role as chief god and king of the pantheon. The folk etymology ("combat") supplanted this older meaning when Yhwh the warrior god replaced El. |

**The Masoretic revision at Hosea 12:5:**
- The MT reads: "He struggled with an angel and he had the upper hand" (*wayyasar 'el-mal'ak wayyukal*)
- Romer argues this is "the result of a dogmatic revision." The Masoretes "wished to avoid too great a closeness between Jacob and God, and so they inserted the word *mal'ak* ('angel')"
- The original text: "El imposed himself and gained the upper hand" (*wayyasar 'el wayyukal*)
- The consonants *'l* originally meant the god El (*'el*); the Masoretes changed the vocalization to make it the preposition *'el* ("toward")

**The further development:** "The folk etymology using the root *s-r-h* ('combat')... was able to supplant the original etymology at the moment when Yhwh, a warrior god, became the tutelary god of the group 'Israel.'" The name was re-etymologized to fit the new god's warrior profile.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The theophoric element *'el* in "Israel" is unambiguous — it's an El name regardless of which etymology is correct. Romer's preferred etymology ("May El reign") is only at MEDIUM confidence, but the core claim that the name contains El, not Yhwh, is solid.

## Stakes

"Israel" means "God strives" or "He struggles with God" in the biblical folk etymology — a name of intimate relationship with the divine. But historically, it probably meant "May El reign" — a declaration of allegiance to the Canaanite high god. The people who became "Israel" originally belonged to El, not Yhwh. Yhwh was a later acquisition. The very name of the chosen people declares allegiance to a different god.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read *'el* as the generic "god" rather than the proper name El. Conservatives who accept the biblical folk etymology ("struggles with God") as the original meaning.

**Alternative reading:** *'el* in "Israel" could simply mean "god" generically — "God strives" or "God reigns" — without specifying which god. The name wouldn't then indicate original El worship, just generic theism.

## Assessment

Romer's argument that "Israel" is an El name is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in his entire book — it's simple, philologically clear, and carries enormous weight. Even if you reject his preferred etymology ("May El reign"), the theophoric element remains *'el*, not *yah*. The Masoretic revision at Hos 12:5 is clever detective work but ultimately unprovable — it might be right, but there's no way to verify.
