# kaufmann-ri-1.2: The Bible consistently characterizes paganism as fetishism

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"Observe now what is said regarding the worship of the 'host of heaven.' Several of the named gods—Ashtoreth (Ishtar), Bel, Marduk, Nebo, etc.—are known from pagan sources to have been astral deities, yet not once does the Bible connect them with the worship of the 'host of heaven'." The Bible treats the "host" and the "idols" as two distinct categories — never linking named gods to their natural or mythological functions. Across all biblical corpora, "the sole argument advanced against pagan religion is that it is a fetishistic worship of 'wood and stone'."

## Evidence

Kaufmann provides an extensive survey:
- **Narratives:** The Dagon story (1 Sam 5) is the model — Dagon is a statue that falls and breaks; not even the Philistines suggest the fall portends anything for the living god.
- **Prophets:** Amos speaks of gods that Israelites "made" for themselves. Hosea parallels "Baals" with "graven images." Isaiah's *Götterdämmerung* is the twilight of "silver and gold images." Jeremiah calls the gods "stone and wood," "no-gods." Ezekiel — despite his erotic symbolism — "never once utilizes the sexual themes of mythology."
- **Second Isaiah:** The classic polemic (Isa 44:9-18) mocks making gods from firewood — but "has not a word about the gods or their myths."
- **Laws:** "There is no law in the Pentateuch interdicting the belief in pagan gods or the telling of their myths." The standing prohibition concerns "making" and worshipping idols.
- **Deuteronomy:** The folly of idolatry is that its gods "see not, and hear not, and eat not, and smell not." "Not that they are gluttonous and drunkards—but that they 'eat not'!"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the descriptive claim — Kaufmann's survey of the biblical data is accurate. The Bible's anti-idolatry polemic really does focus overwhelmingly on the fetishism argument, and really does not engage with pagan mythology in any substantive way. This is a real pattern that any theory of Israelite religion must account for.

## Stakes

This is Kaufmann's empirical foundation. If the pattern is real (it is), the question becomes: what explains it? Kaufmann's explanation (genuine Israelite ignorance of paganism) competes with alternative explanations (polemical strategy, monotheistic editing, the Bible reflecting elite rather than popular religion).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one seriously disputes the *pattern* — the Bible's polemic really is focused on fetishism. Scholars disagree about what explains it. Dever would say the Bible's elite authors deliberately caricatured popular religion they found embarrassing. Smith would say the monotheistic editors suppressed mythological material. Römer would say the fetishism polemic is a late (exilic/post-exilic) development.

**Alternative reading:** The fetishism argument is rhetorically effective precisely because image-worship was a real, visible, and salient aspect of pagan cult — not because Israelites didn't know about the myths. The prophets attack what ordinary Israelites could SEE: the statues, the rituals, the cult objects. The fetishism polemic is a form of satirical hyperbole — like a modern atheist mocking the Eucharist as "cannibalism." The mockery doesn't mean the mocker doesn't understand; it means they've chosen a reductive frame for polemical purposes.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's strongest descriptive contribution. The pattern he identifies is real, pervasive, and genuinely puzzling. The Bible DOES seem strangely incurious about what pagans actually believed. But Kaufmann's explanation assumes that what the Bible *doesn't say* is evidence of what Israelites *didn't know* — and this only works if you treat the Bible as comprehensive ethnographic reportage rather than theological polemic. Silence in polemical literature is weak evidence for ignorance.
