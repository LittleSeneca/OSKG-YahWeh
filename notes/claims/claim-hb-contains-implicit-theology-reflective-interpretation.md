# schmid-ht-6-9.6: The HB contains "implicit theology" — reflective interpretation of religious phenomena that qualifies as theology even without explicit theological genre

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§6-9 — Jewish Reception to Implicit Theology]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §9

## The Claim

The concept of theology, as defined by Scholasticism, is "anachronistic" for the HB. But the HB "neither contains theology nor contains no theology." The HB is "to a large degree...the reflective interpretation of preexisting religious texts. It thereby fulfills — at least for the texts containing reflective interpretations — a basic requirement of theology, if 'theology' is understood as the reflective examination and interpretation of religious phenomena."

## Evidence

- Köhler: "God is spirit" / "God is light" / "God is love" — these New Testament theological statements have no OT parallel. The HB doesn't make abstract theological assertions
- But the HB DOES contain Fortschreibung (updating interpretation): "the Hebrew Bible should, to a large degree, be viewed as the reflective interpretation of preexisting religious texts"
- Rudolf Smend: the HB has "at least a strong convergence with theology"
- Christoph Levin: "the way of the Old Testament to its theology"
- Norbert Lohfink: theology appears in the HB "only implicitly, indirectly, or reduced"
- Rudolf Bultmann distinguished "explicit" and "implicit" theological thoughts in the NT — Schmid extends this to the HB
- Jan Assmann independently developed "implicit theology" for Egyptian texts: implicit and explicit theology relate "like language and grammar"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The definition of "implicit theology" as reflective interpretation of religious texts is coherent. Whether this really counts as "theology" depends on definition. Schmid has spent the entire Part B arguing that "theology" has no stable definition — so his definition is as legitimate as any other. But the circularity is obvious: he's defined theology to include what the HB does, then claimed the HB contains theology.

## Stakes

This is Schmid's CONTRIBUTION to the field: a definition of theology that fits the HB's actual character as scribal, interpretive literature. If accepted, it resolves the century-long debate about whether the HB has a theology. The resolution is: yes, but it's implicit — it happens through interpretive literary activity, not through abstract doctrinal formulation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that "theology" requires explicit, systematic, propositional content. Traditional OT theologians who would find "implicit theology" too weak to support normative claims. Scholars who would collapse "implicit theology" into "inner-biblical interpretation" and question the added value of the term "theology."

**Alternative reading:** If all Fortschreibung is theology, the term loses specificity. "Implicit theology" may be relabeling "inner-biblical interpretation" to claim disciplinary continuity with Christian theology.

## Assessment

This is genuinely clever. Schmid's definition sidesteps the deadlock: the HB doesn't have explicit theology (the Scholastic kind), but it has implicit theology (reflective interpretation of religious tradition). The definition emerges naturally from the HB's character as Fortschreibung literature — which Schmid established in §1. The question is whether this is just relabeling "inner-biblical interpretation" as "theology." If all Fortschreibung is theology, the term loses specificity.
