# fleming-ybi-1.7: Yahweh was "embedded in nearby spaces" to early Israel, not distant and southern

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 1 — Introduction]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"I will propose that the two names — Israel and Yhwʒ — and the populations associated with them were probably embedded in nearby spaces in the Levantine highlands and inland east, whatever their back-stories" (p. 36). "Rather than explain Yahweh as foreign to what became Israel, needing importation... this approach transforms the social landscape into which both Israel and Yahweh were embedded so that both the 'foreign' and the specifically southern become unnecessary" (p. 37).

## Evidence

1. **Shasu were not exclusively southern:** The Shasu were associated with the entire inland back country "from Arabia north through Jordan and Syria" (p. 32). The southern concentration is an artefact of Egyptian record-keeping, not demographic reality
2. **Temporal and spatial overlap:** Both Israel and Yahweh appear to have belonged to the landscape of southern Palestine while Egypt was still present (Late Bronze/Iron I transition) (p. 36). They emerged in the same general region at the same general time
3. **Mesha Stele as analogy:** The Mesha stele places "men of Gad" in territory later associated with Israel, without connection to Israel (p. 27). This shows that groups later absorbed into "Israel" had independent identities — analogous to what Fleming proposes for Yahweh-people
4. **Absence of connection in earliest evidence:** Neither name (Israel, Yhwʒ) appears in connection with the other in the earliest evidence (p. 36). The two names belong to overlapping but distinct social landscapes

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** This is the positive proposal that replaces the Midianite Hypothesis, and it's the sketchiest part of the chapter. "Nearby spaces" in the "Levantine highlands and inland east" is a big area. Much depends on the specific readings developed in later chapters — particularly Ch 2 (Egyptian evidence) and Ch 6 (Judges 5).

## Stakes

This is Fleming's alternative framework. If it's right, the entire 150-year origins debate was asking the wrong question in the wrong geographical frame. Yahweh wasn't borrowed from far away; he was always nearby, among groups that eventually became part of Israel.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Both the Midianite Hypothesis camp (southern origin) and the Berlin hypothesis camp (local highland origin from the start, no Shasu connection). Fleming is charting a third path: Shasu connection but NOT distant southern origin.

**Alternative reading:** The evidence for Shasu presence across the inland Levant doesn't mean Yhwʒ specifically was in the central highlands. The one geographical clue we have (Seir in the Ramses II list) points south, and all the biblical poetry points south. Fleming's "nearby" proposal requires setting aside the only geographical data we have.

## Assessment

This is the chapter's most ambitious and least-supported claim. Fleming is essentially asking for a paradigm shift on the basis of (a) a new reading of the Egyptian evidence (Ch 2) and (b) a new reading of Judges 5 (Ch 6). The positive proposal is thin here because the book is structured to build it cumulatively. The key question is whether Ch 2 and Ch 6 can deliver enough specificity to make "nearby spaces" more than a placeholder. Judgment must wait for the full argument.
