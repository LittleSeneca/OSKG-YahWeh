# albertz-hir-4.4: Exilic worship was predominantly lamentation — and this became the forum for theological reorientation

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 4 — Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"The main cult of the exilic period was predominantly lamentation... this occasional form of worship, which even in the pre-exilic period was not necessarily tied to a holy place, became the element which supported the regular main cult in the exilic period." This made worship "more open, a forum to which the various groups could contribute their own theological ideas."

## Evidence

- Four annual public fasts commemorating the catastrophe dates (Zech 7.2ff.; 8.18ff.) continued even after the exile
- Those who remained made vegetable and incense offerings on the devastated temple site (Jer 41.5)
- Exiles oriented prayer toward Jerusalem (1 Kings 8.46-51), enabling lamentation even in unclean foreign lands
- Lamentations 1-5 as the prime textual evidence: the poet moves from descriptive lament to theological interpretation, accepting guilt and revising Zion theology
- The poet of Lamentations explicitly concedes that official theology (priests, temple prophets) was wrong and the prophets of judgment were right (Lam 2.14; 4.13; 2.17)
- The liturgy incorporated multiple genres beyond traditional lament: elegiac poems, individual thanksgivings, prophetic judgment collections

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The liturgical use of Lamentations and the four annual fasts is well-attested. The claim about "openness" is more inferential — we don't have direct descriptions of exilic worship. But the diversity of genres in Lamentations itself supports the idea.

## Stakes

If worship was the primary site of theological processing, then liturgy — not just prophetic or scribal activity — drove the theological transformation of the exile. This gives cultic practice a generative role often overlooked in histories that focus on "great ideas."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize scribal schools and literary production over liturgical practice. The extent to which exilic worship "became more open" is debatable — we have only elite textual products, not ethnographic descriptions.

**Alternative reading:** Lamentations is a literary-theological work, not a transcript of actual worship. The diversity of genres reflects literary artifice, not liturgical practice.
