# keel-ggi-1.2: Preexilic Israel was polytheistic, but that tells us almost nothing — the real question is WHICH gods, WHERE, WHEN, and in WHAT RELATION to Yahweh

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch I — Starting Point and Points of Departure]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter I

## The Claim

"Recent Old Testament research... generally assumes that the religion of preexilic Israel and Judah is to be characterized as thoroughly polytheistic." They quote Lang, Knauf, and Weippert establishing the consensus. But they immediately problematize it: "The texts cited by Weippert (Psalms 82, 89; Deut 32:8f.) do in fact assume that other gods existed in addition to Yahweh. But the texts say nothing about whether these deities were venerated in any way in Israel and Judah." The dating problem is severe — "widespread uncertainty about how to date the biblical texts" means "such textual material is of little value for providing good evidence when one tries to reconstruct the religion of Israel and Judah for the period of the monarchy."

## Evidence

- Lang (1981, 1983): preexilic Israel had a triad of protector god, national god (Yahweh), and functional deities
- Knauf (1988): different triads at Bethel, Jerusalem, Hebron
- Weippert (1990): even "official" Jerusalem theology was polytheistic; Yahweh had a goddess partner
- Counterpoint: all of these are deductions from texts — none from stratified archaeological evidence
- Tigay (1986) as methodological model: "external controls" from archaeology to balance the "notorious subjectivity" of dating texts

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The consensus on preexilic polytheism is well-established; the dating crisis in Hebrew Bible scholarship is real.

## Stakes

If all we can say is "preexilic Israel was polytheistic" without specifying which gods, in which regions, in which periods, and in what relationship to Yahweh, then the statement is nearly contentless. Their entire chronological survey is designed to answer these specific questions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Maximalists who trust the biblical chronology would push back on the dating skepticism. Dever has argued that the biblical texts contain recoverable historical memory even if late. Smith explicitly builds his case FROM the texts (Ugaritic and biblical) rather than archaeological evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical texts, even if late, may preserve genuine preexilic traditions through oral transmission. Keel and Uehlinger's skepticism about texts could lead them to underweight genuinely ancient material embedded in late compositions.

## Assessment

Their critique is measured. They don't reject textual evidence — they integrate epigraphic finds (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom) throughout the book. They're saying texts ALONE can't do the job, not that texts are worthless.
