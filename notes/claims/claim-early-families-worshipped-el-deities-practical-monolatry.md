# albertz-hir-2.1.2: Early Israelite families worshipped various regional forms of El as their family god in a practical monolatry without exclusiveness

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §2.1 — Patriarchal Religion as Family Piety]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §2.1

## The Claim

Before Yahweh worship was established at the tribal level, early Israelite families worshipped various regional manifestations of the high god El. The Priestly writing correctly preserved the memory that the patriarchs worshipped God under the name El-Shaddai. The family god was worshipped monolatrously (one god per family) but without exclusiveness or intolerance toward other families' gods — because "what is expected and experienced from various gods is in any case more or less identical" at the family level.

## Evidence

- The patriarchal narratives connect the ancestors with a series of El deities: El-'Elyon (Jerusalem), El-Bethel (Bethel), El the God of Israel (Shechem), El-'Olam (Beersheba), El-Ro'e (Negeb)
- The Priestly writing says the patriarchs knew God as El-Shaddai (Gen 17:1; Ex 6:3)
- No proper names containing Yahweh appear in the patriarchal narratives — all theophoric names are El-compounds (Yishma'el, etc.)
- The "God of my father" designation is the most original type of family god reference — a personal god passed down through family tradition
- Family onomastics: theophoric elements in names can change (adapting to the religious environment), but the predicates (what is experienced from the god) remain constant — showing that *function* matters more than *name* at the family level
- Striking absence of religious polemic in the patriarchal narratives — "only in the late Deuteronomistic text Gen 35:2ff. does Jacob call on his family to put away the strange gods"
- Even in the monarchical period, it was "in no way customary to give one's children names containing Yahweh; this only changes in the late period"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The El-name connections are real textual data. But the argument that these represent *early family practice* (rather than later aetiological constructions by sanctuary priesthoods) is hard to verify. The Priestly writing's El-Shaddai tradition could be theological archaizing rather than authentic memory.

## Stakes

If early Israelite families worshipped El (not Yahweh), then Yahweh worship was *introduced* at the tribal level — Yahweh was not originally the god of Israelite families. This supports the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis (Yahweh came from outside) and complicates any claim that Israel was "always Yahwistic." It also means the identification of Yahweh with El was a secondary theological move.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue that El and Yahweh were always identified in Israelite religion, or that "El" in the patriarchal narratives is just a generic term for deity, not a distinct god. Richard Hess argued that El and Yahweh are too intertwined in biblical Hebrew to separate neatly.

**Alternative reading:** The El deities associated with patriarchal sites could be retrojections from later sanctuary traditions. El-Bethel might reflect Bethel's sanctuary theology, not family practice. The entire El → Yahweh progression could be a theological construct of the Priestly writer rather than historical memory.

## Assessment

Albertz is on solid comparative ground — the pattern of family-level monolatry with interchangeable divine names is well-attested in ANE family religion. His reading of the patriarchal El-names as authentic memories of pre-Yahwistic family religion is plausible but not proven. The strongest single piece of evidence is the absence of Yahweh theophoric names in the patriarchal narratives — this is hard to explain if the tradents were simply projecting their own Yahwism backward. They would have used Yahweh-names if they didn't know better.
