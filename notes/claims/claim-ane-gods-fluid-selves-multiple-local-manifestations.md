# sommer-bog-1.1: ANE gods had fluid selves — multiple local manifestations with partial independence

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 1 — Mesopotamia and Canaan]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"A single deity could exist simultaneously in several bodies. Further, a deity could have a fragmented or ill-defined self, for gods were not fully distinct from each other, at least not all of the time."

Sommer clarifies what this is NOT: (1) it is NOT contradictory personality (Ishtar as goddess of love AND war) — that's complex characterization, not fragmented selfhood; (2) it is NOT syncretism (historically independent gods merging) — he's studying how worshippers experienced these gods synchronically, not diachronic development.

## Evidence

**Ishtar's fragmentation:**
- Assyrian treaties list "Ishtar of Arbela," "Ishtar of Nineveh," and Venus (planet = Ishtar) as SEPARATE witnesses with separate curse obligations
- Assurbanipal's hymn addresses them as plural ("their lips," "their names") — grammatically distinct goddesses
- Yet functionally "one and the same": "The Lady of Nineveh, the mother who bore me... The Lady of Arbela, my creator" — both claim motherhood. The next lines use feminine PLURAL verbs: "They decreed as my fate..."
- "Somehow, it was possible for various local and even heavenly manifestations of a single god to be effectively identical with each other and also distinct from each other"

**Divine overlap/merger:**
- Ninurta's body as all gods: A late 2nd-millennium hymn describes other gods as Ninurta's body parts. "Your face is Shamash, your head is Adad, your neck is Marduk." This is not monotheism — it's imagistic theology where divine selves overlap.

- Marduk absorbs the high gods: In *Enuma Elish*, Anu, Ea, and Enlil are equated with Marduk. "Your word is Anu!" — not "like Anu's" but IS Anu. Ea says Marduk "shall have the name Ea, just like me." In Mesopotamia, "name and essence were inseparable." BUT "all these deities act quite independently of each other in the epic... In one sense, Marduk and Ea share a self, but at the same time Ea has a self that is not Marduk."

- The *tākultu* ritual (K. 252): Lists offerings to combined deities like "Dagan-Ashur" — single divine determinative, single deity in the ritual moment. "The texts are intriguing because of the curiously ambivalent concept of the divine as both one and many that they present."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Akkadian and Ugaritic texts are unambiguous. The treaty lists, hymns, and ritual texts consistently attest to divine fragmentation and overlap.

## Stakes

If ANE gods had fluid selves as Sommer claims, then the "many gods" of polytheism were already partially overlapping with each other. The step from polytheism to monotheism was not a leap from "many distinct gods" to "one god" but a gradual consolidation of already-overlapping divine identities. The fluidity model makes the emergence of monotheism more intelligible — it was latent in ANE polytheism itself.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat ANE deities as fully discrete personalities with stable identities; those who attribute the overlap patterns to syncretism (diachronic merger) rather than synchronic fluidity.

**Alternative reading:** The local manifestations and divine overlap Sommer describes are evidence of syncretism — historically distinct gods being merged over time — not evidence of a synchronic "fluidity" concept. Worshippers of Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh worshipped different goddesses who were later identified.

## Assessment

Sommer's distinction between synchronic fluidity and diachronic syncretism is crucial and persuasive. The evidence that ANE worshippers experienced local Ishtar manifestations as "distinct yet the same" simultaneously (not historically merged) is strong. The Ninurta hymn and Marduk-absorption texts show that divine overlap was a live theological concept, not just a scribal convenience.
