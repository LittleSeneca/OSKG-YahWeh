# keel-ggi-5.4: The Lyre Player Is a Human Woman, Not a Goddess

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part V — Iron Age IIB — Baal El Yahweh and His Asherah]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part V

## The Claim

"We have no clear evidence that ever shows a goddess playing a lyre... it is only with a measured degree of confidence that one can say that this is more likely a human figure rather than a divine one." Dever (1984) identified the seated lyre player on Pithos A as the goddess Asherah enthroned on a cherub throne. Keel & Uehlinger systematically dismantle this: the figure has small but clear breasts (so female), but the chair is a courtly armchair (not a cherub throne), the garment length does not match enthroned goddess conventions, and no known iconographic type shows a goddess playing a lyre.

## Evidence

- The figure has "two small, but clearly drawn, breasts" — favoring female identification (contra Hadley who argues male)
- Dever's parallels are three Ugaritic LB statuettes/pendants of enthroned goddesses — but none holds a lyre, they are from a different region and 500+ years earlier
- The chair has a slightly angled back, no footrest, grill-work panelling — this is Gubel's Type II-g Phoenician courtly armchair, not a cherub throne
- "Lion's feet are not enough to make this a cherub throne since they are included on all kinds of furniture during the first millennium"
- A Phoenician scarab from Acre shows the same chair type — with a woman seated on it, not necessarily a goddess
- A scaraboid from Nippur shows a lyre player on a similar chair, probably playing for the moon god — a human musician, not a deity
- No iconographic type of "Enthroned Goddess with a Lyre" exists in the entire ancient Near Eastern record

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The art-historical analysis is precise and the parallels are better than Dever's. The absence of a "goddess with lyre" iconographic type is particularly damning.

## Stakes

If the lyre player is human, then neither of the two humanoid figures near the inscription (the Bes figures and the lyre player) can be identified as a goddess. The entire visual case for a goddess at Kuntillet Ajrud collapses — there is no goddess in the paintings.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984, 1990), Hadley (1987b, 1989 — argues the figure could be male), some feminist scholars who want the lyre player to be a goddess.

**Alternative reading:** Even if not a goddess, a female musician playing in a cultic context near Yahwistic inscriptions is still significant for understanding women's roles in Iron Age religion. The lyre player does not need to be a goddess to be important.

## Assessment

Dever's identification was always the weakest part of his Kuntillet Ajrud analysis. He wanted to find Asherah in the paintings so badly that he overlooked basic art-historical conventions. Keel & Uehlinger's restraint is methodologically superior: when you cannot identify a figure definitively, say so. The lyre player is probably human. She is interesting for what she tells us about musical performance at the site — not for goddess worship.
