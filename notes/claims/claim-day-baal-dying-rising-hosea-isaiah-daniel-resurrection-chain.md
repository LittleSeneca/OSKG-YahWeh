# day-ygc-4.4: Baal dying and rising god → Hosea → Isaiah → Daniel resurrection chain

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 4 — Yahwehs Appropriation of Baal Imagery]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 4

## The Claim

The chain of influence runs: Baal (dying and rising god) → Hosea 5-6 and 13-14 (death/resurrection imagery for national exile/restoration) → Isaiah 26:19 (same, still metaphorical) → Daniel 12:2 (literal individual resurrection, "remythologized"). Jewish resurrection belief has a Canaanite genealogy, not a Zoroastrian one.

## Evidence

**Part 1: Baal as dying and rising god:**
1. Ugaritic Baal myth: Baal swallowed by Mot (Death), repeatedly declared dead, buried and lamented, land becomes dry — then El has vision, declares "Mightiest Baal is alive," Baal resumes throne (KTU² 1.4.VIII-1.6.VI) (p. 118)
2. Against Mark Smith (merely "disappearing and returning"): The two myths use "clearly distinct language" — Telepinus VANISHES; Baal DIES and is declared ALIVE. "The contrast between life and death is basic to the myth" (citing Mettinger) (pp. 118-119)
3. Against the "substitute" theory: "There is no indication of this in the text" (Waterston) (p. 119)
4. Zech 12:11: "The mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the valley of Megiddo" — Hadad = Baal, confirming death-and-mourning ritual survived into monarchic period (p. 119)

**Part 2: Hosea uses Baal's death/resurrection imagery:**
1. **Hos 13:1 is deliberately ironical:** "Ephraim incurred guilt through Baal and DIED." For Hosea, it's not Baal who dies and rises — it's ISRAEL who dies through Baal worship, then (if repentant) rises through Yahweh (p. 121)
2. **Death, not illness:** Hiphil of *qûm* "raise up" and piel of *ḥāyâ* "revive" in Hos 6:2 — "All the other places in the OT where these two verbs appear as word pairs the meaning clearly relates to resurrection from death, not simply healing" (Isa 26:14, 19; Job 14:12, 14) (p. 120)
3. **Lion imagery = death:** In Hos 5:14 Yahweh is a lion carrying off prey "and none shall rescue" — elsewhere in OT this implies certain death. Confirmed by Hos 13:7-9 where lion = destruction (p. 120)
4. **Parallel images between Hos 5-6 and 13-14 prove death:** Both use lion imagery, "return" language, healing imagery, rain/dew — and Hos 13 explicitly speaks of death. Therefore Hos 5-6 must also mean death, not illness (pp. 120-121)
5. **Rain/dew connection:** Israel's resurrection associated with rain (Hos 6:3) and dew (Hos 14:6) — "This is striking, since in the Ugaritic Baal myth we read that Baal took the rain and two of the dew goddesses with him when he went into the underworld, and it is implied that they reappeared when he rose again" (p. 121)
6. **The third day:** NOT derived from a fertility god's resurrection timetable. "After two days... on the third day" is "a poetic way of saying 'after a short while'" (pp. 122-123)

**Part 3: Isaiah 26 depends on Hosea 13-14:**
- Eight parallels between Hosea 13-14 and Isaiah 26-27, all in the same order (with one minor exception) — Day's own discovery, published in *JTS* 1980 (pp. 122-123)
- Both passages have birth-pangs imagery immediately before (Hos 13:13; Isa 26:17-18) — "something too remarkable to be ascribed to mere coincidence" (p. 124)
- Isa 26:19 is STILL metaphorical (national restoration, like Ezekiel 37). The decisive proof: Isa 27:8 speaks of exile — and both verses correspond to adjacent verses in Hosea 13 (vv. 14 and 15) (p. 125)

**Part 4: Daniel 12 depends on Isaiah 26:**
- Both use "awake" (*qîṣ*) from "dust" (*ʻāpār*) (p. 125)
- Daniel 12:2's unique word *dērāʼôn* "abhorrence" occurs only one other place: Isa 66:24, also eschatological judgment (p. 125)
- Dan 12:3's "those who turn many to righteousness" echoes Isa 53:11's suffering Servant (pp. 125-126)
- Now "remythologized" — literal individual resurrection, not national restoration

**Against Zoroastrian origin:** Jewish resurrection speaks of "awakening from sleep" — "such a concept is completely alien to the Zoroastrians." But Canaanite tradition preserves exactly this: Melqart's resurrection festival was called *egersis* "awakening" (Menander, in Josephus *Ant.* 8.5.3), and Ugaritic texts call Aqhat's death "sleep" (*šnt*) (KTU² 1.19.III.45) (pp. 126-127)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The Hosea-Baal connection is strong. The Isaiah-Hosea dependence (eight parallels in order) is convincing. The Daniel-Isaiah dependence is solid. The weakest link is whether Baal was genuinely a "dying and rising" god or merely "disappearing and returning."

## Stakes

This is the single most important chain of influence Day traces in the entire book. If correct, the doctrine of bodily resurrection — arguably the most distinctive feature of Jewish and Christian eschatology — originated in Canaanite Baal mythology, was demythologized by Hosea into political metaphor, and was remythologized by Daniel into literal afterlife hope. This is a radically different genealogy than either Zoroastrian influence or divine revelation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (Baal is "disappearing and returning," not dying and rising). H.M. Barstad (the words don't imply resurrection). Those favoring Zoroastrian origin (Bousset, Boyce, Lang). Those who read Hos 5-6 as illness/healing, not death/resurrection (Mays, Wolff, Rudolph, Davies).

**Alternative reading:** Jewish resurrection derives from Zoroastrian influence during the Persian period. Baal was not a dying and rising god. Hosea 5-6 describes national illness and healing, not death and resurrection. The Isaiah-Hosea parallels are coincidental.

## Assessment

Day's eight-parallel argument for Isaiah's dependence on Hosea is airtight — that's his own research and it's been well-received. The Baal-as-dying-god argument is the most contested link. Mettinger's subsequent work would provide the most thorough defense of dying and rising gods. The Zoroastrian counter-argument is weakened by the "sleep/awakening" language difference — if Israel got resurrection from Persia, why does the vocabulary differ so dramatically? The three-stage model (mythological appropriation → demythologization → remythologization) is a genuinely sophisticated literary-historical model, not a crude "borrowing" claim.
