# albertz-hir-2.2.1: The Exodus group was a small, declassed, ethnically mixed group of conscripted laborers — not "all Israel" and not nomads

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §2.2 — The Exodus Group and Yahweh's Origins]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §2.2

## The Claim

"There was only one group of what was later to become Israel in Egypt." It was "an economically assimilated but socially declassed group of foreign conscripts to forced labour... whose solidarity had been undermined by state measures." The group was not nomadic: "from the start Yahweh religion is not a nomadic religion, as is constantly asserted."

## Evidence

- The biblical tradition itself notes a "clear jump" in group size from patriarchal families to the Exodus group — the social form shifts
- The Egyptians set the group to build "store cities Pithom and Ramses" (Ex 1:11), consistent with Ramesside construction in the eastern Delta (13th c.)
- The group is described as settled in the Delta (Ex 9:26) and unfamiliar with nomadic survival strategies in the wilderness — they need help from actual nomads (Num 10:29-32)
- Moses has an Egyptian name (msy, "give birth," like Thut-mose, Ramses) and is mistaken for an Egyptian by Midianites (Ex 2:19)
- The group is frequently called "Hebrews" ('ibrî) in Ex 1-12, and Yahweh is called "God of the Hebrews" (Ex 3:18; 5:3; 7:16; 9:1, 13; 10:3) — a recollection that the Exodus group belonged to the 'Apiru/Habiru class
- Helck's theory: the group was a detachment of prisoners-of-war of ethnically differing origin, conscripted for state building work
- An Egyptian letter of Ramses II mentions 'pr.w (Apiru) "bringing stones for the great pylon" — direct evidence of Apiru in building projects

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The social description (declassed forced laborers) is well-supported by Egyptian parallels. But the size ("one group, not all Israel") and the identification with 'Apiru are debated. The "Hebrew/Apiru" connection has a long and contested history in scholarship.

## Stakes

If Yahweh religion began with a small, marginal outsider group rather than "all Israel," then: (a) Yahweh was adopted by other Israelite tribes later — Yahweh religion spread, not descended; (b) the Exodus was a minor event that later became the founding myth of a much larger entity; (c) the anti-domination, liberationist character of Yahweh religion is rooted in its actual sociological origin, not later theology.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a larger Exodus (though most critical scholars accept a small-group model). Minimalists (Thompson, Lemche) would question whether ANY Exodus group existed. Evangelical scholars would dispute the small-group reconstruction.

**Alternative reading:** The "Hebrew/Apiru" identification could be later editorial glossing rather than authentic memory. And the small-group hypothesis, while common, makes the Exodus tradition's centrality in later Israelite identity genuinely puzzling — why would a minor escape become THE founding story?

## Assessment

Albertz's reconstruction is standard critical scholarship, well-argued and well-sourced. The strongest single point is the Egyptian name of Moses — if Moses were an Israelite invention, why give him an Egyptian name? The weakest point is the size of the group — Albertz assumes a small group but provides no mechanism for how its traditions became normative for all Israel. That mechanism is presumably what §2.3 (the tribal alliance) will explain.
