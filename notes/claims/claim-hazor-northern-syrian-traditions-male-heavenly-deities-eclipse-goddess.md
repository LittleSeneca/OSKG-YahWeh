# keel-ggi-p2.2: Hazor maintained northern Syrian traditions — male heavenly deities eclipsed the goddess

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

"Hazor, to name one site, remained very much under northern influence (northern Syria, southeastern Anatolia) during the entire Late Bronze Age." The dominant cults at Hazor were the weather god (on a bull) and the moon god — and "this is the first evidence of a piety in Palestine/Israel that is oriented toward heavenly beings, apparently having originated in northern Syria."

## Evidence

- **Architectural evidence:** Temple H is a Syrian-style longroom temple with parallels at Ebla (ca. 2000/1900) and Alalakh (ca. 1720-1650). Rebuilt three times with "almost the identical floor plan."
- **Basalt orthostats:** Wall-ornamentation technique is "a typical Anatolian technique." Reclining lion (190 cm long) served architectural function like one at Alaja Huyuk in central Anatolia.
- **Weather god on a bull:** Basalt bull fragment (barely 30 cm long) with human feet on its back, found in front of Temple H (14th c.). Shows "the same Anatolian sun or moon symbol, the disk with a cross, on its chest." Since deity on a bull is weather god in northern Syrian-Anatolian contexts, this is "a most interesting combination of a weather god and a sun or moon god." Small bronze bull from Holy of Holies (13th c.) confirms weather god cult.
- **Moon god stele:** Stelae temple in Area C features central stele with "a crescent moon with a disk above two outstretched arms" — the symbol of the Moon God of Haran. Seated basalt figure wears crescent moon breast pendant. Silver-covered bronze standards with crescent moons.
- **No anthropomorphic goddesses in later Temple H phases:** "Except for the three engraved figures with silver and bronze overlay, that are very likely from the Middle Bronze Age or from Late Bronze Age I, no anthropomorphic representations of female deities have been found in the later Late Bronze Age phases of Temple H."
- **Mitannian seals:** 18 cylinder seals in "popular Mitannian style" with sacral trees flanked by worshipers and animals. "Mitannian glyptic art is generally reserved when it comes to depicting deities anthropomorphically" — preferred "stylized tree, caprids, and birds."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High for northern Syrian/Anatolian influence — basalt lion, orthostats, and moon god symbolism have clear parallels. Medium for "first evidence of piety oriented toward heavenly beings" — depends on interpretation of earlier astral symbolism.

## Stakes

Hazor was the largest Canaanite city of the LB, and its religious profile is markedly different from southern sites. If Hazor's religion was dominated by a weather god and moon god — with goddesses absent from temple iconography — this challenges the picture of LB Canaanite religion as goddess-centered. It also means that northern Israelite religion in Iron Age I may have inherited more from Hazor's traditions than from coastal/southern traditions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Kempinski (1989a) argued for a triad of El, Baal, and a fertility goddess at Megiddo. Yadin's original Hazor reports emphasized MB-LB continuity; Keel & Uehlinger build on this but draw sharper conclusions about the goddess's eclipse.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of anthropomorphic goddess figurines could reflect preservation bias or the temple's specific function. Mitannian seals with stylized trees may *imply* the goddess without depicting her — which is not the same as her eclipse.

## Assessment

The Hazor evidence is genuinely striking because it's the largest LB site and its temple was continuously used. The contrast with Middle Bronze Age Nahariyah (where doves, lions, and goddess figurines abounded) is stark. However, Keel & Uehlinger may overstate the "absence of goddess" at Hazor. The sacral tree on Mitannian seals and painted pottery may indeed represent the goddess in aniconic form.
