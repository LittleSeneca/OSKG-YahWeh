# lewis-ocg-7.1: There was no anthropomorphic cult statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 7 — Iconography of Divinity — Yahweh]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 7

## The Claim

"It seems more likely that we should take the biblical tradition at face value in its assertion that there was not an anthropomorphic statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple." Lewis systematically rebuts the seven arguments of Niehr, Uehlinger, Becking, van der Toorn, and others who argue for a Yahweh statue.

## Evidence

(1) Uniformity is a bad comparative method — Israel was different. (2) Image-ban texts prove images existed, but not necessarily of Yahweh in Jerusalem. (3) "Coalition of concealment" is too sweeping — individual texts must be examined case by case. (4) Anthropomorphic language need not imply a physical statue — metaphors are the "most simple and most plausible explanation" (contra Niehr). (5) "House of Yahweh" doesn't require a resident image — modern houses of worship function without them. (6) The Nimrud prisms' "gods in whom they trusted" is historically suspect — Na'aman shows the author freely manipulated sources; the Khorsabad annals omit the divine image claim. (7) Vessel inventories from the Temple's destruction list pillars, basins, shovels, snuffers, lampstands — but no divine image. "If the divine image of Yahweh was such a preeminent focal point, would not we expect its specific mention?" (8) The lethal-God-sightings tradition (Hendel) makes no sense if people regularly viewed Yahweh's statue. (9) Archaeological evidence: "I cannot find a single object that was found in a cultic site in the kingdoms of Israel or Judah and that could be unequivocally interpreted as an anthropomorphic cult statue of a god" (Na'aman). Mettinger: "Do we have even one three-dimensional representation of Yhwh in metal or terracotta that may be classified as a cultic image? The answer is no."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Lewis makes a compelling cumulative case; the vessel inventory argument and the lethal-God-sightings tradition are the two strongest points.

## Stakes

The entire nature of Israelite aniconism. If there was no Yahweh statue, Israelite religion IS genuinely distinctive in the ancient Near East on this point — not a "Deuteronomistic concealment" but a real feature of pre-exilic cult. If the statue-thesis is right, the aniconic tradition is late theological propaganda.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Niehr, Uehlinger, Becking, van der Toorn, Berlejung, Dietrich and Loretz — a who's who of continental scholarship. This is one of the most polarized debates in the field.

**Alternative reading:** There may have been a statue in the First Temple that was removed or destroyed before the Deuteronomistic editors got to the text. The silence is the product of successful erasure, not absence. The Arad massebah shows aniconism in the provinces — but Jerusalem, as the royal capital, may have been different.

## Assessment

Lewis wins this debate on current evidence. The burden of proof is on those claiming a Yahweh statue, and they haven't met it. The vessel inventory argument is especially telling — Nebuchadnezzar's looters catalogued bronze snuffers but not a divine statue? The statue-thesis requires special pleading.
