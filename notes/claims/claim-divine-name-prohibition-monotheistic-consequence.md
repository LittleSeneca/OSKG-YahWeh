# romer-inv-1.2: The prohibition on pronouncing the divine name was a monotheistic development

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 1 — The Name]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 1: The God of Israel and His Name

## The Claim

The gradual prohibition on speaking Yhwh's name was driven by monotheistic theology: "Within the framework of a monotheistic view it is inappropriate for the one god to have a proper name, because if he had one, this might suggest that there was some need to distinguish him from other gods." A god with a proper name is a god among gods — you need a name to specify WHICH god. The one and only God doesn't need a name because there's no one to distinguish him from. The prohibition was a consequence of monotheism, not its cause.

## Evidence

- The Septuagint (3rd-2nd century BCE) already substitutes *kurios* (Lord) or *theos* (God) for Yhwh
- The Mishnah (1st-2nd century CE) permits the high priest to pronounce the name only on Yom Kippur in the Holy of Holies
- The Masoretes (3rd-10th century CE) systematically applied the vowels of *'adonay* to Yhwh as a reading instruction, producing the hybrid forms YeHWaH / YeHoWaH
- The substitution of "the Name" (*has-sem*) in Judaism
- The later Christian mistake: "Jehovah" — pronouncing the Masoretic replacement vowels as if they were the original

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the fact of the prohibition and its gradual development. MEDIUM on Romer's claim that it was specifically monotheistic theology that drove it (magical concerns were also a factor, as Romer acknowledges).

## Stakes

The name "Yahweh" — the personal name of God, the name revealed to Moses at the burning bush — was deliberately suppressed by Jewish tradition. The God you pray to as "Lord" was originally a god with a specific name, and that name was systematically hidden. The "Lord" of Jewish and Christian liturgy is a replacement word.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue the prohibition was driven primarily by magical concerns (fear of misusing the powerful divine name) rather than theological concerns (monotheism making a proper name awkward).

**Alternative reading:** The prohibition could have had multiple overlapping causes: monotheistic theology, magical caution, and the desire to protect the name from profanation by non-Jews. Romer's monotheistic reading prioritizes one factor over others.

## Assessment

Romer's claim about the prohibition being monotheistically driven is plausible but not the only explanation. Magical name-anxiety was widespread in the ancient world. The prohibition's gradual development is well-documented; its motivation is less certain.
