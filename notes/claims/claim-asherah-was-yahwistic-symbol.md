# smith-ehg-3.1: The asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol, not evidence of a separate goddess cult in monarchic Israel

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 3 — Yahweh and Asherah]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (1990, rev. 2002), Chapter 3: Yahweh and Asherah

## The Claim

The asherah (a wooden pole or stylized tree) was present in both northern and southern royal cults and in popular religion. It was associated with Yahweh, not Baal. But it was a SYMBOL within Yahwism, not evidence that a goddess named Asherah was actively worshipped as Yahweh's consort during the monarchy. The goddess may have existed earlier (Genesis 49:25 possibly preserves her epithets), but by the monarchic period, her cult had been absorbed into Yahweh's.

## Evidence

### Distribution of the Asherah

- **Geographic spread:** Attested at Ophrah (Judges 6), Samaria (1 Kings 16:33, 2 Kings 13:6), Jerusalem (2 Kings 21:7, 23:6), Bethel (2 Kings 23:15), and generally "on every high hill and under every green tree" (1 Kings 14:23, 2 Kings 17:10)
- **Chronological spread:** From Judges period through Josiah's reform
- **Cultic association:** "Olyan has argued that the asherah was associated historically with Yahweh and not with Baal." In 1 Kings 18:19 the prophets of Asherah appear only once vs. Baal's prophets five times — "some critics view the single reference as a secondary addition"
- **No prophetic opposition before the 8th century:** Jehu opposed Baal but not the asherah. Hosea opposed Baal but not the asherah. "Olyan observes that no prophet opposed the asherah until the eighth century, and the prophetic passages that criticize the asherah appear to be Deuteronomistic or derivative."
- **Function:** Next to altars (Deut 16:21, Judges 6:25-26), possibly used for divination (Hosea 4:12, Habakkuk 2:19, Micah 5:11-13) and healing (Talmud Pesahim 25a prohibits using asherah wood for remedies — implying it WAS so used)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The evidence for the asherah's widespread presence in Yahwistic contexts is overwhelming. Whether it represented a goddess or not is a separate question, but the basic point — that Yahwism included this symbol for centuries — is solid.

## Stakes

If the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol (not a foreign goddess), then the Deuteronomistic reformers weren't purging foreign contamination — they were destroying elements of their own Yahwistic tradition that they had come to view as unacceptable. The "idolatry" they condemned was, in many cases, traditional Yahwism. This reframes the entire biblical narrative of reform.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Almost no one disputes the asherah's presence and Yahwistic association. The debate is about what it MEANT. Majority: it represented the goddess Asherah as Yahweh's consort (see [[claim-asherah-el-consort-transferred-to-yahweh]]). Smith: it was a symbol that had lost its goddess connection.

**Alternative reading:** The asherah WAS a goddess symbol — the symbol of Asherah as Yahweh's consort. The Yahwistic association doesn't disprove the goddess; it confirms the goddess was Yahweh's partner. See [[claim-asherah-goddess-in-ot-passages]] (day-ygc-2.1) for the direct biblical evidence.

## Assessment

This is Smith's strongest claim in the chapter. The evidence for the asherah's widespread presence in Yahwistic contexts is overwhelming. Whether it represented a goddess or not is a separate question, but the basic point — that Yahwism included this symbol for centuries — is solid. The unresolved tension is WHY the Deuteronomists condemned it so violently if it was just a symbol. Smith's explanation (secondary confusion with Astarte, offense at divinatory/healing functions) is weak. The simpler explanation — that the symbol DID represent a goddess — fits the evidence better.
