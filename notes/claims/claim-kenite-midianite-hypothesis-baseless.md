# kaufmann-ri-6.6: The Kenite/Midianite Hypothesis Is Baseless

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

The theory that Moses derived Yahwism from the Kenites or Midianites (via Jethro) has no support in the biblical text. The Bible consistently distinguishes a "prophetic-only" sanctity south of Beersheba from a "cultic + prophetic" sanctity north of it — no patriarch builds altars south of Beersheba, no Israelite ever returns to Sinai/Horeb to worship, and the desert period is remembered as cultless (Amos 5:25; Jer. 7:22). Jethro is priest "of Midian," not "of YHWH." Moses discovers the holy mountain unwittingly; Jethro told him nothing about it. If the Midianites really worshipped YHWH, "there is no reason why the biblical tradition should have obscured the fact."

## Evidence

Kaufmann's strongest argument is his survey of the consistent biblical distinction between southern (revelation-only) and northern (cultic) sacred geography. He also notes that Israel never entered Midianite territory, that Kadesh plays no role in later Israelite worship, and that the Kenites joined Israel and its God — not the reverse.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's negative case (the biblical text doesn't support the Kenite hypothesis) is largely correct — the text indeed presents YHWH as revealed to Moses directly, not mediated through Jethro. But his positive historical claim (therefore the hypothesis is false) ignores why the biblical texts might obscure foreign origins. The biblical text was produced by Yahwists who had every reason to downplay or erase foreign origins for their God.

## Stakes

The Kenite hypothesis is one of the few critical theories that actually SUPPORTS Kaufmann's non-evolutionary model. If Moses imported YHWH from Midian, that IS a kind of revolution — an external introduction of a new god to Israel, not an organic evolution from Canaanite religion. Kaufmann paradoxically REJECTS the theory that would help his case, because his theological commitments require that YHWH be absolutely new — not borrowed from anywhere.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** A substantial scholarly tradition. The Egyptian topographical lists mentioning "Yhwʒ of Shasu-land" place a Yahweh-like name in the region of Edom/Midian in the 14th-13th centuries BCE — exactly where the Bible locates Moses' Midianite connection. The biblical texts themselves contain traces: Jethro presides at a sacrifice (Exod. 18:12), Moses' father-in-law is called both "Jethro the priest of Midian" and "Hobab the Kenite," and the Kenites appear as Yahwists in later Israelite tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The Kenite hypothesis may be correct in a modified form. Perhaps Moses DID encounter YHWH through Midianite contacts, but transformed the deity from a local mountain/storm god into the sole sovereign God of a covenant people. This would preserve both the historical connection to Midian AND the genuinely innovative character of Mosaic Yahwism.

## Assessment

This is where Kaufmann's theological commitments most clearly distort his historical judgment. He rejects the Kenite hypothesis not because the evidence is weak (it's actually reasonably strong for ANE history), but because admitting foreign origins for YHWH would compromise the absolute novelty he needs. The irony is that a modified Kenite hypothesis — Moses transforms a Midianite YHWH into something radically new — would actually preserve much of Kaufmann's revolutionary picture while accommodating the epigraphic evidence. His blanket rejection of the hypothesis weakens rather than strengthens his case.
