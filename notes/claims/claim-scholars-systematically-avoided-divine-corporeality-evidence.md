# sommer-bog-intro.2: Scholars have systematically avoided or denied the evidence for divine corporeality

**Source:** [[Sommer — Introduction]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Introduction

## The Claim

"It is surprising that many scholars ignore or even deny the corporeality of the biblical God. Others acknowledge the evidence but attempt to minimize it or to claim that it is to be understood only symbolically."

Sommer diagnoses this avoidance as stemming from "medieval Christian supersessionism" — the idea that Judaism's God is corporeal and therefore primitive, while Christianity's God is spiritual and therefore advanced. Modern scholars, even Jewish ones, have unconsciously inherited this bias.

## Evidence

Three case studies in scholarly avoidance:

1. **Gerstenberger** (*Theologies of the Old Testament*): A lengthy work that "highlights unexpected facets" of Israelite religion with strong Canaanite connections, yet "never mentions the embodied nature of Yhwh." Gerstenberger refers to Yhwh as "the invisible God" — completely ignoring the biblical evidence.

2. **Scarry** (*The Body in Pain*): A scholar of comparative literature who devotes a third of her book to analyzing biblical notions of body, yet "maintains that throughout the Old Testament God's power... are in part extreme and continual amplifications of the fact that people have bodies and He has no body." Sommer: "Scarry seems genuinely unaware of the fact that the Hebrew Bible contains not a single verse denying that God has a body."

3. **Eichrodt** (*Theology of the Old Testament*): Acknowledges anthropomorphism but dismisses it as "Canaanite influence" against which "pure Yhwhism fights violently." Blames "the great mass of the people" for the "inadequate conception" of embodied God. Sommer: "The claim that God's nonphysicality is 'left veiled'... is but a clever way of importing into the Hebrew scriptures a notion they lack."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Sommer documents the avoidance persuasively through concrete examples from major scholarly works.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, then much of modern biblical scholarship has systematically misread the Hebrew Bible on a fundamental point — the nature of God — because of an inherited theological bias that equates "spiritual" with "advanced" and "embodied" with "primitive."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The scholars Sommer critiques (Gerstenberger, Scarry, Eichrodt) and the broader tradition of biblical theology that reads anthropomorphism as metaphor rather than theological claim.

**Alternative reading:** Anthropomorphic language is a deliberate literary device — the biblical authors used human imagery to convey God's personal nature while understanding that God transcends physical form.

## Assessment

Sommer's critique of scholarly avoidance is sharp and well-documented. The Gerstenberger example is particularly striking — a major OT theology that never mentions divine embodiment despite the biblical evidence being overwhelming. The supersessionism diagnosis is debatable but provocative: it suggests that even critical scholars have inherited theological biases from the very traditions they claim to analyze objectively.
