# lewis-ocg-9.1: Israelite kings were cultic actors but never priests and never received cult

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 9 — King and Judge]] — Theodore Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"Apart from Psalm 110:4 with its idyllic mention of the priest-king Melchizedek, kings in ancient Israel and Judah were not called 'priests' (kōhănîm). And apart from a single monarch (King Ahaz...), they did not engage in the distinctive priestly activity of blood manipulation." Yet kings were "religious officiants involved in the performance of the cult as well as the primary sponsors who built and maintained altars, sanctuaries, and temples."

## Evidence

- Saul makes burnt and peace offerings (1 Sam 13:9-14), builds altars (1 Sam 14:35)
- David is divinely anointed, brings the Ark to Jerusalem with sacrifices and ritual dance, uses priestly divination (ephod)
- Solomon builds the Temple, dedicates it with massive sacrifices (22,000 oxen, 120,000 sheep), offers three times yearly
- Jeroboam I builds sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan, installs bull images, reforms the calendar, sacrifices, and offers incense
- Ahaz goes furthest: he scatters blood against the altar — explicitly priestly activity. "Nowhere else does a king manipulate blood in such a fashion."
- Comparative context: In Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Ugarit, the king WAS the chief priest. Israel is the exception.

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** VERY HIGH on the biblical data. HIGH that this reflects historical reality — the texts are ideologically shaped but multiple independent traditions converge.

## Stakes

The boundary between royal and priestly authority. In Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Ugarit, the king WAS the chief priest. In Israel, the king was a cultic actor but was NOT a priest — creating a structural tension between palace and temple that runs through the entire biblical record.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars argue David's sons were called priests (2 Sam 8:18) and that the distinction is later priestly retrojection. The Melchizedek tradition (Ps 110:4) suggests some circles DID envision a priest-king.

**Alternative reading:** The priestly prohibition on royal cultic activity is a post-exilic retrojection; earlier kings may have functioned as priests in practice.

## Assessment

This is one of Lewis's most important observations. The structural separation of royal and priestly authority in Israel is genuinely distinctive and has massive consequences for the development of Israelite religion — especially the priestly monopoly on holiness that Ch10 will explore.
