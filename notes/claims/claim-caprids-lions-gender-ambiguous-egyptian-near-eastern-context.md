# keel-ggi-p1.1: Caprids and lions are gender-ambiguous symbols revealing a religious world without rigid gender boundaries

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part I

## The Claim

"Whether the figure is to be assigned to the male or to the female sphere depends in each case on the overall classification of the motifs and the tradition history, in other words, on whether one detects stronger influences from Egypt or from the Near East." Caprids (wild goats) appear on scarabs since MB IIA and surge in MB IIB. They are symbols of virility — "strength and power... symbols of virility" — but also belong unmistakably to the goddess's sphere. Lions are more complex: 57% combine lion with uraeus (apotropaic/protective) and only 14% with branches (fertility). The Egyptian tradition shows the lion as triumphant pharaoh; Near Eastern tradition places the lion in the goddess's sphere as "Mistress of the Animals" (belet seri).

## Evidence

- 53 caprid scarabs: 39.6% with branch (fertility emphasis), 15% with uraeus (protection)
- 42 lion scarabs: 57% with uraeus (defense dominant), 14% with branch
- 9 examples show lion attacking caprid from behind — aggression motif
- Caprids have deep indigenous Palestinian tradition: Chalcolithic ibex depictions, Early Bronze Age examples
- Old Syrian cylinder seals show lions in goddess scenes (8 references to Winter 1983 catalog)
- Lion-striding-over-crocodile motif is completely without Near Eastern parallel — unambiguously Egyptian
- One unique scarab from Jericho shows hybrid: lion raised on hind legs facing worshiping man

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The statistical breakdown of branch vs. uraeus combinations provides hard data. The dual tradition is well-established.

## Stakes

This gender ambiguity — the same animal belonging to male or female divine spheres depending on cultural context — reveals a pre-monotheistic religious imagination where divine domains weren't sharply segregated by gender. The rigid "Yahweh = male warrior, Asherah = female fertility" binary that many scholars retroject onto preexilic Israelite religion is not present in MB IIB iconography. The goddess could be aggressive (lion); the male deity could be associated with vegetation (branch). This fluidity is what monotheism would eventually erase.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Hornung and Staehelin (1976) group all these animals together as "wild beasts of the desert" representing generic life-force with no attention to gender. Keel & Uehlinger explicitly critique this: "This interpretation pays no attention to the gender of the animals, nor does it show any interest in identifying the specific powers that the animals might be representing."

**Alternative reading:** The dual attributions could reflect different local traditions rather than genuine gender fluidity — a lion in a goddess scene at one site means something different from a lion at another site.

## Assessment

The quantitative approach (branch vs. uraeus percentages) is methodologically sound and reveals real patterns. The key finding is that these symbols *cannot* be assigned to a single gender sphere without examining the full iconographic context. A seal with a lion doesn't automatically mean "Yahweh as lion"; it could just as easily mean "goddess as mistress of lions." Context is everything.
