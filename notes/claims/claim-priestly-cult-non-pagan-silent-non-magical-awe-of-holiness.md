# kaufmann-ri-9.3: The priestly cult was radically non-pagan — silent, non-magical, defined by awe of holiness

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

The priestly temple is "the kingdom of silence." P makes no reference to the spoken word in describing temple rites. All acts are performed without incantation, spell, or prayer. The aim of the cult is not to secure blessing (do ut des) but to "hallow the sanctuary, to guard it from all impurity, to surround it with a cordon of awe, so that it should be a fit place for God's revelation." There is "no magical or mythological rite designed to heighten the power of the deity" — no fertility rites, no resurrection drama, no sexual elements, no funerary cult, no ecstasy or enthusiasm. The priestly cult excluded everything that in paganism reflected the life-and-death cycle of the gods.

## Evidence

- P's temple is the "tent of meeting" — the place appointed for God to meet Israel, not to hear prayer
- The festivals are marked only by additional sacrifices for atonement and "pleasing odor" — the first sheaf is waved for acceptance, not interpreted as a fertility rite
- The Day of Atonement in P involves only purification and atonement — no activity aimed at seeking a good decision
- Blessing is "on the periphery of priestly ritual, mere adjuncts to the main rites"
- No psalm is attributed to priests; song is no part of the priestly cult
- No priestesses, no musical or orgiastic element, no Dionysiac ecstasy
- Priests are severely enjoined from contact with death — high barriers between priest and the realm of the dead
- The priest's torah is law and statutes, cult and morality — "not an esoteric lore of cosmic magic"
- The spoken word in pagan cult articulated magical-mythological sense; its elimination "made the details of ritual lose their intrinsic meaning and become a vehicle expressive of human submission to the command of God"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Perceptive about what P OMITS compared to pagan ritual texts, but P is prescriptive ideal, not descriptive — may reflect exilic/post-exilic theology rather than pre-exilic reality.

## Stakes

Whether the evidence for Israelite cult (standing stones, figurines, high places) represents pagan practice that Kaufmann must dismiss as deviant, or whether even the "official" cult was more continuous with Canaanite practice than he allows.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who find traces of Canaanite cultic practice THROUGHOUT the biblical record — not just in condemned "popular deviance" but in the very institutions Kaufmann describes. The cherubim throne, the ark as footstool, the temple as divine dwelling — these have clear Near Eastern parallels. Lewis (Ch 10) argues that even the priestly holiness system was an ideological tool for constructing priestly hierarchy, not a theological revolution.

**Alternative reading:** The Israelite cult WAS distinctive — Kaufmann is right about that. But it was a gradual differentiation from Canaanite norms, not an absolute break. The silence and anti-magic emphasis may be exilic/post-exilic theologizing, not pre-exilic reality. P is our latest source, and its anti-mythological rigor may reflect Second Temple piety, not early Israelite practice.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's most rhetorically powerful but methodologically problematic section. He reads P as transparent historical description of actual pre-exilic temple practice. But if P IS late (as virtually all scholars hold), the "silent temple" may be a theological ideal projected backward. The striking absence of incantation, spell, and myth from P tells us about P's theology — not necessarily about what happened in Solomon's temple. Kaufmann is right that P's vision is remarkably non-pagan, but wrong that this proves early popular religion was non-pagan.
