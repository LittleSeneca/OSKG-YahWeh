# keel-ggi-6.7: The "Queen of Heaven" Cult Was a Local Ishtar-Asherah Synthesis, Not Assyrian Imperial Imposition

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VI — Iron Age IIC — Astralization and Orthodox Reaction]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VI

## The Claim

"Unless the relation between the 'Queen of Heaven' cult and the Assyrian Ishtar can be explained by means of some intermediary factors, then there is no evidence that the Assyrian administration made a deliberate attempt to propagate this cult through force." The "Queen of Heaven" condemned in Jeremiah 7 and 44 — where women bake cakes for the goddess, pour out libations, and burn incense — was not an Assyrian imperial imposition. It was a local Judean synthesis: the Assyrian Ishtar (known from seal amulets found in Palestine) merged with the indigenous Asherah to create a new, popular, astral-tinged goddess figure.

## Evidence

- Ishtar appears on Assyrian stamp seals found in Palestine — "she is found most frequently among the deities represented anthropomorphically"
- But "iconographic evidence does not support her being accepted in Judah or Jerusalem" — the Assyrian Ishtar imagery does not penetrate Judean glyptic art the way the Moon God of Haran does
- The "Queen of Heaven" cult described in Jeremiah 7 and 44 is focused on family piety, especially women's concerns — baking, libations, incense burning for food, health, security (Jer 44:17f.)
- "The aim of this cult was the transmission of blessing in the form of sufficient food, health, and security" — the EXACT same function as "Yahweh's asherah" at Kuntillet Ajrud
- "The Judahite 'Queen of Heaven' was probably none other than a resurrected form of the female human form of Asherah" — with the difference that she functioned as an INDEPENDENT entity, not as Yahweh's subordinate mediating symbol
- The iconographic evidence "documents both extremes": Assyrian Ishtar in glyptic art, Judean Asherah in terra-cotta production — "the two were never merged into one divine form"
- No Assyrian religious coercion: "there is no evidence in the country during the time of Assyrian occupation for the ostensibly Assyrian cult of the 'Queen of Heaven.' That cult appears only long after the collapse of the Assyrian superpower, in the sixth century"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The synthesis hypothesis is plausible but speculative. The lack of inscriptions naming the "Queen of Heaven" in 7th-century Judah is frustrating — we have only the biblical polemic.

## Stakes

If the "Queen of Heaven" is an indigenous revival rather than an Assyrian import, then Israelite religion's "Canaanite" elements are not foreign corruptions — they are internal developments that re-emerge under changing social conditions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Olyan (1987a — identifies Queen of Heaven as Astarte), Koch (1988 — identifies her as Asherah). Spieckermann (1982) argues for Assyrian religious coercion.

**Alternative reading:** The timing problem (Queen of Heaven cult appears AFTER Assyrian collapse) could mean it was always there but only became visible in the sources at that point — or that the collapse of Assyrian authority removed whatever was suppressing local cults.

## Assessment

The timing IS suspicious — why does the Queen of Heaven cult surface in Jeremiah only in the early 6th century? Keel & Uehlinger's answer (it was a delayed synthesis that took time to develop) is plausible but not fully satisfying. I suspect the cult existed throughout the 7th century and Jeremiah's focus on it reflects the crisis of the Babylonian siege, not the cult's novelty.
