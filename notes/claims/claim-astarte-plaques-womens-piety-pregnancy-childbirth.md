# keel-ggi-excursus.3: The goddess was primarily a figure for women — associated with pregnancy, childbirth, and female life-cycle rituals

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Excursus — Astarte Plaques]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Excursus §§57-60

## The Claim

"The relationship of the 'Astarte plaques' to the Gravidae flasks, with their emphasis on female sexuality leading up to motherhood, permits us to assume that the 'Astarte plaques' played a special role in the piety of women." "No evidence of males in this posture has been found, showing that the goddess was first and foremost an identification figure for women."

## Evidence

- **Gravidae flasks:** Small pitchers in the shape of the female body, filled with oil "to help prevent torn tissue and stretch marks during advanced pregnancy." Found in LB II through Iron IIA contexts.
- **Nursing vessel from Tell Qasile:** A vessel with holes in the breasts "through which the liquid from the container may be poured" — suggesting "the presentation of the breasts... could be understood as an offering of, or the pressing out of, the mother's milk."
- **Hand-on-belly gesture:** On many plaques, the hand position emphasizes the lower stomach region — pregnancy and childbirth concerns.
- **Female worshipers only:** On shrine plaques, smaller female figures appear alongside the goddess — "probably a worshiper or donor" — always female. "No evidence of males in this posture has been found."
- **Mold inscription:** A mold from Tall Munbaqa (northern Syria) is inscribed on the back with the cuneiform sign for "woman" (sinnistu) — "probably to be understood as identifying the goddess as an exemplary woman."
- **Grave contexts:** Where documented, "the dead individuals next to which the plaques were placed were female" (W. Helck).

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The Gravidae flask connection is strong. The exclusively-female grave associations are suggestive but sample size unspecified. The "woman" inscription on the mold is a single piece of evidence but striking.

## Stakes

This radically reframes the figurine discussion. The "Astarte plaques" aren't evidence for a generic "goddess worship" that included men and women equally. They're evidence for a specifically FEMALE religious practice centered on the female body and its life-cycle. The goddess here isn't a consort or a cosmic power — she's a protectress of pregnancy, a model of womanhood, a figure with whom women identified.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the plaques as evidence for a broadly popular goddess cult that included male worshipers. Also scholars who connect them to "sacral prostitution" (which Keel & Uehlinger mention as a possible "initiation rite" but don't endorse).

**Alternative reading:** The plaques could have been used by men ON BEHALF OF women (husbands seeking protection for pregnant wives), which would broaden the cult's social base beyond the interpretation offered here.

## Assessment

The female-specific reading is well-supported by the comparative evidence (Gravidae flasks, female grave associations). But it's also a reading that fits neatly with the broader Keel & Uehlinger thesis — that goddess worship in the Iron Age was becoming increasingly compartmentalized into "women's sphere" rather than being a central element of the shared religious symbol system.
