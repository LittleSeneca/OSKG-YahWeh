# dever-dghw-6-9.6: Women were primary domestic religious practitioners suppressed by male reformers

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapters VI-IX — Asherah, Women, Monotheism]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter VII

## The Claim

Folk religion — centered on Asherah, fertility, healing, and the family — was primarily women's domain. "Official" Yahwism — centered on the Temple, the priesthood, written law, and exclusive monotheism — was men's domain. The Deuteronomistic reform was, in effect, a male clerical coup against women's traditional religious authority.

## Evidence

- The household was the primary locus of religious practice for most Israelites — and the household was women's domain
- "Women were the primary practitioners of household religion" — tending family shrines, making offerings, using figurines for fertility and protection
- Asherah was associated with fertility, childbirth, nursing, and the life cycle — all women's concerns
- The figurines were used by women: "prayers in clay" for conception, safe delivery, milk production, children's health
- "Women's work was mainly although not exclusively at home: harvesting some things; preserving, preparing, and cooking food (a constant task); spinning, weaving, and making all the family's clothes, bedding, and floor coverings; making baskets and pottery; caring for both the elders and a house full of children; and, as I shall show, tending to the family shrine and all of the religious rituals"
- The Deuteronomistic reformers were ALL men — priests, scribes, kings. They centralized worship in Jerusalem, which most women could never visit, and eliminated the domestic cults that women had practiced for centuries.
- "Official religion was a largely male preserve: priests, Temple functionaries, scribes, royal officials — all men. Women were excluded from the priesthood, from writing, from public office."
- "It is women's cults, centering on the veneration of Asherah, that the reformers set out to destroy."

**Feminist dimension:** Dever distinguishes between "mainstream feminism" and "doctrinaire feminism," but his argument is inherently feminist: the biblical religion we've inherited is a male construction that systematically suppressed women's religious experience. Recovering Asherah is recovering women's history.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Women's role as primary domestic religious practitioners is supported by ethnographic parallels and archaeological context. The gendered dimension of the reform is real but hard to prove was the PRIMARY motivation rather than anti-polytheism generally.

## Stakes

The Bible's religion — monotheistic, aniconic, male-priest-led — is the religion of the winners. The religion it suppressed — polytheistic, image-rich, woman-led — was the religion of most Israelites. This challenges not just historical reconstructions but contemporary religious practice: if the tradition's founding moment was the suppression of women's religion, what does that mean for women in those traditions today?

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the Deuteronomistic reform as primarily theological rather than gendered; those who argue women's religious role was not as central as Dever claims; Kaufmann (the reform was an organic Israelite development restoring original monotheism).

**Alternative reading:** The reformers targeted polytheism wherever they found it; domestic cults happened to be women's domain, but the motivation was theological purity, not gender politics. Women may have supported the reform as much as men.

## Assessment

This is Dever's most distinctive contribution — no other scholar in the Truth Project corpus makes the gendered argument as forcefully. The domestic locus of Asherah worship is well-established; the inference that the reform was anti-women is more speculative. Dever's care in distinguishing mainstream feminism from doctrinaire feminism is methodological honesty, but his conclusion is still a feminist one. The reform DID dismantle women's religious spaces; whether that was its purpose or its side effect is harder to determine.
