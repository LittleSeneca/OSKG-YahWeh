# keel-ggi-3.5: Erotic couple imagery on MB IIB seals depicts the intimacy of god and goddess on a human level — and this entire religious system is specific to MB IIB, not an eternal "Canaanite religion"

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch III — MB Temples Tel Kitan and Megiddo]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter III

## The Claim

"Perhaps we should treat a series of representations of couples that convey strongly erotic connotations as analogous to the encounter of the weather god with the 'Naked Goddess.' In any case, such representations are common for the Middle Bronze Age but disappear in the later periods." And in the summary: "The religious system of signs that used massebahs, sacred trees, deities and rituals that had a patently sexual character, regarded by many as typical of 'the' Canaanite religion for the entire period from 1500 to 500, may in reality have played a decisive role only during Middle Bronze Age IIB and might have merely undergone radical modifications during the following periods, surviving only in a marginal way as the religion of the pagani, the villagers."

## Evidence

- Old Syrian hematite cylinder seal from Tell el-Ajjul: man and woman drinking together from a vessel through a tube; palms and eight-rayed star (Ishtar/Astarte sphere); a dove replaces the star on a similar seal in Krakow
- Scarabs from second half of MB IIB depict a man and woman "being intimate with one another, sometimes showing this more graphically, sometimes less"
- Scarab from Megiddo Stratum XII (ca. 1750–1700): ruler in fringed garment with a woman who has a long thick braid; secondary motifs of flower bud and fully opened bloom
- These representations disappear in later periods
- The summary emphasizes: the religious system with patently sexual character was NOT "typical of 'the' Canaanite religion" for the entire 1500–500 period, but was dominant only in MB IIB

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The descriptive claim (erotic imagery is common in MB IIB and disappears later) is a falsifiable empirical claim about the seal corpus. The interpretive claim (that this imagery represents the divine couple on a human level) is more speculative. The sweeping summary claim about the entire religious system is the chapter's most provocative and potentially its most vulnerable argument.

## Stakes

This is one of the most consequential claims in the chapter. If the "patently sexual" Canaanite religion — with its sacred prostitution, fertility rites, and goddess worship — was primarily an MB IIB phenomenon rather than a permanent feature of Canaanite culture, then the entire biblical polemic against "Canaanite" religion is attacking a system that was already historically obsolete (or at least radically transformed) by the time Israel emerged. The biblical writers may have been fighting a ghost — or at least a much-diminished remnant. This also challenges the scholarly tendency (especially in older works like Albright's) to treat "Canaanite religion" as a static, monolithic entity defined by fertility cults. Keel and Uehlinger are historicizing what others essentialized.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves are the primary "dissenter" — they treat Canaanite religion as a contemporary threat throughout the monarchy. Scholars who take the biblical polemic at face value as describing actual Iron Age practices would resist the idea that the sexually-charged fertility religion was already a marginal "religion of the pagani" by Iron II. The Ugaritic texts (Late Bronze) DO contain sexually explicit divine narratives (Baal and Anat, El's exploits) — so the system did not disappear entirely. Dever's work on folk religion emphasizes the continuity of goddess worship (Judean Pillar Figurines, Astarte plaques) well into Iron II.

**Alternative reading:** The disappearance of erotic couple imagery from SEALS may not mean the disappearance of the religious system. It could mean a shift in MEDIA — perhaps such imagery moved to perishable materials (wood, textile) or became too sensitive for public display on seals. The biblical polemic against Canaanite practices is so intense and sustained that it likely had a real target. "Marginal" does not mean nonexistent — and marginal folk practices can still generate vigorous orthodox opposition.

## Assessment

This claim is the chapter's most provocative and potentially its most vulnerable. The empirical observation about seals is solid — the iconographic record does show a decline in sexually explicit imagery after MB IIB. But the leap from "seals changed" to "the entire religious system became marginal" is large. The authors acknowledge this with their hedging: "may in reality have played a decisive role only during MB IIB and MIGHT have merely undergone radical modifications." I will watch in later chapters whether they can sustain the argument that LB and Iron Age Canaanite/Israelite religion was fundamentally DIFFERENT from the MB IIB pattern, not just a modified version of it. The tension with Romer is worth noting: Romer (The Invention of God) emphasizes CONTINUITY of Canaanite religious patterns into Israelite Yahwism, while Keel and Uehlinger are emphasizing DISCONTINUITY and transformation.
