# schmid-ht-10-12-13.6: The canon's boundaries cannot be absolute for a historically oriented theology — a "center and periphery" model is better

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§10, 12, 13 — Canon Pluriformity and Relativity]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §13

## The Claim

"The boundaries of the canon cannot serve fundamentally as absolute for a historically oriented approach to the theology of the Hebrew Bible." Instead, adopt a "center of recognized texts that is surrounded by different concentric circles of further writings that established themselves to successively lesser degrees in Judaism and Christianity."

## Evidence

- Other literature from the Second Temple period (Jubilees, Enoch, Temple Scroll, pesharim) was "dedicated to the theological pursuit of treating and resolving difficulties in the preexisting tradition"
- These texts "are highly important for the reconstruction of the historically complex thought world of the Bible, particularly because they are reckoned as part of the Bible in some canons"
- Jubilees: "little Genesis" — resolves the problem of how ancestors could follow the law before Moses gave it (through "heavenly tablets")
- Enoch literature: elaborates Gen 6:1-4, reveals heavenly secrets, preserved in Ethiopian canon
- Temple Scroll (11Q19-21): systematizes Torah law as direct divine speech, possibly intended to supersede Torah
- "What belongs to the center and to the periphery depends on multiple factors determined more by historical coincidence than by the importance of the content of each text"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The historical observation (that non-canonical texts contain theology comparable to canonical ones) is correct. The normative claim (that canon shouldn't be absolute) is a methodological judgment.

## Stakes

This is Schmid's final word on canon: it's historically contingent, theologically non-absolute, and should not constrain historical-theological inquiry. This frees him to use extra-canonical literature (Jubilees, Enoch, Qumran) as theological data alongside canonical texts.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Canonical approach scholars (Childs, Seitz) who argue the canon IS the theologically significant boundary. For them, excluding non-canonical texts is not arbitrary — it reflects the community's theological discernment.

**Alternative reading:** The canon's boundaries, while historically contingent, represent genuine theological judgments by the communities that preserved these texts. Schmid's "historical coincidence" framing undervalues the theological intentionality of canonical decisions.

## Assessment

Schmid's "center and periphery" model is historically accurate and methodologically liberating. But he may overstate the "historical coincidence" factor. The exclusion of Enoch from rabbinic Judaism was not arbitrary — it reflected theological judgments about pre-Mosaic revelation and apocalyptic speculation. Canonical decisions may be historically contingent AND theologically motivated. Schmid's reduction of canon to "accident" is his most reductive move.
