# keel-ggi-6.3: The Judean Pillar Figurines Represent a Goddess Revival in Iron Age IIC — Not Continuous Tradition

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VI — Iron Age IIC — Astralization and Orthodox Reaction]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VI

## The Claim

"Notwithstanding this 'continuity of intent'... the hiatus in the local production of small terra-cottas with representations of the goddess... is too clear for one to assume an unbroken transition from one type to another in Israel or Judah." This is the critical finding for the Asherah debate. The pillar figurines that Dever cites as evidence of widespread goddess worship are an Iron Age IIC phenomenon — they appear at the END of the 8th century, flourish in the 7th, and represent a REVIVAL after a hiatus during Iron IIB.

## Evidence

- "More than eight hundred pillar figurines from Judah have been found" — the most common terra-cotta type of Iron IIC
- Upper body: anthropomorphic breasts, arms, female head (often with elaborate "tear drop" curl hairstyles from molds)
- Lower body: hand-formed pillar pedestal (hence the name), sometimes wheel-turned and hollow
- At least one-third have crude "pinched" faces ("birdhead" appearance) — cheaper, locally produced versions
- Dating: "Pillar figurines, or fragments of such, are first found in contexts that can be dated with confidence... toward the end of the eighth century" — NOT in Iron IIB, NOT in the 10th century as Engle claimed
- The hiatus: Iron IIB has very few goddess figurines in Israel/Judah. The pillar figurines represent a new phenomenon, not an unbroken chain from LB "Astarte plaques"
- "More can be said in favor of this revival of the 'Canaanite' tradition being a harmonization linking indigenous forces with a new impulse that came 'from the outside'"
- Context: found in private homes and tombs — usually ONE per household. "The figurines represent a kind of 'household icon'"
- "The motherly goddess, whose image emphasizes especially the face (accessibility) and full breasts (blessing), appears to be none other than the (repersonalized) Asherah"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH for the dating and the revival interpretation. MEDIUM for the Phoenician/Assyrian trigger mechanism.

## Stakes

Dever's entire figurine argument depends on these being evidence of continuous, widespread goddess worship. Keel & Uehlinger agree they are evidence of goddess worship — but in the 7th CENTURY, NOT the 9th-8th centuries of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions. The pillar figurines are not proof that "Yahweh had a wife" during Iron IIB. They are proof that the goddess made a dramatic COMEBACK in Iron IIC under Assyrian imperial conditions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever (2005, who dates the figurines earlier and sees continuity), Engle (1979, who dated them to the 9th century). Hadley (1989) is closer to Keel & Uehlinger's dating.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the pillar figurines appear first in IIC, they could represent the SURFACING of a folk religion that had always existed but was not recorded in official glyptic art during IIB. The "hiatus" might be an artifact of what media survive (seals vs. cheap terra-cottas).

## Assessment

The hiatus argument is strong. During Iron IIB, not only are pillar figurines absent — ALL goddess imagery is minimal across ALL media (seals, ivories, terra-cottas). The revival in IIC is dramatic and visible across multiple media. Something changed in the late 8th century that made goddess imagery popular again. The Assyrian disruption of traditional social structures is the most plausible trigger — in times of imperial stress, people turn to mother goddesses for protection and fertility. Dever's error is chronological, not interpretive: the pillar figurines ARE Asherah — but they are Asherah in her 7th-century come-back, not evidence of her 8th-century consort status.
