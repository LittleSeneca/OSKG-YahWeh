# smith-obm-9.2: Priestly creation theology transforms royal model — humanity replaces king as divine image, cosmos becomes sanctuary

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch9 — Formation of Monotheistic Theologies]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"The creation of the human person in Genesis 1:26, in the 'image' and 'likeness' of God, represents a major shift from the old royal model. The idea of 'the image' of the gods was, in ancient Near East, applied to the king. It was the king who was the image of the deity." Genesis 1 democratizes this: "humanity, understood as the participant in the cosmic Sabbath, is to be the holy image of God on earth."

## Evidence

- ANE background: The king as divine image is widespread. Egyptian instruction of Meri-ka-re: "They who have issued from his body are his images" — applied to humanity generally, which Genesis 1 may echo. But the biblical passage shifts the concept from royal privilege to human vocation.
- Three priestly themes embedded in creation: (1) divine resting (*sbt*) on the seventh day anticipates the Sabbath institution (Exod 20, 31); (2) the lights mark feast-days, weeks, and years — the priestly cultic calendar (Gen 1:14); (3) the division of the universe into heavens, earth, and seas with animals assigned to each sphere foreshadows the dietary system of *kashrut*.
- Cosmos as sanctuary: "One may suggest that the cosmos in Genesis 1 was to be understood as a holy place, such as a sanctuary." The Temple in Jerusalem "mirrored the cosmos," and "biblical descriptions of creation and temple-building have influenced one another and constitute, in J. D. Levenson's view, a 'homology.'"
- Psalm 78:69: "He built his sanctuary like the high heavens, like the earth which he has founded for ever." Psalm 150:1 uses sanctuary and sky as parallel terms.
- W. Houston: *kashrut* arose from priestly protection of temple purity — if so, Genesis 1's delineation of cosmic realms by animals "may evoke temple practice and in turn a vision of the world as a kind of temple."
- Historical context: "Genesis 1 reflects this change: to the royal model has been added a priestly model. The politics of creation have changed. There is still a king in this world, but it is the King of Kings... This outlook would serve Israel well in exile and beyond when responsibility for community order passed from the Davidic dynasty to the priesthood of Aaron."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the democratization of the divine image and the priestly themes embedded in creation. MEDIUM on the cosmos-as-sanctuary reading — it's suggestive but Smith acknowledges it "may lie behind Genesis 1 or may be evoked by it."

## Stakes

If Genesis 1 encodes priestly institutions (Sabbath, calendar, *kashrut*) into the fabric of creation itself, these are not later human inventions but built into the cosmic order by divine design. The Priestly theology legitimates the post-exilic priestly regime by making its institutions primordial.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Genesis 1 earlier (pre-exilic) and see the priestly themes as inherent to creation theology rather than reflecting exilic/post-exilic priestly ascendancy. Those who read the divine image language as ontological rather than functional/vocational.

**Alternative reading:** The cosmos-as-temple idea could work the other way — the Temple was built to model the cosmos, not the cosmos envisioned as temple. The direction of influence matters and isn't settled.

## Assessment

The threefold embedding of priestly institutions (Sabbath, calendar, *kashrut*) is the strongest part of this argument — it's concrete and textually supported. The cosmos-as-temple idea is more speculative but the Psalm citations (78:69, 150:1) do support a genuine homology in Israelite thought. The democratization of the royal divine image is a genuinely radical theological move, well-contextualized by the shift from monarchy to priesthood.
