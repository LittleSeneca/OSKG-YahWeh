# kaufmann-ri-7.3: The Tribal League Was a "Kingdom of God" Theocracy

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VII — The Conquest and Settlement]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VII

## The Claim

Israel had a political organization before the monarchy — a confederation of independent tribes whose unity "became visible only under certain conditions." Authority was vested in two institutions: (1) the secular "primitive democracy" of elders (tribal council of clan heads, the *nasi'*), unrelated to any temple or sacral function — "unlike that of the Greek amphictyony"; and (2) above this, the "judges" — men of the "spirit," messengers of God, raised up ad hoc by YHWH. "This unparalleled political institution" was "the religious essence of the period." It was "a kingdom of the spirit, a theocracy whose divine king exhibited his rule through apostle-saviors, one of the most remarkable manifestations of the idealism of the early religion of YHWH."

## Evidence

- **Non-consecutive authority:** The judges don't form an unbroken chain. "Non-consecutiveness was the very essence of prophetic authority; it depended upon the action of the divine spirit." The people "looked for the appearance of an apostle-savior and confidently expected that YHWH would raise up such a man by his spirit."

- **Deborah as national figure:** Sisera's pressure targeted Zebulun and Naphtali, but Deborah (based between Ramah and Bethel in Ephraim) "roused the whole confederation." Judg 5 ("the ancient song") treats it as a national event — "the people of YHWH" participate, those who didn't come "to the aid of YHWH" are cursed.

- **Gideon:** Begins as local war with Midian and Amalek, "but it ends by involving Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, Naphtali, and even Ephraim." Centuries later, Isaiah still speaks of "the day of Midian" as national memory.

- **Jephthah:** Ammonites harass Transjordan, Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim — Jephthah defeats them and "delivers all these tribes."

- **Samson:** Even without mustering tribes to battle, he "could still become a national hero."

- **Contrast with Greek amphictyony:** Unlike the Greek model, the elders' authority had no sacral function and was not connected to a temple. The "primitive democracy" was secular — the religious element was the judges.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's description of the judges as charismatic, spirit-empowered, non-consecutive leaders is accurate to the biblical portrayal. His argument against the "local heroes later nationalized" view has some force — the Song of Deborah (Judg 5) is genuinely ancient and already treats the battle as a national event. But the pattern of charismatic military leaders emerging in segmentary societies under external threat is well-attested cross-culturally. The distinctive Israelite element isn't the *pattern* but the *theology* — and whether that theology was early or a later framework is precisely what's contested.

## Stakes

Whether the pre-monarchic period genuinely embodied a distinctive religious-political ideal (Kaufmann) or was just a typical segmentary tribal society whose charismatic leaders were later theologized by biblical editors (critical view).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Cross (Parts II-III) explicitly compares Israel's league to Greek amphictyonies, arguing that central sanctuaries (Shechem, Shiloh, Bethel) DID have sacral functions for the league. Fleming (Ch 6) describes the "people of Yahweh" as a collaborative political identity but not a formal theocratic institution. Critical scholarship generally sees the "judges" framework as a Deuteronomistic construction imposed on older, disparate local hero stories.

**Alternative reading:** The judges were local military leaders whose stories were collected, expanded, and given a national-theological framework by later editors. The "kingdom of God" concept is a theological retrojection — it describes how later Israel understood its past, not how that past was actually experienced. The Greek amphictyony comparison that Kaufmann rejects actually illuminates the Israelite league's sacral character — as Cross argues, the central sanctuary at Shiloh (where the ark resided) was precisely a league sanctuary.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's description of the judges as charismatic, spirit-empowered, non-consecutive leaders is accurate to the biblical portrayal. His argument against the "local heroes later nationalized" view has some force — the Song of Deborah (Judg 5) is genuinely ancient and already treats the battle as a national event. But Kaufmann's insistence on "uniqueness" again overreaches. The pattern of charismatic military leaders emerging in segmentary societies under external threat is well-attested cross-culturally. The distinctive Israelite element isn't the *pattern* but the *theology* — the interpretation of these leaders as agents of a single divine king. That theology may be early or may be a later interpretive framework; Kaufmann assumes the former without adequately arguing for it.
