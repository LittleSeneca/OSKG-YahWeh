# schmid-ht-11.2: The Synod of Jamnia is an academic fallacy — there was no council that decided the canon

**Source:** [[Schmid — §11 — The Tanakh as the Hebrew Bible]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §11

## The Claim

"The scholarship of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries often brought the verifiable evidence of a canon from Josephus and the book of 4 Ezra together with the assumption of a synod in Jamnia, which supposedly decided upon this canon. In 1871 Heinrich Grätz interpreted statements from the Mishnah and Talmud as supporting such a synod. While Jamnia did in fact become a center of Jewish scholarship after 70 CE, there was neither a synod that took place there, nor was the canonical nature of the writings of the Hebrew Bible as a whole discussed, but only the status of Qoheleth and Song of Songs."

## Evidence

- Grätz's (1871) misinterpretation of Mishnaic/Talmudic statements
- Only Qoheleth and Song of Songs were debated at Jamnia — not the canon as a whole
- Citation of Günter Stemberger, "Jabne und der Kanon," JBTh 3 (1988): 163-74

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The Jamnia synod is now universally recognized as a scholarly myth. Stemberger's work is definitive.

## Stakes

The Jamnia myth supported the idea that the canon was established by authoritative decision. Without Jamnia, the canon's formation was a gradual, decentralized process. This supports Schmid's "center and periphery" model of canon formation rather than a "council decision" model.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No serious scholars today. The Jamnia myth persists in popular literature and some confessional circles despite being debunked.

**Alternative reading:** Even without a formal council, Jamnia represents the rabbinic community's consolidation of which texts were authoritative — the process was real even if the "synod" framing was historiographical fiction.
