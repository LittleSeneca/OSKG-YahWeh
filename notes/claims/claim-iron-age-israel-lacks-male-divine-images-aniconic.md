# lewis-ocg-5.1: Iron Age Israel Lacks Male Divine Images — Aniconic Exceptionalism

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 5 — Iconography of Divinity — El]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"In contrast, Iron Age Israel, with its lack of physical representations of male divinity, is exceptional. ... In contrast to the absence of concrete representations, literary texts freely employ anthropomorphic language for El and Yahweh." He quotes Uehlinger: "On the whole, the notion of 'Ancient Israel' as an 'aniconic nation' is erroneous... The prohibition [of cultic images] presupposes the knowledge and practice of iconolatry in at least some circles of Judahite society."

## Evidence

- All the bronze figurines discussed (Hazor, Beth-Shemesh, Megiddo, Beth-Shean, Tel Kinneret, Nablus/"Shechem") are Late Bronze Age types
- Hendel (1997): these bronzes "are Late Bronze Age types that represent... 'the end of the Canaanite tradition in the region to be occupied by the kingdoms of Judah and Israel.' These types were no longer produced after the eleventh and tenth centuries BCE"
- Dever's famous 1983 statement: "no representations of a male deity in terra cotta, metal, or stone have ever been found in clear Iron Age contexts"
- The Gezer stick figure, often cited as the exception, comes from destruction debris with intrusive Persian material — its tenth-century date is questionable
- The Hazor bronze Ahlström called "Yahweh" is from a LB hoard
- Female figurines (Judean pillar figurines) ARE abundant in Iron Age contexts — making the absence of male ones all the more striking
- Textual evidence for anthropomorphism (God walks, sits, has a body) contrasts sharply with material-culture evidence (no images)

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** Very high on the absence of Iron Age male divine figurines; high that this reflects aniconism — the fact that female figurines ARE abundant in Iron Age contexts makes the absence of male ones all the more striking.

## Stakes

The aniconic tradition is often treated as a late theological development (Deuteronomic, prophetic). The archaeological evidence suggests it was operational much earlier — affecting actual cultic practice, not just elite theology. This complicates the standard narrative that aniconism emerged FROM monotheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Niehr and others argue for a cult statue of Yahweh in the Jerusalem Temple. Mettinger argues that masseboth ARE divine images, so Israel wasn't aniconic in a strict sense. Uehlinger says the prohibition proves iconolatry existed — you don't ban what nobody does.

**Alternative reading:** The absence could be due to preservation bias — wood and clay images may not survive. The biblical image-ban texts describe precisely the kinds of images we DON'T find (gold, silver, wood), suggesting they existed but were destroyed or didn't preserve.

## Assessment

Lewis's treatment of this is excellent. He doesn't claim absolute aniconism — he documents the masseboth as possible exceptions. But the contrast with Ugarit (where multiple clear ʾIlu images exist) and with female figurines (abundant in Iron Age Israel) is telling. Something was different about how Israel represented its male deity.
