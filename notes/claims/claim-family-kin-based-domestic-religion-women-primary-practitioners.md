# dever-dghw-1-2.1: Religion was kin-based and domestic, with women as primary practitioners

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction and Chapters I-II — Methodology]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Introduction and Chapters I-II

## The Claim

The "house of the father" (*bet-'av*) was the basic social and religious unit in ancient Israel. Women were the primary practitioners of household religion. Family shrines — not temples — were where most religious activity happened. Ancestor veneration was central (the *rephaim*, feeding the dead). "Book religion" was a late male clerical construction imposed on a much older family-based religion that was largely women's domain.

## Evidence

- **The "house of the father" (*bet-'av*)** was the basic social and religious unit — the extended family household, not the tribe or nation.
- **Women were the primary practitioners** of household religion — responsible for the domestic cult, including figurines, standing stones, asherah poles, and offerings to ancestors.
- **Family shrines** — not the Jerusalem Temple or even local "high places" — were where most religious activity actually happened.
- **Ancestor veneration** was central — the *rephaim* (shades of the dead), feeding the dead, maintaining family tombs — this was the domestic religious practice that the Deuteronomistic reformers most vigorously suppressed.
- **"Book religion" was a late male clerical construction** — the religion of the Hebrew Bible was created by male priests and scribes and imposed on a much older family-based religion that was largely women's domain.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The archaeological evidence for domestic cult installations is well-established. The claim that family religion was women's domain is a reasonable inference from figurine distributions and ethnographic parallels, though direct evidence is limited.

## Stakes

If Dever is right, the erasure of the goddess from Israelite religion was simultaneously the erasure of women's religious authority. The deuteronomistic reform wasn't just a theological shift — it was a gendered power struggle in which male clerics displaced female household practitioners.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize the patriarchal structure of Israelite society might argue that official religion was always male-dominated and the household cult was supervised by male heads of household. The claim that women were *primary* practitioners may overstate the evidence.

**Alternative reading:** Household religion may have been practiced by both men and women in different roles, with women responsible for certain rites (fertility, childbirth) and men for others (ancestor veneration, clan rituals).

## Assessment

Dever's emphasis on women as primary practitioners of household religion is one of his most distinctive contributions. It connects the archaeological evidence (female figurines, domestic cult installations) to the gendered politics of the deuteronomistic reform. The question is whether he overstates the case — but the basic point that household religion was the domain where women exercised religious authority is well-supported by the material evidence.
