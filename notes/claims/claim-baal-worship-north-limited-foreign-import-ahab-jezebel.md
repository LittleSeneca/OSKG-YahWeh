# kaufmann-ri-8.5: Baal worship in the north was a limited, foreign import under Ahab/Jezebel

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"The Baal worship of the north was limited to court circles" and "no evidence for syncretism in the religion of YHWH during the period of the Omrides exists." Jezebel's Baalism was a Phoenician import that "had evaporated by the time of Joram." Jehu's revolution "cannot be interpreted as a life struggle of the religion of YHWH against the menace of Baalism." Rather, "No menace of 'syncretism,' but the strength and zealotry of the religion of YHWH are what demanded that the last vestiges of Baalism be eradicated, even after Baal had long since ceased being a living issue."

## Evidence

- After Elijah's Carmel victory, Baal worship "no longer appears... as a factor in the relations between Elijah and Ahab." Ahab goes to war surrounded by YHWH prophets (I Kings 22)
- The new conflict between Elijah and Ahab arises over Naboth's murder, not Baal worship
- "The dynasty's doom is sealed, according to this, not by Baal worship, but by the murder of Naboth"
- Under Joram, the "worshipers in [the Baal temple] were a mere remnant of survivors from Ahab's time" (II Kings 10:18). Joram removes a "pillar of Baal" (II Kings 3:2)
- "The narratives concerning Elisha furnish glimpses of life throughout the north and involve persons of various condition; nowhere do we meet with Baal worship"
- Jehu's ruse (announcing he would "outdo Ahab in his zeal for Baal") "could have been successful only because in its first stages Jehu's revolt did not appear to have as its chief end the annihilation of Baalism"
- "What roused prophets and zealots into action was the national distress after the death of Ahab" — military defeats interpreted as YHWH's punishment for bloodguilt

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann needs Baal worship to be foreign and ephemeral to protect his thesis; the evidence from Hosea, archaeology, and theophoric names suggests it was more indigenous and persistent.

## Stakes

Whether Baal worship in Israel was (a) an indigenous development from Canaanite religion that periodically threatened to syncretize with YHWH worship, or (b) a foreign import that was always alien, limited, and quickly eradicated. For Kaufmann's larger thesis, (b) is essential.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The archaeological record complicates Kaufmann's picture. Smith documents extensive evidence for Baal worship in Israel through the monarchy period — not just under the Omrides. The Samaria ostraca contain Baal-ist theophoric names. Day traces the Baal-Yahweh conflict as a recurring, not one-time, phenomenon. Hosea's polemic (generations after Jehu) still fights Baalism.

**Alternative reading:** Baal worship in Israel was both foreign AND indigenous. The specific Tyrian Baal (Melqart) introduced by Jezebel was a Phoenician import, but Baal worship more broadly had Canaanite roots that predated the monarchy. Kaufmann conflates the specific Tyrian Baal cult with Baal worship generally.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reading has some textual support — the biblical narrative really does suggest that Jehu's revolution was more about bloodguilt than Baal worship per se, and Jezebel's Baal really was a foreign import. But Kaufmann overplays his hand. The persistence of Baal polemic in Hosea, the archaeological evidence for Baal theophoric names, and the structural similarities between Baal and YHWH's imagery all suggest that the Baal-Yahweh relationship was more complex than a foreign import that was quickly eradicated. Kaufmann needs Baal worship to be foreign and ephemeral to protect his thesis; the evidence suggests it was more indigenous and persistent than he allows.
