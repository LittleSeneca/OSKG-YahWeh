# smith-obm-4.5: Athtart as "Name of Baal" represents a divine hypostasis with biblical parallels

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch4 — Pluralities Pairings and Other Divine Relations]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 4

## The Claim

Athtart's title šm b'l, "name of Baal," marks her as the hypostasis or cultic presence of Baal. The biblical divine "Name" (šēm) operates similarly: it is the theophanic manifestation that accompanies Yahweh, especially in martial contexts.

## Evidence

- Athtart's title šm b'l in CAT 1.16 VI 56 and KAI 14:18 (Phoenician).
- Philo of Byblos (PE 1.10.31): Astarte and Baal rule together as king and queen under El's consent.
- Cross, Mullen, McCarter, Olyan: Athtart as name-hypostasis of Baal.
- Biblical parallels: Exodus 23:20-21 — Yahweh's messenger has "my name within him." The name acts as warrior in Isaiah 30:27.
- Psalm 29:1-2: the "glory of his name" (kěbôd šěmô) in a storm theophany context — the name manifests radiance comparable to Akkadian melammu.
- Personal names from Ugarit and Amarna: šm b'l, šm 'nt, šum-adda — "name of [deity]" as a personal name pattern.
- McCarter: hypostases as "cultically available presence in the temple."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The "name" as hypostasis has strong support in biblical theology and reasonable Ugaritic backing; the direction of influence (Ugaritic → Israelite or shared West Semitic) is unclear.

## Stakes

If the biblical "Name theology" (Deuteronomic emphasis on God's name dwelling in the temple) has a polytheistic background in divine hypostases, it transforms how we read a central biblical concept. The Name wasn't an abstraction — it was a mode of divine presence inherited from polytheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read šm b'l as simply "the renown/fame of Baal" rather than a hypostasis. Those who see Name theology as a deuteronomic innovation, not an inheritance.

**Alternative reading:** The title šm b'l could reflect Athtart's role as Baal's herald or representative rather than an actual hypostatic manifestation. The name "dwelling" in the temple could be a metaphor rather than a theophanic reality.

## Assessment

This is one of Smith's most interesting arguments — and it has implications for Parts II-III. If the divine Name in Israel inherits the function of a goddess-hypostasis, then monotheism absorbed and transformed a feminine divine presence. Smith doesn't quite say this, but it's the implication.
