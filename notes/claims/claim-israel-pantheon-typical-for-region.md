# smith-ehg-1.5: Israel's pantheon was typical for the first-millennium Levantine region.

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2nd ed., 2002), Chapter 1

## The Claim

First-millennium Levantine states (Ammon, Moab, Edom, Phoenician city-states) all had small pantheons of 10 or fewer deities headed by a national god. Israel's configuration (Yahweh as national god, El/Baal/Asherah as subordinate or absorbed deities, divine council) was unremarkable. The transition from second-millennium pantheons of 200+ deities (Ugarit) to first-millennium pantheons of ~10 was a regional trend. Israel participated in it — and eventually took it to its logical extreme (one god).

## Evidence

Survey of Ammon (Milkom, El, ~10 deities), Moab (Chemosh, Ashtar-Chemosh), Edom (Qaws, possibly El and Baal), Byblos (Baal Shamem, Baalat Gebal, ~8), Sidon (Eshmun, Astarte, ~6), Tyre (Bethel, Baal Shamem, Melqart, Astarte, ~8-10).

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The data is sparse — we have far fewer texts from these cultures than from Ugarit. "10 or fewer deities" might reflect gaps in the evidence, not actual pantheon size. Smith acknowledges this: "deriving historical claims on the basis of the actually attested texts... is highly problematic."

## Stakes

This contextualizes Israelite religion. Israel wasn't uniquely polytheistic (as the Bible's polemic implies) or uniquely monotheistic (as traditional theology claims). It was typical for its time and place. What WAS unique was taking the regional trend toward fewer gods to its conclusion: zero gods besides Yahweh.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the comparative data as such. The question is what weight to give it for understanding Israel. Conservatives would argue Israel's distinctiveness was theological, not structural — Israel had a pantheon structure but a fundamentally different concept of the divine.

**Alternative reading:** The comparative data is too sparse to draw strong conclusions. We don't know how many deities these cultures actually had — we only know what survived in inscriptions. Israel's biblical record is fragmentary too but far richer than the comparative data. Comparing attested deities in Ammon (from a handful of inscriptions) with attested deities in Israel (from a massive literary corpus) is methodologically problematic.

## Assessment

The comparative data is genuinely useful for countering both "Israel was always monotheistic" and "Israel was uniquely depraved in its polytheism." Neither is true. Smith's regional survey is one of the most underappreciated sections of the chapter.
