# stav-god-pro-1.4: Genesis 1:26 ('Let US make humankind in OUR image') reflects a polytheistic divine council where God addresses other deities — this is the scholarly consensus, not a fringe reading.

**Source:** [[Stavrakopoulou — Prologue + Chapter 1 — Dissecting the Divine]] — Francesca Stavrakopoulou, *God: An Anatomy* (2021), Chapter 1

## The Claim

Stavrakopoulou asserts that Genesis 1:26 depicts God addressing a divine council of other deities. "In academic circles, there is nothing particularly contentious" about this reading. The God of Genesis 1 "had yet to be imagined as the only divine being in the universe." Like the Babylonian deity Marduk or the Greek god Zeus, "this ancient deity had long been cast as the king of the cosmos, but like them, he was far from alone in the heavens."

## Evidence

- The plural language of Genesis 1:26: "Let US make humankind in OUR image, after OUR likeness"
- The ANE context: supreme deities addressing their divine councils is a standard trope in Mesopotamian and Canaanite literature
- The scholarly consensus: Heiser, Sommer, Smith, and essentially all critical biblical scholars read this as a divine council scene
- The plural is not a "royal we" (no evidence for this usage in ancient Hebrew) nor a reference to the Trinity (anachronistic)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The divine council reading of Genesis 1:26 is the scholarly consensus, shared by Heiser, Sommer, Smith, and essentially everyone except fundamentalists.

## Stakes

The first chapter of the Bible — the foundational text of Jewish and Christian theology — describes a polytheistic divine council. "God" is one deity among others, the chief of a pantheon. The Bible's opening act is not "In the beginning, God created alone" but "In the beginning, the high god addressed his council." This reframes the entire biblical narrative as emerging from a polytheistic context, not a monotheistic one.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Religious traditionalists who read the plural as a "royal we" or a Trinitarian reference.

**Alternative reading:** The plural is a "plural of majesty" (royal we), a "plural of deliberation" (God deliberating with himself), or a reference to the Trinity (anachronistic Christian reading).

## Assessment

Stavrakopoulou is on solid ground here — the divine council reading is genuinely the consensus. Her framing is deliberately provocative (calling it "polytheistic" rather than the more neutral "divine council" or "henotheistic"), but the underlying claim is correct. The question is not WHETHER Genesis 1:26 depicts a divine council (it does), but what theological significance to assign to it.
