# keel-ggi-6.1: Astralization Replaces Solarization — Assyrian Conquest Brings Shift from Sun to Stars

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VI — Iron Age IIC — Astralization and Orthodox Reaction]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VI

## The Claim

"The chief characteristic of the glyptic art of Iron Age IIC is not the generally modest presence of Assyrian deities but the general tendency toward the astralization of the religious symbol system. During the seventh century, preference was given to the divine powers being accepted and worshiped in their astral form, with emphasis on their nocturnal appearance in the starry heavens." The Egyptian solar motifs that dominated Iron Age IIB (sun child in lotus, winged solar disks) give way to Mesopotamian astral motifs: the eight-rayed Venus star (Ishtar), the crescent moon (Sin), and the Pleiades. This represents a fundamental shift in how the divine was conceptualized: from the constant, life-giving sun to the remote, mysterious stars of the night sky.

## Evidence

- Cylinder seals reappear after centuries of absence — "not even three dozen" from 8th-7th centuries in Palestine, found almost exclusively at Assyrian administrative centers (Megiddo, Dor, Samaria, Gezer, Beth-Shean)
- The most frequent cylinder seal motif: the Assyrian king as bareheaded worshiper before astral symbols (crescent moon standard, eight-rayed star, Pleiades) — "the emphasis in the illustration is on the king... loyalty shown by the king to the heavenly powers effects, or ought to effect, the growth and prosperity of the fertile land"
- Assyrian cylinder seal from Gezer: winged genius fighting a bovine creature, with Venus star, crescent moon, and Pleiades in the upper field
- Only four Assyrian deities appear anthropomorphically: Ninurta (warrior god vs. dragon), Adad (weather god with bow), Ishtar (goddess in star nimbus), and occasionally other figures
- Most Assyrian gods appear only as cultic symbols: the crescent moon standard of the Moon God of Haran, the stylus of Nabu, the spade of Marduk
- "Mediating creatures, such as winged, hybrid creatures, no longer play a significant role in Iron Age IIC iconography" — the cherubs and sphinxes of Iron IIB recede, replaced by astral symbols and (male) worshipers

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The shift from solar to astral iconography is documented across hundreds of seals and is one of the clearest period markers in the archaeological record.

## Stakes

The solarization-to-astralization shift tracks the broader geopolitical shift from Egyptian to Mesopotamian cultural dominance. More importantly, it shows that Israelite religion was not developing in isolation toward monotheism — it was adapting to imperial religious languages. The "astral cults" condemned in 2 Kings 23:5 (Josiah deposed priests "who burned incense to Baal, to the sun and moon, to the constellations and to all the host of heaven") are not an invention of the deuteronomistic editor — they describe a real 7th-century religious landscape that the iconographic evidence confirms.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who minimize Assyrian influence and see astral cults as indigenous Canaanite revivals (McKay 1973). Cogan (1974) argues the astral cults are Syrian/Aramean, not Assyrian.

**Alternative reading:** The astral motifs could represent the religious practice of Assyrian officials stationed in Palestine, not the local population. Keel & Uehlinger note this possibility but argue the local adoption is visible in provincial seal production.

## Assessment

The astralization is unequivocally real. The question is how deeply it penetrated local Israelite/Judean religion vs. remaining an elite/official phenomenon. Keel & Uehlinger's evidence from locally produced seals suggests significant penetration — but the aniconic reaction (Claims 6.5-6.6) shows there was also resistance.
