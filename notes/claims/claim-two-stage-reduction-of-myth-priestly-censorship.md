# smith-obm-9.6: The reduction of myth occurred in two stages driven by monolatry, priestly censorship, and scribalism

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch9 — Formation of Monotheistic Theologies]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"Two stages of avoiding and muting anthropomorphism (and thus mythic language) are discernible, one at the outset of known Israelite texts and another perhaps in the eighth to sixth centuries."

## Evidence

### Stage 1 (early Israel)
- Early biblical poetry shows many points of contact with Ugaritic literature in describing the storm-god, but "Yahweh is not as personified in the oldest biblical poetry as Baal is in Ugaritic tradition."
- Baal's conflict with Yamm (CAT 1.2 IV) is "discernibly more anthropomorphic than any biblical descriptions of Yahweh's conflict against cosmic enemies."
- Divine sex: Ugaritic texts graphically describe Baal copulating with a heifer "seventy-seven times"/"eighty-eight times" (CAT 1.5 V 19-21). The Bible "shows no example of sex or death in the case of Yahweh comparable to the sexual behavior and death in Ugaritic poetry."
- Theriomorphism: Ugaritic deities are bulls (El) and birds (Anat). "Divine theriomorphous depictions in Israelite literature are rare and slight by comparison."

### Stage 2 (8th-6th centuries)
- Earlier works like the Yahwist show "uninhibited anthropomorphism" (H. Bloom); later works further mute it.
- Deuteronomy 4:12, 15: Yahweh has no "form" (*temuna*) — linked to the prohibition against graven images.
- Ezekiel 1 deliberately reduces the anthropomorphic presentation of the divine council compared to Isaiah 6.

### Three reasons for the reduction
1. **Monolatry concerns:** "The inclusion of other divine images may have been influenced by concerns about monolatry."
2. **Priestly censorship:** "The presentation of Yahweh generally as sexless and unrelated to the realm of death was apparently produced by a priesthood who viewed this deity as fully removed from realms of impurity, especially sex and death."
3. **Scribalism:** The role of scribalism in forming Israel's religious tradition — Yahweh presented as divine scribe (Exod 31:18; 34:1; Deut 4:13; 5:22).

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the two-stage model. HIGH on the contrast between Ugaritic and Israelite anthropomorphism. MEDIUM on the priestly censorship model — it's plausible but hard to verify.

## Stakes

If myth was reduced rather than rejected, and the reduction was driven by specific social groups (priests, scribes) with specific agendas (holiness, monolatry), then the Bible's relative absence of myth is not evidence of Israel's theological uniqueness but of the editorial success of particular interest groups. The "demise of myth" is a sociopolitical process, not a theological achievement.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israel's reduced myth as evidence of early monotheism (Kaufmann, Albright). Those who question whether the two stages can be distinguished given the difficulty of dating biblical texts.

**Alternative reading:** The reduction could be a function of genre rather than chronology. The early poetry (Judges 5, Psalm 29) is hymnic; the Baal Cycle is narrative myth. Different genres have different conventions for anthropomorphism. The "reduction" might be an artifact of what was preserved, not what was produced.

## Assessment

The Ugaritic-Israelite contrast on divine sexuality is Smith's strongest evidence — the difference is stark and genre alone can't explain it. But the two-stage model is sketchy: "early" texts are hard to date, and Smith acknowledges "the sources for the first stage are unfortunately inaccessible to modern students." The priestly censorship argument, while plausible, risks circularity: the priests edited out myth because they edited out myth.
