# kaufmann-ri-intro.5: Israelite monotheism was a popular, national creation — not an elite doctrine

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Introduction]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Introduction

## The Claim

Israelite monotheism "was not a theological doctrine conceived and nurtured in limited circles or schools" but "the fundamental idea of a national culture" from the beginning.

## Evidence

None in the introduction — this is a thesis statement. The evidence will presumably come in Chapters IV (The Religion of the People) and throughout the historical survey.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The claim that Israelite monotheism was popular rather than elite runs counter to the biblical texts themselves, which CONSTANTLY depict the prophets as a minority calling an unwilling people back to YHWH. If monotheism were genuinely popular, why does the entire prophetic corpus consist of rebuke for idolatry?

## Stakes

Whether the Bible's own picture of Israel's religious history (constant apostasy, prophetic rebuke) is essentially accurate or fundamentally misleading. Kaufmann wants to say: the Bible's picture is CORRECT that monotheism was original, but the "apostasy" was a superficial phenomenon, not a reversion to genuine polytheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves, if read straightforwardly. The Deuteronomistic History depicts a people constantly falling into worship of other gods. The prophets thunder against precisely this. Kaufmann has to argue that all this testimony is about something OTHER than genuine polytheism.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical picture of constant apostasy IS accurate — Israel WAS polytheistic through most of the monarchy, and monotheism was indeed an elite prophetic position that only became the popular norm after the Exile. This reading has the virtue of taking both the biblical texts and the archaeological evidence at face value.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is doing something genuinely clever here — he's accepting the biblical picture of early monotheism but reinterpreting the evidence of "apostasy" as something less than genuine polytheism. Whether this works depends entirely on whether he can characterize the archaeological and biblical evidence for polytheistic practice in a way that's intellectually honest. The danger is that he simply DEFINES genuine polytheism in a way that excludes the Israelite evidence by fiat.
