# kaufmann-ri-8.10: The new eschatology emerged during the Aramaean wars, before the exile

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"Before the Aramaean wars the possibility that YHWH would really break his covenant with Israel and expel it from its land could hardly have been seriously contemplated." But during the terrible century between Ahab and Jeroboam II, "a significant change took place in the mood of the people." The incessant wars "ravaged and impoverished the land," and to the pious "it seemed as if YHWH were withdrawing his favor." "The earlier curses and threats become an eschatological certainty." The hope for a "new act of divine grace" was born, and "the 'end of days' was transferred from the present to the future."

## Evidence

- Deuteronomy 4:25ff, 31:16ff — "Coming into the 'rest and the inheritance' are no longer the final promise. After a period of well-being, Israel will stray from YHWH to serve other gods; then a time of punishment and distress will come"
- The Song of Moses (Deut 32) — "enslavement to a 'no-people,' a 'foolish nation' (the Aramaeans), drought, famine, plague and wild animals, and death by the sword" — but no exile
- Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 4:25-28 — exile appears as the final calamity, followed by repentance and restoration
- "These monitions... reveal acquaintance neither with the conditions of the real exile nor with the historiosophy of classical prophecy" — they are pre-classical
- Elijah's vision (I Kings 19:15ff.) — Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha charged with eradicating Baal — "Here the idea glimmers forth that religious faith, not national existence, is the ultimate value"
- The "remnant" concept appears as "the 'seven thousand' who will be saved by YHWH" in Elijah's vision
- "In the south, such visions centered always about the house of David; this was the reason of their enduring importance"
- The "day of YHWH" mentioned by Amos (5:18ff.) took shape in this period
- Elisha and Jonah's prophecies of salvation for the Jehu dynasty — "northern prophecy expected that through these kings YHWH would deliver his people"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Plausible reconstruction that is textually grounded, but dating specific Deuteronomy/Leviticus passages to this period precisely is contestable — some layers may be exilic.

## Stakes

The development of Israelite eschatology and its relationship to historical crisis. If Kaufmann is right, the key elements of later prophetic eschatology — judgment, remnant, ideal king, day of YHWH — emerged in the 9th-8th centuries in response to Aramaean pressure, not in the 6th century in response to the Babylonian exile.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Many scholars date the eschatological passages in Deuteronomy (especially those mentioning exile) to the exilic period or later, seeing them as *vaticinia ex eventu* — prophecies written after the events they predict. Kaufmann's argument that they "reveal acquaintance neither with the conditions of the real exile" is contestable.

**Alternative reading:** The eschatology in Deuteronomy and Leviticus 26 developed over time, with earlier layers reflecting Aramaean and Assyrian crises and later layers incorporating the experience of the Babylonian exile. The texts as we have them are composite. However, the basic observation that eschatological thinking began before the exile — that the fall of Samaria in 722 generated theological reflection that laid groundwork for later developments — is widely accepted.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reconstruction of an emerging eschatology during the Aramaean crisis is one of the chapter's more valuable contributions. The observation that exile language appears in contexts that seem to predate the actual exile is worth taking seriously. However, his insistence on dating specific texts to this period with precision overreaches. The Deuteronomy passages are composite texts that almost certainly include exilic and postexilic layers. Kaufmann's observation that they reflect a pre-exilic eschatological *mood* is valuable; his claim that they are pre-exilic in their *entirety* is less convincing.
