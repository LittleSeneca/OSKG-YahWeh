# cross-cmhe-pre-i.1: Israelite religion emerged from Canaanite culture in an orderly historical transformation

**Source:** [[Cross — Preface + Part I — Canaanite Religion and El]] — Frank Moore Cross, *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), Preface

## The Claim

Cross positions himself methodologically between two extremes. Against Wellhausen's evolutionary idealism: the older synthesis based on Hegelian idealism is "obstinately surviving" and must be cleared away. Against Kaufmann: "Kaufmann's insistence that Israelite religion 'was absolutely different from anything the pagan world ever knew' violates fundamental postulates of scientific historical method."

Cross's alternative: Israelite religion emerged from Canaanite culture in an "orderly set of relationships which follow the usual typological sequences of historical change." Novelty is real — but it must be described as transformation of existing materials, not creation ex nihilo.

"Characteristic of the religion of Israel is a perennial and unrelaxed tension between the mythic and the historical."

The "epic" as key genre: "Perhaps the term 'epic' best designates the constitutive genre of Israel's religious expression. Epic in interpreting historical events combines mythic and historical features in various ways and proportions." Israel took Canaanite MYTH and transformed it into historical EPIC.

## Evidence

- Cross rejects BOTH Wellhausen's idealist evolutionary model AND Kaufmann's radical uniqueness thesis as methodologically unsound.
- The "epic" genre concept: Israel's religious literature is neither pure myth (Canaanite) nor pure history (modern) but "epic" — myth historicized. This is Cross's signature contribution.
- Throughout the book, Cross traces specific transformations: Ba'l's defeat of Sea → Yahweh's defeat of Egypt; El's divine council → Yahweh's divine council; El's epithets → applied to Yahweh; Ba'l's temple on Saphon → Yahweh's temple on Zion.
- Cross is the bridge between Albright (early Mosaic monotheism) and Smith (late evolutionary monotheism). He emphasizes CONTINUITY with Canaanite culture while preserving Israelite NOVELTY — a productive tension that allowed his students to move in different directions.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Cross makes this case definitively — the specific philological and literary demonstrations throughout the book are the evidence for the methodology.

## Stakes

Cross's methodology is the intellectual foundation of the entire evolutionary consensus. Smith, Romer, Dever, and Day all work within the framework Cross established: continuity with Canaanite culture is the baseline; novelty must be demonstrated, not assumed. If Cross's methodological framework is wrong, the entire field's approach to Israelite religion needs reexamination.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]] — Kaufmann's thesis that Israelite religion was "absolutely different from anything the pagan world ever knew" is the primary target of Cross's methodological critique. Albright's early Mosaic monotheism is a softer version of the same position.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion IS fundamentally unique — not evolutionarily derived from Canaanite culture — and the parallels Cross identifies are superficial or represent common ANE cultural vocabulary rather than genetic relationship.
