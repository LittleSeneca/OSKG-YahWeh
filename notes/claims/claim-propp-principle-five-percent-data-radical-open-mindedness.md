# lewis-ocg-3.5: The Propp Principle — 5% Data, Radical Open-Mindedness

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 3 — Methodology]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Lewis endorses William Propp's argument that "we are obliged to adopt as provisional truth the most likely and parsimonious reconstruction" is a "pernicious" scholarly axiom. Propp argues: "We can and must take into account the 95 percent of information hidden from our view, the sea bottom connecting solitary islands of data. The only sensible response to fragmented, slowly but randomly accruing evidence is radical open-mindedness."

## Evidence

- The Hebrew Bible is silent on incantations, yet Ketef Hinnom amulets prove they were used — a whole category of religious practice entirely absent from our textual sources
- The Pentateuch and Deuteronomistic History are silent on cultic singers, yet they were "a constituent feature of temple worship throughout the ancient Near East" (Knoppers)
- Statistical arguments about word distribution assume near-complete datasets when we have "a very small dataset"
- The fragmentary nature of archaeological discovery means we are always missing categories of evidence we cannot even name
- What survives is determined by chance (preservation conditions, excavation coverage) not by ancient importance

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High that our dataset is extremely limited; medium on whether parsimony is "pernicious" — Occam's razor exists for a reason, and "radical open-mindedness" can shade into "anything is possible."

## Stakes

If the Propp Principle is right, most confident historical reconstructions in the field are overconfident. This includes much of what Lewis himself argues in Chapters 4-10. There's a productive tension between Lewis's endorsement of Propp and his willingness to make positive claims.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Smith, Cross, and Dever all make confident reconstructions that Propp would call "failures of imagination." The minimalist school (Thompson, Lemche) would say Propp doesn't go far enough — if we have 5%, we should be radically skeptical, not just open-minded.

**Alternative reading:** Parsimony has genuine value as a heuristic — Occam's razor is not just aesthetic preference. "Radical open-mindedness" without constraints produces unfalsifiable claims.

## Assessment

This is the most intellectually honest passage in the book so far. Lewis is essentially saying: "I'm going to argue for X, but I could be wrong, and here's why." That's the right tone for a reference work. The question is whether he maintains this epistemic humility in the substantive chapters or defaults to confident assertion.
