# smith-obm-4.3: Ugaritic binomial divine names reflect theological reflection, not fusion

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch4 — Pluralities Pairings and Other Divine Relations]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 4

## The Claim

The prevalence of paired divine names (Kothar wa-Hasis, Shahar and Shalim, El and Athirat, etc.) reflects "theological reflection" on relationships within divinity. Against de Moor's "fusion" theory: "In many cases, we do not possess enough information to confirm or preclude such a conclusion."

## Evidence

- Extensive catalogue of paired names from ritual and mythological texts: 'nt w'ttrt, ktr wḫss, šḥr wšlm, 'il w'atrt, 'arṣ wšmm, mt wšr, etc.
- Distribution is strongest in ritual texts (esp. CAT 1.100) — "one might interpret many of them largely as associated pairings for offering sacrificial provisions."
- Some binomials lack coordinating w- (Kothar Hasis), analogous to biblical compounds: Yahweh Elohim (Gen 2), El Elyon (Gen 14:18-20), Adonay Yahweh (Ezekiel).
- De Moor's fusion theory: Anat and Athtart as "two 'Anatu-goddesses who were regarded as aspects of one divine being." Smith: "Is there really enough evidence?"
- Del Olmo Lete's syncretism theory: double names reflect merging of Amorite and Canaanite pantheons.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Smith's resistance to de Moor's fusion theory is well-taken — we simply don't know the prehistory of these pairings. But his alternative ("theological reflection") is also speculative.

## Stakes

If binomials are theological reflection, Ugaritic scribes were doing something conceptually sophisticated — not just listing gods but exploring their interrelations. This supports Smith's larger claim that polytheism had conceptual coherence.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** De Moor (fusion from separate deities), del Olmo Lete (syncretism of two pantheons).

**Alternative reading:** Binomials could be simple scribal convention — a way to organize offering lists efficiently. The "theological reflection" might be modern over-reading.

## Assessment

Smith's resistance to de Moor's fusion theory is well-taken — we simply don't know the prehistory of these pairings. But his alternative ("theological reflection") is also speculative. The biblical parallel (Yahweh Elohim as redactional combination) is interesting but may not generalize to Ugarit.
