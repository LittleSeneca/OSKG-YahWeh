# sommer-bog-4.1: The fluidity model explains the paradox of sacred space

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 4 — Sacred Space 1]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 4

## The Claim

The fluidity model resolves the paradox that has troubled Jewish and Christian theology for millennia: how can God be simultaneously "in heaven" AND "in the Temple"? The traditional solution — "God is everywhere," "God transcends space" — is a philosophical answer, not a biblical one. The Bible's own answer is simpler: God has multiple bodies. One in heaven, one in the Temple. No paradox — just multiple simultaneous embodiments. The text means what it says.

## Evidence

**The problem:** Throughout the Hebrew Bible, God is described as dwelling in heaven AND dwelling in the Temple AND traveling with the Ark AND appearing at Sinai. Traditional theology resolves this by spiritualizing the Temple presence ("God's presence is everywhere") or by making the Temple presence symbolic.

**The fluidity solution:** In the ANE, gods routinely had multiple simultaneous bodies in different locations. Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh were distinct-yet-one. Baal of Saphon and Baal of Ugarit were distinct-yet-one. The biblical texts apply the same model to Yhwh: Yhwh of heaven, Yhwh of Jerusalem, Yhwh in the Ark — one God, multiple bodies.

**No paradox:** The texts never try to reconcile these statements philosophically because the fluidity model makes reconciliation unnecessary. The God who travels with the Ark IS the God who dwells in heaven. Different bodies, same God.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The fluidity model provides an elegant, textually grounded solution to a paradox that has troubled Jewish and Christian theology for millennia; the biblical texts mean what they say.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, the entire tradition of spiritualizing divine presence (God is "omnipresent," "transcends space") is a philosophical imposition on texts that never needed it. The biblical authors were not primitive philosophers struggling with spatial paradox — they were sophisticated theologians working within an ANE conceptual framework that made the paradox disappear.

## Disagreement

**Alternative reading:** The Temple presence is metaphorical or symbolic. God "dwells" in the Temple the way a king "dwells" in every province through his representatives. The language is political, not ontological.

## Assessment

This is the most elegant payoff of Sommer's model. The sacred space paradox has generated centuries of theological contortions. Sommer's solution is almost embarrassingly simple: the texts don't have a paradox because the ANE didn't have a problem with gods being in multiple places at once. The paradox only arises when you impose Greek philosophical categories (a being can only be in one place at a time) onto ANE texts.
