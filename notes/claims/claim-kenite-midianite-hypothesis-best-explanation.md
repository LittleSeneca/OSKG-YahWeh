# romer-inv-3.3: The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis is the best available explanation, but it remains unproven

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 3 — Moses and the Midianites]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapter 3

## The Claim

The tradition connecting Moses to Midian "is perhaps based on a historical recollection of the importance of the Midianites and of some kind of close contact between them and Moses." The Kenite/Midianite hypothesis — Yhwh was a Midianite god whom Moses encountered through his Midianite father-in-law and then brought to Israel — connects the Soleb evidence (Chapter 2) with the biblical narrative in a coherent way. But it can't be proven.

## Evidence

**The chain of reasoning:**
1. Soleb inscriptions (14th c. BCE) place Yhwʒ among Shasu nomads in Seir/Edom — the south (→ [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]])
2. Midianites were a Shasu subgroup in NW Arabia/southern Transjordan (→ [[claim-midianites-historical-people-northwest-arabia]])
3. Moses flees Egypt, marries Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, "priest of Midian" (Exodus 2-3)
4. Jethro already worships Yhwh and offers sacrifice to him (Exodus 18) — Yhwh was known among Midianites before Moses
5. The Kenites (a Midianite subgroup) appear as metalworkers associated with Israel (Judges 1:16, 4:11)
6. Therefore: Yhwh was a Midianite/Kenite god → Moses encountered him through his Midianite father-in-law → Moses brought Yhwh worship to the Israelites

**Romer's caution:**
- The Moses narrative is "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance"
- The Jethro story may be a literary device rather than a historical report
- The hypothesis is plausible, elegant, and underdetermined

**Romer's conclusion:**
- The hypothesis "provides the best explanation currently available of the relevant literary and archaeological data" (citing Blenkinsopp)
- But it can't be definitively proven or disproven

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The hypothesis elegantly connects the dots — Soleb + Shasu + Midian + Moses — but depends on taking the biblical narrative as preserving genuine historical memory. Given Romer's own acknowledgment that the Moses story is literary artifice, this is a significant tension.

## Stakes

This is the hinge of Romer's entire historical reconstruction. If the Kenite hypothesis is wrong, the mechanism by which Yhwh moved from the southern deserts to Israel is unknown. We have a starting point (Soleb) and an endpoint (Yhwh as Israel's god) with no bridge between them. The hypothesis is also the primary vehicle for the claim that Yhwh was a "foreign" god adopted by Israel rather than Israel's indigenous deity.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critics of the Kenite hypothesis (Tebes, 2021, for archaeological reasons). Scholars who see the Jethro narrative as a late literary construct with no historical value. Scholars who argue Yhwh was indigenous to Canaan and the Midianite connection is fictional.

**Alternative reading:** The Jethro/Midian connection is a LITERARY device — the biblical author uses Midian to explain how a foreign god (Yhwh) became Israel's god. The story preserves the memory of foreign origin while domesticating it ("Moses' father-in-law was a Midianite priest, therefore Yhwh is in the family"). It explains a problem, not a history.

## Assessment

This is where Romer's "traces of memory" methodology faces its hardest test. The Kenite hypothesis is attractive because it makes sense of scattered data. But attractive explanations aren't necessarily true. The Jethro narrative could be a late attempt to explain how a foreign god became Israel's god, rather than a memory of how it actually happened. Romer's honesty about the uncertainty is commendable. His willingness to proceed with the hypothesis anyway is questionable.
