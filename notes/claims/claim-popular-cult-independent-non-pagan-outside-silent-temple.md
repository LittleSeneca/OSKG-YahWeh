# kaufmann-ri-9.4: The popular cult was an independent, non-pagan sphere outside the temple

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"The priestly cult in the temple of silence could not contain the abundance of popular religious sentiment. Around the silent sanctuary throbbed the joyous popular cult, all tumult and passion." Because the folk remained outside the temple, their activity "was not linked with any specific symbol of sanctity" and "what might otherwise have savored of magic became innocuous." Prayer belonged "almost exclusively to the popular religion" — it is not mentioned in P. Prayer was spontaneous, unformulated, separate from sacrifice, and valid anywhere (even outside the land). Psalms were popular, not priestly; the Psalter is pre-exilic.

## Evidence

- The great festivals (Passover, Weeks, Tabernacles) got their distinctive character from popular celebration, not priestly rite
- Passover was a home sacrifice; mazzoth was unrelated to temple; first fruits were brought in festive procession
- Sabbath and new moon had social-moral rationales reflected in popular observance, not temple ritual
- The autumn new year was the popular festival; the spring new year was priestly
- Psalms mentioning YHWH's kingship and cosmic judgment (Ps 47, 81, 89, 95-100) are popular new year psalms
- Prayer: no fixed forms in early religion; the only formulated prayer in the Torah is the non-priestly tithe-confession
- Psalmody: incongruities between psalms and their narrative settings (Hannah's hymn, Jonah's psalm) prove psalms were fixed stereotypes already in pre-exilic times
- "There is no psalm whose plain sense requires a dating later than the exilic Psalm 137"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The popular/priestly distinction is analytically useful, but Kaufmann's dating of the entire Psalter as pre-exilic is tendentious and rejected by most scholars.

## Stakes

Kaufmann needs the popular cult to be non-pagan to maintain his thesis. By separating it physically from the temple (outside, not inside), he can argue that any magical or pagan elements were "innocuous" — not linked to the symbols of sanctity. This is a spatial argument doing theological work.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Form critics (Gunkel, Mowinckel) who see the Psalms as integral to temple liturgy, not separate from it. Scholars of Israelite festival calendars who find Canaanite agricultural festival patterns underlying the biblical festivals. The sharp popular/priestly distinction may be Kaufmann's construct, not ancient reality.

**Alternative reading:** The popular cult WAS closely connected to the temple — processions entered it, psalms were sung in it, prayer was offered toward it. The distinction between "silent priestly temple" and "tumultuous popular outside" is Kaufmann's idealization. In reality, temple and people were integrated, and the cult included elements Kaufmann wants to attribute to an innocuous popular sphere.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is onto something real — Israelite psalmody IS distinctive in its monotheistic and non-magical character, and prayer WAS relatively unformulated compared to pagan incantation. But his dating of the entire Psalter as pre-exilic is tendentious, and his spatial theology (outside = innocuous, inside = silent holiness) is a theoretical construct that lets him quarantine evidence of popular religious exuberance from the official cult. It's clever but not falsifiable.
