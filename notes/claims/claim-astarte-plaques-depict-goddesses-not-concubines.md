# keel-ggi-excursus.1: The "Astarte plaques" depict goddesses, not mortal women or "concubine" figures — the Tadmor thesis is wrong

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Excursus — Astarte Plaques]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Excursus §§57-60

## The Claim

"Three mutually complementary paths of argumentation... suggest that the nude woman on the 'Astarte plaques' is a goddess." The authors explicitly reject M. Tadmor's argument that the plaques are Egyptian-style "concubine" figures related to funerary practice. The rebuttal operates on three fronts: (a) typological — most plaques are long oval, not rectangular bed-shapes, and the same shape is used for qudshu-type plaques that Tadmor accepts as goddesses; (b) archaeological context — the vast majority come from living quarters, not graves; approximately 80% of LB graves contained no plaques; at Lachish only two were found in dozens of graves; (c) iconographic context — shrine models from Tell Qasile (11th c.) show standing naked female figures inside a shrine facade with pilasters and cornice; a cultic stand from Pella shows the same figures standing on lion heads.

## Evidence

- **Typological:** Only a few terra-cottas are rectangular (bed-shaped); most are long, extended oval — interpreting these as beds "is improbable." The qudshu-type plaques Tadmor ACCEPTS as goddesses share the same oval shape. The figures should be understood as standing, not reclining.
- **Archaeological context:** The plaques are NOT primarily funerary. The "vast majority" were found in LIVING QUARTERS, not graves. At Gezer: 26 LB plaques, at most 4 from graves. "Approximately 80% of the Late Bronze Age graves contained no plaques at all." At Lachish: "only two plaques were found" in dozens of LB graves. "Their main function, and the setting in which their use made sense, was in the family living quarters."
- **Iconographic context:** Shrine models from Tell Qasile (Iron I, 11th c.) show two standing naked female figures INSIDE a shrine with pilasters and cornice. The feet point forward, not sideways — same as the plaques. "To represent naked female figures either in front of or actually inside a sanctuary shows clearly that they are to be interpreted as (twin?) goddesses associated with this shrine." A cultic stand from Pella (Iron I/IIA transition) shows the same figures standing on lion heads.

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The three lines of evidence converge. The shrine-model evidence is particularly decisive — you don't put mortal women inside shrine facades flanked by pilasters.

## Stakes

If Tadmor were right, the "Astarte plaques" would be evidence for funerary practice, not goddess worship — removing a major piece of evidence from the goddess-religion argument. Keel & Uehlinger RESTORE the plaques as goddess evidence, but with crucial qualifications (claims 2-5) that limit what they can tell us about ISRAELITE goddess worship specifically.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Tadmor (1982), followed by H. Weippert (1988), Lipinski (1986), and Hadley (1989). They read the lack of divine attributes as indicating mortal status.

**Alternative reading:** Tadmor could be right about SOME of the rectangular plaques (which might be beds with reclining women/concubines) even if the oval plaques are goddesses. The corpus may be mixed.
