# smith-obm-intro.3: Monotheism and polytheism are modern categories that the ancients would not have recognized

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Introduction

## The Claim

"Monotheism and polytheism in themselves hold little meaning for the ancients apart from the identity of the deities whom they revered and served. No polytheist thought of his belief-system as polytheistic per se. If you asked ancient Mesopotamians if they were polytheists, the question would make no sense." Same for monotheism: "If you asked ancient Israelites around the Exilic period (587-538) if they were monotheists, they would not have understood the question."

## Evidence

- The terms are a product of modern Western encounter with non-Western cultures.
- Monotheism has become a "civil religion" for secularized Westerners — a "sublime idea" substituting for actual religious practice.
- Biblical scholars have traditionally elevated monotheism at polytheism's expense: monotheism as "historical" vs. polytheism as "nature/fertility religion"; monotheism as "ethical" vs. polytheism as "deification of power." Smith: "This is apologetics, not history."
- No evidence for "fate" as an independent order in West Semitic mythologies: "Ugaritic lacks a word even approximating this notion, and Akkadian *šimtu*... refers to a 'determined course' that can be changed."
- Recent treatments still make "polytheism... immoral and impotent, reflecting a 'pessimism'... 'a pantheon of disillusion.'"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is one of Smith's strongest points and is broadly accepted in critical scholarship.

## Stakes

If the modern categories distort the ancient data, then scholarly narratives about "the rise of monotheism" may be imposing a framework the ancients wouldn't recognize. The question becomes not "when did Israel become monotheistic?" but "what was the function of monotheistic rhetoric in its ancient context?"

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Albright and his school treated monotheism as an objective religious stage. Some conservative scholars still treat the terms as transparent. Heiser would say the Bible's "monotheism" always acknowledged other elohim — the categories don't map cleanly.

**Alternative reading:** While the ancients lacked OUR terms, they clearly distinguished between their god and others' gods. The absence of "-ism" vocabulary doesn't mean the conceptual distinction didn't exist.

## Assessment

Smith is right about the historical contingency of our terminology, and his critique of scholarly apologetics is bracing. But he may overstate the case: the distinction between "Yahweh alone exists" and "many gods exist" is a real cognitive distinction even if the vocabulary wasn't theoretical.
