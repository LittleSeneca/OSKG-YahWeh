# lewis-ocg-conc.2: Divinity as organizing principle was productive but partial

**Source:** [[Lewis — Conclusion]] — Theodore Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Conclusion

## The Claim

"Using divinity as an organizing principle runs a certain risk. Obviously, Israelite divinity and religious culture were more holistic and pluralistic than what is presented here." Lewis lists the divine attributes he couldn't cover in depth: Yahweh as creator, agrarian deity, transcendent god, eternal god, lord, storm god, "living God," solar deity, forgiving/merciful god, shepherd, rock, healer, strengthener of the weak, jealous god, source of wisdom, teacher, patient god, divine lover. "The list of divine features is unending."

## Evidence

- The list itself is the evidence: Lewis's seven chapters cover 5 major aspects of Yahweh (warrior, family god, king, judge, holy one) — leaving at least 15+ attributes underexplored
- The "distillate" metaphor is honest: seven tastings are better than one, but they're still only seven
- Lewis chose depth over breadth, but the depth is concentrated on a subset of divine attributes
- This self-assessment is a rare example of an author explicitly listing what his book does NOT accomplish

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** VERY HIGH. This self-assessment is accurate.

## Stakes

The book's status as a "reference work." It's an excellent reference for what it covers; it's not a reference for what it doesn't. Most academic books end by claiming to have solved their problem; Lewis ends by listing everything he couldn't solve.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one. Lewis is critiquing his own book here.

**Alternative reading:** The partiality is a feature, not a bug — selectivity is necessary for depth, and Lewis's seven tastings cover the most theologically significant divine attributes.

## Assessment

This conclusion is admirably honest. Most academic books end by claiming to have solved their problem. Lewis ends by listing everything he couldn't solve. The Astarte excursus is a particularly effective rhetorical move — "here's what doing justice to just ONE goddess would require" — and it makes the point more powerfully than any abstract acknowledgment could.
