# lewis-ocg-1.4: The Hebrew Bible preserves 'opposing voices' — Israelite religion was not monolithic

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 1 — Introductory Matters]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 1

## The Claim

Throughout Chapter 1, Lewis emphasizes plurality. He notes "ongoing negotiations over sacerdotal power, status, and cultic privilege," the variety of priestly groups with different claims to holiness, and how "each tradition (e.g., Priestly Source, Holiness Code, Ezekiel, Deuteronomy, Deuteronomistic History, and Chronicles) has its own nuances." In the chapter roadmap, he promises to show "opposing voices with regard to restricting and expanding holiness."

## Evidence

Lewis cites specific examples: P says *only* Aaronids are holy; H extends holiness to the entire community; prophets could be symbiotic with or adversarial to priests; royal religion coexisted with family religion; incantation specialists operated despite being "often deemed illicit."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This claim is beyond dispute in critical scholarship. The Bible is an anthology, not a unified treatise, and it preserves competing traditions. Even conservative scholars acknowledge surface tensions (they just resolve them differently).

## Stakes

Lewis is signaling that he won't harmonize contradictory evidence — he'll let the tensions stand. This separates him from confessional approaches and from older scholarship that treated "Israelite religion" as a unified system.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars would say the tensions are resolvable through careful exegesis and that Lewis overstates the diversity. Heiser would argue that apparent polytheism is actually a divine council of subordinate beings, not competing cults.

**Alternative reading:** The diversity is real but it operated within a shared framework — Israelite religion was diverse but not infinitely so. The "opposing voices" still shared basic assumptions about YHWH's supremacy.

## Assessment

Lewis's emphasis on plurality is standard critical procedure and aligns with Smith, Römer, Dever, and essentially the entire post-1970s scholarly consensus. What will matter is whether he fairly represents the internal logic of each tradition or reduces them all to power struggles.
