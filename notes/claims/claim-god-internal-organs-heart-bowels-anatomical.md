# stav-god-9-11.2: God had internal organs — heart, lungs/breath, bowels/womb, and belly — described in anatomically specific Hebrew terms, making his interior as embodied as his exterior.

**Source:** [[Stavrakopoulou — Part III — Torso]] — Francesca Stavrakopoulou, *God: An Anatomy* (2021), Chapter 10

## The Claim

Stavrakopoulou argues that the biblical God is not only armoured in an outer layer of skin and glory, but constructed of inner organs too. "His anatomy is interior as well as exterior." The Hebrew Bible describes God's heart, breath/wind, bowels/womb, and belly using anatomically specific vocabulary that cannot be dismissed as mere metaphor without losing the corporeal register of the text.

## Evidence

- **Heart (*lēb*):** God's heart grieves (Genesis 6:6), is moved to compassion (Hosea 11:8), plans and intends (Jeremiah 23:20)
- **Breath/Wind (*rûaḥ*):** God breathes — into Adam (Genesis 2:7), into the dry bones (Ezekiel 37), as storm theophany
- **Bowels/Womb (*raḥămîm*):** God's "compassion" derives from the word for womb/innards — his mercy is visceral, located in reproductive/abdominal anatomy
- **Belly (*me'eh*):** The seat of deep emotion — God's innards "roar" and "yearn" (Jeremiah 4:19, Isaiah 16:11)
- The cumulative catalogue maps a complete internal anatomy: cardiac, respiratory, digestive, reproductive

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Hebrew terms are anatomically specific; the challenge is whether they are literal or metaphorical — but the cumulative evidence across multiple terms supports embodied reading.

## Stakes

If God's interior anatomy is described in the same register as his exterior (hands, feet, face), then the distinction between "literal" body parts and "metaphorical" inner organs is an artificial one imposed by later theology. The biblical writers did not distinguish between God's external and internal anatomy — both were real.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Kaufmann and allegorical interpreters who treat emotional language (heart, bowels) as metaphor even while acknowledging hands and feet as bodily

**Alternative reading:** The internal organ vocabulary is entirely metaphorical — "heart" means mind/intention, "bowels" means compassion, none of it implies physical organs.

## Assessment

The internal-organ catalogue is Stavrakopoulou's boldest philological move: she treats the Hebrew vocabulary for God's interior with the same literalism she applied to his feet and genitals. The case is strongest for *lēb* (heart) and *rûaḥ* (breath) — these ARE physical organs/processes even when used for mental states. The case weakens slightly for *raḥămîm* (womb-compassion), where the anatomical origin may be etymological rather than referential. But the cumulative weight is significant: God's insides are described in the same embodied vocabulary as his outsides.
