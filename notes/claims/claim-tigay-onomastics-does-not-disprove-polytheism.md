# smith-ehg-intro.4: Tigay's onomastic argument does not disprove widespread polytheism.

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 0 — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2nd ed., 2002), Introduction

## The Claim

Tigay's finding that 94%+ of Hebrew personal names use YHWH as the theophoric element does NOT prove Israel was monotheistic or even monolatrous. It only proves Yahweh was the most popular national deity. Personal names are "notoriously difficult to assess for historical purposes" and reflect naming conventions, not personal theology.

## Evidence

- **Ugaritic counter-example:** Few Ugaritic names contain Asherah (*'atrt*) as a theophoric element, yet ritual texts prove she was actively venerated. Absence in names ≠ absence in worship.
- **Punic counter-example:** Tannit was the most popular Punic goddess, yet Punic names "likewise rarely contain *tnt* as a theophoric element."
- **General principle:** Naming conventions are governed by factors other than religious devotion. A child named "Yohanan" (YHWH is gracious) could grow up in a household that also venerated Asherah.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Smith's counter-examples are real and damaging to a naive onomastics = theology equation. But they cut against Smith too: if Ugaritic names don't reflect worship, then ISRAELITE names also might not reflect worship — including the predominance of YHWH names. Smith uses onomastic silence to argue AGAINST Asherah worship (no one named their kid after her) while dismissing onomastic predominance as evidence FOR Yahwism. That's inconsistent.

## Stakes

This is the strongest empirical challenge to the polytheism consensus. If Tigay is right that 94%+ names are Yahwistic in the 8th-6th centuries, then popular religion was far more Yahwistic than Smith's model predicts. Smith NEEDS to neutralize this evidence for his case to work.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Tigay, obviously. His book *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986) is the definitive statement. Smith's counter hasn't persuaded most onomastics specialists.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's data + the archaeological evidence together suggest a society where Yahweh dominated PUBLIC identity (names) but private practice was more diverse (figurines, household shrines). This is actually Smith's own distinction between "official" and "popular" religion — but applied to onomastics, it weakens his case.

## Assessment

This is the weakest part of Smith's Introduction. His counter-examples are real but they're a defensive move, not a positive case. He's saying "onomastics can't DISPROVE polytheism" — which is true. But he's not saying "here's positive evidence FOR polytheism from names." The onomastic data remains a genuine problem for the consensus that Smith hasn't fully solved. Flag for further investigation.
