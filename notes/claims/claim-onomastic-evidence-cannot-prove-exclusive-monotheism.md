# tigay-nog-1.4: Onomastic evidence cannot prove exclusive monotheism

**Source:** [[Tigay — Chapter I — The Onomastic Evidence]] — Jeffrey H. Tigay, *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986), Chapter I

## The Claim

"Even if the names of a particular society should reflect the predominance of a single deity to the total exclusion of all others, this would tell us only that members of that society did not expect from other gods the kinds of actions that are mentioned in personal names, and perhaps that they did not worship other gods." But — crucially — "the absence of other gods from the onomasticon would not by itself tell us whether that society denied the existence or divinity of those gods."

This is Tigay's own methodological caveat. His data shows overwhelming YHWH-devotion in naming practices. He does NOT claim this proves monotheism (denial of other gods' existence). It proves monolatry (exclusive worship of one god). The ontological question — did other gods exist? — is not answered by onomastic data.

## Evidence

- Tigay explicitly distinguishes between what names show (devotion, worship patterns) and what they don't show (belief about other gods' existence)
- Names express "simple and elemental" beliefs — thanks, hope, submission
- The absence of nature gods from the onomasticon IS significant (Israelites didn't name children after sun/rain/fertility gods, unlike Ammonites), but this shows worship patterns, not ontology
- Tigay's conclusion is "polytheism was limited" (a worship claim), not "monotheism was universal" (a belief claim)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Tigay himself explicitly states this caveat; it is not in dispute.

## Stakes

This caveat is what separates Tigay from Kaufmann. Kaufmann claimed Israel was monotheistic from the beginning as a matter of belief. Tigay is more careful: his data shows YHWH-devotion was overwhelming, but he stops short of claiming this proves exclusive monotheism. The gap between naming practice and ontological belief is where the debate between Tigay and the consensus actually lives.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one disagrees with the caveat itself — it's a methodological observation. The disagreement is about whether the caveat is FATAL to Tigay's conclusions.

**Alternative reading:** Smith, Day, and Dever would argue that the caveat IS fatal: if onomastics can't prove monotheism, then Tigay's data is compatible with widespread polytheism where YHWH was simply the chief (and most name-worthy) deity.

## Assessment

Tigay's methodological honesty is admirable. He doesn't overclaim. The caveat is why his book's title is "You Shall Have No Other Gods" (a monolatry command) rather than "There Are No Other Gods" (a monotheism declaration). The gap between naming and believing is where the consensus critique lands hardest.
