# keel-ggi-5.5: The disappearance of the anthropomorphic goddess from glyptic art represents a genuine theological transformation — fertility becomes a depersonalized "numinous power" rather than a personal divine force

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

"As far as the glyptic art is concerned, the female aspect of fertility was no longer represented in anthropomorphic form, but only by means of symbols, substitute entities, and other entities from nature such as the suckling mother animal, the tree, or the scorpion." And the key interpretive move: "As the goddess disappeared, being represented only by the use of her attribute symbols, the personality of this power faded into the background as well. Blessing and fertility were no longer directly connected with a personal power who was acting consciously but was thought of increasingly as a numinous power and force."

The payoff: "The disappearance of the depiction of the goddess in anthropomorphic form is an important precondition for what will have to be demonstrated in the succeeding chapters. During the Iron Age II, the icons that depict blessing will be assigned to a male deity (Yahweh), will be virtually taken over by him, and will finally be used as a way to express his blessings." The goddess had to be depersonalized FIRST before Yahweh could absorb her functions.

## Evidence

- The "Branch Goddess" and Qudshu type disappear completely from glyptic art
- The suckling mother animal appears on conoids (new medium, new material — quartz, black calcite, limestone) from Megiddo Str. VI, Tell el-Far'ah (south) Grave 134, Beth-Shemesh Str. III, Tel Eitun Grave C1
- The suckling animal is accompanied by a scorpion — "the scorpion appeared frequently already on Middle Bronze Age Syrian cylinder seal glyptic art with the goddess of love"
- The stylized tree flanked by caprids, which "during the Late Bronze Age [was interpreted] as showing the goddess and her activity," continues on Iron Age I conoids — but WITHOUT the anthropomorphic goddess figure
- "The crude, light-colored limestone seals that are typical of Iron Age I use, as an icon that is supposed to bring blessing, only the stylized tree"
- The archer + suckling mother animal combination may link to the Ben-Anat warrior class — but even here, the goddess is represented by an animal, not anthropomorphically

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The iconographic pattern (attributes without goddess) is empirically solid. The inference that this represents a conceptual shift from personal to impersonal fertility is more interpretive but well-argued.

## Stakes

This is the theoretical bridge between Chapters V and VI. If Keel and Uehlinger are right, then Yahweh's absorption of fertility functions in Iron Age II was possible PRECISELY BECAUSE those functions had already been detached from the goddess's personhood. Yahweh did not have to defeat a goddess to take her powers — he simply stepped into a role that had been vacated and depersonalized. This is a very different model from the "Yahweh-alone movement suppressed Asherah" narrative. It suggests theological evolution rather than theological revolution.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever argues that Asherah worship persisted robustly through Iron I and II, evidenced by figurines and cult stands. Smith likely argues for polytheistic continuity. If the goddess was being actively worshipped through figurines, the "depersonalization" Keel and Uehlinger see in glyptic art may reflect elite/scribal theology only, not popular religion.

**Alternative reading:** The survival of goddess ATTRIBUTES without the goddess herself could be read differently — not as depersonalization, but as the goddess becoming so closely identified with her symbols that the symbols BECOME her. The tree IS the goddess, not a replacement for her. This interpretation would be more congruent with Dever's Asherah-as-sacred-tree argument.

## Assessment

Keel and Uehlinger's reading is elegant but vulnerable. The glyptic evidence supports it, but glyptic evidence is only one stratum of religious expression. The terracotta figurines (which they acknowledge but downgrade) may tell a different story. The truth may be that BOTH processes occurred simultaneously: elite glyptic art depersonalized the goddess while popular terracotta art kept her anthropomorphic. This would parallel the later Iron II pattern where seals show no consort for Yahweh but thousands of JPFs suggest popular goddess devotion.
