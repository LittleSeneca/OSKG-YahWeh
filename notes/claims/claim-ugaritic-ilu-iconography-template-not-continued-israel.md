# lewis-ocg-5.2: Ugaritic ʾIlu Iconography — Clear Template, NOT Continued in Israel

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 5 — Iconography of Divinity — El]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 5

## The Claim

Four Ugaritic artifacts (Figs. 5.16, 5.18, 5.19, 5.20) "seem to represent the deity ʾIlu (a consensus among scholars) because their physical representations coincide with the description of ʾIlu we find in Ugaritic literature (bearded, aged, bull [= horned], benevolent, enthroned with footstool, etc.)." The bronze bull from the South City (Fig. 5.32), found alongside the ʾIlu and Baal figurines, is the best candidate for a theriomorphic ʾIlu image.

## Evidence

- Typological distinction: striding smiting figures = warrior/storm gods (Baal); enthroned benedictory figures = El
- ʾIlu's textual epithets ("Bull ʾIlu," "father of years," "Gracious One") align with the iconography (bearded, aged, horned, benevolent, enthroned)
- One figurine (5.16) was found in a residential area — not a temple — underscoring family religion
- The bull image (5.32) has "strongly emphasized sexual organs" matching ʾIlu's ribald texts (KTU 1.23, 1.4.4.38-39)
- Cornelius: "the title 'bull' is the prerogative of ʾIlu"
- This template (enthroned, bearded, horned, benedictory, bull) was known in the LB Levant but NOT replicated in Iron Age Israel

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High for the Ugaritic identification; high that this template wasn't continued in Israel.

## Stakes

If Israel knew about ʾIlu/El iconography from Ugaritic/Canaanite predecessors but chose NOT to replicate it, the aniconic tradition is a deliberate rejection, not ignorance. This supports Uehlinger's point: the prohibition exists because the practice existed.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Ornan identifies the large Hazor bronze as Baal, not El, based on the "tree and horned animals" crown motif. Lewis argues El is equally associated with agricultural bounty (KTU 1.23).

**Alternative reading:** The absence of El images in Israel could reflect the merger with Yahweh — images of El may have been repurposed or reinterpreted as Yahweh images, or the prohibition may have applied retroactively.

## Assessment
