# fleming-ybi-1.3: "Little Israel" was geographically limited, excluded Judah, and expanded terminologically over time

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 1 — Introduction]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"We begin by separating 'greater Israel' from what we call 'little Israel,' neither one to consider a fixed entity... Little Israel certainly excludes Judah, but its specific location and extent remain elusive" (p. 22). "Earliest Israel is first visible in the Bible linked to distinct kingdoms identified with Saul and David in the southern central highlands and with the town of Tirzah further north" (p. 22).

## Evidence

- Work developed with Lauren Monroe (Monroe forthcoming; Fleming forthcoming)
- Finkelstein (2019) likewise considers Israel to have grown in geographical extent
- The Song of Deborah lists participants without interest in Jerusalem or Judah (p. 26)
- This is part of a broader archaeological conversation (Near Eastern Archaeology 82/1, 2019)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The general point that "Israel" was not a fixed entity and expanded over time is well-supported archaeologically. The specific reconstruction of Saul-David + Tirzah as earliest Israel is more speculative. Monroe's work is still in progress.

## Stakes

If "Israel" is a moving target, then asking "where did Israel get Yahweh?" is the wrong question. Yahweh could have been associated with groups that eventually became Israel without ever being "borrowed" by "Israel" as a unified entity. This dissolves the foreign/indigenous binary.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional biblical scholarship that treats "Israel" as a unified entity from the conquest/settlement period. Also, the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE) already uses "Israel" as a people-name — though Fleming would note the determinative shows a people-group, not a territorial state.

**Alternative reading:** The Merneptah Stele already attests "Israel" as a recognizable entity in the late 13th century, so the terminological expansion argument may be overstated.

## Assessment

The "little Israel" concept is useful heuristically but risks becoming a black box. Fleming acknowledges the location is "elusive." The key point — that "Israel" wasn't always what it became — is sound. The specific geography is less important to his argument than the principle that terminological expansion happened.
