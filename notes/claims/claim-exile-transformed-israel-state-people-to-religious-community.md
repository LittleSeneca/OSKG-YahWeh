# albertz-hir-4.3: The exilic period transformed Israel from a state people to a "community with a religious constitution"

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 4 — Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"On the one hand, as a result, membership of one's own group was no longer something to be taken for granted, but had to be proved time and again by the individual's decision. Here religious confession assumed greater significance as a guarantee of personal identity. Thus the Israel of the exilic period for the first time took on features of a community with a religious constitution."

## Evidence

- Loss of central political authority → revival of decentralized kinship organization (family/clan, elders)
- Constant contact with foreign nations → identity became elective rather than automatic
- Religious confession (circumcision, sabbath, dietary laws) became markers of group membership
- "The constant fluctuation between universalist and abruptly particularistic theological approaches" reflects the new social reality

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is one of Albertz's most important insights. The shift from ethnic-national to religious-confessional identity is the defining transformation of the exilic period and the birth of Judaism as we know it.

## Stakes

This is perhaps the single most important claim about the exile: it explains how Israelite religion survived the loss of statehood. If identity hadn't shifted from political to religious grounds, Yahwism would have gone the way of Moabite or Ammonite religion — dissolved into the imperial melting pot.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars (e.g., Shaye Cohen, The Beginnings of Jewishness) locate the shift later, in the Hellenistic period. Others see continuity rather than transformation.

**Alternative reading:** Israel was already a religious community before the exile — the exile simply intensified existing tendencies. Or: the "religious constitution" was imposed top-down by Persian imperial policy (the Torah as Persian law), not organically developed.
