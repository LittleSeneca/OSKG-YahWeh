# fleming-ybi-1.5: Southern geography of old poetry does not establish Yahweh's origin there

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 1 — Introduction]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"In my analysis, Yahweh's residence in distant southern realms and his worship as Yahweh of Teman attribute to the god a power in the southern wilderness without attaching his worship to a population there. The only indication of a population would come with the earlier Yhwʒ group in the company of Seir in the 13th century, and we will see in Chapter 2 that this geography is far from secure" (p. 36).

## Evidence

1. **Theophanic movement, not origin:** The old poetry (Judg 5:4-5, Deut 33:2, Hab 3:3, Ps 68:8-9) describes Yahweh's theophanic movement FROM the south, not worship BY southern peoples (p. 31). This is a divine journey motif, not an origin tradition
2. **Ugaritic analogy:** Baal's home on Mt Sapan does not make him "from" Sapan in the sense of historical origin — divine mountains in Ugaritic mythology are residences, not birthplaces. Yahweh is no more "from" Seir than Baal is "from" Sapan
3. **Kuntillet Ajrud as geographical epithet, not origin evidence:** The inscriptions (c. 800 BCE) refer to "Yahweh of Teman" — a geographical epithet, not evidence of origin. Teman was a known southern region; designating Yahweh "of Teman" is like "Our Lady of Lourdes" — a manifestation, not a birthplace
4. **The Egyptian Seir link is late and contested:** The Egyptian list linking Yhwʒ to Seir is from the later Ramses II text; the earlier Amenhotep III list from Soleb lacks the Seir connection (p. 37). The one geographical anchor may be a secondary addition

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Fleming is right that "coming from the south" and "originating in the south" are different claims. The Ugaritic analogy is genuinely illuminating. But the convergence of Egyptian, poetic, and inscriptional evidence ALL pointing south is hard to dismiss as coincidence. The question is whether this convergence indicates origin or a shared trope of divine theophany from the wilderness. Ch 2 and Ch 4 must carry this weight.

## Stakes

If the southern geography is a theophanic motif rather than an origin memory, the Midianite Hypothesis loses its strongest non-biblical line of evidence. The Egyptian texts become evidence for Shasu identity rather than southern geography.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Essentially all proponents of the Midianite Hypothesis, who read the southern geography as preserving genuine memory of Yahweh's origin point. Römer (2015: Ch 2-3) gives the southern geography substantial weight. Cross (1973: 60-75) built his entire "Yahweh as epithet of El" argument around the southern league concept.

**Alternative reading:** The convergence of independent traditions (Egyptian, poetic, epigraphic) all pointing to the same southern region is unlikely to be coincidental. The most parsimonious explanation is that Yahweh genuinely originated among southern peoples and the poetry preserves this memory.

## Assessment

Fleming is setting up a crucial distinction that many scholars blur: "comes from the south" ≠ "was first worshipped in the south." A theophany tradition could develop for theological reasons (the wilderness as locus of divine power) rather than historical ones. The Ugaritic analogy — Baal is no more "from" Sapan than Yahweh is "from" Seir — is the chapter's most illuminating single comparison. But the convergence problem is real: if it's all a coincidence, that's a lot of coincidence. Ch 2 and Ch 4 must carry this weight.
