# lewis-ocg-2.5: The Fribourg school of iconographic study transformed the field

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 2 — History of Scholarship]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 2

## The Claim

Othmar Keel's 1972 work "offered a massively detailed study of how visual symbols could supplement the written text" and "systematic studies were rare in a discipline that preferred philology and theology to the worlds of archaeology and art history." The Fribourg school has turned out "numerous volumes" and "exhaustive studies of previously overlooked seals and amulets."

## Evidence

- Keel and Uehlinger's *Gods, Goddesses and Images of God* (1998) as landmark
- Schroer's four-volume *Die Ikonographie Palästinas/Israels* (2005–2018)
- Cornelius's 2004 comprehensive iconographic study of the goddess
- Female divine iconography became especially relevant after the Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom discoveries mentioning Asherah
- "Extensive new databases such as Schroer's four-volume *Die Ikonographie Palästinas/Israels und der Alte Orient* provide researchers with unparalleled resources"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The integration of iconography is one of the major methodological advances of the last 50 years. Lewis's own Chapters 5 and 7 are built on Fribourg-school foundations.

## Stakes

If iconography is treated as supplementary rather than primary, a huge swath of evidence is missed — especially for popular religion (seals, amulets, figurines) that the textual record largely ignores.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Text-centric philologists who see iconography as inherently ambiguous and subjective. The Fribourg school has been criticized for over-interpreting images — Keel himself acknowledged the risk: "We constantly run the risk of reading these pictures too concretely, or having avoided that risk, of treating them too abstractly."

**Alternative reading:** Iconography is essential but must be used in conjunction with textual evidence, not in isolation. The Fribourg school's strongest contribution is showing that text and image are mutually illuminating.

## Assessment

Lewis's embrace of iconography is one of the book's strengths and a direct inheritance from his teacher Cross (who applied typological method to artifacts) and his Johns Hopkins environment (with its Near Eastern archaeology focus). Chapters 5 and 7 will be the test of whether he uses iconography well.
