# day-ygc-5.1: The vocalization "Ashtoreth" is a deliberate scribal distortion using the vowels of bošet ("shame")

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 5 — Yahweh and Astarte and Anat]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 5

## The Claim

It is generally accepted that the vocalization of the name of the goddess *ʻaštōret* in the Old Testament is a deliberate scribal distortion of an original *ʻaštart*. The distorted vowels reflect *bošet* "shame," the same polemical device used for Baal in personal names. Day argues: "It is virtually certain that the distorted vocalization reflects the vowels of the Hebrew word *bošet*."

## Evidence

1. Extra-biblical forms uniformly presuppose Astarte (Greek *Astartē*, Akkadian *Ištar*) — not Ashtoreth
2. The same *bošet* distortion pattern operates on Baal names (Ish-bosheth for Eshbaal, etc.) — "It is virtually certain that the distorted vocalization reflects the vowels of the Hebrew word *bošet*" (p. 129)
3. Molech's vowels are probably similarly distorted (should be *mōlek* or *melek*)
4. **Against Cooper's objection** (that *a→o* is natural vowel shift, like *dāgān→dāgôn*):
   - We should expect *ʻaštōrt* not *ʻaštōret* — extra-biblical forms lack the vowel between r and t
   - Greek sources (all late) consistently render *Astartē*, not a form with o — surprising if the change had already occurred
   - The Syrian goddess Atargatis (conflation of Astarte + Anat) preserves the *a* vowel (p. 130)

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is settled scholarship — the *bošet* pattern, already demonstrated in Chapter 3 for Baal names, extends to Astarte, and Greek transcriptions show no trace of the o-vocalization.

## Stakes

Every time you read "Ashtoreth" in the OT, you're reading a theological insult. The scribes weren't just recording a goddess name — they were deforming it. This is the same scribal strategy applied to Baal-zebub and Molech.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** A. Cooper (1990) argues the vocalization is authentic vowel shift, not polemical.

**Alternative reading:** The *a→o* shift is natural phonetic change (like *dāgān→dāgôn*), requiring no polemical intent.

## Assessment

Day's rebuttal of Cooper is effective. The absence of any *o* vocalization in Greek transcriptions is decisive — if the name naturally shifted to Ashtoreth in Hebrew, Greek sources (which postdate the Hebrew Bible) should show some trace of it. They don't.
