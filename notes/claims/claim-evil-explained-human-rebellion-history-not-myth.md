# kaufmann-ri-9.1: Evil explained through human rebellion, replacing myth with history

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

Paganism, with its plurality of divine principles, had no problem of evil. Israelite religion, positing One God, HAD to explain the origin of evil. Genesis 1-11 is the answer: "In place of myth Israelite religion conceived the historical drama of human rebellion and sin." The legends explain all evils — natural (death, pain), moral (murder, violence), and religious (idolatry) — as consequences of human freedom misused. No primordial evil principle, no theomachy, no metadivine realm. "The Bible does not regard the idolatry of the nations as a sin for which they must be punished... idolatry is the fruit of sin, a punishment, rather than a primary sin itself."

## Evidence

- Genesis 1 (P) pronounces each act of creation good; man created in God's image
- JE's Eden story: evil is veiled from man but accessible; the serpent seduces but does not compel; the tree is not the source of knowledge generally but of the knowledge of and desire for evil
- Sexual desire appears as the archetypal sin; procreation is not a blessing but the outcome of sin
- Cain brings murder; the Flood generation brings universal depravity; Babel brings the confusion of tongues and the origin of nations
- No story dedicated to the origin of idolatry — it appears after Babel as the consequence of God hiding himself from man
- Paganism is depicted as presumptuous worship of no-gods, rooted in man's boundless arrogance
- The absence of theomachy (YHWH vs. Rahab/dragon) in Genesis 1-11 is deliberate: biblical legend "did not root sin and evil in Rahab or the dragon — conceived of as a Satanic figure"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The descriptive observation that Genesis 1-11 represents evil as consequence of human rebellion is genuine; but Kaufmann overstates uniqueness — Mesopotamian literature also grapples with the origin of suffering.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite monotheism was truly a new metaphysical insight or a variation on Near Eastern theodicy themes. If Genesis 1-11 can be shown to share structural features with Mesopotamian primeval traditions, Kaufmann's claim of radical originality weakens.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who trace Genesis 1-11 to Mesopotamian antecedents (Atrahasis → Flood; Enuma Elish → creation; Sumerian paradise myths → Eden). The uniqueness is real but may be more a matter of theological inflection than absolute novelty.

**Alternative reading:** Genesis 1-11 IS distinctive in its monotheistic framing and its insistence that evil originates in human will, not divine conflict. But it's not de novo — it's a monotheistic recasting of shared Near Eastern narrative material. Kaufmann's "absolutely different" rhetoric overstates what is better described as a profound transformation.

## Assessment

One of Kaufmann's strongest sections. His reading of Genesis 1-11 as theodicy — not primitive myth but sophisticated answer to the problem evil poses for monotheism — is genuinely illuminating. The observation that no story explains the ORIGIN of idolatry (it's a consequence, not a primordial reality) is sharp. But he ignores the Mesopotamian parallels that would contextualize his claim of uniqueness. He reads the biblical text as if it emerged in a vacuum.
