# smith-obm-intro.5: The etymology of 'ēl is unresolved — "strength" etymology is unproven but not impossible

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Introduction

## The Claim

"We should exercise great caution before accepting and using this etymology" (the derivation from *'y/wl, "to be strong"). But Smith doesn't dismiss it outright.

## Evidence

- Common view: 'ēl from *'y/wl, "to be pre-eminent, strong." Related to 'ayil ("leader/chief/ram") and 'eyāl ("strength") in Psalms 22:20, 88:5.
- But the idiom *yeš/'ên lě'ēl* ("it is/is not in the power of X") has been reanalyzed by Watson: he reads *'yn l' lyd-* ("there is no power to the hand of"), removing 'ēl entirely. Smith doubts this: "l'y is hardly a productive root with this meaning in BH."
- Ugaritic *'ul* and *'awl* may mean "army" not "strength."
- M.H. Pope: "None of [the etymological proposals] carries conviction."
- D. Pardee: counterindications to the *'y/wl theory.
- The root was not productive in Ugaritic or Hebrew, so "strength" was probably not a live connotation.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that the etymology is unsettled. Smith's agnosticism is well-supported.

## Stakes

Low stakes for the book's main argument, but important for anyone claiming to know what "El" originally "meant." If El = "the Strong One," that shapes how we understand his character.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who confidently derive 'ēl from "strength" (common in older reference works). Cross uses the etymology to connect El with power imagery.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the etymology is uncertain, the FUNCTION of El in the texts (patriarch, creator, wise judge) may be more informative than any putative original meaning.

## Edges

**Depends on:**
- [[claim-ugaritic-texts-best-available-background]] — The etymology debate lives or dies on Ugaritic evidence (*'ul*, *'awl* cognates), and Ugaritic texts are our best available background for West Semitic philology.

**Supports:**
- [[claim-monotheism-polytheism-modern-categories-ancients-different]] — Both are methodological caution claims: don't impose modern certainty on ancient data.
- [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]] — Smith's broader rhetorical framing of monotheism doesn't depend on etymology, and this etymological caution reinforces that independence.
- [[claim-israel-broadly-west-semitic-religion-not-pristine-monotheism]] — If the etymology of the head god's name is unsettled, strong claims about "what El originally meant" lose force, which supports framing Israel as broadly West Semitic rather than exceptional.

**Contradicts:**
- [[claim-el-shaddai-means-el-mountain-one]] — Albright/Cross confidently propose etymology for *šadday* (as "the mountain one") in a way that assumes the general "strength" derivation of *'ēl* is settled. Smith's caution undercuts etymological confidence more broadly.

**Challenged by:**
- Frank Moore Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) — Cross's entire framework assumes 'ēl = "the Strong One" and builds theological arguments on this etymology. Cross is not yet extracted, but his claims will challenge this methodological caution directly.

**Primary sources:**
- Psalms 22:20, 88:5 (possible Hebrew cognates of *'y/wl*)
- Ugaritic *'ul*, *'awl* (possible cognates — or unrelated terms for "army")

## Assessment

Smith's caution is admirable. Etymology is a weak reed for this kind of work, and he's right to note it and move on. Nothing in his argument depends on the etymology.
