# heiser-ur-7.1: The serpent in Eden was a divine being, not merely a talking snake

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 2 — The Households of God]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 7

## The Claim

The *nāḥāš* (serpent) in Genesis 3 is not a literal talking snake but a divine being — a member of the heavenly council who appears in serpentine form. The Hebrew word *nāḥāš* has semantic overlap with terms for divination and supernatural knowledge, and the serpent's role (tempting to divine knowledge, "you will be like *elohim*") fits a divine-council rebel rather than an animal.

## Evidence

The chapter note provides only a summary — Heiser's full argument draws on:
- The semantic range of *nāḥāš* (serpent, divination, omen)
- The serpent's knowledge of divine matters (the tree, the consequences, the promise of *elohim* status)
- ANE parallels of serpentine divine beings (seraphim, cherubim throne guardians)
- The serpent's punishment (crawling on belly) as demotion from a prior divine status

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The note provides only a summary of Heiser's argument, not the full philological and comparative evidence. The claim is plausible within Heiser's divine council framework but the detailed exegetical case is not captured in this note.

## Stakes

If the serpent is a divine being, the Eden narrative is not about a talking animal deceiving gullible humans — it is about a cosmic rebellion in the divine council spilling into the human realm. This connects Genesis 3 to the divine council framework and to the Genesis 6 transgression. The Fall becomes a divine-council event, not merely a human moral failure.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional interpreters who read the serpent as a literal snake (perhaps possessed by Satan). Critical scholars who read Genesis 3 as an etiological myth (explaining why snakes crawl) with no divine-council framework.

**Alternative reading:** The serpent is a literal snake, perhaps the most cunning of the animals. The talking snake is a folktale element. Satan is read back into the text by later tradition (Revelation 12:9), but the original author had no concept of a fallen angel in the garden.

## Assessment

This is Heiser at his most speculative. The serpent-as-divine-being reading is not standard in either evangelical or critical scholarship. It fits Heiser's system elegantly — the serpent is a divine council rebel before the Genesis 6 watchers — but the evidence for reading *nāḥāš* as a divine being rather than an animal is thin. The strongest argument is contextual: the serpent speaks with knowledge of divine matters and promises *elohim* status, which an animal shouldn't have. Whether that requires a divine-council reading or simply reflects the folkloric character of the narrative is debated.
