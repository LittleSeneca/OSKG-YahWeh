# keel-ggi-5.8: The two dominant themes of Iron Age I iconography — aggressive domination and agricultural/herd fertility — correspond to the dual concerns of the Judges era in the Hebrew Bible

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

"The iconographic symbol system used during Iron Age I highlights two factors that each take their own unique form. One factor is aggressiveness, oriented toward superiority and domination; it appears in the Hebrew Bible, roughly speaking, in the exodus narratives, in the book of Judges, and in the narratives describing the battles against the Philistines in 1 Samuel." "The second factor is evident in a desire for human, animal, and agricultural fertility, as can be detected most clearly in the imagery of the suckling mother animal. This is shown most obviously in the Hebrew Bible in the patriarchal histories in Genesis (cf., e.g., the significance of fertility among the flocks in the Jacob cycle, Genesis 25-35)."

Crucially, "these two emphases are not mutually exclusive. This is shown, for example, in the Gideon traditions in the book of Judges (Judges 6-8), as well as in the evidence discussed in §78 for the existence of the Ben-Anat warrior class." Gideon is both warrior AND owner of vast flocks — the two themes coexist in a single figure. The significance of the Baal-(Seth) tradition for the hill country settlers "appears also in the names of the numerous newly established sites in the region that made up their names by using the pattern Baal-X."

## Evidence

- Aggression/domination iconography: Seth-Baal on lion, Reshef on gazelle, pharaoh striking enemies, lion attacking caprids, archer dominating animals and humans, bull's military prowess (NOT fertility) emphasized
- Fertility iconography: suckling mother animal (all variations), stylized tree, scorpion, caprids at tree — but notably WITHOUT anthropomorphic goddess
- Gideon cycle (Judges 6-8): warrior who also has concerns about flocks and harvest
- Baal-X toponyms in hill country: Isserlin 1957, Rosen 1988
- Jacob cycle (Genesis 25-35): fertility of flocks as central concern

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The correlation between the two iconographic themes and the two dominant concerns of the Judges narratives is persuasive and well-supported.

## Stakes

This is the capstone synthesis of the chapter. It shows that the iconographic evidence is not just an independent dataset but one that correlates meaningfully with the biblical picture of the period — WITHOUT depending on the Bible's theological framework. The Baal toponym evidence is particularly important: if early Iron Age hill country settlers named their villages after Baal, then the Baal-Yahweh conflict of later centuries (Elijah, Hosea) may reflect a real religious history in which Yahweh gradually displaced Baal as the primary deity of the hill country.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Biblical maximalists might argue that the Judges narratives are late and reflect Iron II concerns projected backward. But Keel and Uehlinger are not using the Bible to DATE the iconography — they are using the iconography to independently establish what Iron Age I concerns WERE, and then noting the convergence with biblical themes. The correlation does not depend on the historicity of any specific Judges story.

**Alternative reading:** The two-theme synthesis may be too neat. Other concerns (lineage, land tenure, tribal identity) may have been equally important but did not leave iconographic traces.

## Assessment

This is a model of how iconography and biblical studies can converge without circular reasoning. The authors do not claim the seals PROVE the Bible — they claim the seals show what Iron Age I people cared about, and those concerns happen to align with what the Judges narratives care about. That is a legitimate correlation. The Baal toponym evidence is the strongest specific link: it independently confirms that Baal was the dominant deity in the very region where early Israel was forming.
