# schmid-ht-6-9.3: The "Biblical Theology Movement" insisted on "what it means" in addition to "what it meant" — Schmid resists this

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§6-9 — Jewish Reception to Implicit Theology]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §7

## The Claim

Krister Stendahl's famous distinction between "what it meant" and "what it means" drove the Biblical Theology Movement. John J. Collins reformulated: "Biblical theology should not...be reduced to the 'historical fact that such and such was thought and believed' but should clarify the meaning and truth-claims."

## Evidence

- Stendahl, IDB article "Biblical Theology, Contemporary": biblical exegesis must consider "what it means"
- Collins, *Encounters with Biblical Theology* (2005): theology must address "truth-claims from a modern critical perspective"
- John Rogerson, *A Theology of the Old Testament* (2009): theology uses historical criticism "in the service of the interpretation...on the assumption that [OT texts] have something to say to the present"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** These are direct citations of programmatic statements.

## Stakes

Schmid's project is pointedly REJECTING the "what it means" half of Stendahl's distinction. He's doing "what it meant" — and calling that theology. This is his most controversial move: can theology stop at description?

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire Biblical Theology Movement (Stendahl, Collins, Rogerson, and others). Any theologian who holds that theology must address contemporary meaning and truth-claims.

**Alternative reading:** The "what it means" question is inherent to theology. Without it, Schmid is doing intellectual history, not theology. The term "theology" implies normative engagement, not just historical description.

## Assessment

Schmid is right to note that the "what it means" demand is historically contingent (its roots are in neo-orthodoxy and the Biblical Theology Movement), not inherent to theology. But he needs to show that his "what it meant" theology actually delivers something worth calling theology. Description alone may not be enough.
