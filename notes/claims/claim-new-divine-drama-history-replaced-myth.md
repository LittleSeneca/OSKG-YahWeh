# kaufmann-ri-6.5: The New Divine Drama — History Replaced Myth

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

Israelite religion created a "new kind of divine saga" in which the conflict is not between gods (mythological drama) but between the will of God and the defiance of man (historical-moral drama). "With the transference of the divine drama from the realm of myth to the realm of history, pagan mythology evaporated." The Exodus legends — Pharaoh's defiance of YHWH — are the prototype. "There is no polemic in the Bible against pagan myth, for when historical saga was born, the sources of myth dried up and it withered away."

## Evidence

Kaufmann points to the structure of the Exodus narrative: YHWH is opposed not by other gods but by a defiant pagan king. The conflict is between divine will and human will. Nature is the instrument of God, not the arena of divine combat. The Bible doesn't argue against myth; it simply replaces it with a different kind of story. In pagan myth, the gods fight each other (Marduk vs. Tiamat, Baal vs. Yam/Mot). In the Bible, the conflict is between God's will and human defiance — Pharaoh, the Israelites in the wilderness, later the apostate kings.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is Kaufmann at his best descriptively and his worst historically. He has identified something genuinely distinctive about biblical narrative — the replacement of divine combat with historical conflict, the moralization of the divine drama. This IS a real difference between the Bible and, say, the Baal Cycle or Enuma Elish. But he compresses a centuries-long development into a single founding moment. The early poetry of Israel — the Song of the Sea with its "Who is like you among the gods, YHWH?", the Song of Deborah with its cosmic participants — shows that mythological thinking didn't "wither away" instantly.

## Stakes

If the "new divine drama" emerged with Moses, then Israelite religion was a genuine revolution from the start. If it developed gradually, then early Israelite narrative may have been more mythological than the Bible's final form suggests.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Smith would argue that early Israelite religion DID share mythological features with Canaanite religion (divine council, combat myths, theomachy) and that the "historicization" of myth was a gradual process. Psalm 82 (God judging the divine council), the Chaoskampf motifs in Job and Isaiah, and the Ugaritic parallels all suggest that mythological thinking persisted in Israel long after Moses.

**Alternative reading:** The move from myth to history WAS a genuine Israelite innovation — but it happened over centuries, not in a single Mosaic moment. Early Israelite literature (the Song of the Sea, the Song of Deborah, various Psalms) retains mythological features. The thoroughgoing historicization of narrative is a product of the Deuteronomistic school and the exilic period. Kaufmann's insight about the DISTINCTIVENESS of the final product is valid; his claim about its SUDDEN emergence is not.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann at his best descriptively and his worst historically. He has identified something genuinely distinctive about biblical narrative — the replacement of divine combat with historical conflict, the moralization of the divine drama. This IS a real difference between the Bible and the Baal Cycle or Enuma Elish. But he compresses a centuries-long development into a single founding moment. The early poetry of Israel — the Song of the Sea with its "Who is like you among the gods, YHWH?", the Song of Deborah with its cosmic participants — shows that mythological thinking didn't "wither away" instantly. It persisted alongside and within the historical narrative, gradually being transformed.
