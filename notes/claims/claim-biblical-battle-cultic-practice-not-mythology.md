# kaufmann-ri-4.1: Biblical battle is about cultic practice, not mythology

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IV — The Religion of the People]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter IV

## The Claim

"The biblical battle with Israel's idolatry restricts itself entirely to matters of practice, to rites and cults. There is no battle over the fundamental question: the mythological conception of deity. The significance of this fact can hardly be exaggerated." If popular religion had genuinely been mythological, the Bible would have had to fight that mythology — and it never does.

## Evidence

- "The Bible does not fight a mythical conception of YHWH either"
- Bible explicitly opposes human sacrifice (Mic 6:7; Jer 7:31), idolatrous worship forms, the idea God goes back on his word — but "it has not a word against mythological conceptions of YHWH"
- "What mythical vestiges are found in the Bible (e.g., the defeat of Rahab and the dragon) are part of the belief of the biblical authors. There is no myth that is regarded with hostility"
- Supposed syncretism of YHWH and Baal is only titular: "Baal means 'lord'; from the mere fact that the people used it as an epithet... what can be learned about their religious beliefs?"
- "Crucial is the utter lack of evidence that YHWH was given any of the mythological attributes of Baal. We do not hear, for example, that he was mated with Ashtoreth"
- No polemic against sacrifice as food of the god — the central pagan concept
- Prophetic rhetoric assumes the people already know God doesn't need food: "Isaiah's 'I am sated with the burnt offerings of rams' clearly assumes that its irony is self-evident"
- Psalm 50 "could hardly have dismissed the matter so lightly" if the audience believed YHWH ate sacrifices

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** A genuinely striking observation often overlooked. The prophets thunder against idolatry, but always about PRACTICE, never THEOLOGY. Whether this reflects monotheism's priority or polemical strategy is debatable.

## Stakes

If Kaufmann is right, the prophets were calling Israel back to a monotheism it already possessed, not fighting to establish monotheism against pagan popular belief.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-monotheism-was-rhetoric-not-religious-stage]] — Smith argues Israelite religion DID have mythological elements (divine council, combat with Sea) that the Bible retained and transformed rather than fought. [[claim-israelite-idolatry-vestigial-fetishism-not-genuine-polytheism]] is Kaufmann's own extension of this claim.

**Alternative reading:** The silence could reflect successful editorial purging, not historical absence. The Deuteronomistic editors systematically reshaped traditions. What Kaufmann takes as evidence of monotheism's antiquity could be evidence of the editors' thoroughness. Alternatively, the Bible DOES contain mythological elements — they were simply absorbed and transformed rather than polemicized against.

## Assessment

Kaufmann has identified a genuinely striking feature. The prophets' polemic is always about PRACTICE — "don't bow down to wood and stone" — never about THEOLOGY — "Baal didn't really defeat Yamm." This IS different from how early Christian apologists argued against paganism. Whether this silence reflects monotheism's priority or a deliberate polemical choice to caricature idolatry as fetishism is the question. Kaufmann's reading is more interesting than the alternatives, but not decisively proven.
