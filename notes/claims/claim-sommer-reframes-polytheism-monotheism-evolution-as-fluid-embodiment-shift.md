# sommer-bog-1-3.1: Sommer reframes the polytheism-to-monotheism evolution as a fluid-to-anti-fluid monotheism evolution

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapters 1-3 — Fluidity Model]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapters 1-3

## The Claim

The standard narrative traced by Smith, Romer, and Dever is: polytheism → monolatry → monotheism. Sommer argues this misses the more fundamental transformation. WITHIN monotheism, there was a FLUIDITY → ANTI-FLUIDITY evolution. J and E were monotheistic (one God) but fluid (multiple bodies, local manifestations, avatar-like *mal'akh*). P and D were monotheistic and anti-fluid (transcendent God, accessed only through the *kavod* or *shem*). The evolution wasn't polytheism to monotheism — it was fluid monotheism to non-fluid monotheism. This reframes the entire debate: the question is not when Israel reduced the number of gods, but when and why Israel changed the nature of the one God's embodiment.

Sommer does not deny the evidence for popular polytheism (Dever's archaeological data) or that Yahweh absorbed characteristics of other gods (Smith's convergence model). He argues that the biblical TEXTS, taken as theology, are monotheistic — but represent two different kinds of monotheism.

## Evidence

- **ANE evidence:** Mesopotamian and Canaanite gods had fluid selves — multiple local manifestations with partial independence (Ishtar of Arbela/Nineveh, Baal of Saphon/Ugarit/Lebanon, Marduk's fifty names). This was the default ANE pattern.
- **J/E texts:** Yahweh of Samaria, Yahweh of Teman, Yahweh of Hebron (Kuntillet Ajrud, 2 Samuel 15:7, Psalm 99:2) — distinct local manifestations. The *mal'akh* functions as an avatar, not a separate messenger (Genesis 18, 21, Exodus 3, Judges 6).
- **P/D texts:** P's *kavod* (Glory) is a blinding fire/cloud that represents God's presence without BEING God's body. D's *shem* (Name) dwells in the temple while God dwells in heaven. Both reject the fluidity model while preserving monotheism.
- **Textual sequence:** J and E (early, fluid monotheism) → D (7th century, God in heaven, Name on earth) → P (exilic/post-exilic, transcendent God, Glory appears) → later Judaism (God has NO body at all).
- **The irony:** What later Judaism and Christianity consider "true monotheism" — a transcendent, noncorporeal God — is the LATER development that rejected the more ANCIENT Israelite conception.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The textual evidence for fluidity in J/E and anti-fluidity in P/D is well-established; Sommer's reframing follows logically.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, the entire Smith/Romer/Dever narrative is asking the wrong question. The question is not "when did Israel become monotheistic?" but "when did Israel's monotheism become anti-fluid?" The standard model (polytheism → monolatry → monotheism) conflates two separate phenomena: the number of gods and the nature of divine presence. Sommer disentangles them.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Smith, Romer, and Dever would not necessarily disagree with Sommer's textual analysis, but they would dispute that the biblical texts can be read as consistently monotheistic. Dever in particular argues that the texts themselves preserve polytheistic traces and that the "Book religion" was a late elite construction.

**Alternative reading:** The standard model reads the fluidity evidence (local manifestations, multiple embodiments) as evidence of polytheism — multiple Yhwhs = multiple gods. Sommer reads the same evidence as evidence of fluid monotheism — one God, many bodies. The disagreement is interpretive, not factual.

## Assessment

Sommer's reframing is the most elegant resolution of the central tension in the Truth Project. He accepts the historical-critical data (polytheistic popular practice, Yahweh's absorption of other gods' traits) while preserving the theological claim that the biblical writers were monotheists. The trick is recognizing that "monotheism" is not one thing — it's fluid or anti-fluid, embodied or transcendent, multiple-manifestation or single-presence. The evolution isn't from many gods to one but from one God with many bodies to one God with no body. This doesn't refute Smith/Romer/Dever — it recategorizes their evidence within a more precise conceptual framework.
