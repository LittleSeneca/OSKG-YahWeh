# tigay-nog-1.2: Epigraphic onomasticon is more Yahwistic than the Bible

**Source:** [[Tigay — Chapter I — The Onomastic Evidence]] — Jeffrey H. Tigay, *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986), Chapter I

## The Claim

Tigay collected ALL theophoric personal names from pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions (seals, ostraca, bullae, jar handles, letters). The epigraphic onomasticon is MORE Yahwistic than the Bible's onomasticon. The Bible preserved a FEW pagan names through editorial oversight. The inscriptions preserve even fewer because there was no editorial process to filter. "The epigraphic evidence, with its lack of a comparable Tendenz, argues against attributing any statistical significance to those [biblical] indictments."

## Evidence

- **Yahwistic names:** Appendix A runs for dozens of pages listing names ending in -yhw, -yh, beginning with yhw-. Overwhelming majority across ALL inscription types (seals, ostraca, bullae, jar handles, letters).
- **Plausibly pagan names:** Tigay's Appendix B lists only a handful. For each, he notes alternative (non-pagan) interpretations.
- The key finding: the inscriptions — which were never edited or censored by later scribes — show even stronger YHWH-dominance than the biblical text that was edited.
- Tigay anticipated and addressed the counter-arguments:
  1. "Names don't reflect belief" — In Northwest Semitic culture, names explicitly invoke deities. "The divine name within Hebrew personal names could not have gone unrecognized." Scribes changed pagan names precisely because the meanings mattered.
  2. "Names only reflect parents, not bearers" — Parents choose names that reflect THEIR religious loyalties. Multi-generational naming patterns reveal community norms.
  3. "Single names don't prove monotheism" — Tigay agrees. "In dealing with the question of how many deities were worshipped within a particular group we must consider the total onomastic picture of that group, not the names of a few individuals."
  4. "Formal theophorics ≠ lived religion" — Tigay acknowledges: "the absence of other gods from the onomasticon would not by itself tell us whether that society denied the existence or divinity of those gods."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Tigay's Appendices A-E list the names exhaustively from all known pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions; the data is publicly verifiable.

## Stakes

This is the empirical core of Tigay's challenge. The Bible could theoretically have been systematically censored to remove pagan names. The inscriptions couldn't. And they show even FEWER pagan names. This undermines the argument that the biblical picture is a tendentious editorial creation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-tigay-onomastics-does-not-disprove-polytheism]] (Smith), [[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]] (Day)

**Alternative reading:** The inscriptions come disproportionately from administrative/military contexts (seals, letters, ostraca from forts) — the very circles most likely to be Yahwistic. Women and rural peasants are underrepresented. Dever's figurines are a different dataset; both could be right: the names show what people SAID (YHWH-devotion); the figurines show what people DID (goddess worship).

## Assessment

Tigay's evidence is strong and his methodological caution is admirable. The epigraphic data is the strongest part of his case — it cannot have been editorialized. The question is whether the inscriptions (disproportionately from administrative/military contexts) capture the FULL picture of Israelite religion, or only the official/elite/literate dimension.
