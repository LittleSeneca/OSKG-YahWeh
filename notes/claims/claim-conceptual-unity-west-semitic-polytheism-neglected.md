# smith-obm-intro.1: The conceptual unity of West Semitic polytheism has been neglected

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Introduction

## The Claim

"No overall investigation has been undertaken for the conceptual unity of West Semitic polytheisms; this book aims to redress this lack." Previous scholarship either catalogued individual deities (atomistic) or imposed external typologies (cosmic/social). "This book attempts to combine the advantages of these approaches in examining the major indigenous conceptual structures that ancient Ugaritic and Israelite societies used to construct their religious reality."

## Evidence

- The scholarly gap is real: a 1989 volume on polytheistic systems covers Hinduism and Greek religion, but "the essay on ancient Israel treats only the issue of monotheism in the Bible, and the author of the essay, a well-known expert on Ugaritic, ignores polytheism."
- Egyptology and Assyriology have broached conceptual coherence of their polytheisms; West Semitic studies have not.
- Smith's four-pronged approach: (1) divine groupings from largest to smallest (Part I), (2) shared characteristics of deities (Part II), (3) monotheism as emergent rhetoric (Part III).

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The gap Smith identifies is genuine and the approach is well-motivated.

## Stakes

If Smith succeeds, West Semitic polytheism gets treated as a coherent thought-system rather than a grab-bag of deities serving as negative foil for Israelite monotheism. This changes how we evaluate the transition to monotheism — it becomes an intellectual shift within a coherent system, not a leap from chaos to order.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat polytheism as inherently inferior to monotheism (the "apologetics, not history" Smith criticizes). Also those who argue Ugarit is too distant from Israel to be relevant (Keel and Uehlinger).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that the lack of West Semitic theological treatises means the "conceptual unity" Smith seeks is being imposed retrospectively — that polytheism's coherence was practical/ritual, not conceptual/theological.

## Assessment

Smith's framing is intellectually honest and fills a real gap. The danger is over-systematizing — imposing coherence where the ancients themselves might not have felt it. Smith acknowledges this risk.
