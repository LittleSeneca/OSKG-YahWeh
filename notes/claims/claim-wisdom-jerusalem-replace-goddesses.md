# smith-obm-9.7: Woman Wisdom and personified Jerusalem functioned as goddess replacements — "counter-advertisements" to Astarte and Queen of Heaven cults

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch9 — Formation of Monotheistic Theologies]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"In view of the divine language and imagery associated with both figures, perhaps both figures represent substitutions for divine female figures." G. von Rad termed Wisdom in Proverbs 9:1-9 "as a contrast [*Kontrastbild*] and a defence against" customs associated with a goddess. Wisdom and Jerusalem "may have usurped imagery associated generally with goddesses, perhaps specifically with the *asherah*... and applied it to the figure of Wisdom as a 'counter-advertisement' to the cults of Astarte and the Queen of Heaven."

## Evidence

- The presence of Astarte in Israel during the monarchy period is archaeologically and textually attested.
- The *asherah* was present in the Jerusalem Temple (2 Kgs 23:6) and widely worshipped.
- The Queen of Heaven is mentioned in Jeremiah 7:18 and 44:17-19 as a popular object of devotion.
- Both Wisdom and Jerusalem are female personifications with divine attributes — they occupy the symbolic space goddesses once filled.
- "Hence, new myths may in part represent replacements in service to monotheistic representations of divinity."

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** This is Smith's most speculative claim in the chapter. The idea that personified Wisdom "replaces" Asherah is plausible as a functional analogy but impossible to verify. There's no smoking-gun text saying "Wisdom instead of Asherah."

## Stakes

If Wisdom and Jerusalem are goddess-replacements, monotheism didn't eliminate the feminine divine — it transformed it into acceptable literary personifications. This is a domestication strategy: the goddess is neutered into a metaphor. Feminist critics might see this as patriarchal erasure rather than creative transformation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Wisdom as a genuinely new theological development unrelated to goddess polemics. Those who read the female personification of Jerusalem as rooted in the ancient Near Eastern city-goddess tradition (which predates and is independent of Asherah worship).

**Alternative reading:** Wisdom and Jerusalem could be independent developments from within Israelite traditions — personified attributes and city laments have their own literary histories. The goddess-replacement theory may overstate the polemical intent and underestimate the creative autonomy of Israelite scribes.

## Assessment

The functional analogy is intriguing but Smith doesn't provide enough evidence to move it beyond suggestion. The von Rad citation gives the idea scholarly pedigree, but Smith needed to develop this more. It's telling that this is relegated to a few paragraphs in the final section rather than receiving its own case study. The "counter-advertisement" framing is vivid but anachronistic.
