# albertz-hir-6.1: The Hellenistic period saw the formation of a genuine hierocracy — high priest as political leader, provoking aristocratic power struggles

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 6 — Hellenistic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 6

## The Claim

"Only in the Hellenistic period did a hierocracy or theocracy form, which, if one ignores the sharp economic control exercised by Ptolemaean government, could give the Judaeans the feeling that they had regained something of the splendour of the former monarchy." This corrects his earlier claim (Ch 5) that post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy during the Persian period.

## Evidence

- The Ptolemies refrained from installing a governor in Judaea — the high priest became prostates (political representative to the king)
- The high priest extended his leadership from the priestly college to the council of elders (gerousia), assuming political leadership
- He controlled the temple treasury — giving fiscal autonomy (though supervised by Ptolemies)
- Sirach 50.1-4 praises the high priest Simon II for his building projects and political leadership
- However, this power concentration provoked resistance: the Tobiad Joseph challenged the prostasia of Onias II (mid-3rd century), and later (175 BCE) Jason and Menelaus bought the high priesthood from Antiochus IV
- "A hereditary office of rule, restricted to the Zadokide line, stood in the way of the economic and power interests of the aristocracy; if it could not be completely abolished, then they could degrade it into a venial state office"
- The social rift continued and deepened: Hecataeus of Abdera (c. 300 BCE) notes land purchases and exploitation; Koheleth and Sirach report oppression of the poor; debt slavery became so threatening the Ptolemies enacted protective laws
- The Ptolemaean tax-leasing system enriched aristocrats further — the Tobiad Joseph's 22-year tax lease is the prime example

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The institutional shift (high priest as political leader) is well-attested; the specific causal mechanism is well-argued.

## Stakes

Albertz is correcting his own earlier correction. In Chapter 5 he insisted post-exilic Judah was NOT a theocracy. Here he says it BECAME one in the Hellenistic period. This shows that "theocracy" isn't a uniform condition — it's a historical development driven by specific political conditions (Ptolemaic administrative policy). The subsequent Hasmonean state (combining high priesthood and kingship) has roots here.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see a continuous theocracy from the Persian period. Those who dispute the dating of the high priest's political powers.

**Alternative reading:** The high priest always exercised political leadership; the change under the Ptolemies was only in the absence of a separate governor — more continuity than Albertz allows.

## Assessment

Albertz's distinction between Persian-period "not a theocracy" and Hellenistic "hierocracy" is nuanced and well-argued. The Ptolemaic decision not to appoint a governor is the key variable. The Tobiad romance provides vivid if tendentious evidence for aristocratic challenge to priestly power. The tax-leasing system as a mechanism for deepening social conflict is an important insight.
