# dever-dghw-5.1: 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines are the strongest archaeological evidence for popular Asherah worship

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter V

## The Claim

The Judean Pillar Figurines (JPFs) — over 3,000 found, overwhelmingly in DOMESTIC contexts — are the strongest archaeological evidence for folk religion and popular goddess worship in monarchic Judah. Mass-produced from molds, cheap, and ubiquitous, they represent material evidence that text-based scholarship (like Smith's) cannot account for.

## Evidence

- **Quantity:** Over 3,000 JPFs found — far more than any other cult object type
- **Context:** Overwhelmingly found in DOMESTIC contexts (houses, not temples) — "Found in almost every excavated 8th-6th century house in Judah"
- **Form:** Nude female with emphasized breasts, hands supporting or holding breasts — typical fertility/ mother-goddess iconography
- **Production:** Mass-produced from molds — cheap, accessible, everywhere. Not elite objects.
- **Date:** 8th-6th centuries BCE — the period of the monarchy, the prophets, the reforms
- **Identification:** Most scholars identify them as Asherah representations
- **Use:** "Prayers in clay" — votive offerings for fertility, conception, safe childbirth, milk production. "They were part of the everyday religious paraphernalia of the typical Judean household." Used by women, for women's concerns.

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The existence, quantity, domestic context, and date of the figurines are beyond dispute. The interpretation as Asherah representations is the scholarly consensus (though not unanimous).

## Stakes

This is Dever's strongest challenge to Smith and text-based scholarship. You cannot explain away 3,000+ nude female figurines in domestic contexts. If Smith is right that Asherah was "only a cult symbol," what are these figurines? Dever's answer: they are the material evidence that the biblical texts suppress — evidence of popular goddess worship that the Deuteronomists condemned and later editors obscured.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mark S. Smith ([[claim-asherah-cult-symbol-not-goddess-monarchic-israel]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]]) — who downplays or minimizes the figurine evidence. Some scholars argue the figurines could represent human worshippers, generic fertility charms, or talismans rather than the goddess herself.

**Alternative reading:** The JPFs are not goddess figurines but votive representations of human women seeking fertility — "prayers in clay" directed to Yhwh, not to a goddess.
