# fleming-ybi-summary.3: Yhwʒ is a Shasu people-name, not a divine name

**Source:** [[Fleming — Book Summary]] — Daniel E. Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021)

## The Claim

Fleming argues that Yhwʒ in the Egyptian Soleb (Amenhotep III, c. 1400 BCE) and Amara (Ramses II) lists is primarily a people-name, not a divine name. This is not a claim that Yhwʒ is NOT Yahweh — Fleming accepts the identification. Rather, he argues that the Egyptian evidence preserves the name in its earliest function: as a designation for a Shasu people-group, not as the name of a god. The divine function came later. Three supporting arguments: (1) the verbal form y-h-wʒ fits the pattern of Amorite shortened personal names; (2) peoples named after persons are common in the ancient Near East (Israel = yiśrā'ēl, Yamutbal, etc.); (3) the Shasu-land designations in the Egyptian lists are consistently people-names (mhwt = "families"), not divine names.

## Evidence

- The verbal form y-h-wʒ matches the pattern of Amorite shortened personal names (imperfect verb form used as a name)
- Peoples named after persons are common in ANE onomastics: Israel = yiśrā'ēl ("El strives"), Yamutbal, etc.
- The Shasu-land names in the Egyptian lists use the term mhwt ("families"), indicating kinship-based people-groups, not cult centers or divine names
- The Egyptian scribes categorized Yhwʒ alongside other Shasu people-groups (Shasu of Seir, Shasu of Laban, etc.), not alongside divine names

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Yhwʒ = Yahweh identification itself is consensus but not certain; Fleming's additional claim that it's a people-name is his original contribution and doesn't have the same consensus support. The Amorite verbal-name parallel is suggestive but not probative.

## Stakes

If Yhwʒ is a people-name, the search for Yahweh's origins shifts from "where did the god Yahweh come from?" to "who were the people of Yahweh, and how did their god become Israel's god?" The Midianite Hypothesis answers the wrong question — it looks for divine origins when the evidence points to social origins. Yahweh was a god who belonged to a specific people-group, and that people-group was one of the constituencies that eventually coalesced into Israel.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Thomas Römer and the Midianite Hypothesis defenders treat Yhwʒ as a divine name from the earliest attestation. Mark S. Smith treats the Shasu-Yhwʒ connection as evidence for the southern origin of the god Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** Yhwʒ is a divine name from the start — the Shasu are named after their god (the "Shasu of Yahweh"), just as other ancient peoples were named after their gods. Fleming's response: the Egyptian lists don't use divine determinatives for Yhwʒ, and the pattern of naming peoples after a founding figure is better attested in the Amorite onomasticon.
