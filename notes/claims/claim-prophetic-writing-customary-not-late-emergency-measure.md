# kaufmann-ri-10.7: Writing was a customary mode of prophetic communication, not a late development

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"Writing was a means of communication hardly less customary than speech." The prophets "were not only speakers, they also created a written literature. There is no internal conflict between speech and writing, nor is there any basis for the view that only in Second Temple times were prophecies set down in writing." The notion that prophets resorted to writing only in emergencies "is naive."

## Evidence

- The prophets could "fulfill their primary task only through speech or action," and they "confronted their audience directly in the first instance"
- But "it is naive to assume that the prophets could really convey their message 'to the people' through speech alone. In fact, they addressed only a limited audience"
- "The limited circle of listeners was supplemented by the broader circle of readers"
- The prophets were poets; "there is no ground for asserting that such poetry was not collected and indited"
- Explicit references to prophetic writing: Isaiah writes "on a large tablet" (8:1) and "in a book" (30:8); Habakkuk writes his vision "on tablets" (2:2); Jeremiah writes prophecies of consolation "in a book" (30:1ff.) and prophecy of woe "in a book" (36:1ff.); Ezekiel receives a "scroll" (2:8-3:4); Isaiah 34:16 mentions a "book of prophecies"
- Ancient Israel had written poetic collections: "the book of the Wars of YHWH" (Num 21:14), "the book of Jashar" (Josh 10:13; II Sam 1:18)
- Against Nyberg's oral transmission theory: "Israel was not at its inception a 'primitive' people" — it had lived among high civilizations; pre-Israelite Canaan had written literature; Egypt and Babylonia were scribal cultures
- The rule: "speech is followed by writing" — God speaks the Decalogue, then writes it; Moses rehearses laws, then writes them; Samuel tells the rule of monarchy, then writes it
- Jeremiah 36 shows the pattern: Jeremiah dictates to Baruch, who compiles. Old prophecies were "not recited by heart, but were written down and read from writing"
- The reason prophetic sermons were preserved in extenso beginning with Amos: "the new level of thought" — "Unlike their predecessors, the classical prophets were important for what they said more than for what they did"
- This literature clusters around the two falls: "it arises just before the fall of Ephraim and ends not long after the fall of Judah"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Well-supported by internal biblical evidence and the comparative ancient Near Eastern context; Kaufmann's arguments against Nyberg's oral-transmission theory are decisive.

## Stakes

Whether the prophetic books are the *original literary compositions* of the prophets (Kaufmann) or the *written deposits of oral tradition* (form criticism). If Kaufmann is right, the literary quality of the prophetic books is authorial, not the product of scribal schools.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The form-critical school (Gunkel, Mowinckel) and Scandinavian oral-tradition theorists (Nyberg, Engnell, Nielsen). They argued that prophetic oracles circulated orally for generations before being written down, and that the written form is late and secondary.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that writing existed from early times and that prophets wrote. But this doesn't mean ALL prophetic material was written by the prophets themselves. Oral performance and written composition could coexist. Jeremiah 36 depicts exactly this — Jeremiah verbally prophesied for 23 years before compiling a comprehensive scroll. That's both oral (initial delivery) and written (subsequent compilation).

## Assessment

Kaufmann is right to push back against the extreme oral-transmission model, which underestimated literacy and scribal culture in ancient Israel. His arguments against Nyberg are solid: Israel wasn't pre-literate Arabia; it was embedded in the literate cultures of Egypt and Mesopotamia. The explicit references to prophetic writing in Isaiah, Jeremiah, Habakkuk, and Ezekiel are real and can't be dismissed. However, Kaufmann's model may be too exclusively literary. Prophets probably DID speak publicly (the temple court, the city gate) AND write. Some oracles may have been composed in writing from the start; others may have been spoken first and written later. The more interesting question is why the clustering around the two falls: the existential crisis of the 8th and 6th centuries generated literature precisely because people needed to understand why God had destroyed what he had established.
