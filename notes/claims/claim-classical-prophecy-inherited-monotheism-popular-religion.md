# kaufmann-ri-10.1: Classical prophecy inherited monotheism from the popular religion

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"Classical prophecy sprang from the soil of Israel's popular monotheism." It "does not so much repudiate the popular religion as rise above it." The classical prophets "do not announce a new God or teach a new concept of the nature of deity." Their God is "the God of the people," and their reproof rests on "the popular legends telling of his wonders in Israel." The rupture between prophets and people is about practice, not theology.

## Evidence

- From the popular religion, classical prophecy "received its idea of the one God, sole creator and judge of the world; a non-mythological, non-magical deity, a supreme will, unfettered by fate or compulsion"
- "Classical prophecy does not recognize or combat mythological or magical beliefs. It arose among a people out of which genuine paganism had been uprooted centuries before"
- The prophets "bewail the lack of 'knowledge of God' in the land, but the God they speak of is the God of the people"
- The historical and moral presuppositions are shared: Israel alone knows YHWH; pagan lands are "impure"; the nations are judged only for gross moral sins, not idolatry; the covenant with YHWH is foundational
- Classical prophets are steeped in past literature: narrative traditions of the Torah, legal literature, psalmody, wisdom forms — even ancient Canaanite poetry
- Jeremiah is "strongly influenced by Deuteronomy"; Ezekiel "by the Priestly Code"
- Like their predecessors, the classical prophets have a mantic element, work wonders, and are moved by an individual call
- Yet unlike popular prophets, they are "exclusively apostles of God" who "come of their own accord to a people who have not sought them" — they do not prophesy for pay

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The observation that prophets never argue for monotheism is important and matches the textual evidence, but the inference that this proves POPULAR monotheism depends on Ch IV's contested case.

## Stakes

The entire direction of causation in Israelite religion. If the prophets inherited monotheism from the people, then monotheism predates classical prophecy — reversing the critical sequence (prophets create monotheism → Torah codifies it).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus. Smith traces monotheistic *rhetoric* as emerging within the prophetic corpus itself, reaching full expression only in Second Isaiah. Römer sees monotheism developing through the prophetic movement, not predating it. The standard reading of Amos and Hosea is that they are *arguing against* polytheistic practice, not assuming monotheistic belief.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets may share theological vocabulary with the popular religion without that religion being monotheistic. Popular religion could have been henotheistic (YHWH supreme among gods) while the prophets pushed toward exclusive monotheism. The "rift" Kaufmann acknowledges between prophets and people suggests the people didn't share the prophets' full vision — even if both used the name YHWH.

## Assessment

This claim is the load-bearing pillar for Kaufmann's entire prophetic section. He is right that the classical prophets never argue *that* YHWH is the only God — they argue *what YHWH demands*. This is a real textual datum that evolutionary models must explain. But the inference from "the prophets don't argue for monotheism" to "the people were already monotheists" is a leap. The prophets might not argue for monotheism because (a) their audience already accepted it (Kaufmann), or (b) their rhetorical strategy was to assert it as premise rather than argue it, or (c) the monotheistic framing was supplied by later editors who collected and shaped the prophetic books.
