# keel-ggi-5.5: "His Asherah" Is a Cultic Symbol (Stylized Tree), NOT the Goddess Asherah

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part V — Iron Age IIB — Baal El Yahweh and His Asherah]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part V

## The Claim

"The controversial reference to *'šrth* in the inscription from Kuntillet Ajrud thus most likely refers to such a cultic object... 'Yahweh's asherah' does not have equal rank with Yahweh but is rather a mediating entity that brings his blessing and is conceived in the mind in the shape of a stylized tree that was thus subordinate to Yahweh." Keel & Uehlinger analyze the Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions and conclude, based on grammar, biblical usage, and iconography, that "his asherah" refers to a wooden cult object — a stylized tree symbol — not the goddess Asherah.

## Evidence

*Grammatical evidence:*
- Hebrew never uses a possessive suffix with a proper name. You cannot say "his Asherah" any more than you can say "his Mary." The suffix *-h* on *'šrth* forces "asherah" to be a common noun, not a personal name
- In all biblical passages where "asherah" might refer to a goddess, it has the article (*hā'ăšērâ* or *lā'ăšērâ*) — double determination (article + proper name) is impossible in Hebrew
- The Ugaritic parallel 'nth (Anat + possessive suffix) is sometimes cited as a counterexample, but it is a single debated text (KTU 1:43.13) and does not establish a pattern

*Biblical evidence:*
- Every biblical description of an asherah treats it as a manufactured object: made of wood, "made" or "set up," "planted" (Deut 16:21), "torn out," "chopped down," "burned"
- The biblical texts never describe the asherah's appearance — they treat it as a known artifact, not a personal deity

*Iconographic evidence:*
- The asherah is consistently depicted as a stylized tree in the iconographic record
- Pithos A at Kuntillet Ajrud shows a stylized tree flanked by caprids, above a lion (motifs E-H, the largest and most prominent composition on the jar) — this is the visual correlate of "his asherah"
- Seal images (illuss. 222, 230, 233a) and a pithos from Lachish (illus. 223) show the same stylized tree motif in Judean contexts

*The blessing formulas:*
- The blessing formula in the pithos inscriptions says "I bless you by/to Yahweh... and his asherah" — but the concluding wish for blessing in both inscriptions uses SINGULAR verbs: "may HE bless you and keep you" — Yahweh alone is the active agent
- A votive inscription on a stone bowl from the same room mentions only Yahweh: "Blessed is/be he of/to Yahweh" — no asherah
- The Khirbet el-Qom inscription: "Blessed is Uriyahu by Yahweh... by his asherah, he has saved him" — singular verb, Yahweh is the active agent, the asherah is the *medium through which* salvation occurs

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The grammatical argument is strong but not universally accepted (some Ugaritic counterexamples exist). The iconographic identification of asherah = stylized tree is very strong. The singular-verb pattern in the blessing formulas is a genuinely important observation that other scholars have overlooked.

## Stakes

This is THE central debate. If Keel & Uehlinger are right, Smith's position (asherah = cult symbol) is vindicated by the iconographic evidence, and Dever/Romer's position (Asherah = goddess consort) is wrong. The entire "Yahweh had a wife" narrative collapses — or at minimum, must be radically reframed as "Yahweh had a cultic symbol that mediated his blessing."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984, 1990, 2005), Romer (2015), Day (1986, 2000), Coogan (1987a), M. Weippert (1990), Olyan (1988), Zevit (1984), and most scholars in the "Asherah as consort" camp.

**Alternative reading:** The grammatical argument, while elegant, may be too rigid. Languages have exceptions. The singular verb in the blessing formula could simply be default singular agreement with the nearest subject. The Ugaritic material does show divine names with possessive suffixes in at least a few cases. The "consort" reading remains plausible even if Keel & Uehlinger have shifted the burden of proof.

## Assessment

After reading Keel & Uehlinger's complete analysis alongside Dever's and Smith's arguments, I find Keel & Uehlinger MORE convincing than Dever but with an important caveat. Their reading accounts for ALL the evidence — grammatical, biblical, iconographic, and epigraphic — while Dever's reading requires dismissing the grammatical problem and treating the iconographic evidence selectively. *However*, Keel & Uehlinger may be underplaying the experiential reality: even if the asherah was "officially" a cultic symbol mediating Yahweh's blessing, ordinary Israelites may have experienced it as a goddess. The distinction between "mediating entity" and "deity" may have been blurrier on the ground than it is in scholarly analysis.
