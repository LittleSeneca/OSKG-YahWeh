# lewis-ocg-2.4: Albright and Kaufmann's insistence on Israel's radical distinctiveness was untenable — Cross offered a better synthesis

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 2 — History of Scholarship]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 2

## The Claim

Kaufmann asserted that "Israelite religion was an original creation of the people of Israel. It was absolutely different from anything the pagan world ever knew" and that "the Bible is utterly unaware of the nature and meaning of pagan religion." Albright shared this passion for uniqueness but, because of his command of Levantine material culture, was forced to acknowledge that "the ignorance presupposed by Kaufmann's view is simply incredible." Cross then offered the synthesis: Israel's religion "stood in a clear line of continuity with the mythopoeic patterns of West Semitic, especially Canaanite myth" while simultaneously exhibiting "radical differentiation" and "the appearance of novelty."

## Evidence

- Albright's direct critique of Kaufmann: "there was so much exchange of cultural influences between Israel and its neighbors on all sides of its tiny territory... that the ignorance presupposed by Kaufmann's view is simply incredible."
- Cross's methodological principle: "I believe it is as illegitimate methodologically to resort to the category of the sui generis in explaining historical sequences, as it is contrary to scientific method to resort to the category of miracles."
- Levenson's critique of Kaufmann: "We cannot conclude from the inaccuracy of the Israelite poet's satire that he had no understanding of the spiritual life of his Canaanite neighbor."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** VERY HIGH that Kaufmann's position is untenable. HIGH that Cross's synthesis has held up. The archaeological evidence from Kuntillet Ajrud alone demonstrates that Israelites worshipped Canaanite deities alongside Yahweh.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite religion is a "miracle" or a historical development. Kaufmann's position supports confessional claims about unique revelation. Cross's position allows for genuine religious innovation within a cultural continuum — which is how most historians understand cultural change.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Heiser would push back on the "polytheism" framing — he'd say Israelites acknowledged other elohim (divine beings) but only ever worshipped Yahweh. Conservative evangelicals (Provan, Long, Longman) would defend a modified form of Israel's distinctiveness.

**Alternative reading:** Cross's continuity-with-novelty is still the best framework, but the balance between continuity and novelty is debated — some scholars (Smith) emphasize continuity more, others (Albertz) see more novelty through social-structural change.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's position is now held only by the most conservative scholars. Albright's modified distinctiveness is closer to the evidence but still overstates the case. Cross's continuity-with-novelty framework is where the field has landed — and where Lewis himself stands.
