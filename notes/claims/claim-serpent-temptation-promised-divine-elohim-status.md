# heiser-ur-11.1: The serpent's temptation in Eden was a genuine promise of divine status — access to the divine council

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 3 — Divine Transgressions]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 11

## The Claim

The serpent's temptation — "you will be like *elohim*, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5) — was not a lie about moral knowledge. It was a genuine promise of divine status: access to the divine council. "Knowing good and evil" is not about moral discernment but about the kind of knowledge that divine beings possess — the knowledge to govern, to judge, to participate in the heavenly council's administration of the cosmos.

## Evidence

The chapter note provides only a one-line summary. Heiser's full argument (Chapter 11 of *The Unseen Realm*) includes:
- The phrase "knowing good and evil" as a merism for comprehensive knowledge — the kind that belongs to *elohim*
- The serpent's specific promise: "you will be like *elohim*" — plural divine beings, not "like God" singular
- ANE parallels of divine knowledge as the prerogative of the gods
- The connection to the divine council: Adam and Eve were being offered promotion into the heavenly realm

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The claim fits Heiser's divine council framework and the serpent-as-divine-being reading from Chapter 7, but the note provides minimal evidence. The full exegetical case would require engaging the Chapter 11 material directly.

## Stakes

If the temptation was a genuine promise of divine status (not a lie about moral knowledge), the Eden narrative is not about disobeying an arbitrary command but about a cosmic power play. The serpent (a divine council rebel) offered humanity what was genuinely available — promotion into the *elohim* realm — but on illegitimate terms. This reframes the Fall as a failed apotheosis rather than a moral failure.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional interpreters who read "knowing good and evil" as moral discernment acquired through disobedience. Scholars who read Genesis 3 as an etiological myth explaining the human condition — the serpent's promise was empty and the knowledge gained was simply awareness of nakedness and mortality.

**Alternative reading:** The serpent lied. "Knowing good and evil" is moral knowledge, and becoming "like God" in this sense was not a genuine possibility — it was a deception. The serpent's claim that "you will not surely die" was the lie; the knowledge gained was real but not divine.

## Assessment

This claim makes more sense within Heiser's system than outside it. If you've accepted the divine council framework and the serpent-as-divine-being reading from Chapter 7, the serpent's promise of *elohim* status follows naturally. But the claim depends heavily on those prior moves, and the evidence for "knowing good and evil = divine council knowledge" requires more support than the note provides.
