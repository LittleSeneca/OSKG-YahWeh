# albertz-hir-3.1-3.2.5: Rebel movements used pre-state anti-domination Yahwism against the monarchy

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.1-3.2 — Formation of Monarchy and Legitimation Crisis]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.2

## The Claim

The rebel movements under David were not anti-monarchy per se but sought "a kind of constitutional monarchy... which gave the tribal groups the right to political co-operation." Their theological argument: "the institutional consolidation of political rule in the dynastic monarchy touches on the rule of Yahweh over Israel" — "the establishment of a human kingship is an attack on Yahweh's kingly rule" (1 Sam 8:7; 12:12). The resistance groups turned the kingship theology on its head: Yahweh's rule doesn't legitimate the king; it delegitimizes him.

## Evidence

- Absalom's revolt: "the elders of Israel" and "men of Israel" backed him; aimed at constitutional monarchy (2 Sam 17:4, 11, 14)
- Sheba's revolt: the slogan "We have no portion in David... Each to his tents, O Israel!" (2 Sam 20:1) "quite certainly implies a total rejection" — possibly of kingship itself
- Jeroboam's revolt: explicitly against forced labor, with appeal to Exodus tradition
- Theological polemics: Jotham's fable (Judg 9:7-15), the "law of the king" (1 Sam 8:11-17), Gideon's refusal (Judg 8:22f.)
- The twelve-tribe scheme (Gen 49; Deut 33) "can only be understood as an alternative scheme of the opposition groups" — "the unity of Israel was not to be constituted by the king but by the tribes"
- The resistance groups used "populist" Yahweh-kingship: Yahweh rules Israel directly, without royal mediation

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The texts exist and the interpretation is plausible, but dating these passages to the early monarchy specifically (rather than later Deuteronomistic retrojection) is debated.

## Stakes

This is the birth of "prophetic opposition" — before there were writing prophets, there were tribal resistance movements using Yahwistic anti-domination traditions. The prophetic tradition isn't an 8th-century innovation but continues a pattern established in the 10th century.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the anti-monarchical texts (1 Sam 8, Judg 8-9) to the Deuteronomistic period (7th century) rather than the early monarchy.

**Alternative reading:** The rebel movements may have been purely political (resistance to taxation and conscription) with theological rhetoric added later by Deuteronomistic editors. Or the anti-monarchical texts may be retrojections that don't reflect actual 10th-century ideology.

## Assessment

The interpretation is plausible and fits Albertz's model well, but the dating question is real. If 1 Sam 8 and Judg 8-9 are Deuteronomistic retrojections, then the rebel movements' theological self-understanding may have been quite different from what the texts now present. Albertz's strongest move is treating Jeroboam's revolt as genuinely Exodus-motivated — this connects the political resistance to the theological tradition in a way that doesn't depend on dating the anti-monarchical polemics.
