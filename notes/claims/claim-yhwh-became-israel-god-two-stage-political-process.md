# romer-inv-4-5.1: Yhwh became Israel's god through a two-stage political process

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapters 4-5 — How Yhwh Became Israel's God]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapters 4-5 synthesis

## The Claim

Yhwh did not become Israel's god through divine revelation or covenant at Sinai. It happened through a two-stage political process: (1) Israel — originally an El-worshipping people named after El — adopted Yhwh as patron deity during the emergence of the monarchy under Saul and David; (2) David then installed Yhwh in Jerusalem, the old El/Elyon city, unifying northern Yhwh worship with southern El traditions. The identification of El and Yhwh — which Mark Smith traced through textual analysis — was accomplished POLITICALLY through David's unification of cults.

## Evidence

**Stage 1 — Adoption by the monarchy (Chapter 4):**
- The name "Israel" (*Yiśrā'ēl*) contains the theophoric element *'ēl*, not *yāhû*. Römer's preferred etymology is "May El reign" (from *ś-r-r*, "rule").
- The Merneptah Stele (c. 1210-1205 BCE) mentions "Israel" as an ethnic group in the central highlands — before any evidence of Yhwh worship.
- Exodus 6:2-3 explicitly acknowledges: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yhwh I did not make myself known to them" — a textual memory of the transition.
- Samuel (an El name: "El is exalted") is the transitional figure — prophet of El at Shiloh who anoints Saul.

**Stage 2 — Installation in Jerusalem (Chapter 5):**
- Jerusalem was originally a Canaanite/Jebusite city whose deity was El Elyon (Genesis 14: Melchizedek, "priest of El Elyon" at Salem).
- David conquered this neutral, non-tribal city and made it his capital.
- David brought Yhwh's ark from the northern/Ephraimite tradition to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) — the cultic unification.
- The royal psalms (Psalm 110, Psalm 2) depict the Davidic king enthroned alongside Yhwh in the old El city.

**The political logic:**
Yhwh now ruled where El had ruled. The theological identification of the two gods was accomplished through political action — a warlord merging cults to build a kingdom. "David's genius wasn't theological — it was political. He knew how to merge cults to build a kingdom."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The individual components have varying confidence (HIGH for Israel-as-El's-people via the name; MEDIUM for David's role in the transition; HIGH for Jerusalem's non-Israelite origin). The synthesis of both stages into a single political process is an interpretive framework — plausible and coherent, but underdetermined by direct evidence.

## Stakes

If Römer is right, the covenant between God and Israel is a royal construction, not an eternal relationship. The God of Jerusalem — the God of the Temple, the God of the Psalms — was introduced there by a political warlord who needed a unifying deity for his new capital. The "city of God" was someone else's city with someone else's god. David's genius was political, not theological.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue Yhwh was Israel's god from the beginning. Heiser's divine council model (Yhwh as second-tier deity under El from the start). Conservatives who read Exodus 6:2-3 as progressive revelation rather than historical memory of a transition. See [[scholarly-directory-yahweh-origins]].

**Alternative reading:** The El-to-Yhwh transition reflects progressive divine revelation — God was always Yhwh, but revealed Himself progressively as El Shadday, then as Yhwh. David's role was providential, not political. Or: El and Yhwh were always identified; the name "Israel" reflects El language because El was a generic term for "god," not a distinct deity.

## Assessment

This synthesis claim is the capstone of Römer's first five chapters. The narrative arc is remarkably clean: southern desert god → Midianite mediation → adopted by El's people → installed in El's city by a political warlord. Römer tells a story; Smith builds a case. Both work from the same evidence. Römer's strength is narrative coherence; his weakness is the gap between the elegance of the story and the thinness of the direct evidence for David's religious policies. But the arc IS coherent, and it explains the data we have.
