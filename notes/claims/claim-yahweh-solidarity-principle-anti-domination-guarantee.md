# albertz-hir-2.3-2.4.3: Yahweh as both solidarity principle and anti-domination guarantee

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §2.3-2.4 — Tribal Alliance and Family Piety]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §2.3

## The Claim

Yahweh religion "made an essential contribution to maintaining the critical balance between the obligation to solidarity and the ideal of freedom." On one hand, Yahweh created solidarity beyond clan and tribe — essential for wars of liberation. On the other, "there was virtually no religious legitimation of political rule." Yahweh's rule blocked human rule: when Gideon was offered the crown, he refused with "Yahweh will rule over you." The temporary charismatic leadership of the Judges (reverting to private life after the crisis) was theologically grounded.

## Evidence

- Judges 8:22f. — Gideon's refusal of hereditary monarchy
- The pattern of Judges: leaders emerge for crises, then disappear
- Only the relatively weak "spokesman" (nāśî') had religious protection (Ex 22:27) — not elders or the assembly
- The idea that Yahweh was the real owner of the land (Lev 25:23) served to protect family inheritance from being sold off

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Gideon story is a key text, but its dating and whether it reflects pre-state reality or later anti-monarchical ideology is contested.

## Stakes

If Yahweh's original function was anti-monarchical, then the monarchy (when it comes) is not just a political innovation but a theological crisis — one that the prophets will spend centuries trying to resolve.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Judges as a later, pro-monarchical or Deuteronomistic composition. The Gideon story may be a 7th-century anti-monarchical polemic projected back.

**Alternative reading:** The charismatic leadership model in Judges may reflect how tribal societies actually functioned (temporary war leaders) without requiring a specific anti-monarchical theology. Gideon's refusal may be a literary trope rather than historical memory.

## Assessment

Consistent with Albertz's overall model but feels slightly idealized. The tension between Yahweh-as-liberator and Yahweh-as-king is real in the texts, but whether it originates in pre-state social organization or in later theological reflection is hard to determine.
