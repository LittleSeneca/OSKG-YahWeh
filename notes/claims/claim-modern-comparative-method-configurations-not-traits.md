# lewis-ocg-3.7: Modern Comparative Method — Configurations, Not Traits

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 3 — Methodology]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Today's comparativists follow Talmon: "our concern should be with difference as much as with likenesses." They avoid Sandmel's "parallelomania" (overdoing supposed similarities) AND Kaufmann's categorical distinctiveness. Instead, following Machinist, they study "configurations of traits and ideas to understand the distinctiveness of ancient Israel — or, for that matter, any culture." The goal is not to find a single trait present in Israel but absent elsewhere, but to see how Israel combined and reconfigured traits shared across the ancient Near East.

## Evidence

- Talmon (1978): difference matters as much as likeness
- Sandmel (1962): "parallelomania" — the tendency to overstate parallels and ignore differences
- Machinist (1991): the "configuration" approach — studying how cultures combine and reconfigure shared traits
- "Establishing traits is an elusive business" because "as the increasing volume of archaeological discoveries makes clear, some correspondence always seems to be waiting to be found somewhere in the ancient Near East"
- Shared pattern of divine identity formation: Tyre chose Melqart, Sidon chose Eshmun, Moab chose Chemosh, Edom chose Qos, Ammon chose Milcom, Israel chose Yahweh — the pattern is shared; the specific god is contingent
- Lewis shows that Cross himself was arguing for both continuity AND novelty, not just continuity

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is the consensus position in the field — Talmon, Sandmel, and Machinist are widely cited and the configuration approach avoids both parallelomania and Kaufmann-style distinctiveness.

## Stakes

Whether Israel is "unique" or "just another ancient Near Eastern culture." Lewis's answer: both. The configuration is distinctive; the individual traits are not.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Only the extremes. Pan-Babylonianists (everything comes from Mesopotamia) and Kaufmann-style apologists (nothing does). Everyone else operates within this framework, even if they emphasize continuity (Smith) or distinctiveness (Cross) more.

**Alternative reading:** No credible alternative — this is the consensus methodological position.

## Assessment

This is a mature methodological statement. It's also a corrective to how Cross is sometimes read — Lewis shows that Cross himself was arguing for both continuity AND novelty, not just continuity. The "configuration" approach is genuinely useful and avoids the sterile "was Israel unique?" debate.
