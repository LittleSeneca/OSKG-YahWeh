# smith-obm-1.9: Divine conflict mythology encodes power, powerlessness, and hope — Mendenhall's political reading is incomplete

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch1 — Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 1

## The Claim

Smith modifies Mendenhall's dictum that "dualistic mythology is always essentially political." He argues that divine conflict narratives can express political mastery (Enuma Elish = Babylon's power, Genesis 1 = inverse of Judah's weakness) but also political weakness, desperation, and hope. "Hope then seems to be the key trope in these texts."

## Evidence

- **Mendenhall's claim:** polytheistic mythologies of power reflect political realities.
- **Smith's modifications:** Psalm 74:12-17 "appeals to divine strength at a moment of perceived divine weakness or indifference." Yahweh's mastery is "not fully realized; for ancient Israelites, this divine dominion is in fact a debatable matter."
- **Isaiah 27:1:** prospect of divine victory held out for future — Yahweh "has not yet exercised complete mastery over the cosmos."
- **Isaiah 51:9-11:** power of Yahweh over cosmic enemy proclaimed "as an expression of hope and herald to human weakness at the time of exile."
- **Genesis 1's divine mastery** stands "in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)" — composed against backdrop of foreign empires imposing power on Judah.
- **Baal's lack of definitive victory:** needs help from other deities, no definitive victory over Sea and Death — also a form of encoded powerlessness.
- "Divine power can encode human power as well as human powerlessness. This, too, is a political statement that a community retains a sort of control over its identity despite its political powerlessness."
- These mythologies are ultimately not "dualistic" — "various lines subtly connect the deities and their blessing and the divine monsters. No hero is great without a great enemy to defeat."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The observation that conflict myths serve different political functions in different contexts is well-taken. The "hope" reading of exilic texts is standard. The claim about Genesis 1's composition under imperial pressure is plausible but not provable from the text alone.

## Stakes

This claim pushes back against a reductive political reading of mythology, arguing that divine conflict narratives are more flexible — they can be tools of imperial propaganda, resistance literature, or expressions of communal hope. It also challenges the clean separation between "polytheistic" and "monotheistic" conflict narratives, noting that both can encode power or powerlessness.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that creation-by-conflict always serves political-legitimation functions (the king = the divine warrior). Also those who see Genesis 1 as a product of the Persian period's relatively stable conditions rather than imperial pressure.

**Alternative reading:** The "hope" reading may impose a modern existential category on ancient texts that were primarily liturgical and doxological. Psalm 74 is a lament — its recollection of past divine victory functions as accusation ("you did this before, why not now?") as much as hope. The emotional register may be protest rather than hopeful expectation.

## Assessment

Smith's modification of Mendenhall is sensible and necessary — Mendenhall's claim was always too blunt. The best part is the observation about Genesis 1: "divine mastery stands in inverse relation to the political status of the text's author(s)" — this is a genuinely illuminating principle that applies well beyond Genesis. The closing reflection that these mythologies are "ultimately not dualistic" because hero and monster are mutually constitutive is philosophically rich but somewhat tangential to the chapter's main argument.
