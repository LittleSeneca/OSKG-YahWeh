# smith-ehg-3.2: The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to the asherah symbol, not the goddess

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 3 — Yahweh and Asherah]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (1990, rev. 2002), Chapter 3: Yahweh and Asherah

## The Claim

The 8th-century BCE inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud reading "Yahweh of Samaria/Teman and his asherah" (*lyhwh... wl'šrth*) most naturally refer to a cult symbol belonging to Yahweh, not to a goddess named Asherah who was his consort. The grammar supports "his asherah" (the symbol) over "his Asherah" (the goddess).

Smith's own words: "If l'šrth refers to the goddess... then it is unclear what 'his Asherah' means. Only by assuming an ellipsis of 'his consort, Asherah'... does this interpretation make reasonable sense." If it means "his asherah" (the symbol), then it straightforwardly means something that belongs to Yahweh.

## Evidence

### The Grammar Debate

- **Possessive suffix on a divine name is unusual in Hebrew:** "Divine names do not appear in Hebrew with a pronominal suffix." The suffix on *'šrth* indicates it is a common noun (a cult object), not a personal name (a goddess).
- **Counter acknowledged — Ugaritic has examples:** Ugaritic texts show divine names with suffixes (*'aṯrty*, *'nth*), so the grammar argument is not airtight. Smith concedes this.
- **Semantic argument:** "If l'šrth refers to the goddess... then it is unclear what 'his Asherah' means." "His asherah" (something that belongs to Yahweh) makes better sense than "his Asherah" (which requires an implied ellipsis — "his consort, Asherah").

### McCarter's Hypostasis Theory

The asherah could be a hypostasis of Yahweh (like "the Name of Baal" or "the Face of Baal" in Phoenician) — a personified divine attribute, not a separate goddess. This would explain the possessive suffix without requiring a goddess consort.

### Etymological Critiques

Proposals to translate *'šrth* as "sanctuary," "consort," "goddess," or "trace" lack etymological basis in Hebrew. The word means what it looks like: "his asherah."

### Contextual Support

- The texts are Israelite, not foreign: they use Hebrew vowel letters (*matres lectionis*), not Phoenician conventions
- The pottery is Judean
- Kuntillet Ajrud was likely a royal Judean way-station

## Confidence

**Rating:** MEDIUM

**Rationale:** The grammatical argument is strong but not decisive (Ugaritic counter-examples exist). The semantic argument ("his X" makes more sense for a possession than a consort) is genuinely compelling but not a proof. This claim would need support from [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] (smith-ehg-3.1) to be coherent — if the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol throughout the monarchy, Smith's reading of Kuntillet Ajrud gains plausibility.

## Stakes

This is the crux of the "did God have a wife?" question. If Smith is right, the answer is no — the asherah was Yahweh's symbol, not his consort, and Kuntillet Ajrud is evidence of Yahwistic cult practice, not divine marriage. If the majority is right, Yahweh had a wife, and Israelite religion included a divine couple. The difference is enormous — arguably the single most explosive question in the field of Israelite religion.

This claim also affects how we read the Deuteronomistic reforms. If the asherah was a symbol (Smith), the reformers were destroying elements of their own Yahwistic tradition. If it was a goddess (majority), they were suppressing genuine polytheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The majority of scholars read Kuntillet Ajrud as evidence for Asherah as Yahweh's consort:

- **[[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (romer-inv-9.3):** Römer argues the possessive suffix on *'šrth* is most naturally read as referring to the goddess. The inscriptions are "unambiguous evidence that Asherah was paired with Yhwh."
- **[[claim-dever-archaeology-proves-asherah-real-goddess-yhwh-consort]]:** Dever reads the inscriptions as unambiguous evidence that Asherah was Yhwh's consort, supported by 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines.
- **[[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-and-el-qom-prove-consort]] (day-ygc-2.x):** Day's reading aligns with the consort interpretation.

Other scholars who disagree: Olyan, Zevit, Ackerman, Hadley, Keel/Uehlinger.

**Scholars who partially agree with Smith:** Lang, Miller, Tigay, Winter, Frevel, Korpel, Cross, McCarter (hypostasis theory), Emerton (cautious).

**Alternative reading:** The suffix on *'šrth* is a genuine possessive ("his Asherah"), and the goddess was Yahweh's consort by virtue of the Yahweh-El identification — El's consort became Yahweh's consort. The Ugaritic parallels for divine names with suffixes show this is grammatically possible, even if unusual in Hebrew. See [[claim-asherah-was-el-consort-transferred-to-yahweh]] (romer-inv-9.1).

## Edges

**Depends on:**
- [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] (smith-ehg-3.1) — Smith's reading of Kuntillet Ajrud only makes sense if the asherah was already a Yahwistic cult symbol. If the asherah was always a goddess (as the majority holds), Smith's reading loses its foundation.

**Contradicts:**
- [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (romer-inv-9.3) — Römer reads the SAME inscription and reaches the OPPOSITE conclusion. These two claims cannot both be correct.
- [[claim-dever-archaeology-proves-asherah-real-goddess-yhwh-consort]] — Dever's reading directly contradicts Smith's.

**Challenged by:**

## Assessment

Smith has the better **grammatical** argument (possessive suffix on a personal name IS unusual in Hebrew), but the majority has the better **cumulative** case (the biblical polemic, the iconography, the Ugaritic background all point toward a goddess). I'd flag this as: grammar favors Smith, everything else favors the majority. Reasonable scholars can disagree. The evidence is genuinely ambiguous.

Smith's reading depends heavily on [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] (smith-ehg-3.1), which is his strongest claim (confidence: HIGH). If that holds, his Kuntillet Ajrud reading is at least defensible. But even if the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol, the inscriptions could still refer to a goddess — the symbol and the goddess aren't mutually exclusive.

After reading Römer ([[Romer — Chapter 9 — Yhwh and His Asherah]]) and Dever ([[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]]), the cumulative case for the goddess reading is stronger than Smith allows. The inscriptions, the figurines, the Ugaritic background, and the biblical polemic converge. Smith's skepticism — while methodologically admirable — increasingly looks like it's fighting the weight of the evidence.

The unresolved question is WHY the Deuteronomists condemned the asherah so violently if it was just a symbol. Smith's explanation (secondary confusion with Astarte, offense at divinatory/healing functions) is weak. The simpler explanation — that the symbol represented a goddess, and the polemicists knew it — fits the evidence better.
