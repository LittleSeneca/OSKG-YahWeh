# keel-ggi-excursus.4: "Goddess or woman?" is a false binary — plaques portray the goddess as a woman and the woman as the goddess

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Excursus — Astarte Plaques]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Excursus §§57-60

## The Claim

"The modern question about whether a figure represents a goddess or a woman, posing strict alternatives, is too short-sighted. The plaques portray the goddess as a woman, and conversely they identify the female worshipers above the plaques with the goddess." The goddess lacks attributes that distinguish major deities (crown, relational iconography) — she looks like an ordinary woman. The cuneiform inscription "woman" (sinnistu) on the mold bridges divine and human. The smaller female figures on shrine plaques mirror the goddess's posture. The goddess IS "an exemplary woman" — not a distant cosmic power but a model of embodied female experience.

## Evidence

- The goddess lacks attributes that distinguish major deities — she looks like an ordinary woman
- The cuneiform inscription "woman" (sinnistu) on the mold is used "as an epithet for Ishtar and other goddesses" in Akkadian texts — the word bridges divine and human
- Smaller female figures on shrine plaques (worshipers/donors) mirror the goddess's posture and appearance
- "It may be that the women pay for the cost of the plaque as a votive gift or they might keep this image in the home in hopes of a blessing"
- The goddess IS "an exemplary woman" — not a distant cosmic power but a model of embodied female experience

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is an interpretive claim about ancient mentality that can't be proven from artifacts alone. But it's consistent with the evidence and avoids the anachronistic either/or thinking that plagues much figurine scholarship.

## Stakes

This dissolves the debate between those who say "these are goddesses" (Pritchard, Winter, Dever) and those who say "these are mortal women/votive figures" (Tadmor, H. Weippert). They're BOTH — the goddess embodied in female form, the woman identifying with the goddess. The boundary between divine and human is porous in a way that modern categories struggle to capture.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Both sides of the goddess-vs-mortal debate. Tadmor's camp would say they ARE mortal. Dever's camp would say they're clearly goddesses. Keel & Uehlinger are saying the question is badly framed.

**Alternative reading:** The "both/and" reading might be too clever by half — a way to avoid choosing between interpretations that have real scholarly consequences. If you can't tell a goddess from a worshiper, then the figurines can't be used as evidence for EITHER.

## Assessment

This is one of the most sophisticated moves in the book. It connects to the "transparency" concept from Ch VII — the idea that symbols can carry divine presence without depicting it anthropomorphically. Here, the goddess is present IN the woman's body, not distinct FROM it. This is a fundamentally different model of divine presence than the "great god on throne" model of official religion.
