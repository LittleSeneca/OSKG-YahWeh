# keel-ggi-1.1: The debate about Yahweh's consort cannot be resolved from texts alone — iconographic evidence is the missing piece

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch I — Starting Point and Points of Departure]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter I

## The Claim

The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions ("Yahweh... and his Asherah") reignited the question of whether preexilic Israel was polytheistic and whether Yahweh had a divine consort. But "anyone who wants to study actual primary sources must focus on the treasure trove of iconographic and epigraphic documents provided by archaeological research in Palestine/Israel." Biblical texts are "difficult to interpret historically" and the scholarly debate has been driven by "minority opinion expressed in the religious literature preserved in the Old Testament" rather than material evidence.

## Evidence

- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions published in 1978 sparked new debate about monotheism's origins (Lang 1981, Haag 1985)
- Feminist theological research intensified the Asherah question — the debate has direct implications for "the status of women in both synagogue and church"
- Scholars like Lang, Weippert, and Knauf assert preexilic polytheism as consensus, but their evidence is almost entirely textual
- The biblical texts are late, polemical, and represent a "minority opinion" — not recoverable popular religion

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is a statement of the problem, not a factual claim. The gap they identify is real and demonstrable — every existing treatment of the Yahweh/Asherah question relies primarily on texts, and the few that use archaeology (Dever, Holladay) ignore seal amulets.

## Stakes

If they're right, then the entire existing debate (Smith vs. Dever, monotheism's dating, Asherah's status as goddess vs. symbol) has been conducted with one hand tied behind its back. The iconographic evidence could shift every major conclusion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Not exactly "disagreement" — more like neglect. Smith (1990, 2001) acknowledges the "missing puzzle pieces" but doesn't systematically incorporate iconography. Dever uses SOME archaeological evidence (figurines, cult stands) but not seal amulets systematically. Tigay (1986) used onomastic evidence and mentioned iconography in an appendix — they see their work as an expansion of his approach.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that iconography is MORE ambiguous than texts, not less — a seal image doesn't come with a caption explaining what the wearer believed. Keel and Uehlinger address this in their constellation methodology.

## Assessment

This is the single strongest framing move in the book. They don't just say "we have new data"; they say "the existing debate is structurally incomplete because it's missing an entire category of evidence." This framing has been vindicated — the book is now cited extensively precisely because it fills this gap.
