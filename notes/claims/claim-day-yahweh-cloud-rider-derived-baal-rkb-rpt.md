# day-ygc-4.5: Yahweh-as-cloud-rider derives from Baal's epithet rkb ʻrpt

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 4 — Yahwehs Appropriation of Baal Imagery]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 4

## The Claim

Yahweh riding a cloud chariot in Ps 104:3 and elsewhere "ultimately derives from Baal, whose stock epithet, *rkb ʻrpt*... may be accepted with the majority of scholars to mean 'rider of the clouds'" (pp. 91-92). However, the widely-held equation of Ps 68:5's *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* with *rkb ʻrpt* is probably wrong — *ʻărābâ* means "desert," and Ps 68:5 should be read "rider through the deserts."

## Evidence

**For Baal origin:**
1. De Moor: The name Be-'-li-ra-kab-bi "Baal of the chariot" is known from Samʻal, and Ramesses III compared himself to Baal driving a chariot (p. 93)
2. Ugaritic *rkb* elsewhere always means "ride, mount" — not "gather" (p. 93)
3. OT texts associating *rkb* with clouds: Deut 33:26; Ps 18:11; 68:34; 104:3; Isa 19:1; 66:16 — "since the Israelites were far more intimately related to the Canaanites than the Greeks, the Old Testament parallels involving rkb are far more relevant" than Zeus epithets (p. 93)
4. Hab 3:8: Yahweh rides horses/chariot of victory against the sea — Zeus defeating Typhon also mounts a winged horse, the conflict taking place at Mt Casius (Zaphon). This motif "is ultimately derived from Baal mythology" (pp. 94-95)
5. KTU² 1.5.V.6b-11: Mot commands Baal to descend to the underworld taking "your clouds, your wind, your chariot team (*mdl*), your rain" — Day argues *mdl* = "chariot team" drawing the cloud chariot (pp. 95-96)

**Against Ps 68:5 = cloud rider:**
- *ʻărābâ* in Hebrew means "desert." "It is a sound principle that if a Hebrew word makes good sense in its normally attested meaning, it should be accepted" rather than creating a hapax legomenon (pp. 93-94)
- Context fits: vv. 8-9 echo wilderness traditions, v. 7 refers to "the wilderness" (p. 94)
- Isa 40:3 has *bāʻărābâ mesillâ* "a highway in the desert" — using *sll* (same root as Ps 68:5's *sollû*), supporting the "desert" reading (p. 94)
- Nevertheless, "it is still likely that Yahweh is here conceived as riding on a cloud... through the deserts" (p. 94). Day even suggests *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* "may be a deliberate distortion of the epithet *rkb ʻrpt*" (p. 94)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Baal origin of cloud-rider imagery is well-supported by multiple converging lines of evidence. Day's position on Ps 68:5 is admirably restrained — he defends the minority conservative reading while acknowledging the cloud-riding context.

## Stakes

"Rider of the clouds" was one of Baal's most characteristic epithets. Its transfer to Yahweh is one of the clearest cases of direct mythological appropriation in the OT. Daniel 7:13 ("one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven") is the apocalyptic endpoint of this same tradition — the cloud-rider imagery runs from Baal through the Psalms and prophets to the New Testament.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who accept the emendation of Ps 68:5 to *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* = "rider of the clouds" (majority position). Ullendorff/Brock's "gatherer" reading (minority).

**Alternative reading:** Ps 68:5 should be emended to *rōkēb bāʻărābôt* = "rider of the clouds," matching Baal's *rkb ʻrpt* exactly. The cloud-rider motif in the OT is independent of Canaanite influence — it's common ancient Near Eastern storm-god imagery.

## Assessment

Day's restraint on Ps 68:5 is characteristic — he doesn't let his enthusiasm for Baal parallels override sound philology. The cloud-chariot-horses chain (Ps 104 → Hab 3 → Zeus/Typhon → Baal) is well-constructed. His *mdl* = "chariot team" reading is tentative but plausible. The suggestion that Ps 68:5 may be a deliberate distortion of *rkb ʻrpt* is intriguing — it would mean the psalmist knew the Baal epithet and transformed it, rather than simply borrowing it. Day consistently favors this "transformation" model over crude borrowing.
