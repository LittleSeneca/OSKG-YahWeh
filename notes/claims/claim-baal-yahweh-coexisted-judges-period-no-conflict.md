# smith-ehg-1.2: Baal and Yahweh coexisted without conflict in the Judges period; the conflict was a ninth-century development retrojected backward.

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2nd ed., 2002), Chapter 1

## The Claim

The Deuteronomistic editors of Judges (8th century or later) projected the ninth-century Baal-Yahweh conflict backward into the Judges period. In the Judges period itself, Baal and Yahweh probably coexisted without major tension. The Baal-names in Saul's Yahwistic family (Eshbaal, Meribbaal, alongside Jonathan) suggest *ba'al* could function as a title for Yahweh or that Baal was acceptable in Yahwistic circles.

## Evidence

- Judges' redaction postdates 722 BCE (Judges 18:30 mentions "the captivity of the land"). The anti-Baal polemic in Judges 2-3 is part of a literary framework imposed centuries later.
- Saul's family had BOTH Yahwistic names (Jonathan) AND Baal names (Eshbaal, Meribbaal). "Why would a Yahwistic family give Baal names, if Baal were a god inimical to Yahweh?"
- Later scribes altered these names: Eshbaal → Ishboshet, Meribbaal → Mephiboshet, Jerubbaal → Jerubbeshet (substituting *bôšet*, "shame," for *ba'al*). The alterations prove the names became embarrassing — they were NOT originally so.
- The name *bĕ'alyāh* ("Yah is Lord") and the seal *ywb'l* ("Yaw is Lord") suggest *ba'al* could simply mean "Lord" and function as a title for Yahweh.
- "Baal was probably not the threat in the Judges period or the tenth century that later tradents considered him. It was the traumatic events of the ninth century and afterwards that shaped the perspective of the tradents."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH on the redaction dating (Judges finalized after 722 is widely accepted). MEDIUM on the coexistence claim — it explains the data neatly but requires treating the Judges narratives as unreliable for religious history while using them for political history. There's a selectivity problem.

## Stakes

If Smith is right, the biblical picture of Israel constantly "falling into" Baal worship during the Judges period is a theological construct, not history. The "cycles of apostasy" in Judges are literary, not historical. This undermines the Bible's own explanation for why Israel needed kings and prophets.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives would argue the Judges narratives preserve genuine historical memory of Baal conflict. The Baal-names could reflect syncretism (some Israelites had already compromised) rather than peaceful coexistence. The redaction dating doesn't prove the content is fabricated — editors can faithfully transmit older traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The Baal-names in Saul's family might reflect exactly what the Bible says: some Israelites (even royal families) had apostatized. The names are evidence of the problem, not evidence that there was no problem. Smith's reading requires the biblical narrative to be systematically misleading — which is possible but needs more evidence than he provides here.

## Assessment

Smith's strongest point is the name alterations (Eshbaal → Ishboshet). You don't censor what was never there. The alterations prove Baal-names existed in early Israelite tradition and later became unacceptable. But Smith's conclusion that this means "peaceful coexistence" goes beyond the evidence. The names could reflect genuine syncretism that the later editors correctly identified as problematic. Smith is extracting a specific conclusion (no conflict) from evidence that supports a range of possibilities.
