# keel-ggi-6.5: The Aniconic Turn — Judahite Name Seals Become Overwhelmingly Image-Free in the 7th Century

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VI — Iron Age IIC — Astralization and Orthodox Reaction]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VI

## The Claim

"The Judahite name seals of Iron Age IIC show a clear tendency toward aniconicity, shown in the way that iconographic decorations are limited to vegetal and ornamental elements or are avoided altogether." This is the material correlate of the "orthodox reaction." While pillar figurines flourish in domestic contexts, the administrative elite — the people using seals — abandon figurative imagery. Over 300 bullae from 7th-6th century Jerusalem show ZERO anthropomorphic deities and virtually no astral symbols.

## Evidence

- Over 80 impressions of private seals on vessel handles from late 8th/early 7th centuries — two-line name inscriptions without iconographic elements
- "Both the site distribution and dating of the context for each find show that this is a typical phenomenon in Judah, one that began at the time of Hezekiah"
- The Ophel archive: 55 bullae, only 4 with iconic decorations — and those are minor (a bird, a griffin, a ladder motif, one crescent moon standard)
- The "Burnt Archive": 255 bullae from 211 different seals — "only 13 have substantive iconographic motifs"
- "To date, neither an anthropomorphic image of a deity nor an astral symbol has appeared on even one single bulla of the more than 300 examples from the seventh and sixth centuries"
- Hebrew name seals are approximately 70% aniconic — compared to ~30% for Ammonite seals, an even more dramatic contrast when considering the total numbers
- Two-sided seals show the pattern consciously: the owner's name on one side (for sealing documents "orthodoxly"), a deity image on the reverse (for personal amulet use)
- On aniconic seals, decoration is limited to: double lines separating name registers, ladder-shaped bands, rosettes with buds — all ornamental/vegetal, never figural

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The statistical pattern across multiple archives and hundreds of seals is overwhelming. This is not a fluke of preservation.

## Stakes

This is the archaeological footprint of the Deuteronomistic reform BEFORE Deuteronomy was written. The aniconic impulse was not a post-exilic theological invention — it was an elite cultural practice in 7th-century Jerusalem, visible in the most mundane administrative artifacts. This connects directly to Romer's argument about the missing statue of Yahweh: the aniconic trend among the elite meant that even if there WAS a statue in the Temple (Manasseh may have put one there), the administrative class had already shifted to text-based, image-free religious expression.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the aniconic turn to the exile or later. H. Weippert attributed the aniconic seals to "growing literacy" — which Keel & Uehlinger reject as insufficient explanation, given that neighboring cultures with comparable literacy did NOT show the same aniconic tendency.

**Alternative reading:** The aniconic seals could reflect practical bureaucratic standardization rather than theological conviction — administrators used simpler seals because they were faster to produce and easier to read.

## Assessment

The bureaucratic explanation fails because (a) neighboring bureaucracies did not go aniconic, (b) the aniconic trend appears alongside other evidence of religious reform (Ketef Hinnom amulets, Josiah's reforms), and (c) the two-sided seals show the owners were consciously managing the tension between amulet function (image) and sealing function (name-only). This was a deliberate religious choice, not just administrative convenience.
