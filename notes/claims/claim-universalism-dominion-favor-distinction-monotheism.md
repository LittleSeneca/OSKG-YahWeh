# kaufmann-ri-4.4: Universalism distinction — dominion vs. favor

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IV — The Religion of the People]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter IV

## The Claim

"The view that the early popular religion was polytheistic or monolatristic... finds its chief support in certain passages... that appear to restrict YHWH's domain to his land. It is assumed that only later under the influence of literary prophecy were the narrow bounds of the popular religion broken... The error of this view arises out of the failure to distinguish adequately between the various meanings of religious universalism." Universalism has TWO meanings: (1) the deity's DOMINION is worldwide, or (2) the deity's FAVOR and SELF-REVELATION are worldwide. "Since monotheism asserts that there is but one creator and ruler of the universe, it is perforce universalistic in the first sense. But there is no inner necessity that compels it to distribute the favor of the one God equally among all men."

## Evidence

- Paganism made the same distinction: Marduk is cosmic sun-god, yet particularly god of Babylon. Jupiter is sky-god, particularly connected with Rome
- Pentateuch and historical books "consistently represent the world as divided into two realms, Israel and the nations, with Israel alone 'the portion of YHWH'"
- Deuteronomy asserts YHWH "allotted" worship of the host of heaven to the heathen (Deut 4:19) — in a monotheistic context
- Jonah: YHWH is "God of heaven, who made sea and dry land" and rules sea, wind, fish, plants — yet Jonah flees "from the presence of YHWH" meaning from the area of divine REVELATION, not from YHWH's power
- David's complaint about banishment "from the land of YHWH" echoed in sources "whose monotheistic outlook is unimpeachable" — Deuteronomist's threat of exile (Deut 4:28; 28:64), Jeremiah (16:13)
- Priestly writings, "uniformly conceded to be monotheistic," maintain territorial limitation: "The exclusive sanctity of the land of Israel is as fundamental a part of the priestly laws as the election of the people"
- Josh 22: Transjordanian tribes fear losing "portion" in YHWH outside the land
- Psalm 137: a Levite exile, "surely no polytheist," refuses to sing YHWH's songs on alien soil
- Naaman (II Kings 5:15ff.): recognizes "there is no God in all the earth except in Israel," yet takes two mules' burden of earth back to Aram — "His monotheism is absolute" but territorial limitation remains in cultic sphere
- Jephthah's "Chemosh your god" (Judg 11:24) is diplomatic language: "Jephthah merely uses language that the king will understand"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The conceptual distinction is genuinely clarifying, supported by many biblical texts, and resolves apparent tensions without special pleading.

## Stakes

Whether passages that seem to limit YHWH territorially (Gen 35:2ff.; Judg 11:24; I Sam 26:19; II Kings 5:17) are evidence of early polytheism/monolatry or evidence of monotheism with national cultic limitation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-yhwh-originated-southern-deserts-edom-seir]] — Römer reads Jephthah's Chemosh statement as reflecting a period when Israelites genuinely acknowledged other national gods. [[claim-baal-yahweh-coexisted-judges-period-no-conflict]] — Smith sees territorial limitation as evidence early YHWH worship was functionally monolatrous.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's distinction is real, but he may be reading later theological sophistication back into earlier texts. Naaman, Jonah, and the Deuteronomist ALL reflect relatively late theological reflection. Early Israelite religion WAS genuinely monolatrous (YHWH rules Israel, other gods rule other nations), and later monotheistic theology reinterpreted this as limitation of cult not dominion.

## Assessment

Kaufmann at his best — a genuinely illuminating conceptual distinction that resolves apparent tensions in the biblical text. His reading of Jonah is particularly good: Jonah's flight "from the presence of YHWH" can't mean flight from YHWH's power (the whole book is about YHWH's universal power), so it must mean flight from YHWH's revelation/commission. However, I'm skeptical early Israelites made this distinction as cleanly as Kaufmann does. The conceptual sophistication required is considerable. More likely: texts reflect a DEVELOPMENT from genuine monolatry to full monotheism, with the fully monotheistic version retrojected onto earlier periods.
