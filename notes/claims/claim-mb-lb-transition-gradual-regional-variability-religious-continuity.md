# keel-ggi-p2.1: The MB→LB transition was gradual and regionally variable — multiple regional subsystems, not a single "Canaanite religion"

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

"Much like the way the Early Bronze Age has been too neatly demarcated from the Middle Bronze Age, the traditional way of separating the Middle Bronze Age from the Late Bronze Age is too rigid." There was a transitional period of 100-150 years (LB I, ca. 1550-1450) between the golden age of MB culture and full Egyptian rule, and "this transition obviously did not take place in all parts of the country at the same time." Some regions (southern coastal plain, Beth-Shean) Egyptianized earlier and more intensively; others (Hazor) remained under northern Syrian influence throughout.

## Evidence

- Destruction of MB cities and hill-country encampments began gradually in the 16th century, not all at once
- The period comes to a "closure of sorts only when Tuthmosis III was victorious over a coalition of Canaanite princes near Megiddo in 1457"
- Hazor's four-phase Temple H shows "a gentle and actually unbroken transition from the Middle Bronze Age to Late Bronze Age" with near-identical floor plans across rebuilds
- Middle Bronze Age votive offerings (engraved goddess figures with silver/bronze overlay) survived into LB strata at Hazor — reused across temple phases
- The transitional period produces distinctive new artifact types, notably pear-shaped gold pendants of the vegetation goddess (replacing earlier silver/bronze leaf pendants)
- The four-city comparison (Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, Beth-Shean) reveals different regional patterns

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The four-city comparative method itself validates this: each tells a different story. Chronological anchors (Tuthmosis III, Ramesses II, Ramesses III) are well-established.

## Stakes

If the LB transition was gradual and regionally variable, then religious change was too — there is no single "Canaanite religion" at any one moment, but multiple regional subsystems. This complicates any attempt to reconstruct a unified pre-Israelite pantheon from texts (as Smith and de Moor do). It also means that whatever "Israelite religion" emerged in Iron Age I did so against a background of regional diversity, not a monolithic Canaanite polytheism. The religion of the central hill country was likely its own regional subsystem, not simply a modified version of coastal Canaanite religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Older scholarship tended to treat archaeological periods as clean breaks. Albright's conquest model assumed sharp transitions. More recent archaeologists generally agree on gradualism, but the religious implications are rarely drawn out.

**Alternative reading:** One could argue the four-city diversity is an artifact of preservation bias rather than genuine regional difference. But the patterns Keel & Uehlinger identify (northern vs. southern, Egyptian vs. indigenous) are robust enough to survive that critique.
