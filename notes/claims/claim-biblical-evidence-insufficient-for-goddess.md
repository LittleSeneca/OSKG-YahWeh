# smith-ehg-3.3: The biblical evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess in monarchic Israel

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 3 — Yahweh and Asherah]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (1990, rev. 2002), Chapter 3: Yahweh and Asherah

## The Claim

The biblical passages usually cited as evidence for Asherah as a goddess are either (a) late polemical constructions, (b) references to the cult symbol rather than a deity, or (c) references to Astarte misidentified as Asherah. "There is no clear reference to the goddess in the Bible, apart from 1 Kings 18:19, possibly a polemic against Astarte."

## Evidence

### Smith's Evaluation of Each Passage

| Passage | Traditional Reading | Smith's Counter |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **1 Kings 18:19** ("prophets of Asherah") | Asherah was a goddess with prophets in Israel | Asherah is not attested in ANY Tyrian/Phoenician text. This is probably a polemic against Astarte, mislabeled as Asherah. Possibly a secondary gloss. |
| **2 Kings 21:7** ("image of the asherah") | *Pesel* (image/idol) implies a goddess statue | *Pesel* could refer to an elaborate form of the asherah pole, not necessarily an anthropomorphic statue. |
| **2 Kings 23:4** (asherah between "the baal" and "host of heaven") | Listed between two deities, therefore a deity | Cultic objects can be made FOR a symbol just as for a deity. Verse 6 describes the SAME asherah being dragged out and burned — it's a wooden pole, not a statue. |
| **Judges 3:7** ("the asherahs") | Plural goddesses | Generic term for foreign goddesses, paired with "the baals." Interchangeable with "the astartes" in Judges 2:13 — this shows generic usage, not a specific goddess. |
| **Jeremiah 2:27** (tree = father, stone = mother) | Asherah as consort of Yahweh (Olyan) or Baal (others) | The maternal language could apply to Yahweh himself (Deut 32:18 uses birth imagery for Yahweh). Not necessarily a goddess reference. |
| **Genesis 49:25** (*šādayim wārāḥam*) | Goddess epithets | Possibly early goddess language, but refers to El's consort, not Yahweh's — and was NOT applied to Yahweh in later tradition. |

### Additional Negative Evidence

- No personal names with Asherah as theophoric element (contrast El, Baal, Yahweh, even Anat)
- Prophetic and legal condemnations never refer to the goddess — only the symbol
- "Asherah was not a Phoenician or Punic goddess during the Iron Age" — so 1 Kings 18:19's Tyrian context doesn't fit

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** Smith is at his most skeptical — and his most vulnerable. The majority of scholars he otherwise agrees with reject his conclusion here.

## Stakes

This is the "did God have a wife?" question. If Smith is right that the biblical evidence is insufficient, then the case for Asherah as a goddess in monarchic Israel collapses — it relies entirely on extra-biblical inscriptions that are themselves ambiguous. If Smith is wrong, the biblical text itself attests to goddess worship alongside Yahweh.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone. Day (see [[claim-asherah-goddess-in-ot-passages]]) identifies five passages where "Asherah" must denote the goddess. Römer (see [[claim-bible-deliberately-obscures-yhwh-asherah-link]]) argues the Bible's obscuring of the Yhwh-Asherah link is evidence the link was real. Dever reads the passages as attesting a goddess.

**Scholars who partially agree with Smith:** Lang, Miller, Tigay, Winter, Frevel, Korpel, Cross, McCarter (hypostasis theory), Emerton (cautious).

**Alternative reading:** Smith is right that each individual passage is ambiguous. But the CUMULATIVE case is stronger than any single passage. When you have an asherah pole next to Yahweh's altar, inscriptions saying "Yahweh and his asherah," biblical polemic treating the asherah as a deity, and Ugaritic background with Asherah as El's consort — the simplest explanation is that Israel inherited the goddess along with the god El. Smith's alternative (the symbol lost its goddess connection early) requires more steps and more speculation.

## Assessment

Smith is probably wrong here, but his caution is methodologically instructive. He's applying the same skepticism to the goddess claim that conservatives apply to the polytheism claim: "the evidence isn't sufficient to prove it." The difference is that Smith applies skepticism EVEN WHEN the conclusion would help his larger argument. That's intellectual honesty. But intellectual honesty doesn't make him right — and the cumulative case for Asherah as a goddess is stronger than Smith allows.

After reading Römer ([[Romer — Chapter 9 — Yhwh and His Asherah]]) and Day ([[Day — Chapter 2 — Yahweh and Asherah]]), the counter-case is substantial. Smith's reading of individual passages is technically defensible but collectively strains credibility. The simplest explanation — that the biblical authors knew Asherah was a goddess and were polemicizing against her — fits the evidence better than Smith's "confusion with Astarte" theory.
