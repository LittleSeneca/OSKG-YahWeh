# keel-ggi-6.4: No Evidence for a Partner/Consort Relationship Between Yahweh and Asherah

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VI — Iron Age IIC — Astralization and Orthodox Reaction]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VI

## The Claim

"Was the female form of Asherah in seventh-century Jerusalem the partner/consort of Yahweh? As attractive as such a conclusion might be, it is difficult to support. Even now, we have no clear evidence for a strict partner/consort relationship between Yahweh and Asherah." Despite the massive increase in goddess imagery during Iron IIC — the pillar figurines, the "Queen of Heaven" cult, Manasseh's reported installation of an asherah image in the Temple (2 Kings 21:7) — Keel & Uehlinger maintain their position: the asherah was a mediating entity, not a consort. The goddess revival produced lots of goddesses. It did NOT produce divine couples.

## Evidence

- "There are no pictures of a pair of deities in Judah" — unlike Bronze Age seals showing divine couples (see Part I), Iron IIC Judahite iconography never pairs a male and female deity
- "The rider terra-cottas and other male terra-cottas are not in a partner relationship with the goddess" — the horse-and-rider statuettes (the "Host of Heaven" in domestic form) are found alongside pillar figurines but never grouped as couples
- "Neither biblical texts nor Iron Age IIC inscriptions offer any evidence for such a relationship"
- The seal in illus. 331b — the only clear male-female divine pair — "is not of Israelite-Judahite provenance" and "can be related only indirectly — if at all — to Yahweh and Asherah"
- "If Yahweh did at some time have a partner, it would probably have been at the time of Manasseh. Nevertheless, we have not been able so far to find any solid evidence"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The absence of divine couple imagery in Judah is a significant finding. But the "Queen of Heaven" cult described in Jeremiah 7 and 44 — where women bake cakes for the goddess and pour out libations — represents independent goddess worship, regardless of whether she was officially "married" to Yahweh.

## Stakes

The popular narrative — "Yahweh had a wife named Asherah!" — collapses under this evidence. There WAS a powerful female divine presence in 7th-century Judah. But she was not depicted as Yahweh's consort. She was worshiped independently (as "Queen of Heaven") or as a mediating entity ("his asherah"). The relationship was hierarchical (she is subordinate to him in his asherah form) or parallel (she is the Queen of Heaven, he is the Lord of Hosts), but not conjugal.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever, Romer, and most scholars who read the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions as evidence of a consort relationship. Also Manasseh's installation of an asherah in the Temple — the deuteronomistic editor clearly thought this was about a goddess, not just a cultic symbol.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of divine couple imagery could mean the couple was SO obvious it did not need depiction — or that the relationship was depicted in media that did not survive (textiles, wood). The biblical texts that pair Yahweh with the asherah polemically suggest the deuteronomists at least perceived the relationship as dangerously close.

## Assessment

Keel & Uehlinger are methodologically rigorous: if the evidence does not show divine couples, do not claim divine couples. But I think they may be holding themselves to an excessively strict standard. The pillar figurines and the "Queen of Heaven" cult represent a genuine goddess presence in Judahite religion. Whether she was "married" to Yahweh or not may be the wrong question — she was worshiped, she mattered, and she was eventually suppressed. The "consort" language may be an unhelpful imposition of human marriage categories onto divine relationships that were more fluid.
