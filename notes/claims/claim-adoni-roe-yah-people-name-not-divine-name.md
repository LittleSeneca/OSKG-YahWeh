# fleming-ybi-2.6: The personal name 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh confirms Yhw3 as a people-name, not a divine name

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 2 — Yhwʒ of Shasu-Land]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 2

## The Claim

"The personal name 'adōnī-rō'ē-yāh would reduce the plausibility of a conclusion that Yhw3 already represented the deity Yahweh... It is the people, not a god, that need a 'shepherd.' Most simply, Yhw3 is one subdivision of some association of groups" (Fleming, p. 84).

## Evidence

- Thomas Schneider (2007) published the name from New Kingdom Egypt (late 18th or 19th Dynasties, c. 1330-1230)
- The divine element in the theophoric name would be 'adōnī ("My Lord"), not yāh — like Amorite Aduna-Addu, "The Lord is Addu"
- Yāh is a hypocoristic (shortened) form of Yhw3
- The name means "My (Divine) Lord is the Shepherd of (the) Yah (People)"
- Schneider himself, in comments on Fleming's draft, agreed this reading "would suit well my interpretation of Yhw3 as a people"
- The name occurs in six variants — it wasn't a one-off

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The reading is grammatically plausible and Schneider (who discovered the name) endorses Fleming's interpretation. But it's one personal name, and alternative readings are possible. Schneider's original 2007 interpretation read it as evidence for early Yahweh worship.

## Stakes

If correct, this is the only evidence from the period between Soleb (c. 1400) and the Mesha Stele (c. 840) that bears on the divine-name-vs-people-name question. It tilts the scales toward "people."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Schneider's original (2007) reading treated Yhw3 as "a mountainous region linked to the worship of a god named Yahweh" — the opposite of Fleming's reading.

**Alternative reading:** Yāh IS a divine name (short form of Yahweh), and the name means "My Lord is the Shepherd of Yah" — evidence for early Yahweh worship, not for Yhw3-as-people.

## Assessment

The name is fascinating but can't carry much weight on its own. It's too ambiguous. Fleming's interpretation is clever but depends on his prior conclusion that Yhw3 is a people-name. The argument risks circularity: we know Yhw3 is a people because this name mentions a shepherd; we know the shepherd is for a people because Yhw3 is a people-name.
