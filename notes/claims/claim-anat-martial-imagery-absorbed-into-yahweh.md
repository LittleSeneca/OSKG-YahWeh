# smith-ehg-2.3: Anat's martial imagery was absorbed into Yahweh even though her cult never existed in Israel

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 2 — Yahweh and Baal]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2002), Chapter 2: Yahweh and Baal

## The Claim

The Canaanite warrior goddess Anat was never worshipped in Israel (no inscriptions, no cult sites, almost no personal names). But her distinctive battle imagery — gory, cannibalistic, ecstatic — appears in biblical descriptions of Yahweh. The monarchy probably mediated this transfer, using Anat's martial language (as Egyptian pharaohs did) to dramatize Yahweh's military power.

Smith's caution: "Since Anat is not attested in the Bible except in a few personal names, the lack of contact between her cult and that of Yahweh forestalls any theory of direct dependence."

## Evidence

### Anat's Battle Imagery (KTU 1.3 II)
- Fights at a mountain, battles are universal ("peoples")
- Produces heaps of corpses and skulls
- "Gleans" (harvest imagery)
- Holds a feast on flesh and blood
- Wades in gore, delights in carnage

### Biblical Parallels
- Divine battle at Zion (Ps 2, 48, Joel 4)
- Universal scope ("the nations")
- Heaps of corpses/skulls (Ps 110:6, Isa 34:2)
- Harvest imagery (Joel 3:13, Rev 14)
- Drinking blood (Isa 49:26, Deut 32:42)
- Wading in blood (Ps 58:11, 68:24)
- Divine laughter (Ps 2:4)
- Drunkenness with battle (Isa 63:3-6)

### Egyptian Mediation
- New Kingdom texts use Anat and Astarte to dramatize pharaonic prowess
- Ramses III is described with Anat and Astarte as his shield
- This provides a possible transmission route: Egyptian royal ideology → Israelite court → biblical texts

### Convergence Pattern
Some biblical passages COMBINE Anat's gore with Baal's storm (Deut 32:42-43, Ps 68:24) — suggesting convergence of MULTIPLE Canaanite divine profiles, not just borrowing from one.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The parallels are striking but direct dependence on Anat traditions specifically is unprovable; some motifs could reflect general ANE battle conventions. Smith himself is appropriately cautious.

## Stakes

Moderate. The goriest descriptions of God in the Bible (drenched in blood, drunk on slaughter, laughing at enemies) aren't unique revelations of divine wrath — they're borrowed from a Canaanite warrior goddess. This contextualizes the violence but doesn't directly challenge theological claims.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Not a major point of scholarly contention. The primary debate is whether Anat parallels are direct borrowings or share a broader ANE martial idiom.

**Alternative reading:** These battle conventions are simply how ANE cultures described divine warfare — no specific borrowing from Anat required. The Egyptian mediation theory (Smith's own suggestion) implies indirect influence rather than direct literary dependence.

The Egyptian mediation theory is interesting but speculative.
