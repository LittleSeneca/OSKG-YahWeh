# romer-inv-intro.5: The Documentary Hypothesis is obsolete — Deuteronomy dates to ~620 BCE, oldest Pentateuchal traditions to ~8th century

**Source:** [[Romer — Introduction]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Introduction

## The Claim

"Along with most European scholars, we think it is no longer possible to accept the 'documentary theory'" — the classic J, E, D, P model. However, he retains two key dates: the first version of Deuteronomy around 620 BCE, and the assumption that "the oldest traditions in the Pentateuch (about Jacob, Moses, and later Abraham) had been written down sometime around the eighth century."

## Evidence

### The scroll recopying argument
"No book, or more exactly no scroll, of the Bible was definitively formulated in one fell swoop. Papyrus and vellum are not durable materials... Their content, therefore, had to be recopied every few decades on new scrolls or sheets. Each time recopying took place, it was possible to add or suppress material, or to introduce modifications in the text."

### Dating anchors
- Deuteronomy core: ~620 BCE, linked to Josiah's reform (2 Kings 22-23)
- Oldest Pentateuchal traditions: ~8th century BCE
- This reflects the European vs. American divide: European scholarship has largely abandoned the classic four-source theory; American scholarship (and Smith) has been more reluctant.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** MEDIUM on the dating. Deuteronomy ~620 is widely accepted (linked to Josiah's reform). The 8th-century date for the oldest traditions is more contested. The rejection of the Documentary Hypothesis is the European consensus but not universally shared.

## Stakes

If the Pentateuch's oldest written traditions only go back to the 8th century, and most texts are even later, then we have NO contemporary written sources for the first 400+ years of Israelite history (1200-800 BCE). Everything we think we know about early Israelite religion is reconstructed from much later texts. The uncertainty is enormous.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** American scholars (including Smith) who retain the Documentary Hypothesis, even if cautiously. Conservatives who maintain earlier dates for Pentateuchal sources. Scholars who date the earliest traditions to the 10th century or earlier.

**Alternative reading:** The Documentary Hypothesis, while in need of modification, captures something real about the Pentateuch's compositional history. The European rejection may be premature — the theory has been modified rather than falsified. The scroll recopying argument explains textual fluidity but doesn't disprove underlying documentary sources.

## Assessment

This is a crucial difference from Smith. Smith retained the Documentary Hypothesis (cautiously, with quotation marks). Romer explicitly rejects it. This reflects the European vs. American divide in biblical scholarship. The scroll recopying argument is persuasive on its own terms — texts WERE recopied and modified — but it doesn't logically entail that no documentary sources ever existed. The 8th-century date for the oldest traditions means we're working with sources at least 400 years removed from the events they describe. That's a lot of time for memory to become invention.
