# smith-obm-1.3: Divine powers map onto spatial zones — blessing from center deities, destruction from periphery monsters

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch1 — Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"The first and fundamental division marks deities who meet human need and functions versus divinities who pose a human threat or destruction." This operates across genres: myths, letters, personal names, and ritual texts. Foreign deities can also bless — the blessing/destruction divide is spatial, not ethnic.

## Evidence

- **Letters:** Opening greetings invoke the gods (*'ilm*) to provide well-being (*slm*) and protect (*ngr*) the addressee. CAT 2.4.4-6 asks gods to strengthen (*'zz*) the addressee. CAT 2.176.4-6 invokes "the gods of Ugarit" (*'ily 'ugrt*).
- **Ritual:** CAT 1.161 invokes blessing from deceased royal ancestors, also called "god" or "divine" (*'il*) in 1.113.14-26.
- **Personal names:** According to Caquot, Ugaritic personal names express desire for health, blessing, protection, and favor from deities.
- **Narrative:** In 1.15 II, Baal asks El to confer blessing upon King Kirta. Anat defeats human enemies in 1.3 II; El supports Kirta's campaign (1.14-1.15); Horon is invoked to smash a rebellious son's head (1.16 VI).
- **Destructive powers:** Yamm connected with Kirta's household demise (1.14 I 19-20). Mot's destruction in 1.127.30-32. Monstrous forces generally do not receive cult.
- **Foreign deities:** Letters distinguish "the gods of Ugarit" (2.16.4-6), "the gods of Egypt" (*'il msrm*, 2.23.22), "the gods of Alashiya" (*'il alty*, 2.42.8). All are invoked for blessing. The divine family metaphor integrates foreign deities — Nikkal (Mesopotamian moon-goddess) wed to Yarih (West Semitic moon-god) in CAT 1.24.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The evidence is distributed across multiple genres and consistently supports the blessing/destruction dichotomy.

## Stakes

This claim establishes that Ugaritic religion was fundamentally practical — deities were evaluated by what they did for humans, not by abstract theological status. It also shows that "foreign" did not mean "hostile" — a point with implications for how Israel later reconfigures foreign gods as non-gods.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Few would dispute the descriptive claim. The more debatable point is whether "foreign deities also bless" reflects genuine theological universalism or simply diplomatic courtesy in letters sent abroad.

**Alternative reading:** The blessing/destruction binary may be too neat. Anat murders Aqhat (1.18) — Smith calls this an "exception" (human disobedience, divine punishment), but it shows deities can be destructive within the center too. The "beautiful gods" of 1.23 are ambiguous — they enter the sown and receive plenty, complicating the spatial scheme.

## Assessment

The pattern is real and well-documented, but Smith acknowledges the exceptions honestly. The foreign-deity point is important and underappreciated — it shows that Ugaritic theology had resources for recognizing the legitimacy of other peoples' gods, which makes Israel's later denial of foreign gods a more dramatic departure than often recognized.
