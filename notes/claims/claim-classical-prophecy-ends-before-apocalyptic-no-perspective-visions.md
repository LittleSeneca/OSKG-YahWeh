# kaufmann-ri-10.2: Classical prophecy ends before apocalyptic — no perspective visions exist

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"The distinctive feature of apocalypse is its anxious inquiry and research into the secrets of the cosmos. The literary prophet, on the other hand, is first of all a messenger whose task is not to reveal hidden things, but to command or reprove in the name of God." The crucial diagnostic: "Nowhere in the entire corpus of classical prophecy... can there be found a vision with historical perspective. No succession of ages and events ever unfolds before our eyes." Therefore, "classical prophecy does not gradually blend into apocalypse" and the traditional claim that prophecy ended by the mid-5th century (Malachi) is correct.

## Evidence

- In apocalypse, the visionary's chief task is "to reveal hidden things" — disclosure of secrets is "the very essence of apocalypse"
- Apocalyptic visions are of two types: cosmic (tours of unearthly realms) and historical (succession of generations/ages). "This perspective vision... is the very heart of apocalyptic"
- Classical prophets see visions of God "but never as ends in themselves; such visions are always either an introduction or an adjunct to a message"
- "There is never a succession of scenes; the vision stands alone, and is followed by the word which the prophet is charged to convey"
- Ezekiel's tours in space make him "the father of the apocalyptic tour," but even Ezekiel "is not taken into extra-mundane realms"
- The classical prophet knows "only of two ages: the kingdom of his time and the visionary kingdom of God at the end of days"
- "All the detail that a prophet may amass adds up to one picture of what will happen 'on that day'"
- The complete absence of perspective visions "testifies that the two are essentially different; no literary commingling of the two realms has taken place"
- Passages assigned to late dates (Hellenistic period) are identified only through "ingenious modern midrash" — when Daniel speaks of Greek kingdoms, "it is in express terms which require no ingenuity to interpret"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's formal distinction (prophetic static visions vs. apocalyptic panoramic visions) is genuinely clarifying and well-supported; but the absence of perspective visions doesn't necessarily prove traditional dating.

## Stakes

Whether classical prophecy is a bounded historical phenomenon (8th-5th centuries) or a fluid tradition that gradually evolved into apocalyptic (extending into the Hellenistic period). If Kaufmann is right, the sharp break between prophecy and apocalyptic supports his larger claim that Israelite religion has distinct ideological stages rather than continuous evolution.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date portions of the prophetic corpus late. The critical consensus places Isaiah 24-27 ("the Isaiah Apocalypse"), Zechariah 9-14, and Joel in the Persian or Hellenistic periods. Plöger, Hanson, and others argue for a gradual transition from prophecy to apocalyptic.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's distinction between "two ages" (prophecy) and "succession of ages" (apocalyptic) is real, but it's a spectrum, not a cliff. Some late prophetic texts (Isaiah 24-27, Zechariah 14) exhibit features that look transitional — cosmic scope, diminished historical specificity, universal judgment — even if they lack full-blown periodization. The absence of perspective visions doesn't prove these texts are early; it proves they're pre-apocalyptic in form, which is compatible with a 4th-century date.

## Assessment

One of Kaufmann's strongest literary-critical arguments. He identifies a formal feature (perspective vision) that genuinely distinguishes prophecy from apocalypse and then observes its complete absence from the prophetic corpus. This is a better argument than the standard apologetic one (tradition says so). However, his inference that "therefore the texts must be early" doesn't fully follow. A late text could still be formally prophetic rather than apocalyptic. The real test is whether the texts he defends as early show other signs of lateness — linguistic features, historical allusions, theological concepts.
