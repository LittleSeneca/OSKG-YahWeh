# smith-ehg-6.1: Israelite monotheism developed through four stages driven by political, social, and scribal factors

**Source:** [[Smith Chapters 5-7 — Cult, Monotheism, Portraits]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (1990), Chapter 6

## The Claim

Monotheism was both "evolution" (grew out of limited Israelite polytheism continuous with neighbors) AND "revolution" (the eventual rejection of all other deities distinguished Israel from its neighbors).

**The Four-Stage Model:**

| Stage | Period | Key Development |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| 1. Judges | 1200-1000 | Convergence begins. Yahweh central. El, Baal, Asherah present. Early poetry mixes imagery. |
| 2. Early Monarchy | 1000-800 | Monarchy drives convergence. Davidic covenant. Centralization. Writing emerges. Baal tolerated. |
| 3. Late Monarchy | 800-587 | Differentiation intensifies. Baal rejected. Prophetic critiques expand. Covenant theology deepens. Hezekiah and Josiah centralize. |
| 4. Exile | 587-538 | Monotheism crystallizes. Second Isaiah: "I am Yahweh, there is no other." Old family/state model collapses; new universal model emerges. |

**Key Causal Factors:**

1. **The monarchy** — both conservative (preserved old practices) and innovative (drove centralization). NOT just the villain.
2. **Writing** — made legal and prophetic norms authoritative for wider audiences. Enabled sustained theological reflection (Ezekiel, Second Isaiah).
3. **Covenant theology** — the Davidic covenant created an exclusive bond between Yahweh and the dynasty; this exclusivity eventually extended to the whole people.
4. **International crisis** — Assyrian and Babylonian pressure made local gods seem impotent. Universal empire required a universal god.
5. **Social change** — collapse of the family patrimony model enabled the rise of the individual as the primary religious unit, corresponding to a single god for the cosmos.
6. **Competition with prophecy** — necromancy competed with prophetic inquiry; condemning the dead strengthened the prophets.

## Evidence

Smith's four-stage model synthesizes evidence from the entire book. The convergence phase is demonstrated by the absorption of El (Chapter 1), Baal (Chapter 2), Asherah (Chapter 3), and solar imagery (Chapter 4) into Yahweh. The differentiation phase is demonstrated by the Deuteronomistic reforms and prophetic critiques (Chapters 2-5). The exile crystallization is demonstrated by Second Isaiah's universalizing rhetoric ("I am Yahweh, there is no other").

**Smith's critique of other models:**

- **Albright/Bright (early Mosaic monotheism):** Wrong. "Monotheism was hardly a feature of Israel's earliest history." The features they call "secondary idolatry" were indigenous Israelite heritage. The "purest form of Yahwism" belonged to the LATE monarchy, not the early period.
- **Mendenhall:** The monarchy was not the villain. It made "several religious contributions crucial to the development of monolatry."
- **Lang ("prophetic minority"):** Partially right, but the Yahweh-alone position "may not have developed as a purely prophetic position."
- **The Exile as sole cause:** The exile was crucial, but "the literary expression of monotheism at a relatively late point... 'overwrites' and obscures the long development involving the earlier phenomenon of monolatry."

**The unresolved problem:** "The development lying behind Israelite monotheism becomes impossible to trace back to the point of ancient Israel's historical appearance ca. 1200." The reasons FOR convergence remain mysterious. We can trace the path FROM convergence to monotheism, but we can't explain why convergence happened in the first place.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The broad outline has considerable explanatory power. But the causal claims (writing drove monotheism, social change enabled it) are more speculative — correlation is clearer than causation.

## Stakes

This is the core question. If Smith is right, monotheism is a human achievement — a product of political centralization, scribal culture, international crisis, and social change. It's not a revelation FROM God; it's a human construction ABOUT God. Whether God was INVOLVED in this process is a theological question Smith doesn't address. But the historical process doesn't require God as an explanation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Albright/Bright school (Mosaic monotheism from the start). Conservatives who maintain early exclusive Yahwism. Scholars who attribute more causal weight to the exile alone or to prophetic minorities.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's onomastic evidence suggests widespread popular monolatry earlier than Smith's model would predict, potentially compressing the timeline. Cross's work on El traditions complicates the convergence model. Schmid's Persian-period dating of key texts would push the literary expression of monotheism even later.

## Assessment

This is the chapter that most directly engages the "so what?" of the entire book. Smith's four-stage model is elegant and evidence-based. His honesty about what can't be explained (why convergence happened at all) is commendable. The chapter reads as a mature scholar's synthetic reflection, not a polemic.
