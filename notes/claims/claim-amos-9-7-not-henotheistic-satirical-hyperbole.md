# kaufmann-ri-11.4: Amos 9:7 and the apparent acknowledgment of other gods do not undermine Amos's monotheism

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XI — Amos and Hosea]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XI

## The Claim

Amos 9:7 ("Are you not to me like the Ethiopians, O house of Israel?") is "a satirical hyperbole." The nations' destinies are decided by YHWH — but Israel's relationship is categorically different. "For to Amos, YHWH's bringing up Israel from Egypt differed radically from his bringing up Aram from Kir. In Israel only did YHWH act as a known God, as Amos himself tells us explicitly: 'Only you have I known out of all the families of the earth...'" (3:1ff). The gentiles "are not required to know YHWH, indeed their very land is impure (7:17)." Amos 5:26 (mention of Sakkuth and Kaiwan) is "almost incidental" — idolatry goes essentially unmentioned.

## Evidence

- Amos 3:1ff explicitly asserts Israel's unique status
- Amos 2:4: YHWH's Torah and laws given to Israel alone
- Amos 2:10ff: YHWH raised up prophets and Nazirites for Israel alone
- Amos 4:6ff: YHWH chastised Israel alone to make them repent
- Amos 2:7: only in Israel can YHWH's name be profaned

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** This is where Kaufmann's reading is most strained. He needs Amos to be a monotheist who doesn't acknowledge real other gods, but Amos 9:7 and 5:26 require explanation. Calling 9:7 "satirical hyperbole" is a convenience label, not an argument.

## Stakes

If Amos genuinely acknowledges that YHWH brought other nations from their respective lands (Aram from Kir, Philistines from Caphtor) just as he brought Israel from Egypt, this suggests a henotheistic framework where each nation has its own patron deity — and YHWH is Israel's, supremely powerful but not uniquely existent. Kaufmann needs this NOT to be the case.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus reads Amos 9:7 as evidence that Amos was a henotheist, not a monotheist. The verse parallels Israel's exodus with other nations' migrations, which makes little sense in a monotheistic framework where YHWH is the only god. Römer and Smith both cite this as evidence of Amos's pre-monotheistic theology.

**Alternative reading:** Amos 9:7 IS henotheistic — but that doesn't make Amos a polytheist. Amos believes YHWH is the supreme god who controls all nations, while other nations may have their own (lesser, perhaps unreal) gods. This is consistent with the "YHWH alone" position that Smith identifies as the precursor to full monotheism. Kaufmann's insistence that Amos was already a full monotheist requires him to do violence to the text.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's weakest argument in the chapter. His treatment of Amos 9:7 as "satirical hyperbole" is hand-waving. The verse genuinely suggests that Amos conceived of YHWH's relationship with other nations as parallel to his relationship with Israel — a henotheistic or "YHWH-alone" framework, not monotheism. Kaufmann's reading of 3:1ff as the "real" Amos and 9:7 as rhetorical excess is special pleading. The two verses can be reconciled in a henotheistic framework (YHWH is supreme over all nations but has a unique covenant relationship with Israel); they CAN'T easily be reconciled in the full monotheistic framework Kaufmann needs.
