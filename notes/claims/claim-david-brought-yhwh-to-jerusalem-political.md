# romer-inv-5.2: David brought Yhwh to Jerusalem in a political masterstroke

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 5 — Entrance into Jerusalem]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 5

## The Claim

Jerusalem was originally a Canaanite city with its own deity (probably El Elyon, "God Most High" — see Genesis 14's Melchizedek, "priest of El Elyon" at Salem/Jerusalem). David conquered Jerusalem and made it his capital — a political masterstroke because it belonged to no tribe, making it neutral ground. David then brought Yhwh's ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) — a THEOLOGICAL masterstroke. He unified northern Yhwh worship (from Shiloh) with Jerusalem's El traditions under a single royal cult.

## Evidence

**Jerusalem was not originally Israelite:**
- It was a Canaanite/Jebusite city that had never been conquered by any Israelite tribe. "David conquered Jerusalem" — the Bible is explicit (2 Samuel 5:6-9).
- Jerusalem belonged to no tribe. This was its political advantage: "David's city" rather than "Judah's city" or "Benjamin's city." It could serve as a neutral capital.
- The original deity was El Elyon. Genesis 14:18-20 has Melchizedek, king of Salem, as "priest of El Elyon" — a tradition that predates Yhwh's arrival. The name "Jerusalem" (*Yĕrûšālayim*) may relate to the god Shalem, a West Semitic deity.

**David's theological synthesis:**
- **The ark transfer (2 Samuel 6):** David brings the ark of Yhwh — symbol of the northern/Ephraimite Yhwh tradition — to Jerusalem. This is the narrative expression of a political-theological merger.
- **The royal psalms:** Psalm 110: "Yhwh said to my lord [the king]: 'Sit at my right hand'" — the Davidic king is enthroned alongside Yhwh in the old El city. Psalm 2 describes Yhwh and "his anointed" ruling from Zion.
- **The double legitimation:** Yhwh gets a new capital and a royal temple. David gets divine sanction for his new dynasty.

**The Tel Dan stele (8th century BCE):**
- The only extra-biblical reference to David. Three Aramaic fragments celebrate Hazael of Damascus's victory. The phrase *btdwd* is interpreted as "house of David" — confirming a Davidic dynasty by the 8th century.
- "This tells us nothing about the historical figure of David but does show that the Aramaeans in the eighth century called the kingdom of Judah 'house of David.'"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** HIGH that Jerusalem was originally non-Israelite and that David conquered it (biblical account consistent, archaeology supports a Canaanite city). MEDIUM that David brought Yhwh specifically (the ark narrative is plausible but late). MEDIUM that the El-Yhwh merger was David's deliberate policy (evidence is circumstantial — we have the RESULT of the merger but not the process).

## Stakes

The "city of God," the "place where God's name dwells," was someone else's city first. Yhwh moved in as a political arrangement — the patron deity of a new dynasty, installed in a conquered capital. The Psalms that celebrate God's enthronement in Zion are royal propaganda. Jerusalem is holy because David made it his capital, not because God chose it from eternity. The theology followed the politics.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Maximalist scholars who take the biblical account of David at face value. Scholars who argue Yhwh was always Jerusalem's deity.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical narrative of David's conquest and ark transfer reflects genuine theological tradition — David was divinely guided to Jerusalem, and the merger of El and Yhwh was God's providential plan, not political calculation.

## Assessment

The political logic is the strongest part of Römer's argument. Jerusalem as a neutral, non-tribal capital makes excellent political sense. The ark transfer as narrative expression of cult unification is elegant. The weakest link is David's personal role — we simply don't have direct evidence of his religious policies. But the OUTCOME (El and Yhwh merged in Jerusalem) is undeniable. Whether David engineered it or it happened under later kings, the merger was political before it was theological.
