# fleming-ybi-5.1: Yhwʒ of Shasu-land is best explained as a shortened personal name-derived people-name

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 5 — The Name Yahweh]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"It is much more likely that the divine name Yahweh derives from the Shasu group without divine association than that the Shasu group already related to the eventual Israelite god" (p. 196). "The problem is not whether a major deity could be identified by a human ancestor but rather whether a people could be identified by a human personal name, a straightforward question with straightforward answer: yes, and fairly often" (p. 203).

## Evidence

- Peoples named after personal names are common: Israel itself (yiśrā'ēl, "May El contend"), Yamutbal/Emutbal ("The lord has died"), and numerous Ya-prefixed Aramean group names (p. 187)
- The Yamutbal people of the early second millennium show the pattern clearly: a population identified by what was originally an individual's name, capable of defining a "land" (māt Yamutbalim) without being tied to a single settlement or king (pp. 189-190)
- "In the Mari material, none of the various peoples named by finite verbal forms is known to be equated with a deity" (p. 196)
- The hypocoristic (shortened) naming type is well-attested: Huffmon (1965) lists numerous Mari examples like I-ba-ás-si-ir, Ya-a-ar, Ya-ab-na-ah, etc. (p. 195)
- Shortened names "are sometimes found without any suffix, even vocalic. In this regard, they resemble many divine names and geographic names" (Huffmon, cited p. 195)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The typological argument (peoples can be named after personal names) is solid. But the specific claim that Yhwʒ IS such a shortened personal name depends on the verbal interpretation of the name, which remains debated. If Yhwʒ is a noun, the personal-name derivation is less obvious.

## Stakes

This is Fleming's positive alternative to the divine-name interpretation. If Yhwʒ was never a divine name, the question becomes: how did a people-name become a god-name? (Addressed in Ch 6.) This inverts the standard approach.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone who treats Yhwʒ as a divine name or as a toponym derived from a divine name (Giveon, Ahituv, Römer, van der Toorn). Cross (1973) treated Yahweh as a liturgical epithet of El. Tropper (2017) argues it's a qatl-type noun.

**Alternative reading:** Yhwʒ IS a divine name — the Soleb list names a region after the god worshipped there (like "Beth-Yahweh"). The personal-name derivation is unnecessary.

## Assessment

Fleming's typological argument is strong: peoples named after personal names are common, and the form of Yhwʒ fits the pattern. The weakness is that we can't PROVE it was a shortened personal name rather than something else (a noun, a place name, etc.). The argument is plausible but not demonstrable.
