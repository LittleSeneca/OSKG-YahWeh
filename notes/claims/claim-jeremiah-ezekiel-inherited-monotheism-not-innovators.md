# kaufmann-ri-13.5: The prophets — Jeremiah and Ezekiel — did not innovate monotheism. They inherited it.

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XIII — The Prophecy of the Fall]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XIII

## The Claim

"Like all the prophets, Jeremiah inherited the foundations of his world view from the popular religion. He lives and creates in the atmosphere of a monotheistic people. His God is non-mythological; the cult he knows is non-magical." Jeremiah's prophecy of destruction "is not a reflex of any particular excessive moral or religious decay of that age" but the child of "the monitions of the Torah."

## Evidence

- Jeremiah shares with the people "the belief in Israel's election and uniqueness and the faith in the everlastingness of YHWH's covenant, of Jerusalem, and of the Davidic monarchy"
- The idea of the fall "was thus no innovation of Jeremiah, nor did his opponents deny what the book of the covenant explicitly threatened"
- The prophecy of destruction and exile "came into being concurrently with the idea of the Torah book" — the warnings of Deuteronomy 28 and Leviticus 26 are its source
- Jeremiah "does not intend to abrogate the sacrificial cult" — he affirms temple, sacrifice, and Torah
- Ezekiel is even more clearly pre-prophetic: "His is the view of the early literature... Ezekiel is rooted in the Torah literature more markedly even than Jeremiah"
- Ezekiel "never enunciates the contrast of morality versus cult" — he's concerned with ritual/cultic sin in a way the classical prophets aren't
- Neither Jeremiah nor Ezekiel invented individual retribution, interior religion, or the primacy of morality over cult — these are misreadings

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann is persuasive that Jeremiah and Ezekiel worked within an existing monotheistic framework rather than creating one. But his claim that the POPULAR religion was monotheistic goes beyond the evidence for the Torah literature's monotheism.

## Stakes

The entire developmental model. If the prophets didn't create monotheism, then monotheism predates them — which means it's earlier than critical scholarship allows. This is Kaufmann's most important historical claim.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus that monotheism emerged THROUGH the prophetic movement. Smith (Origins Ch 10) traces monotheistic language developing within Isaiah 40-55. Römer sees the exile as the crucible in which monotheism crystallized.

**Alternative reading:** The Torah literature IS monotheistic, but it reached that form LATE — after the prophets. The monotheism Jeremiah and Ezekiel "inherit" from the Torah is actually the monotheism the Torah learned from them (or their tradition). Kaufmann mistakes the final form of the literature for its original form.

## Assessment

This is the core methodological issue. Kaufmann treats the Torah literature as a fixed, early corpus that the prophets drew upon. Critical scholarship treats it as a fluid tradition that reached its monotheistic form in dialogue with — and partly after — the prophets. The evidence of Jeremiah's dependence on Deuteronomy is real, but it doesn't prove Deuteronomy is pre-prophetic in its FINAL form. It could prove that the Deuteronomic school and the prophetic movement developed together. Kaufmann's conclusion only follows if you accept his dating of the Torah literature (Chapter V).
