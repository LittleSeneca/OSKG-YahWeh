# sommer-bog-1.2: Cult statues were the god, not representations of the god

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 1 — Mesopotamia and Canaan]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"In Mesopotamian religions, divine bodies differ from nondivine ones in that a deity's presence was not limited to a single body; it could emerge simultaneously in several objects."

The *ṣalmu* (cult statue) was not a REPRESENTATION of the god — it WAS the god, in one of its bodies. A god could have multiple cult statues in different cities, each of which WAS the god. The god was present in its statue, its celestial manifestation (star/planet), and its mythological body simultaneously.

Sommer identifies three types of divine bodies: cultic (the statue), cosmic (the astral body), and mythic-narrative (the body described in myths).

## Evidence

**The *mīs pî* and *pīt pî* rituals:**

The "mouth-washing" and "mouth-opening" rituals transformed a crafted statue into a living god:
- Before the ritual: "This statue without its mouth opened cannot smell incense, cannot eat food, nor drink water"
- During: The human artisans' hands are "symbolically cut off" — emphasizing that "in reality the idol was created by the gods"
- After: "The statue was in fact divine." The priest whispers into the statue's ear: "You are counted among your brother gods... From today may your destiny be counted as divinity"
- The wood was called "the flesh of the gods" and "the bones of the gods"

**Key point — the statue IS the god, not a symbol:**

"A ṣalmu, then, did not merely direct the worshipper's mind toward a god who dwelled in some other sphere; it did not depict the god. Rather, once the *mīs pî* or *pīt pî* ritual was complete, the divine presence entered into the statue, and the ṣalmu was the god."

**The consequence — multiple simultaneous embodiments:**

- The god dwelled in heaven AND in its cult statue AND potentially in other statues at other temples
- "The god is indeed the statue, but also is not only the statue"
- Idol polemics in the Bible miss this: "The idol was not an idol, i.e., merely an object of worship, it was the god"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The ritual texts are explicit and unambiguous. The *mīs pî* and *pīt pî* texts leave no doubt that the statue was understood to become the deity, not represent it.

## Stakes

If cult statues were the god, not representations, then the biblical polemic against idols fundamentally misrepresents what ANE idol worship was. The prophets mock idols as inert wood and stone that "cannot see, cannot hear" — but no Mesopotamian worshipper thought the raw material was the god. They knew the god entered the statue through ritual. The biblical critique attacks a straw man — or, more charitably, it refuses to grant the ANE theological premise that divinity can inhabit material objects.

This also illuminates the Israelite aniconic tradition: the prohibition against images of Yhwh was not primarily about preventing "idolatry" in the sense of worshipping the wrong god. It was about preventing the localization and multiplication of Yhwh's presence — exactly what the fluidity model enabled.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who treat ANE cult statues as symbols or dwelling places rather than embodiments; the biblical authors themselves.

**Alternative reading:** Cult statues were dwellings (*subtu*) for the god, not the god itself. The *mīs pî* ritual invited the god to dwell in the statue, not to become the statue. The distinction between representation and embodiment is modern, not ancient.

## Assessment

This is one of Sommer's strongest claims. The *mīs pî* ritual texts are devastating to the common assumption that ANE peoples worshipped "idols" in the naive sense the Bible polemicizes against. The statue theology was sophisticated: the statue WAS the god AND the god transcended the statue. This both- and logic is exactly the fluidity Sommer sees surviving in J and E's *mal'akh* theology.
