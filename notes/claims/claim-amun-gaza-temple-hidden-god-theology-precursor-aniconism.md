# keel-ggi-5.2: The Amun temple at Gaza propagated a "hidden god" theology throughout Palestine via mass-produced seal amulets — a possible precursor to Israelite aniconism

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

The Papyrus Harris I (compiled under Ramesses III's successor) records a temple in Gaza — "a 'house that no one can enter,' dedicated to Amun in the country D3hy (Palestine)" — where "the foreigners from Rtnw [the Levant] come to him [to the cultic image of Amun] and present him with their gifts." This temple was supported by tribute from the southern coastal plain and Shephelah. The mass-produced seal amulets bearing Amun's name were likely produced in its workshops. The Philistines "apparently continued to emphasize the cult of the god" after Egyptian withdrawal.

Crucially, Amun's nature as the "hidden one" (Egyptian *Ymn* = "the hidden") was expressed iconographically through cryptographic writing: "Amun's nature could be praised simultaneously in cryptographic writing by using the lion both to show him as the 'hidden one' and as the one who had the power of a lion." The hiddenness WAS the theology — and the cryptography WAS the expression of that theology. "The cult of this Egyptian deity figure could have influenced the cult of the Canaanite El, who was thought of in a similar way and then consequently might have had an impact on the way Yahweh was conceived of as well."

## Evidence

- Papyrus Harris I, 9.1-3: the "house that no one can enter" for Amun in Gaza
- Three scarabs from Beth-Shemesh and Tell el-Far'ah (south) indicating tribute to the Gaza temple (Uehlinger 1990a)
- Hieratic ostraca from Tel Sera and Tel Haror connected with the tribute system
- A unique scarab from Tell el-Far'ah (south) apparently depicting a Philistine prince before a ram-headed god — "classic Amun iconography"
- Truncated pyramid seals (new in Iron Age I, found only in Philistine-controlled areas): explicit Amun-Re inscriptions on one seal from Tel Qasile Str. X, cryptographic Amun writing on a seal from Tel Gerisa, and a third from near Jaffa
- The Megiddo hoard: 9 of 25 identifiable pieces bear Amun's name (5 cryptographic via lion, 1 via uraeus acrophony, 4 as Mn-hpr-r' throne name of Tuthmosis III) — "36% of the pieces"
- Mass-produced Ramesses Period goods concentrated on the coastal plain but penetrating the Shephelah and hill country

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Papyrus Harris reference is unambiguous. The concentration of Amun seals in Iron Age I strata is statistically significant. The cryptographic writing-as-hiddenness argument is philologically sound (Egyptian *imn* = "hidden").

## Stakes

This is a bombshell for the aniconism debate. If a "hidden god" theology — expressed through concealment, cryptography, and a temple "no one can enter" — was operating in 12th-11th century Palestine, then Israelite aniconism may not have been a unique theological innovation but rather an adaptation of an existing Egyptian-Canaanite theological tradition. This directly feeds into the debate about whether Yahweh was originally aniconic.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Israelite aniconism as a radical break with Canaanite/ANE tradition (e.g., Mettinger's "programmatic aniconism") would resist the idea of Egyptian theological influence. The "house that no one can enter" might refer to a restricted-access inner sanctuary rather than a truly imageless cult — the text mentions a "cultic image."

**Alternative reading:** The cryptographic writing could simply be a stylistic fashion in mass-produced seal amulets, not a theological statement. The dominance of Amun in the glyptic record may reflect production centers rather than popular devotion — people bought what was available.

## Assessment

This is one of the chapter's most original and consequential arguments. Keel and Uehlinger do not overclaim — they say Amun's cult "could have influenced" El and Yahweh, not that it DID. But the convergence of (a) a temple described as unenterable, (b) a god whose very name means "hidden," (c) cryptographic writing that conceals while revealing, and (d) the eventual emergence of aniconic Yahwism in the same geographic region is too suggestive to dismiss.
