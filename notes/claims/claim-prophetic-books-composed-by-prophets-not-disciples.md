# kaufmann-ri-10.3: The prophetic books were composed by the prophets themselves, not disciples

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"The fixity of the text implies that prophecies were written down by the prophets themselves." The common view — that prophetic literature developed through (a) the prophet, (b) disciples who transmitted and colored the words, and (c) generations of compilers and annotators — is wrong. "A purposeful, continuous, large-scale revising of the ancient prophecies themselves is nowhere in evidence." Later editors did not penetrate the text of the prophecies; their work was limited to arrangement and narrative framework.

## Evidence

- The historical horizon of each prophet is unified and natural. Amos doesn't mention Assyria; his words were preserved "in their original vagueness" — "Assyria was nowhere explicitly inserted"
- Isaiah son of Amoz knows only Assyria as the final enemy; "that it in turn was to fall before and be succeeded by Babylonia is nowhere indicated." Even though tradition *did* annex Isaiah 40-66, "no suggestion of a succession of empires was interpolated into the early visions"
- Micah has one genuine revision: "and you shall come to Babylon" (4:10) — "one of the few instances in the whole corpus of prophecy of a genuine revision in light of later events. It is characteristically obvious, no exegetical subtlety being required to detect it"
- Jeremiah 1-19 never mentions Babylonia; only after 605 does the Babylonian theme enter (chap. 21ff.). Even though Jeremiah later identified the "nation from the north" as Babylon, his earlier prophecies "have not been penetrated by the Babylonian motif"
- Ezekiel "nowhere hints that Persia would have a hand in" Babylon's fall
- "The rift between the plain sense of prophecies and the actual course of events passes through the whole of the literature." If prophecies were fluid, how did this gap survive? "How is the intact transmission of prophecies that failed... to be explained except by the assumption that already during the time of the prophets themselves their words had become sacred?"
- Jeremiah 36 is paradigmatic: Jeremiah dictates to Baruch, who compiles faithfully — "despite the fact that henceforth Jeremiah's entire prophecy was dominated by the theme of Babylonia, in setting down all the earlier prophecies the scribe does not insinuate into them a single line of the new theme"
- Each prophetic book "bears the stamp of one creative personality"
- No classical prophet is depicted as head of a prophetic guild; none works through "sons of prophets"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The observation that historical horizons weren't artificially extended is genuinely impressive, but the inference that this proves authorial composition is too strong — redaction could take forms other than historical updating.

## Stakes

The entire model of how prophetic literature was produced. If Kaufmann is right, the prophets were *writers* who composed their own books — not oral performers whose words were later collected and reshaped by tradition. This supports his larger claim that classical prophecy is a literary phenomenon with fixed texts, not a fluid oral tradition.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Form criticism and tradition history. The standard critical model (Gunkel, Mowinckel, von Rad) holds that prophetic oracles circulated orally among disciples before being written down, and that the books underwent extensive redaction. The presence of third-person narrative, superscriptions, and editorial frameworks is taken as evidence of later hands.

**Alternative reading:** Redactors could have worked *within* the existing historical framework rather than extending it. They might have rearranged, expanded, or reinterpreted oracles without adding explicit references to later empires. The unity of each book could reflect the work of a single *editor* rather than a single *author*. The case of Micah 4:10 ("you shall come to Babylon") actually demonstrates the opposite of what Kaufmann claims — it shows that glossators *did* alter the text.

## Assessment**

Kaufmann makes a genuinely powerful case for the relative fixity of the prophetic text. The limited historical horizons ARE striking — if later editors were freely revising, why didn't they make Amos name Assyria? The "rift" argument is Kaufmann at his most ingenious: the very fact that prophecies don't match history is evidence they weren't tampered with. However, Kaufmann may overstate. Redaction can take forms other than updating historical referents — theological glossing, rearrangement, expansion of existing themes. A redactor who adds "on that day" expansions isn't changing the historical horizon but IS changing the text. Still, this is one of his best chapters methodologically — he's identified a genuinely testable claim about the text and supported it with systematic evidence.
