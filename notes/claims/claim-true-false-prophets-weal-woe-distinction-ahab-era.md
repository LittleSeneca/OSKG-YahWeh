# kaufmann-ri-8.7: The true/false prophet distinction emerged as weal vs. woe in Ahab's time

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"In Ahab's time, the dichotomy of 'true' and 'false' prophets makes its first historical appearance." The theoretical distinction is older, but "what is new is the division into what seem to be two parties of prophets, each marked by a distinctive outlook and ideology." "Crucial for the history of Israelite religion is the distinction that develops during this period between the prophet of weal and the prophet of woe." Both types are "rooted in Israelite faith" — one linked to "a religious-moral doctrine," the other to "a national-territorial promise."

## Evidence

- I Kings 22: Micaiah opposed by four hundred prophets headed by Zedekiah who "prophesy good things to the king"
- "National crises gave rise, on the one hand, to a mood of penitence; the expression of which was the prophecy of woe. But it also aroused the hope of deliverance; the prophet of weal was the organ of this mood"
- Both types are "rooted in Israelite faith" — one linked to "a religious-moral doctrine," the other to "a national-territorial promise"
- The prophets of weal "whom we meet in the writings of the literary prophets... represent a type that belongs to the stage of Israelite religion prior to the rise of classical prophecy"
- It is a "mistaken notion that the classical prophets were exclusively prophets of woe" — even Elijah "runs before Ahab's chariot in the hour of favor"; Isaiah "appears in II Kings 19 as a prophet of weal"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The 'weal vs. woe' distinction is a useful analytical tool, and Kaufmann's insistence that classical prophets also delivered oracles of salvation is correct and important.

## Stakes

The nature of prophetic conflict in Israel. If both "weal" and "woe" prophecy are rooted in authentic Israelite tradition, the "false prophets" weren't necessarily charlatans — they were giving voice to genuine aspects of Israelite faith (the national promise) while neglecting others (the moral demand).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The biblical texts themselves characterize the conflict in starker terms — false prophets are liars, adulterers, and drunkards who prophesy for money (Mic 3:5; Jer 23:14; Ezek 13:10). Kaufmann is deliberately complicating the biblical picture.

**Alternative reading:** The "weal vs. woe" framework captures a real dynamic but may be too schematic. Some conflicts between prophets (Jeremiah vs. Hananiah in Jer 28) involved genuine theological and political disagreement. The "false prophets" may have been court functionaries whose "prophecy of weal" served royal interests — propagandists, not just representatives of a legitimate strand of Israelite faith.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's framework is genuinely helpful for understanding the prophetic conflicts. His observation that classical prophecy contained both judgment and salvation is important and often overlooked in popular characterizations of the prophets as purely doom-sayers. However, his attempt to salvage the "prophets of weal" as legitimate representatives of Israelite tradition may be too generous — the classical prophets' condemnation of their opponents is so fierce that it suggests more than a difference in emphasis.
