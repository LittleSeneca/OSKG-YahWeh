# lewis-ocg-10.1: The earliest understanding of divine holiness was about power, not cultic purity

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 10 — Holy One]] — Theodore Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 10

## The Claim

"Many of these traditions are surprisingly non-cultic in nature... The notion of Yahweh being holy is found in our earliest biblical traditions." Exodus 15:11 — "Who is like you among the gods, Yahweh? Who is like you, feared in holiness?" — is embedded in a divine warrior hymn. "The earliest passages of the Hebrew Bible on holy divinity resonate with what we see in Late Bronze Age Levantine antecedents... to say that Yahweh is holy is to say that he is powerful."

## Evidence

- Exodus 15 (one of the oldest poems) mentions holiness three times in a military context: "feared in holiness" (15:11), "your holy abode" (15:13), "your holy sanctuary" (15:17)
- Habakkuk 3:3: "the Holy One (qādôš) from Mt. Paran" marches in military theophany with pestilence and plague
- Deut 33:2: Yahweh comes with "myriads of holy ones" as combatants
- Psalm 68, Psalm 89 connect holiness with divine power and theophany
- The Kuntillet ʿAjrud plaster fragment KA 4.2 connects holiness with theophanic power
- Northwest Semitic background: Ugaritic ʾIlu is called "the Holy One" (qdš); the gods collectively are "sons of the Holy One" (bn qdš); the Arslan Tash incantation mentions "the assembly of all the Holy Ones" (dr kl qdšn)
- At this early stage, "there is no hint of what comes to define holiness in priestly and Deuteronomic traditions (e.g., sacerdotal rank, cultic purity, social and ethical behaviors, and being withdrawn from common use)"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** VERY HIGH. This is one of Lewis's most original contributions. The early military/power meaning of qdš has been underappreciated because later priestly traditions dominate our understanding of holiness.

## Stakes

Whether holiness is primarily about separation/purity (the standard theological definition) or about power. Lewis argues the latter is chronologically prior. This has implications for how we read EVERY mention of holiness in the Hebrew Bible. It explains why the Ark narrative (Uzzah struck dead for touching it) makes sense: the Ark is not "pure" in a ritual sense; it's DANGEROUSLY POWERFUL.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who define holiness exclusively through the priestly lens (separation, purity, distinctness). The standard theological dictionaries (e.g., TDOT) emphasize separation as the core meaning.

**Alternative reading:** The power and separation meanings coexisted from the beginning; Lewis overstates the developmental sequence.

## Assessment

This is a genuinely important insight. It explains why the Ark narrative (Uzzah struck dead for touching it) makes sense: the Ark is not "pure" in a ritual sense; it's DANGEROUSLY POWERFUL. Holiness as power also explains the Nadab and Abihu deaths (Lev 10) better than ritual infraction alone.
