# sommer-bog-4.3: 1 Kings 8 reflects D's anti-fluid theology

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 4 — Sacred Space 1]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 4

## The Claim

Solomon's Temple dedication prayer (1 Kings 8) represents the anti-fluid revolution in action. The prayer explicitly denies that God dwells in the Temple: "The heavens and the highest heaven cannot contain You; how much less this house which I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27). This is D theology — a CORRECTION of the older fluid theology that assumed God had a body in the Temple.

## Evidence

**The anti-fluid reaction — 1 Kings 8:27:**
"The heavens and the highest heaven cannot contain You; how much less this house which I have built!"

**Sommer's reading:** This is D theology. Solomon's prayer represents the anti-fluid revolution. The Temple does NOT contain God — it only contains God's Name. God is too transcendent to dwell in a building. This is a CORRECTION of the older fluid theology.

**The D context:**
- 1 Kings 8 is thoroughly Deuteronomistic — the prayer uses D's characteristic "Name" theology throughout
- Verse 29: "that Your eyes may be open toward this house night and day, toward the place of which You said, 'My Name shall be there'"
- The Name is in the Temple; God is in heaven. God's "eyes" look toward the Temple from heaven. This is anti-fluid: God is not IN the Temple; God looks TOWARD it.

**The older tradition being corrected:**
- The Tent of Meeting (Exodus 33): God comes down to the tent, speaks face to face
- The Ark: God's body sits enthroned on the cherubim
- These older traditions assumed God had a body in the sanctuary. 1 Kings 8 overwrites that assumption.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The text explicitly denies that the Temple contains God; Sommer's reading that this is a deliberate correction of older fluid theology is strongly supported by the D source's consistent *shem* theology.

## Stakes

1 Kings 8 is the classic prooftext for Jewish and Christian theology of divine transcendence. Sommer's reading recovers the polemical context: Solomon's prayer is not timeless theology but a SPECIFIC argument against the older fluid model. The prayer is trying to convince worshippers that God is NOT in the Temple — because that's what they used to believe.

## Disagreement

**Alternative reading:** 1 Kings 8 is not a correction of fluid theology but a statement of divine transcendence that was always part of Israelite religion. The "Name" language is poetic, not polemical.

## Assessment

This is where Sommer's model pays off most dramatically for biblical interpretation. 1 Kings 8 is one of the most important theological texts in the Bible, traditionally read as a statement of divine transcendence. Sommer shows it's actually a polemic — Solomon is arguing AGAINST an older, widely-held view that God literally dwells in the Temple. The prayer only makes sense as a correction. If everyone already believed God was transcendent, why would Solomon need to argue the point at such length?
