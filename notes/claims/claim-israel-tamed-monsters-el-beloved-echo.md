# smith-obm-1.7: Israelite texts transform cosmic enemies through "taming" — echoing El's "beloved" relationship with monsters

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch1 — Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"The biblical texts treating the monstrous figures instead as pets may echo their 'beloved' relationship with El. Just as the biblical material coalesced the differing imagery involving El and Baal with the national god of Yahweh, so too the differing roles of the cosmic forces as foes and beloved of the divine perhaps coalesced."

## Evidence

- **Job 40:25-29 (NJPS):** Leviathan is caught by God's fishhook, drawn by rope and nose-ring. "Will you play with him like a bird...?" (40:29).
- **Psalm 104:26:** "Leviathan whom you formed to sport with."
- **Job 38:8-11:** Sea treated at creation not as enemy but as newborn babe.
- **Contrast with Ugaritic:** In the Baal Cycle, these are serious opponents — Baal is initially slumped beneath Yamm's throne (1.2 IV 6-7), Baal and Mot reach stalemate (1.6 VI 16-22).
- In Ugaritic, cosmic enemies are explicitly divine: River/Yamm called *'il rbm* ("Great God," 1.3 III 39), Mot called *bn 'ilm* ("son of El/son of the gods," 1.4 VIII 45-46), personal name *ym'il* ("Yamm is god," 4.75 V 14).
- In Israel, their divine status is largely denied. But traces remain: personal name Yemuel (Genesis 46:10; Exodus 6:15) may = *ym'il*.
- Ugaritic enemies are divine in rank and power; biblical enemies are hardly comparable in power (Psalm 74). Contrast Baal needing Kothar's magical weapons (1.2 IV).

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The taming pattern is clearly present in the biblical texts. The link to El's "beloved" relationship is more speculative — it's an attractive synthesis but depends on the coalescence model.

## Stakes

This claim offers a unified explanation for why biblical texts oscillate between treating cosmic enemies as foes (Psalm 74) and as pets (Job 40-41, Psalm 104). The answer: both El's beloved-monsters tradition and Baal's warrior-vs-monsters tradition were absorbed into Yahweh's character. The tension in the biblical portrait reflects the tension in its sources.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the "pet" passages as simply late, domesticated wisdom traditions with no connection to El mythology. The Yemuel/*ym'il* parallel is slim evidence for early Israelite recognition of Yamm's divinity.

**Alternative reading:** The taming tradition could be an independent Israelite development — part of a wisdom theology that emphasizes God's sovereign control over all creation, including chaos monsters — rather than an inheritance from El traditions. Psalm 104's dependence on the Egyptian Aten Hymn suggests multiple influences.

## Assessment

The coalescence argument is elegant and fits Smith's larger thesis, but it's underdetermined by the evidence in this chapter alone. The taming/domestication pattern is real regardless of its source — the biblical data clearly show both the conflict tradition (Psalm 74, Isaiah 27:1) and the domestication tradition (Job 40-41, Psalm 104:26, Genesis 1) coexisting. That's significant even without the El-connection.
