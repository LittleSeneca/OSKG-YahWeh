# kaufmann-ri-1.1: The Bible displays utter ignorance of the nature and meaning of pagan religion

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"If one examines the biblical account of the origins of Israelite monotheism and the story of its battle with and eventual triumph over paganism, he will discover a strange fact: the Bible is utterly unaware of the nature and meaning of pagan religion." Despite centuries of fighting "idolatry" and producing abundant polemical literature, "No one, apparently, has ever doubted this assumption or criticized it in the light of the data." Named gods (Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh, etc.) are "mere names" stripped of mythological content. The Bible's anti-idolatry polemic attacks only the worship of manufactured objects, never the belief in living gods with mythological biographies.

## Evidence

The entire chapter is the evidence for this claim. Kaufmann surveys the biblical corpus — narratives, prophetic polemics, and legal codes — and argues that nowhere does the Bible engage with pagan religion as pagans knew it. The Bible's anti-idolatry polemic attacks only the worship of manufactured objects, never the belief in living gods with mythological biographies.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the *observation* (the Bible's polemic really does focus overwhelmingly on fetishism); LOW on the *explanation* (that this reflects genuine ignorance rather than polemical strategy or editorial selection).

## Stakes

If Kaufmann is right about the observation, every scholar working on Israelite religion needs to explain this pattern. If he's right about the explanation, the entire evolutionary model — which assumes Israel gradually shed pagan beliefs it once held — collapses.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Almost everyone in the critical tradition assumes the biblical writers knew pagan religion and polemically misrepresented it. Smith argues extensively that Israelite religion shared mythological categories with Ugaritic/Canaanite religion. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism from polytheistic roots and treats biblical anti-idolatry polemic as a late development. Dever's archaeological work documents extensive continuity between Israelite and Canaanite material religion.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical writers knew pagan religion perfectly well and chose to misrepresent it. Reducing pagan gods to "wood and stone" is a deliberate polemical strategy — a form of satirical degradation that refuses to dignify pagan beliefs by engaging with them seriously. See especially the satirical tone of Isaiah 44:9-20 — the humor depends on the audience recognizing the absurdity of idol-making.

## Assessment

Kaufmann has identified a genuinely interesting pattern that deserves more attention than most scholars give it. The Bible really IS strangely silent about pagan mythology — it never names Marduk killing Tiamat, never describes Baal's battle with Yamm, never mentions the death and resurrection of Baal. For a corpus supposedly engaged in a life-or-death struggle with paganism, this silence is notable. BUT — Kaufmann's leap from "the Bible doesn't describe pagan mythology" to "Israelites didn't know pagan mythology" is a non-sequitur. The Bible is elite literature produced by a specific scribal/theological class with a specific agenda. Its silence about pagan mythology tells us about the interests and rhetorical strategies of its authors, not about what ordinary Israelites knew or believed.
