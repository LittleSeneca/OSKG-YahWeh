# albertz-hir-5.1: The post-exilic period was extraordinarily productive, not a "late Judaism" decline — and it was characterized by a dialectic of splintering and integration

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 5 — Post-Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"More recent research... shows increasingly clearly that the post-exilic period, and especially the early post-exilic period (538-400), was one of the most productive eras in the history of Israelite religion." The reason for its neglect is "a deep-seated dogmatic Christian view, which sees the church as the goal of the history of Israelite religion, and therefore likes to go straight from the lofty heights of Deutero-Isaiah... to the New Testament."

## Evidence

- Foundation of a universally binding holy scripture (the Torah)
- The temple became the central symbol of religious life
- Eschatological expectation horizons developed beyond political history
- Multiple "theological currents" (Deuteronomistic, Priestly, Chronistic, wisdom, prophetic, psalmic) stood side by side and crossed
- Ploger's bipolar model (theocratic vs. eschatological) is "much too simple" — it ignores Deuteronomistic and Wisdom traditions
- Steck's "diagram of currents" does more justice to the evidence but fails to give sociological grounding
- Crusemann's tripolar model (small farmers + Gola/priests; local aristocracy; prophetic conventicles) is better but still "too crude"
- Albertz's own model emphasizes: (a) rifts within the upper class, (b) rifts within the priesthood, (c) wisdom theology as a form of personal piety, not a separate "current" alongside Torah

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Textual evidence for literary productivity (Pentateuch, prophetic corpus, Writings shaped in this period) is overwhelming.

## Stakes

Albertz is fighting a long Christian theological tradition that dismisses "late Judaism" as legalistic degeneration. If he's right, this period is the creative matrix of both Judaism and Christianity — not a barren interlude between prophets and Jesus.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire Christian supersessionist tradition. More specifically, scholars who locate key developments earlier (in the exile) or later (in the Hellenistic period). Wellhausen's model saw post-exilic Judaism as a decline from prophetic vitality to priestly ritualism.

**Alternative reading:** The post-exilic period was primarily a period of consolidation and institutionalization, not creativity — the creative work was done in the exile; the post-exilic period merely codified it.

## Assessment

Albertz's polemic against Christian prejudice is well-taken and the evidence of literary productivity in this period is overwhelming. The more interesting question is his model of "splintering and integration." This dialectic is a powerful analytical framework that avoids both the "unified Judaism" picture and the "warring sects" picture.
