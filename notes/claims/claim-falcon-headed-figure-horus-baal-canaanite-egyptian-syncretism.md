# keel-ggi-p1.6: The falcon-headed figure represents a deliberate merging of Horus and the Canaanite weather/fertility god

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part I

## The Claim

"The self-confident Canaanites who lived at the end of the eighteenth century and during the seventeenth and sixteenth centuries associated their royal god, their fertility god, with the chief Egyptian royal god, so they placed a branch or blossom scepter into the hand of their own royal god." The falcon-headed figure appears much more frequently than the weather god on MB IIB scarabs. On the "jasper group" cylinder seals (Byblos or further south, 1720-1650 BCE), the falcon-headed figure with Egyptian double crown is "clearly shown as the royal god, which means he is probably Horus." On scarabs, the double crown disappears but the falcon head remains. The non-Egyptian element is the branch or flower in the figure's hand — "probably a branch scepter or a blossom scepter, adapted for the falcon-headed figure from the Canaanite weather god." The result: "a merging of attributes from the Egyptian royal god with those of the Canaanite royal god."

## Evidence

- Falcon-headed figure on jasper group cylinder seal (Collon 1986, 58 no. 4)
- Impression from Alalakh Stratum VII palace (1720-1650; Collon 1975, 78f. no. 144)
- Seal of Sumirapa of Tuba (same period; Nougayrol/Amiet 1962)
- On scarabs: falcon-headed figure without double crown but with branch/blossom (illuss. 33a-c)
- Falcon-headed figure victorious over crocodile (illuss. 34a-b; Keel et al. 1989, 268-275) — Egyptian motif taboo in Egypt because crocodile was holy, acceptable in Canaan as Baal's victory over Leviathan
- Horus falcon with outstretched wings on jasper group scarabs (illus. 34c)
- Five gold earrings in falcon-with-outstretched-wings form from Tell el-Ajjul (Petrie 1931, pl. 15)
- Tell el-Dab'a scarab: pharaoh with club AND branch — Egyptian royal icon plus Canaanite vegetation attribute
- Egyptian reaction: "regarded this equating of deities as impudent and insolent"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The identification of the falcon-headed figure with the jasper group is securely established. The "merging" interpretation is inherently plausible given the Hyksos context. Key uncertainty: whether Canaanites understood it as deliberate theological syncretism or casual visual borrowing.

## Stakes

This is evidence for religious syncretism driven by political ambition. The Hyksos needed ideological legitimation to rule Egypt, and one strategy was to claim that their god was the same as the Egyptian royal god — just with a branch instead of an ankh. This is the same strategy that later produced Greco-Egyptian Sarapis and Roman-Imperial syncretisms. It also foreshadows the later question of whether Yahweh absorbed solar and royal imagery from Egyptian models — if Canaanites were already merging their royal god with Horus in MB IIB, then the solarization of Yahweh in Iron Age IIB-C has deep precedent.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who identify the falcon-headed figure as purely Egyptian (Re) or purely Canaanite (Horon). The Egyptians themselves clearly rejected the equivalence: Egyptian reaction to Hyksos-era syncretism was hostile.

**Alternative reading:** The falcon-headed figure with a branch might simply depict an Egyptian deity (Horus) in a Canaanite artistic style where branches were a standard attribute of any deity associated with vegetation. This would be iconographic borrowing without theological syncretism.

## Assessment

The Byblos evidence is the strongest support for the syncretism thesis — the kings of Byblos were literally wearing Egyptian royal symbols while ruling a Canaanite city. The branch/blossom scepter in the falcon-headed figure's hand is genuinely non-Egyptian and requires explanation. Keel & Uehlinger's explanation — deliberate merging of Canaanite and Egyptian royal gods — is the most economical. But the fact that these are mass-produced amulets, not royal inscriptions, suggests the merging may have been more organic and popular than centrally directed.
