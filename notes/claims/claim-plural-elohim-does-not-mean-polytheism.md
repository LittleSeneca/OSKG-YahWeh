# heiser-ur-4.2: Plural *elohim* does NOT mean polytheism — the biblical writers never viewed Yahweh as interchangeable with other *elohim*

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 2 — The Households of God]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"There is no warrant for concluding that plural *elohim* produces a pantheon of interchangeable deities. There is no basis for concluding that the biblical writers would have viewed Yahweh as no better than another *elohim*." A biblical writer would not presume another *elohim* had the same attributes as Yahweh. "That is polytheistic thinking. It never enters the mind of any biblical writer."

## Evidence

**The logical distinction:**
- Polytheism requires a pantheon of comparable deities — gods who share a basic ontological status
- The biblical writers use *elohim* for Yahweh AND for subordinate beings, but they NEVER treat them as comparable
- Yahweh is *elohim* in a qualitatively different sense than other *elohim*

**The hierarchy:**
- Yahweh presides over the divine council — he judges the other *elohim* (Psalm 82)
- The other *elohim* are created beings (Psalm 82:6-7: "you will die like men")
- Yahweh alone receives worship (Deut 6:4, the Shema)

**The ANE comparison:** In actual polytheistic systems (Ugarit, Mesopotamia), deities existed in a pantheon where power relationships could shift and gods could challenge each other. Nothing like this exists in the Hebrew Bible. Yahweh is never challenged. His supremacy is absolute.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is definitional. Heiser defines polytheism as interchangeability of deities within a pantheon. By this definition, the Hebrew Bible is not polytheistic. But other scholars define polytheism more broadly as the belief in multiple divine beings, which the Hebrew Bible clearly attests. The dispute is over the definition of polytheism, not the data.

## Stakes

If Heiser is right, the entire polytheism→monotheism narrative collapses. Israel was never polytheistic — it always had a hierarchical worldview with Yahweh supreme over other created *elohim*. What scholars call "polytheism" is simply the divine council, which is fully compatible with exclusive Yahwism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Virtually all critical scholars. Smith, Römer, Day, Dever, and Lewis all read the plural *elohim* as evidence that early Israel was polytheistic — they worshipped multiple gods. Sommer reads it as evidence of "fluid monotheism." Kaufmann reads the plural *elohim* language as empty polemic — the other gods aren't real.

**Alternative reading:** Early Israel worshipped multiple deities (Yahweh, Asherah, Baal, El) as a genuine pantheon. The divine council language is a fossilized remnant of this earlier polytheism. The hierarchy (Yahweh supreme) developed later as part of the monolatry→monotheism trajectory.

## Assessment

This is the claim where Heiser's project most clearly diverges from critical scholarship. He accepts ALL the data — multiple *elohim*, divine council, ANE parallels — but rejects the polytheism label. The question is whether this is a genuine alternative framework or a semantic dodge. If you define "polytheism" to mean "interchangeable gods in a flat pantheon," Heiser wins the argument by definition. If you define it as "belief in multiple divine beings," Heiser loses. The real question is whether Heiser's definition captures what scholars mean by Israelite polytheism.
