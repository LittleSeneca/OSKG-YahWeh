# dever-dghw-2.1: Archaeology has been systematically ignored in the study of Israelite religion

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter II — History of Scholarship]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter II

## The Claim

Dever surveys the entire history of scholarship on Israelite religion — from the late 19th-century "History of Religions" school through the "Myth and Ritual" school, Old Testament Theology, sociological approaches, and the 1990s-2000s revival — and concludes that at every stage, the field has produced studies of texts about religion rather than studies of religion itself. Even the best recent text-based scholars (Miller, Day, Smith) are "oblivious to revolutionary archaeological data." The rare exceptions — Albertz, Zevit, Nakhai, Keel/Uehlinger — prove the rule by demonstrating what becomes possible when archaeology is taken seriously.

## Evidence

**Dever's survey of six scholarly traditions:**

1. **"History of Religions" School (late 19th-early 20th c.):** "What resulted from early studies based largely on the biblical texts themselves was what I would call 'histories of the history of the texts about religions,' rather than of religion itself." Predated archaeology.

2. **"Myth and Ritual" School:** Fascinated by "fertility cults" and "sacred marriage" — "There never was much actual evidence for the more titillating aspects of such cults." Mostly fantasy built from texts alone.

3. **Old Testament Theology (Brueggemann, Gerstenberger, Childs):** "I have concluded, albeit somewhat reluctantly, that it is useless in the attempt to reconstruct a reliable portrait of ancient Israelite religion. Indeed, it is a barrier to understanding." Dever's ten-point critique: clerically dominated, androcentric, establishment-oriented, focused on unrealistic "unity of the Bible," static, apologetic, lacking historical foundations, obsessed with verbal formulations, insensitive to symbolic representations, "oblivious to revolutionary archaeological data."

4. **Sociological Approaches (Gottwald):** Praised as "perhaps the single most significant influence in contemporary biblical studies" — but sociology is still working from texts.

5. **The 1990s-2000s Revival:**
   - **Albertz:** "One of the first to make considerable, and generally competent, use of the pertinent archaeological data." Exception that proves the rule.
   - **Day:** "Scarcely seems concerned with the actual realities of religious life and experience for most folk." Ignores archaeology.
   - **Miller:** "Perhaps the most expert, sophisticated, and balanced discussion yet of the biblical texts. BUT: It is little more than a study of these texts."
   - **Mark Smith:** "I regret to say that I find Smith's work is not informed by any archaeological data that would change his almost exclusively text-based approach in any essential way."
   - **Zevit:** "The most ambitious, the most sophisticated, the most important study of ancient Israelite religion ever undertaken." 821 pages. "No other current biblicist can match Zevit's command of a broad range of archaeological data."

6. **Works on Folk Religion:** Ackerman, van der Toorn, Berlinerblau, Keel/Uehlinger — the minority tradition that takes non-textual evidence seriously.

**Dever's key quote:**
> "It would be good if [Miller's book] were the last work of its kind, because it seems to me that it says all that can be said as far as the biblical texts are concerned."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH — Dever documents this extensively across multiple scholarly traditions. The evidence is his survey itself. The question is not whether archaeology has been ignored (Dever proves this) but whether the neglect was as damaging as he claims.

## Stakes

If Dever is right that the field has systematically ignored the material evidence, then most of what we think we know about Israelite religion — from textbooks, seminary courses, and even advanced scholarship — is based on a partial and distorted source base. The correction requires a fundamental reorientation of method, not just the addition of a few archaeological footnotes to text-based arguments.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Text-based scholars (Smith, Römer, Day, Miller) would argue that they DO engage archaeology, just not in the way Dever demands — and that texts provide access to beliefs, concepts, and historical narratives that material culture alone cannot deliver.

**Alternative reading:** The best recent scholarship (Albertz, Keel/Uehlinger, Zevit) already integrates texts and archaeology. Dever's claim of systematic neglect was more true in 1985 than in 2005, and the field has moved toward integration faster than Dever acknowledges.
