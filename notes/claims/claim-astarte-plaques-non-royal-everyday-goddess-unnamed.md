# keel-ggi-excursus.2: The plaques depict a NON-ROYAL, everyday goddess — not a great goddess of the pantheon, and not identifiable with a specific divine name

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Excursus — Astarte Plaques]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Excursus §§57-60

## The Claim

"The combination of variable iconography and the spread of this form by means of many inexpensive plaques gives one reason to think that this image is not of a great, distant, royal deity (note the lack of a crown and the fact that the nakedness contradicts a Late Bronze Age tendency to clothe deities). Instead, it portrays a goddess who takes care of the everyday needs of her worshipers." The goddess "never appears in relation to other identifiable deities" and has no place in the pantheon's relational network. "It is not possible to associate her with any one particular name (Anat, Astarte, Asherah)." The term "Astartes" (plural) in Hebrew and Akkadian "is not to be understood as a plural proper name... but as a generic indicator and is to be translated as '(protecting) goddesses.'"

## Evidence

- No crown — unusual for a major deity in the LB period when deities are increasingly shown clothed
- Nakedness — in an era when deities are increasingly clothed, nakedness signals something other than royal status
- The goddess "never appears in relation to other identifiable deities" — unlike the qudshu type which appears with Reshef, Seth, etc.
- She has no place in the pantheon's relational network
- "It is not possible to associate her with any one particular name"
- The doubled images might suggest twin goddesses (Anat AND Astarte) but this is speculative
- "Astartes" (plural) in Hebrew/Akkadian is a generic indicator — "(protecting) goddesses" not a plural proper name

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The absence of crown, nakedness-in-an-era-of-clothing, and lack of relational iconography are all well-documented. The name question is genuinely unresolvable — which is itself an important finding.

## Stakes

If these figurines can't be assigned a specific divine name, then calling them "Astarte plaques" is misleading. They're "naked goddess plaques" — and which goddess they represented probably varied by time and place. This complicates Dever's tendency to treat ALL female figurines as "Asherah."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who identify the plaques specifically with Astarte (Pritchard 1943), Asherah (Dever), or Anat. Winter (1983) leans toward multiple possible identifications.

**Alternative reading:** The lack of specific attributes doesn't mean the goddess HAD no name — it means the NAME wasn't iconographically marked. Worshipers knew which goddess they were addressing even if we can't tell from the image alone.

## Assessment

Their agnosticism about the name is methodologically honest. The important point is that these are goddesses of a specific TYPE — personal, accessible, female-identified — not that they can be matched to a specific name from the Ugaritic pantheon.
