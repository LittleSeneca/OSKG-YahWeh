# romer-inv-2-3.1: Yhwh originated in the southern deserts, not in Canaan

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin and Moses]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapters 2-3

## The Claim

"The oldest attested occurrences of Yhwh are from the south of Palestine, the territory of Edom and Araba." This is supported by Egyptian epigraphic evidence (Soleb/Amara inscriptions), biblical poetry (four war poems describing Yhwh coming from the south), and archaeological context (Shasu nomads, Timna copper mining). Yhwh was a southern desert/war god before he was the god of Israel.

## Evidence

**The four proposed origins Romer evaluates:**

| Site | Evidence | Romer's Verdict |
|------|----------|----------------|
| **Ebla** (Syria, 3rd millennium) | Personal names ending in *-ya* | **False.** The *-ya* is a hypocoristic (diminutive) ending, not a divine name. No god Yhwh appears in Ebla offering lists. |
| **Ugarit** (Syria, 13th c.) | Fragmentary text (KTU 1.1) mentioning "YW" | **Inconclusive.** Could be Yhwh, could be Yam (Sea god), could be a scribal error. "We cannot therefore definitively exclude a link between Yw and Yhwh, but this passage is too fragmentary and unclear." |
| **Mari** (Euphrates, 2nd millennium) | Proper names like *Yahwi-ilum* | **False.** The root means "to manifest oneself" — a verb, not a divine name. "Cannot be linked with Yhwh." |
| **Egypt/Soleb** (Sudan, 14th c. BCE) | Inscriptions of Amenophis III and Ramses II listing "the Shasu of Yhw(h)" | **The strongest evidence.** "Yhwʒ seems to be a geographic term (referring to a mountain?) and perhaps also a divine name." |

**The Soleb evidence — the smoking gun:**
- Amenophis III (c. 1370 BCE) at Soleb in Sudan: a list of Shasu nomad territories includes "The country of the Shasu of Yhw(h)"
- Same designation repeated in Ramses II's temple at West Amara
- The Shasu are located in the Negev, Seir, Edom — "the south"
- Papyrus Anastasi VI mentions "Shasu of Edom" being allowed to sojourn in Egypt with their herds
- Papyrus Harris I (c. 1150): Pharaoh Ramses IV boasts "I destroyed the people of Seir among the Shasu tribes"
- Relief at Karnak (Seti I, c. 1290-1280): depicts Shasu with distinctive goatees and hairbands
- The Shasu were involved in copper mining in the Araba valley (Timna)

**The biblical evidence — four poems describing Yhwh "coming from the south":**
1. Deuteronomy 33:2 — "Yhwh came from Sinai, he shone forth from Seir, he was resplendent from Mount Paran"
2. Judges 5:4-5 — "Yhwh, when you came forth out of Seir, when you advanced from the land of Edom, the earth trembled"
3. Psalm 68:8-9, 18 — "O God, when you came forth at the head of your people, when you advanced over the arid land... the earth trembled"
4. Habakkuk 3:3 — "God comes from Teman, the Holy One comes from Mount Paran"

All are WAR poetry — Yhwh is described as a warrior god advancing from his southern home to fight for Israel. Judges 5 and Psalm 68 are so close they appear to be the same poem in two versions.

**The strange phrase *zeh sinay*:** Appears in both Judges 5:5 and Psalm 68:9. The grammar is odd — literally "this Sinai." Romer discusses but doesn't resolve: Is Sinai a divine name (a god, not just a mountain)? Is it a scribal gloss?

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Soleb inscriptions provide clear epigraphic evidence placing Yhwh in the south in the 14th century BCE. The four biblical poems provide independent corroboration from a different cultural tradition (Israelite war poetry). Romer's systematic elimination of alternative origin sites (Ebla, Mari, Ugarit) strengthens the case by showing the competition is weak.

## Stakes

If Yhwh originated in Edom/Midian, then the God of Israel is geographically foreign to Israel. The Bible's own story (God reveals himself in Egypt and the wilderness) preserves a distorted memory of this foreign origin. But the theological implication is sharper: Yhwh wasn't just one god among many in Canaan. He wasn't FROM Canaan at all. He was an import — a desert god adopted by highland tribes who then rewrote their own history to make him seem indigenous.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a Canaanite origin of Yhwh. The Ugaritic fragment (YW) is their best evidence, but Romer considers it too fragmentary. Scholars who date Judges 5 to the Hellenistic period would say the "southern origin" poems are late theological constructs, not historical memory.

**Alternative reading:** The southern origin poems could be late theological retrojections — scribes in the Persian period creating a mythic origin story for a god who was actually indigenous to Canaan. The Soleb inscriptions prove Yhwh was KNOWN in the south, not that he ORIGINATED there. He might have been a Canaanite god whose cult spread south and was later re-imported.

## Assessment

Romer's chapter is a model of accessible scholarship. He evaluates each piece of evidence honestly, discards the weak claims (Ebla, Mari), flags the uncertain (Ugarit), and builds the case around the strongest evidence (Soleb + biblical poetry). His conclusion is appropriately cautious: "The archaeological, epigraphic, and iconographic evidence all place the Shasu in the territory of Edom or Seir... and among these Shasu there might have been a group whose tutelary god was called 'Yhw.'" The word "might" is doing honest work.
