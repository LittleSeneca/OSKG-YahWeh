# tigay-nog-intro.1: The polytheism-monotheism debate lacked external controls

**Source:** [[Tigay — Introduction]] — Jeffrey H. Tigay, *You Shall Have No Other Gods* (1986), Introduction

## The Claim

The debate about pre-exilic Israelite religion was deadlocked between two positions, both based entirely on the biblical texts:

1. **The critical consensus:** Israelites were polytheistic until shortly before or after the exile. The biblical writers admit polytheism was rampant; therefore monotheism was a late development.

2. **Kaufmann/Albright:** Monotheism was essential to Mosaic religion from the start. Polytheism was fetishistic (using figurines without genuine mythology), and the prophets exaggerated.

"Both sides agreed that literary texts such as those in the Bible cannot be used for historical research before the historian evaluates what they are reliable for, but so long as this evaluation was based on evidence from within the Bible itself, with no external controls, it could not escape its notorious subjectivity."

Tigay's solution: Use INSCRIPTIONS as external controls. Gather ALL personal names and religious references from pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions and see what they indicate about actual religious practice.

## Evidence

- **The origin story (from Tigay's Preface):** Visiting the Israel Museum in 1978, Tigay examined the catalogue of Hebrew seals. "I expected to find on the Hebrew seals numerous examples of pagan personal names which were not found in the Bible because of the kind of scribal revisions which had turned Eshbaal into Ishbosheth and Beel(=Baal)i ada into Eliada. But the index showed so few examples of such names that I was forced to wonder whether they had been as common as I had assumed."

- **What previous scholarship had done:** "Only scattered details have been cited in such arguments, as their proponents found helpful. So far as I am aware, no attempt has been made to interpret either the pagan or the Yahwistic personal names in the light of the total onomastic picture from Israelite inscriptions."

- **The epistemological problem:** Both the critical consensus and Kaufmann/Albright used the same biblical texts — the same evidence interpreted through different hermeneutical lenses. Without an external dataset to adjudicate, the debate was circular. Each side's interpretation of the biblical evidence was unfalsifiable.

- **The solution architecture:** Tigay's study gathers ALL available pre-exilic Hebrew inscriptions — seals, bullae, ostraca, graffiti — and tabulates every personal name containing a theophoric element (Yahwistic, Baalistic, or other). If polytheism was widespread, pagan theophoric names should appear frequently in the epigraphic record. If Yahwism was dominant, Yahwistic names should predominate. The data would provide an objective test.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The methodological observation that the debate lacked external controls is simply a fact about the state of the field in 1986 — and Tigay's identification of onomastics as the available external dataset is sound.

## Stakes

If Tigay is right that onomastic evidence can adjudicate the debate, the entire polytheism-to-monotheism narrative is subject to empirical testing. The consensus built on literary evidence alone must answer to external data. If the onomastic evidence shows overwhelming Yahwistic dominance in the pre-exilic period, the consensus model collapses — or must be radically revised to account for why the epigraphic record contradicts the biblical picture of rampant polytheism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical consensus. Smith's rebuttal of Tigay (in his Introduction to *The Early History of God*) is the most direct counter-argument. Smith says personal names are "notoriously difficult to assess" and gives counter-examples from Ugarit and Punic culture. [[claim-tigay-onomastics-underestimates-pre-exilic-polytheism]] — Day's specific critique of Tigay's methodology. [[claim-dever-folk-polytheism-outlasted-monarchy-monotheism-book-religion]] — Dever's argument that epigraphic evidence reflects elite practice, not folk religion.

**Alternative reading:** Onomastic evidence is not a reliable proxy for religious belief — people name their children with traditional names regardless of personal religious practice. The onomasticon reflects naming conventions, not theology. A person named "Yahweh-is-gracious" might still worship Asherah at home. Tigay's method conflates naming patterns with religious commitment.

## Assessment

Tigay's methodological observation — that the debate needs external evidence — is unassailable. Whether his data actually proves what he claims it proves is the question for Chapter I. The critique that onomastics is "notoriously difficult to assess" (Smith) is valid as a caution but not as a dismissal — the question is whether the pattern of the data, taken as a whole, is more consistent with widespread Yahwism or widespread polytheism. Tigay's method deserves to be taken seriously even by those who disagree with his conclusions.
