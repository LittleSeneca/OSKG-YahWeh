# albertz-hir-3.7.3: Child Sacrifice and Queen of Heaven Cult Were Crisis Expressions of Family Piety

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.7 — Family Piety Under the Late Monarchy]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.7

## The Claim

Child sacrifice (offering children to "Molech") was practiced in Judah in the 7th century, including by kings Ahaz and Manasseh. The Queen of Heaven cult (Jer 7:18; 44:17-19, 25) was a female-dominated family cult involving baking cakes, pouring libations, and burning incense. Both represent family piety adapting to extreme conditions.

## Evidence

- Child sacrifice attested at Topheth in the Hinnom Valley (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31; 19:5f.; 32:35)
- Phoenician/Punic evidence (Carthage) demonstrates mlk-sacrifice was a real practice — not just polemical invention
- The Queen of Heaven cult: women took the lead; the cult promised prosperity and protection ("we had plenty of food, and prospered, and saw no evil," Jer 44:17)
- Jeremiah's polemic shows the cult was popular and persistent — the women explicitly defend it

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that child sacrifice and Queen of Heaven cult existed. MEDIUM on interpretation as "crisis expressions of family piety."

## Stakes

Albertz's model of internal religious pluralism reaches its limit here. The very family piety that was supposed to be "pre-political and pre-moral" has become deeply syncretistic. The Deuteronomic reform (§3.8) will attempt to suppress these family-level practices — marking the first systematic attempt by official religion to control family piety.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars argue Molech was not a deity but a type of sacrifice (the mlk-offering). The Carthage evidence is analogical, not directly Israelite. Kaufmann would read the Queen of Heaven cult as a foreign import with no genuine Israelite syncretism.

**Alternative reading:** Child sacrifice may have been a rare, extreme practice limited to royal circles (Ahaz, Manasseh), not a widespread family piety phenomenon. The Queen of Heaven may have been a local syncretistic development, not necessarily a "crisis" response.

## Assessment

Albertz's framing of these practices as "crisis expressions of family piety" is both humane and historically plausible. He doesn't moralize; he explains. These were desperate families trying to secure divine protection in conditions of Assyrian domination, deportations, and economic collapse. The Queen of Heaven women are not apostates but rational actors: "we prospered when we worshipped her; when we stopped, disaster came." The Deuteronomic reform's attempt to suppress these family-level practices marks the first systematic effort by official religion to control what had always been an autonomous sphere.
