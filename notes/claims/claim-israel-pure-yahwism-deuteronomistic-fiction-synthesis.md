# romer-inv-6-7.1: The biblical picture of pure Yahwism in Israel is Deuteronomistic fiction

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapters 6-7 — Cults of Israel and Judah]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapters 6-7 synthesis

## The Claim

The editors of Kings created a theological framework where good kings centralized worship at Jerusalem and bad kings tolerated other sanctuaries. This vision "does not correspond to historical reality." It was a 6th-century retrojection. The actual cult of Yhwh in Israel (930-722 BCE) was diverse, decentralized, and operated on three socio-religious levels (family/clan, local, national/royal). The bull iconography at Dan and Bethel was NOT foreign idolatry but indigenous Yahwistic tradition — El's bull imagery transferred to Yhwh.

## Evidence

**The Deuteronomistic distortion:**
- Manasseh, presented as the worst king of Judah, reigned 55 prosperous years under Assyrian protection. Hezekiah, praised as the best, pursued a suicidal anti-Assyrian policy that reduced Judah's territory. The Deuteronomists judged by theology, not results.
- Israel was the geopolitically dominant kingdom — not the apostate backwater the Bible portrays. "Israel contained fertile land... Judah was a tiny monarchy."
- "The idea of a great kingdom, united under David and Solomon, comes more from the imagination of the authors... than from any historical reality."

**The diversity of Yhwh worship:**
- Yhwh was worshipped in multiple local manifestations, each with its own epithet: "Yhwh of Samaria" and "Yhwh of Teman" (Kuntillet Ajrud), "Yhwh, god of Jerusalem" (Khirbet Beit Lei), "Yhwh at Hebron" (2 Samuel 15:7), "Yhwh in Zion" (Psalm 99:2), Yhwh at Bethel (Genesis 28:10-22)
- This was normal ANE practice — local manifestations of a national god. "Shared gods existed elsewhere in the Levant."

**Three levels of religion (critical framework):**
1. **Family/clan:** Personal gods, deified ancestors, pater familias as ritual mediator. No sanctuary needed.
2. **Local:** Open-air sanctuaries, stele (*maṣṣēbôt*), sacred pole (*'ăšērāh*). These are what the Bible polemicizes against as "on every hill and under every green tree."
3. **National/royal:** The king mediates, organized around the national god and associated deities.

**The bull iconography — the golden calves:**
- The Bible presents Jeroboam's bulls at Dan and Bethel (1 Kings 12:28-31) as the great apostasy of the north
- Römer argues they were **legitimate Yhwh symbols** — bull imagery for El (the original god) transferred to Yhwh
- The story in Exodus 32 (golden calf) was written to delegitimize northern bull worship retroactively: "The golden calf story is probably a big shade on the Northerners"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on diversity of Yhwh worship (inscriptional evidence clear). HIGH on Deuteronomistic distortion (scholarly consensus). MEDIUM on golden calves as legitimate Yhwh symbols (depends on dating Exodus 32 polemic).

## Stakes

The "golden calf" — the paradigmatic biblical story of idolatry — was probably a polemic against legitimate Yahwistic worship in the north. The sin wasn't worshipping a false god; it was worshipping Yhwh with the wrong symbols in the wrong places. This inverts the Bible's own moral framing.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives read the golden calf story as historical. Some scholars argue the bulls really did represent foreign gods (Baal or Egyptian imports). The dating of Exodus 32 is contested.

**Alternative reading:** The bulls at Dan and Bethel really were syncretistic — Baal worship masked as Yahwism. The Deuteronomistic condemnation was justified.

## Assessment

This is the most thorough articulation of Römer's argument that the Bible's history of Israel's religion is literary propaganda. The three-levels framework provides the sociological scaffolding that makes the diversity claim operational. The golden calves — the most famous biblical sin — were probably just Yhwh worship the southerners didn't like.
