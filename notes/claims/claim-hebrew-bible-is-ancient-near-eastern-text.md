# lewis-ocg-3.6: The Hebrew Bible IS an Ancient Near Eastern Text

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 3 — Methodology]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Quoting Peter Machinist: "a phrase such as 'the Bible and the ancient Near East' assumes that the former was not a part of the latter." Lewis argues that "the Hebrew Bible is at its core an ancient Near Eastern text that emerged out of an ancient Near Eastern world" and that this obvious point is "embraced" far less than it should be, especially in religious institutions that require "less and less Hebrew and Aramaic of their students."

## Evidence

- The Hebrew Bible was written in Hebrew and Aramaic — Northwest Semitic languages — by people living in Iron Age Israel and Judah
- It uses literary conventions (parallelism, divine council imagery, treaty forms, wisdom genres) shared with Ugaritic, Akkadian, and Egyptian literature
- Even post-historical-critical approaches (feminist, post-colonial) have grown at the expense of studying the Hebrew Bible as an ancient Near Eastern production in secular universities
- Declining language requirements in seminaries and religious studies departments mean fewer scholars can engage the text in its original languages
- The category error is embedded in how the field names itself: "the Bible and the ancient Near East" rather than "the Bible as an ancient Near Eastern text"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** Definitionally true — the Hebrew Bible was written in Hebrew and Aramaic by people living in Iron Age Israel and Judah, using literary conventions shared with Ugaritic, Akkadian, and Egyptian literature.

## Stakes

Whether the Hebrew Bible should be read primarily as Scripture (for communities of faith) or as an ancient artifact (for historians). Lewis is clearly on the artifact side, but he's generous enough to acknowledge that both approaches have value.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars who read the Bible primarily as divine revelation that transcends its cultural context. Kaufmann would be the extreme example. More moderately, scholars like Sommer argue for reading the Bible both ways simultaneously.

**Alternative reading:** Sommer's approach — reading the Bible BOTH as Scripture and as artifact — is more productive than choosing one over the other.

## Assessment
