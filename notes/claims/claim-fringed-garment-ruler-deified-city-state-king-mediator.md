# keel-ggi-3.4: The ruler wearing the garment with thick fringes represents the powerful Canaanite city-state kings who functioned as mediating figures between divine and earthly realms — perhaps deified after death

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch III — MB Temples Tel Kitan and Megiddo]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter III

## The Claim

"Schroer has correctly determined that the depiction of the ruler who is wearing the garment with thick fringes and appears all alone provides an indication of the strong position that the Canaanite city-state kings assumed toward the end of the Thirteenth Dynasty (ca. 1700–1650). Politically, this led to the founding of the Hyksos Dynasty in Egypt. Religiously, the sovereigns appear as some sort of mediating figures who bridge the gap between the divine and earthly worlds. Perhaps they were deified after they died."

## Evidence

- The ruler with the "garment with thick fringes" is the dominant figure on jasper-group cylinder seals — centrally positioned
- On Old Syrian cylinder seals (18th c.), the garment is worn by both worshipers and the worshiped — they appear side by side on a Hazor piece found in a 13th-century context
- In early MB IIB, the Syrian ruler appears often on steatite scarabs. A scarab from Barqai (ca. 1650) is the only piece where the ruler wears BOTH the fringed garment AND the tall Syrian cap
- The ruler appears alone — which Schroer interprets as evidence of the strong position of city-state kings
- "The use of their images on seal amulets should thus come as no surprise" — implying the ruler-image functioned apotropaically, like a deity-image
- One unique piece shows an enemy prostrate under the sovereign's throne
- Fragments of stelae from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem portray the ruler in raised relief — "perhaps these stelae were used in the cultic worship of the person who is depicted"
- On jasper-group and steatite scarabs, it is not the ruler but the WORSHIPER who holds the flower and/or raises a hand in greeting — the wearers "seem to have come to the conclusion that depicting their (own) piety and loyalty apparently would possibly guarantee them greater protection"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The iconographic evidence for the ruler's prominence is strong. The claim that he was "deified after death" is explicitly tentative ("perhaps"). The claim about worshipers preferring self-depiction over deity-depiction is speculative and based on inference from the relative frequency of motifs.

## Stakes

If MB IIB city-state kings were depicted on seals and stelae in ways normally reserved for deities, and if their images were used apotropaically, then we have evidence for a form of ruler cult in Canaan BEFORE the Israelite monarchy. This complicates the standard narrative that ruler divinization was a foreign (Egyptian/Mesopotamian) import that "corrupted" pure Yahwism. It also provides context for the later debate about whether the Davidic king was considered divine (Ps 2:7, Ps 110, Isa 9:5-6) — this would be a Canaanite inheritance, not an innovation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that Canaanite religion was strictly theocentric (gods only, no ruler cult) would push back. The biblical texts themselves polemicize against any blurring of the divine/human boundary — but that very polemic suggests the practice existed. Minimalists who doubt the historicity of the United Monarchy might see the MB IIB ruler cult as evidence that monarchy and ruler-exaltation were indigenous Canaanite phenomena that Israel inherited, not foreign imports.

**Alternative reading:** The ruler imagery might be political propaganda rather than religious devotion. Wearing a seal with the king's image could signify political loyalty, not worship. The enemies-under-the-throne motif is a standard Near Eastern royal trope (compare Pharaoh smiting enemies) that does not necessarily imply divinization. The "garment with thick fringes" might identify an office rather than a specific individual.

## Assessment

The authors present this claim cautiously, which is appropriate. The evidence is suggestive but not definitive. The most important observation is the shift they identify: on earlier cylinder seals, the fringed-garment ruler is the central figure receiving worship; on later scarabs, the worshiper himself appears, displacing the ruler-image. This suggests a growing emphasis on personal piety in the later MB IIB — the seal-wearer wants to depict HIMSELF as a pious worshiper rather than relying on the ruler's mediating power. This is a genuinely interesting diachronic observation that fits their seismograph methodology.
