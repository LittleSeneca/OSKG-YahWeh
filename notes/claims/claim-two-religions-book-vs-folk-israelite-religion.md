# dever-dghw-intro.2: Ancient Israel had TWO religions — "Book religion" and "folk religion"

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife? Archaeology and Folk Religion in Ancient Israel* (2005), Introduction

## The Claim

The religion described in the Hebrew Bible ("Book religion") was the theology of a tiny elite — priests, scribes, reformers. It was monotheistic, aniconic, centralized in Jerusalem, and focused on obedience to written law. The religion actually practiced by most Israelites ("folk religion") was polytheistic, image-rich, domestic, and focused on fertility, healing, and survival. These two religions coexisted, overlapped, and eventually the Book religion won — but only at the very end of the monarchy, and only on paper. "Folk religion was, in fact, everything that Josiah and his reformist colleagues wanted to put a stop to."

## Evidence

**Dever's chart of contrasts:**

| Book Religion | Folk Religion |
|--------------|---------------|
| Idealism / theology | "Real life" practice |
| National / centralized | Local / family-centered |
| Priests, prophets, sages | Ordinary people, especially women |
| Jerusalem Temple | Local shrines, high places, homes |
| Male deity alone | Female deity (Asherah) as consort |
| Abstract principles / morality | "Care and feeding of the gods" |
| Written / literate | Oral / popular |
| Orthodoxy (right belief) | Orthopraxy (right practice) |
| "Intellectual" | Emotional / experiential |
| Exclusionary | Inclusionary |
| Elitist | Populist |

**Dever's definition of "Book religion"** (following Karel van der Toorn): The canonical religion of the literary tradition preserved in the Hebrew Bible. It represents "their final crystallization" of themes long in development — but it was never the religion of most Israelites.

**Dever's definition of "folk religion":** What ordinary people actually practiced — at home, at local shrines, with figurines and standing stones and asherah poles.

**Terminology survey:** Dever reviews how other scholars have named this distinction — Holladay ("established/State" vs. "distributed/local"), Ackerman ("popular religion"), Albertz ("official religion" vs. "family religion"), Berlinerblau ("official" vs. "popular"), Keel/Uehlinger (family/local/national levels), Miller (State vs. family), Smith ("popular religion"), Zevit (pluralism). He settles on "Book religion" vs. "folk religion."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH that such a distinction existed (every scholar in the field recognizes some version of it). MEDIUM on how sharp the distinction was in practice (the categories are heuristic, not absolute — Dever acknowledges this).

## Stakes

If the religion of the Bible was never the religion of most Israelites, then what the Bible condemns as "idolatry" and "apostasy" was actually the normal, legitimate religion of God's people. The prophets weren't calling Israel back to its roots — they were calling Israel to abandon its roots for something new. The religion you inherited as "biblical" is the innovation, not the original.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives who argue the biblical religion WAS the original and folk religion represents later corruption. Minimalists who say we can't reconstruct "folk religion" at all from the available evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The two-religions distinction may be too binary. There was probably a spectrum — from fully Yahwistic monotheism at one end to fully polytheistic practice at the other, with most people somewhere in between, mixing elements of both depending on context.

## Assessment

Dever's binary is a useful heuristic but probably oversimplifies. He acknowledges this himself: "These categories are not rigid, of course. And they are somewhat artificial, since the ancients would not have recognized such distinctions." The two-religions thesis is a lens, not a fact. Used carefully, it illuminates. Used dogmatically, it distorts.
