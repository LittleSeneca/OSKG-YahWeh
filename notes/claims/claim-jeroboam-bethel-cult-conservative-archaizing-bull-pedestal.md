# albertz-hir-3.3-3.4.5: Jeroboam's Bethel cult was a conservative, archaizing alternative to Jerusalem

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.3-3.4 — State Cults of Jerusalem and Bethel]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.4

## The Claim

"Jeroboam, who had been guided by the Yahweh traditions from the time before the state, also wanted to develop an archaizing alternative to the state cult of Jerusalem." Bethel had "old tribal legitimation" (unlike Jerusalem). The bull was a pedestal for the invisible Yahweh — parallel to the cherubim throne in Jerusalem, not a divine image. The cult cry "Behold your god, Israel, who brought you out of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28) was "a regular cultic cry with which the cultic image was presented to the assembled community."

## Evidence

- Bethel had pre-Israelite El-worship (El-Bethel, Gen 31:13; 35:7); the bull symbolism was inherited from El ("Bull El" is a Ugaritic epithet)
- A bronze bull figurine from Iron Age I was found in the Samarian hill country — evidence of pre-monarchic bull iconography in the North
- The Deuteronomistic description is systematically polemical: plural "calves" to charge polytheism; "calf/heifer" ('ēgel) to discredit; interpretation as "divine images" to charge idolatry
- Near Eastern parallels: the storm god Hadad stands on a bull — the bull is a pedestal, not the deity
- The cult cry was in the singular (contra the MT's polemical plural) — "This is your god... who brought you out of Egypt"
- Unlike Jerusalem (hidden in the holy of holies), Bethel's cult symbol was visible to the community — people "blew kisses to it" (Hos 10:5; 13:2)
- Jeroboam refused to build a permanent residence, maintained multiple sanctuaries (Dan, Shechem, Penuel), didn't intervene in local priesthoods — all "continuity with conditions existing before the state"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The Deuteronomistic polemic is clearly tendentious, and Albertz's reconstruction (bull as pedestal, singular, Exodus-oriented) is standard critical scholarship. The populist character of the Bethel cult is well-supported by Hosea's descriptions.

## Stakes

If Bethel was the legitimate conservative Yahwistic alternative to Jerusalem's syncretistic innovations, then the entire Deuteronomistic narrative (which portrays Jeroboam as the arch-apostate) is propaganda — and the Northern Kingdom's religion was more faithful to pre-state Yahwism than the Southern Kingdom's.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who accept the Deuteronomistic portrayal of Jeroboam's bulls as idolatrous (Kaufmann reads them as fetishism). Those who argue the bull was indeed an image of Yahweh, not just a pedestal (the distinction may have been lost in popular practice).

**Alternative reading:** The bull may have been intended as a divine image from the start — the pedestal/image distinction is a modern scholarly construct. The Deuteronomistic polemic, while tendentious in its rhetoric, may accurately identify the bulls as a theological problem.

**Contradicts:**
- [[claim-golden-calves-yhwh-worship-not-pagan-idolatry]] — Kaufmann agrees the bulls were Yahwistic, not pagan, but reads them as legitimate bull-iconography for Yahweh; Albertz reads them as deliberately archaizing (protest against Jerusalem)

**Challenged by:**

- The "bull as divine image" reading (pedestal/image distinction is artificial)

- Bronze bull figurine (Iron I, Samarian hill country)

## Assessment

This is the strongest claim in §3.4. Albertz's reading against the Deuteronomistic polemic is convincing, and the reconstruction of Bethel as a conservative, Exodus-oriented, populist alternative to Jerusalem is well-supported. The contrast between Jerusalem's hidden, priest-controlled cult and Bethel's visible, community-accessible cult is analytically sharp. The tension with Albertz's own claim 6 (Bethel's populism led to Baalization) is real but not fatal — it's possible for a cult to begin as a conservative reform and later slide into syncretism.
