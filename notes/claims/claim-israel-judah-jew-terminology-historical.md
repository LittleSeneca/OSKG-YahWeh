# romer-inv-intro.6: Terminology matters — "Israel," "Judah," "Jew," and "Canaan" have specific historical meanings

**Source:** [[Romer — Introduction]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Introduction

## The Claim

Using the right terms prevents retrojecting later ideas onto earlier periods. Each term has a specific historical referent that must not be confused.

## Evidence

### Romer's definitions
- **"Israel"** originally meant the northern kingdom (capital Samaria) until 722. Only after Assyria destroyed it did "Israel" become a theological term for all Yhwh-worshippers. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1210) attests "Israel" as an ethnic group in Ephraim, not a kingdom.
- **"Judah"** was the southern kingdom (capital Jerusalem) until 587, then the Persian province "Yehud."
- **"Jews" / "Judaism"** cannot be spoken of before the end of the Persian period (4th century BCE). Before that: "Israelites" or "Judeans."
- **"Canaan"** is a vague term for Syria-Palestine west of the Jordan. Sometimes neutral geographic term, sometimes pejorative ("Canaanites" = indigenous population to be displaced).

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** These distinctions are widely accepted in critical scholarship and are essential for clarity.

## Stakes

If you retroject "Judaism" onto pre-exilic Israel, you create a false continuity. The religion of the Israelite monarchy was not Judaism — it was Israelite religion, which was polytheistic in practice and included elements (Asherah, high places, figurines) that later Judaism condemned. The "Jews" didn't exist yet.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish and Christian readers who use "Israel" to refer to all Yhwh-worshippers across all periods. Scholars who use "Israelite religion" and "early Judaism" interchangeably.

## Assessment

These terminological distinctions are essential and well-established. Romer does the reader a service by defining them upfront. The most significant is the "Jews/Judaism" distinction — the religion of monarchic Israel is NOT Judaism, and calling it that creates a false continuity that obscures the radical transformation that exile produced. This is a helpful corrective to both popular and some scholarly discourse.
