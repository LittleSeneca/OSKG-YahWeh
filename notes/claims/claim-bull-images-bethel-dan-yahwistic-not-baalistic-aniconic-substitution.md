# keel-ggi-5.7: The Bull Images at Bethel and Dan Were Yahwistic — the Aniconic Turn Begins with Attribute Animal Substitution

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part V — Iron Age IIB — Baal El Yahweh and His Asherah]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part V

## The Claim

"According to the Old Testament, worship of Yahweh that included the use of one or more bull calf images (*'ēgel*, *'ăgālîm*) was the officially established state religion in the Northern Kingdom." The bull images that the biblical texts polemicize against (1 Kings 12; Hosea) were not foreign Baal worship — they were Yahwistic. The bull was Yahweh's attribute animal, just as it had been El's in Ugarit. The biblical polemic is not against worship of the wrong god but against worship of the right god through the wrong image.

## Evidence

- 1 Kings 12:28 — Jeroboam's formula: "Here are your gods/God, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt" uses the same language as the golden calf episode (Exodus 32), indicating Yahwistic identification
- The bull as divine pedestal/attribute animal has deep roots in Syrian/Canaanite iconography — El is called "Bull El" (*tr 'il*) at Ugarit
- The general trend in Iron Age IIA → IIB: "anthropomorphic deities recede and are replaced by their attribute animals and entities" — the aniconic turn begins here, not in the exile
- "Lord of the Ostriches" and "Lord of the Caprids" type figures decline; the animals alone appear, without the anthropomorphic deity
- This substitution of attribute animal for deity is a key mechanism in the development of aniconism — the animal becomes the symbol that stands for the god

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH for the Yahwistic identification of the bull images. MEDIUM for the aniconic trajectory claim — it is a pattern but the timing is debated.

## Stakes

The aniconic turn in Israelite religion is usually dated to the exile or later. Keel & Uehlinger's evidence pushes it back to Iron Age IIA-IIB — centuries earlier. If anthropomorphic deity images were already being replaced by attribute animals in the 10th-8th centuries, the later "image ban" has deep cultural roots, not just theological innovation. This connects to Romer's argument about the missing statue of Yahweh (Ch 8) — perhaps there was no statue because the aniconic turn had already shifted representation from anthropomorphic to symbolic.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the aniconic turn late (exilic/post-exilic). Also those who interpret the bull images as evidence of Baal worship rather than Yahwism.

**Alternative reading:** The bull images could represent a syncretistic Yahweh-Baal fusion, not pure Yahwism. The biblical polemic against them might be justified in seeing them as Canaanizing corruption.

## Assessment

The Yahwistic identification of the Bethel/Dan bulls is well-supported. The attribute-animal substitution pattern is genuinely interesting and provides an *internal* developmental mechanism for Israelite aniconism — it was not imposed from outside (no "Moses brought monotheism/aniconism from Midian") but grew organically from within Canaanite/Syrian iconographic conventions. This is a much more plausible historical reconstruction than the revolutionary model.
