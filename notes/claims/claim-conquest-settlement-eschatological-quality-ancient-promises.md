# kaufmann-ri-7.6: Conquest-Settlement Had a Genuine Eschatological Quality

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VII — The Conquest and Settlement]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VII

## The Claim

"The military success and the settlement gave rise to pride and a sense of well-being. The period had an eschatological quality; ancient promises were being fulfilled. This was 'the end of days,' the final, permanent bliss. All the ancient visionary and prophetic literature is informed by this sentiment." The blessings of Jacob (Gen 49) and Moses (Deut 33) reflect pre-monarchic conditions and give "clear expression" to this eschatological confidence. Gen 49 is "the earliest vision dealing expressly with the 'end of days.'"

## Evidence

- **Gen 49 and Deut 33 reflect pre-monarchic conditions:** "The tribes fight, but are always victorious; they have settled and enjoy the bounty of the earth."

- **Deut 33:26-29 as eschatological climax:** "YHWH has driven out the enemy and has commanded, 'Destroy!' He has given Israel a land of grain and wine, and has settled it in insulated security. Happy Israel! There is none like God, nor any like Israel, 'a people saved through YHWH.' Its enemies will dwindle away before it and Israel will tread on their backs."

- **The horizon is bright:** "Despite an occasional cloud," the vision is ultimately one of confidence and fulfillment.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The observation that early poetry has a triumphant, even utopian quality is valid. But calling it "eschatology" is arguably anachronistic — eschatology proper (a definitive future transformation of the world order) develops in the prophetic period as a response to the failure of the monarchy. The "end of days" framework in Gen 49 is widely regarded as a later editorial frame, not the original Sitz im Leben of the tribal sayings. The claim is underargued (less than a page) and depends on contested dating.

## Stakes

Whether Israel's eschatological hope developed in response to later historical *failure* (the prophetic critique, the fall of the kingdoms) or was already present as a celebratory mode in the earliest period of success. This matters for dating the origins of Israelite eschatology.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Most critical scholarship dates Gen 49 and Deut 33 — in their current form — considerably later than the settlement period. The blessings of Jacob contain references that seem to presuppose the monarchy (Gen 49:10, "the scepter shall not depart from Judah"). The "end of days" language (*be'acharit hayamim*) is typically associated with later prophetic eschatology. Cross would see the Divine Warrior hymns of the early period (Exod 15, Judg 5) as hymnic celebration rather than "eschatology" in the developed sense.

**Alternative reading:** The early poetry DOES contain a confident, triumphant mode — but calling it "eschatology" is anachronistic. Eschatology proper develops in the prophetic period as a response to the failure of the monarchy and the destruction of the kingdoms. The early poetry's confidence is *prospective* in a different sense — it celebrates current triumphs as evidence of YHWH's power and Israel's election.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's eschatology claim is creative but underargued — it takes up less than a page in the chapter and feels like an appendix rather than a fully developed argument. He's right that Deut 33:26-29 has a triumphant, even utopian quality. But whether this reflects the "eschatological quality" of the conquest generation or the retrospective idealization of a later poet is not established. The claim that Gen 49 is "the earliest vision dealing expressly with the 'end of days'" depends on dating Gen 49 to the Judges period, which is contested. This is the weakest sustained argument in the chapter.
