# kaufmann-ri-9.5: Israel had no ancestor cult — the realm of death was de-religionized

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"The realm of the dead, the rites connected with death and burial, as well as the destiny of the soul in the other world, play no part in the religion of YHWH. This is one of the most astonishing features of Israelite religion." Although the dead were believed to exist (Sheol, shades), "biblical faith draws no religious or moral inferences from this notion." Funeral rites "lost their pagan meaning in Israel without striking fresh roots in the new faith." Caring for the dead is not a cult act but "steadfast loyalty" — ethical behavior, not religious devotion. The dead have no power to help or harm; they are tranquil, asleep. "Nowhere is ancestor-worship or a cult of the dead referred to."

## Evidence

- Deuteronomy 26:14 forbids only consecrated food to the dead — profane food gifts are not offensive, proving such gifts were not regarded as offerings
- The dead is 'elohim only in the narrow mantic sense (invoked by necromancers) — "no other divine quality is referred to"
- No cultic rites at graves of national heroes or ancestors are mentioned
- The dead have no demonic power — the concept of an autonomous demonic realm is "altogether lacking in the Bible"
- The rite of the heifer (Deut. 21:1 ff.) no longer aims at appeasing the ghost but is directed "wholly toward YHWH"
- The blood of a murdered man "cries out to YHWH" — it doesn't seek vengeance on its own; YHWH is the avenger (Gen. 4:10)
- The famine in David's time comes from YHWH's wrath, not the revenge of murdered Gibeonites (2 Sam 21)
- Self-mutilation, shaving, and tattooing were banned — but not rending clothing or fasting, because their original meaning was forgotten
- The Bible considers treatment of the dead "a matter of universal, human culture... altogether secular"
- "The dead do not praise him; those who go down to the pit do not call upon him" (Isa. 38:18; Pss. 6:6; 30:10)
- "Biblical religion knows nothing of a judgment of souls in an afterlife" — a central motif of Egyptian faith
- Why did biblical faith not create a monotheistic afterlife? Because "the especial intransigence of these pagan materials" made them impossible to assimilate without importing pagan concepts (infernal gods, dying gods, apotheosis)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The descriptive claim that the Hebrew Bible lacks an elaborated afterlife theology is genuinely striking; but the claim that this characterized POPULAR religion is undermined by evidence of necromancy and grave offerings.

## Stakes

One of Kaufmann's strongest arguments for Israelite distinctiveness. If Israel truly de-religionized death — while Egypt built pyramids and Mesopotamia had elaborate netherworld theology — this IS a radical departure requiring explanation. But was it popular or elite?

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who find evidence of an Israelite ancestor cult in the archaeological record. Elizabeth Bloch-Smith argues that Judahite burial practices (family tombs, grave goods, food offerings) represent a functioning ancestor cult. Karel van der Toorn finds ancestor cult as the background to teraphim and the reverence for family tombs. Kaufmann's claim that "nowhere is ancestor-worship referred to" is true only if we accept the biblical texts' own framing.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical SILENCE about ancestor cult is itself evidence of its existence — the texts suppress and polemicize against practices that were real and widespread. The very prohibitions (Deut. 18:11; Lev. 19:31) prove people were doing these things. Kaufmann reads the silence as evidence of absence; critical scholarship reads it as evidence of suppression.

## Assessment

This is where Kaufmann's most original insight (the de-religionization of death) and his most glaring blind spot coexist. He IS right that the Hebrew Bible is remarkable for its lack of developed afterlife theology — compare Egypt's Book of the Dead. This absence requires explanation. But his claim that this reflects POPULAR religion rather than elite theological exclusion is undermined by the very evidence he cites: necromancy was practiced, food was given to the dead, people cared desperately about proper burial. The popular religion MAY have been more concerned with the dead than Kaufmann allows, and the biblical silence may reflect a theological program of suppression, not popular indifference.
