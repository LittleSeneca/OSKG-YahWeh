# keel-ggi-4.1: Bronze and Terra-Cotta Anthropomorphic Deity Figurines Cease Production in Iron Age IIA

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part IV — Iron Age IIA]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part IV

## The Claim

The rich Canaanite tradition of bronze deity figurines — male "striking gods" and female "naked goddesses" — stops during Iron Age IIA (ca. 1000-925 BCE). Every anthropomorphic bronze found in Iron II contexts proves on examination to be a residual Late Bronze Age piece, not new production. "It is more important, when one assesses the piece religio-historically, to note that god-figurines of this type were no longer being produced in Iron Age II." The famous "no graven images" commandment has a material precursor — the aniconic turn was not a theological innovation imposed from above but a cultural shift already underway by the 10th century.

## Evidence

- A bronze clenched fist (4 cm) from Jerusalem Str. 14 (10th century): originally part of a ~38 cm "striking god" statue. But this separate fist is so rare it must have been a repair piece, and analogous artifacts from Megiddo and Kamid el-Loz are LB II. "The bronze fist from Jerusalem ought to be dated to Late Bronze Age II"
- An enthroned blessing figure from Tell el-'Oreme Str. II (8th century): dated to LB IIB, "found as an antique" in an Iron II context, perhaps in a "cultic room at the gate" (cf. 2 Kgs 23:8)
- A figurine from Tel Zeror (8th century house): possibly Iron I origin "at the very latest"
- A Hazor Str. IX B figurine (late 10th century): "neither an interpretation nor a date for it are possible"
- "Anthropomorphic depictions of the chief deities are attested in literary texts that refer to neighboring states (Dagon in Philistia, Milcom in Ammon)... but they were apparently no longer produced in Israel during Iron Age IA"
- Bronze goddess images "had not been produced in Canaan since the end of the Middle Bronze Age"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The converging dating of every single Iron II anthropomorphic bronze as an heirloom is statistically significant. This is not a gap in preservation — it is a genuine cessation of production.

## Stakes

This pushes the aniconic turn timeline centuries earlier than the exile-centered model most biblical scholars assume. The image ban was not imposed from above — it was already underway culturally by the 10th century.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Galling (1977) and others who argue for Iron II production of anthropomorphic bronzes. Scholars who date the image ban to the exile or later.

**Alternative reading:** The absence could reflect economic factors (bronze became scarce/expensive after the Bronze Age collapse) rather than religious aniconism. Different media might have been used (wood, which does not survive).

## Assessment

The economic argument is weakened by the fact that glyptic art ALSO shows the decline. Seals were cheap and mass-produced — if people wanted deity images, they would appear on seals. They don't. The shift is cultural, not economic.
