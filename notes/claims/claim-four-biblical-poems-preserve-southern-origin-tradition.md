# romer-inv-2.2: Four biblical poems preserve an ancient tradition of Yhwh's southern origin

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 2 — Geographic Origin]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 2: The Geographic Origin of Yhwh

## The Claim

Four poetic texts — Deuteronomy 33:2, Judges 5:4-5, Psalm 68:8-9, and Habakkuk 3:3 — all describe Yhwh as coming "from the south." These are war poems. They preserve a tradition of Yhwh as a warrior god advancing from Edom/Seir/Teman/Paran to fight for Israel.

## Evidence

### The four poems:

| Text | Yhwh's Origin Point | Description |
|------|--------------------|-------------|
| **Deut 33:2** | Sinai, Seir, Mount Paran | "Yhwh came from Sinai, he shone forth from Seir, he was resplendent from Mount Paran" |
| **Judg 5:4-5** | Seir, Edom | "Yhwh, when you came forth out of Seir, when you advanced from the land of Edom, the earth trembled, the sky quaked, the clouds poured down water" |
| **Ps 68:8-9** | "Arid land" (*yesimon*) | Nearly identical to Judges 5, but with Yhwh replaced by Elohim (part of the "Elohistic Psalter" — Pss 42-83 systematically replaced Yhwh with Elohim). "It is clear that this originally read: 'Yhwh, the god of Israel.'" |
| **Hab 3:3** | Teman, Mount Paran | "God comes from Teman, the Holy One comes from Mount Paran. His splendor covers the sky, his praise fills the earth." |

### The relationship between Judges 5 and Psalm 68:

- "The two texts are in large part identical." Both describe Yhwh "coming out" to battle. Both use the same sequence: address in second person → description in third person. Both use the strange phrase *zeh sinay* ("this Sinai").
- Romer argues Judges 5 is the older version, which Psalm 68 reworks and expands.
- Psalm 68:13-14 ("Would you linger in the camp?") alludes to Judges 5:16 ("Why did you remain with the baggage?"). Psalm 68:12's celestial army evokes the combat of the stars in Judges 5:20.
- **"Judges 5, in any case, is at least in its primitive form often considered to be one of the oldest texts in the Hebrew Bible."**

### The dating problem (Romer is honest about this):

- "Many experts today consider this text to be particularly archaic, with origins in the period even before the institution of the Israelite monarchy." BUT "Others, however, claim that it is a late text, composed on the basis of the narration of this episode in the previous chapter (Judg. 4) in order to give that narration a poetic conclusion."
- "So the current dating of the song in Judges 5 varies from the twelfth century to the Hellenistic era."
- "It is extremely difficult to know whether the text refers to a real historical event or whether, as some have suggested, it is rather a mythological text."
- The Hebrew "is either archaic or consciously archaizing."
- The names are suspicious: Deborah = "bee," Barak = "thunderbolt," Yael = "goat" — parallels with Greek mythology (Melissa the bee nursing Zeus, Amalthea the goat giving him milk).

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH that the poems preserve a genuine tradition of southern origin (the consistency across four independent poems is hard to explain as coincidence). MEDIUM on the dating (Romer himself acknowledges the range from 12th century to Hellenistic). If the poems are late, they tell us about later theology, not early history.

## Stakes

If these are among the oldest texts in the Bible, they preserve Israel's own memory that Yhwh came from outside — from the south, from Edom. The Bible itself testifies to Yhwh's non-Israelite origin. You don't need critical scholarship to reach this conclusion; you just need to read the poetry carefully.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the poems to the Persian or Hellenistic period. The mythological reading (the names are symbolic, not historical).

**Alternative reading:** The "coming from the south" tradition might reflect a later theological construction — scribes in Jerusalem creating a dramatic origin story for their god. The poems would then tell us about 6th-5th century theology, not 12th century history.

## Assessment

The consistency of the southern origin tradition across FOUR independent poems (Deuteronomy 33, Judges 5, Psalm 68, Habakkuk 3) is impressive. But the dating problem is real. If Schmid is right that the Pentateuch is Persian-period, then these poems — or at least their final form — are much later than the events they seem to describe. Romer's optimism about early dating is the vulnerable point.
