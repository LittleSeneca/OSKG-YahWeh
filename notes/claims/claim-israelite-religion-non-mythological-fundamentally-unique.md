# kaufmann-ri-1.10: Israelite religion is non-mythological — and this is what makes it unique

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"It is precisely this non-mythological aspect that makes it unique in world history; this was the source of its universal appeal." "The Bible's ignorance of the meaning of paganism is at once the basic problem and the most important clue to the understanding of biblical religion. It underscores as nothing else can the gulf that separates biblical religion from paganism."

## Evidence

Kaufmann argues that "fossil-remains of ancient myths cannot obscure the basic difference between Israelite religion and paganism." The Bible is not just monotheistic — it's non-mythological. There are no theogonies, no divine biographies, no cosmic dramas involving multiple gods. Even the mythological fragments (Rahab, Leviathan, the dragon) feature only YHWH as the active divine being — "There are no active foreign gods."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** MEDIUM on the claim that Israelite religion is "non-mythological" in a distinctive way; LOW on the claim that this was true "from the beginning" and makes Israelite religion absolutely unique. The non-mythological character is the endpoint of a centuries-long process, not the starting point.

## Stakes

Kaufmann's ultimate thesis. He's not just arguing for early monotheism — he's arguing that Israelite religion is categorically different from every other ancient religion, not just in its number of gods but in its entire conceptual framework. If he's right, comparative study of Israelite religion within its ANE context is fundamentally misguided.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire comparative project of modern biblical scholarship. Smith demonstrates that Israelite religion shares fundamental categories with Ugaritic/Canaanite religion: divine council, theomachy, divine family, anthropomorphic deity, temple as divine dwelling. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism through the transformation of these shared categories. Day documents specific mythological borrowings.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite religion IS distinctive — but distinctiveness doesn't require absolute categorical difference. Israelite religion shared fundamental categories with its neighbors while developing these categories in increasingly distinctive directions. The non-mythological character Kaufmann observes is the *endpoint* of a centuries-long process, not the starting point. The Bible's non-mythological character is an achievement, not an inheritance.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is right that there's something genuinely distinctive about the biblical conception of deity, and the comparative approach can sometimes flatten this distinctiveness into sameness. Israelite religion DID move in a direction that was unusual in the ancient world. But Kaufmann's insistence on ABSOLUTE difference "from the beginning" forces him into a framework that can't accommodate the evidence of continuity. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, the figurines, the Ugaritic parallels — these are not "fossil remains" that can be dismissed as insignificant. The "non-mythological" character of the Bible is a literary-theological accomplishment, not a primitive inheritance. Kaufmann has mistaken the destination for the point of departure.
