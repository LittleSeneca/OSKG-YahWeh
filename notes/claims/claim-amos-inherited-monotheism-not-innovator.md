# kaufmann-ri-11.1: Amos was not an innovator of monotheism or ethical religion — he inherited both from popular religion and Torah

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XI — Amos and Hosea]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XI

## The Claim

Amos's monotheism and ethical demands are not argued for or introduced as novelties — they are the assumed starting point. "From the early religion he inherits the conception of YHWH as lord of history." The notion "that the destiny of all nations was decided by YHWH (9:7) was an old idea that Amos does not have to argue." The prophetic demand for social justice "echo[es], for the most part, the ancient covenant laws." Amos draws on the Exodus, the forty-year wandering, Sodom and Gomorrah, the plagues of Egypt, "YHWH's Torah and laws" (2:4) — all inherited tradition. He "utilized styles and material that were ready at hand" and "feels himself a link in the succession of Israelite prophecy."

## Evidence

- Amos uses standard prophetic formulas ("Thus said YHWH," "Hear," "In that day") that predate him
- Wisdom forms (ascending numbers "three...four," epigram in question form) show cultural inheritance, not innovation
- The elegy in *qina* meter (5:1f) uses an established genre
- Amos 9:7's assertion that YHWH brought Philistines from Caphtor and Aram from Kir is presented without argument — it's a given, not a thesis

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Strong on the descriptive claim (Amos uses inherited forms); weak on proving this means the tradition was monotheistic from the start. Drawing on tradition doesn't tell us whether that tradition was monotheistic or the product of later redaction.

## Stakes

If Amos is an innovator, then monotheism and ethical universalism emerge in the 8th century as prophetic creations. If he's a traditionalist, then monotheism predates classical prophecy by centuries — exactly the thesis Kaufmann needs to overturn Wellhausen.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus reads Amos as a genuine innovator. The very fact that Amos has to assert YHWH's rule over other nations (9:7) could indicate this was NOT an old, settled idea but a contested one. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheistic rhetoric; Amos would be an early stage, not a late traditionalist.

**Alternative reading:** Amos DID inherit traditions, but he transformed them radically. Inheriting the "day of YHWH" concept and INVERTING it (from Israel's vindication to Israel's judgment) IS innovation. Inheriting covenant laws and ELEVATING social morality over cult IS innovation. Kaufmann conflates "using inherited materials" with "not innovating," which is a category error.

## Assessment

Kaufmann scores a real point: Amos demonstrably draws on prior traditions and doesn't argue for monotheism as if it were a new idea. But this doesn't require Kaufmann's conclusion that the traditions were monotheistic FROM THE BEGINNING. Amos could be drawing on traditions that had developed in a more monotheistic direction over the preceding centuries. The presence of inherited tradition is compatible with both Kaufmann's model and the critical model — it's the DATING and CONTENT of those traditions that's contested.
