# day-ygc-8.1: Tigay's onomastic argument for widespread pre-exilic monolatry is methodologically flawed

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 8 — Conclusion — Rise of Monotheism]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 8

## The Claim

Tigay's conclusion that only a small proportion of Israelites worshipped other deities — based on the 16:1 ratio of Yahwistic to pagan theophoric names in inscriptions — "need not imply that Yahweh was the only deity worshipped but is equally compatible with the idea that Yahweh was rather the most important deity worshipped" (p. 228). The net effect of several caveats "is to suggest that the worship of other gods and goddesses was more frequent than he allows" (p. 228).

## Evidence

1. **Chief god, not only god:** A 16:1 ratio is "equally compatible with the idea that Yahweh was rather the most important deity worshipped. Those who worshipped other gods and goddesses surely still saw Yahweh as the chief god, with the other deities being regarded as subordinate members of his pantheon" (p. 228)
2. **Goddesses invisible in onomastics:** "The names of female deities hardly ever occur in Ugaritic personal names, even though we know that Asherah, Anat and Astarte were prominent goddesses at Ugarit" (p. 228). If this is true of Ugarit — where goddesses were undeniably important — then the absence of goddess names in Hebrew onomastics tells us nothing about goddess worship. Meanwhile, Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom, and thousands of pillar figurines all attest Asherah worship in the very period and region from which Tigay's epigraphic material comes
3. **Geographical and temporal limitations:** Most epigraphic names come from Judah, not the Northern Kingdom (where Samaria ostraca show Baal theophoric names). Most material is from the late monarchy — earlier centuries might show different patterns
4. **Graham Davies' review:** Tigay overestimates Yahwistic names and underestimates pagan names — the ratio may be "nearer ten to one rather than sixteen to one" (p. 229)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Day's Ugaritic counter-example is devastating — if goddesses are invisible in Ugaritic onomastics despite being major deities, the argument from absence in Hebrew names collapses. The pillar figurine evidence (contemporary with Tigay's inscriptions) is an independent archaeological contradiction.

## Stakes

Tigay's study is the strongest empirical challenge to the consensus model of widespread pre-exilic polytheism. If Tigay is right, the OT's picture of rampant apostasy IS rhetorical exaggeration and monotheism was already the popular norm centuries before Deutero-Isaiah. Day's rebuttal preserves the consensus while acknowledging Tigay's genuine contribution.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** J.H. Tigay (*You Shall Have No Other Gods*, 1986). Mark Smith and others who accept Tigay's data more readily.

**Alternative reading:** Tigay's onomastic data, combined with the epigraphic silence about other gods in personal inscriptions, suggests genuine popular monolatry rather than elite-imposed rhetoric. The 16:1 ratio is too lopsided to reflect a genuinely polytheistic population.

## Assessment

Day's Ugaritic counterexample is so elegant it almost feels unfair to Tigay. The point about goddess names being absent at Ugarit — where we KNOW goddesses were worshipped from mythological texts — demonstrates that onomastics simply cannot measure goddess devotion. This is a fundamental methodological limitation of Tigay's approach that Day exploits effectively.
