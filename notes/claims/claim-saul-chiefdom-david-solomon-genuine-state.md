# albertz-hir-3.1-3.2.2: Saul was a chiefdom; David and Solomon created a genuine state

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.1-3.2 — Formation of Monarchy and Legitimation Crisis]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.1

## The Claim

Saul's kingship was an "experiment of a charismatic 'chiefdom' close to the people" that "was still compatible with the structure of tribal organization." David, by contrast, built a mercenary army, captured Jerusalem as a neutral capital, created a bureaucracy, and imposed taxation and forced labor — transforming Israel into "a sacral monarch on the oriental pattern" under Solomon.

## Evidence

- Saul: modest residence at Gibeah, small staff (Abner as sole officer), limited professional troop of fellow tribesmen, voluntary offerings not taxes, charismatic legitimation (I Sam 11)
- David: "systematically built up a powerful troop of mercenaries" from renegades (I Sam 22:2); gained land holdings making him economically independent; captured Jebusite Jerusalem by trick (2 Sam 5:6-9); created a power base "independent of the tribes"
- Solomon: elevation was "simply a matter for the parties at court" (1 Kings 1); full sacral kingship on ANE pattern
- Population tripling in the hill country (Finkelstein's archaeology) created internal pressure for larger political organization — external Philistine threat was "only a catalyst"
- The transition from chiefdom to statehood "was by no means automatically mapped out" — African societies show segmentary societies can resist state formation

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The chiefdom-to-state transition model (Flanagan, Frick) is well-established in political anthropology. The contrast between Saul and David is textually grounded. Finkelstein's population data adds archaeological support.

## Stakes

If Saul's monarchy was still Yahwistically legitimate (charismatic war leader) while David's was a rupture, then the anti-monarchical polemics in the OT are not anti-monarchy per se but anti-Davidic/Solomonic state-building. The monarchy isn't intrinsically evil — it's the transformation into an ANE-style extractive state that's the problem.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more continuity between Saul and David, or who see the Saul-David contrast as a later literary (pro-Davidic) construction. "Minimalists" would question the historicity of the United Monarchy entirely.

**Alternative reading:** The chiefdom-to-state model may impose anachronistic categories on Iron Age Palestine. David's "mercenaries" could be interpreted as a standard tribal war-band, and the differences from Saul exaggerated by pro-Davidic historiography.

## Edges

**Depends on:**

**Challenged by:**
- Minimalist skepticism about the historicity of the United Monarchy
- The possibility that the Saul-David contrast is a literary construction

**Primary sources:**
- I Sam 11, 22:2
- 2 Sam 5:6-9
- 1 Kings 1, 4:7-19

## Assessment

The chiefdom-to-state model is analytically useful even if the specific details of Saul's and David's reigns are debated. The key insight — that state formation in Israel was contested and that David's innovations represented a qualitative break — holds regardless of one's position on the historicity of specific episodes.
