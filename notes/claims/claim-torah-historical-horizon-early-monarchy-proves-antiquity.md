# kaufmann-ri-5.6: Torah Literary Horizon Ends at Early Monarchy — Proves Antiquity

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch V — The Sources]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch V

## The Claim

"There is, then, no doubt that the Torah literature was crystallized at the latest by the beginning of the monarchy." The Torah "alludes briefly to the new political institution of kingship, but did not flourish on its soil."

## Evidence

1. **The promised land boundaries pre-date the conquest.** The boundaries include Philistia, Lebanon, Tyre, Sidon, Byblos — places Israel never conquered or aspired to conquer later. Crucially, Transjordan is EXCLUDED — "why should territory conquered by Moses, settled by Israelite tribes...have been excluded by patriots from their dreams?" The boundaries "are a legacy from before the conquest...an ancient oracle promising the land of Canaan to Israel."

2. **The Torah's national enemy is Amalek, not later foes.** Amalek was "the first of the nations" (Num 24:20) — defeated by David and never a major threat afterward. Aram, the bitter enemy from David's time, is still "part of the family" (Jacob's covenant with Laban). "Edom's later enmity is nowhere foreshadowed." No reference to Assyria, Egypt, or Babylon as enemies.

3. **The patriarchal narratives contain genuinely ancient details.** The connection of Simeon and Levi, the primogeniture of Ishmael/Esau/Reuben/Manasseh "corresponds to nothing in later times." The notion of Edom's fraternity "has no ground in later events."

4. **Only one law deals with monarchy (Deut 17:14-20).** The Torah legislation "reflects not the monarchy, but the early 'kingdom of God' based on the primitive democracy that existed in premonarchic Israel." Property laws "know only the tribal and familial basis."

5. **Torah's exile descriptions are pre-exilic fantasies.** The Torah imagines exiles as "dispersed bands, wandering in the lands of their enemies, harried and persecuted. Their lives hang daily in the balance." This bears no resemblance to the Babylonian exile, where exiles "built houses, planted vineyards" (Jer 29:4ff). "The image of exile in the Torah must, therefore, be an ancient one, antedating the historical experience."

6. **Genesis is the most ancient stratum.** Genesis preserves unique anthropomorphisms (God walking in the garden "at the cool of the day"), folkloristic naïveté ("sons of God" consorting with women), customs attested in Hammurabi and Hittite law, and "no trace of the influence of literary prophecy."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Torah contains genuinely ancient traditions, and its limited historical horizon is a real datum requiring explanation. But the inference to early crystallization conflates the antiquity of CONTENT with the date of COMPOSITION. Late authors writing about the Mosaic period wouldn't mention the Assyrian empire — they'd maintain the fiction of the narrative setting.

## Stakes

If Kaufmann is right about the early crystallization, the critical model (in which Torah literature was still being composed in the exile) is impossible. The Torah becomes a direct witness to pre-monarchic Israelite religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition acknowledges that the Torah contains ancient traditions but maintains the FINAL LITERARY FORM is a different question. The absence of later historical references could reflect the literary SETTING (the Mosaic/Conquest period) rather than the date of COMPOSITION.

**Alternative reading:** The Torah literature was composed OVER CENTURIES but deliberately maintained its Mosaic-period setting. The absence of references to the monarchy (except one law) and later enemies is a function of literary genre, not date of composition. Just as a modern historical novelist writing about ancient Rome doesn't mention airplanes, a post-exilic priestly writer composing Mosaic-period legislation wouldn't mention the Babylonian exile.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's observations about the Torah's limited historical horizon are genuinely valuable. The Promised Land boundaries argument is particularly striking — the exclusion of Transjordan IS hard to explain on a late-composition model. The exile descriptions ALSO present a real problem: if the Torah's exile passages were composed after the Exile, why do they describe something completely different from what actually happened? But Kaufmann's inference is too sweeping. "Contains ancient traditions" ≠ "was crystallized before the monarchy." The Torah could preserve pre-monarchic oracles about land boundaries, pre-monarchic enmity toward Amalek, and pre-exilic fantasies about exile WHILE STILL being compiled and edited centuries later.
