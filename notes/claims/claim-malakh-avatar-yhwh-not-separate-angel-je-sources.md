# sommer-bog-2.3: The *mal'akh* in J and E is an avatar of Yhwh, not a separate being

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 2 — Fluidity in Israel]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 2

## The Claim

"In many passages, the word *mal'akh* (literally, 'messenger,' but usually translated 'angel') means a small-scale manifestation of God's own presence, and the distinction between the messenger and God is murky. This *mal'akh* is something very similar to an avatar in Indian religions... The *mal'akh* in these cases is not a being separate from Yhwh whom Yhwh sent on a mission; rather, it is a part of the deity that can act on its own."

## Evidence

**1. Genesis 18 (J text) — Yhwh as three men:**
- "Yhwh manifested Himself to Abraham amidst the trees of Mamre... He lifted up his eyes and saw three men coming toward him" (18:1-2)
- "The juxtaposition of these two sentences implies that Yhwh appears in the form of three men, or at least in the form of one of the three men"
- One visitor speaks prophetically in God's voice without being identified as God
- "In 18.13 the narrator stops being coy and simply refers to one of the visitors as Yhwh"
- Two visitors leave; one stays — "the one who remains with Abraham is now clearly identified as Yhwh (18:22)"
- Three men, one God. Yhwh can be in three bodies simultaneously.

**2. Genesis 21:17-21 — The mal'akh speaks as God in first person:**
- "God heard the voice of the boy, and the *mal'akh* of God called to Hagar from heaven... 'Do not fear, for God has heard the voice of the boy... I will make of him a great nation'"
- The *mal'akh* doesn't say "God will make him" — says "I will make him." Speaks as God.

**3. Exodus 3 (E text) — The mal'akh IS Yhwh:**
- "The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appeared to [Moses] in a flame of fire from amidst the bush... Yhwh saw that he turned to look, and God called to him from amidst the bush"
- No clear transition between *mal'akh* and Yhwh — they're the same presence

**4. Judges 6 — The mal'akh alternates with Yhwh from the same mouth:**
- "*Mal'akh* of Yhwh came... Yhwh turned to him and said..." The speaker alternates identity mid-conversation

**Sommer's interpretive conclusion:** "Alternatively, it is possible that Yhwh temporarily overlaps with some heavenly beings who do God's bidding (which suggests a model different from that of an avatar)." He remains open to multiple interpretive models — the key point is that the boundary between God and *mal'akh* is fluid, not fixed.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the textual evidence (the alternation between *mal'akh* and Yhwh is unambiguous). MEDIUM-HIGH on Sommer's "avatar" interpretation (the traditional "separate messenger" reading is possible but requires more interpretive work to explain the first-person divine speech).

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, the mal'akh traditions in J/E are not evidence of a developing angelology (post-exilic) but of an ancient fluid theology that Christianity and Judaism later suppressed. The angel of the Lord is not a subordinate being — it's God in small-scale manifestation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish and Christian exegesis treats the mal'akh as a separate created being sent on missions.

**Alternative reading:** The mal'akh is an emissary who speaks with the full authority of the sender (the "agent equals sender" principle in ANE messengers). The first-person speech is delegated authority, not identity.

## Assessment

This is one of Sommer's best exegetical demonstrations. Genesis 18 is the strongest case: three men appear, one is Yhwh, two leave, the remaining one IS Yhwh. The narrator is not confused — the text describes a God with three simultaneous bodies. The mal'akh texts extend this pattern: the messenger isn't delivering God's message but IS God in reduced-scale manifestation.
