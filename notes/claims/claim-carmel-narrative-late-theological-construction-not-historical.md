# albertz-hir-3.5.3: The Carmel Narrative Is a Late Theological Construction, Not Historical

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.5 — Ninth-Century Syncretism Dispute]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.5

## The Claim

"Alt's theory has been taken up by many scholars... they have simply overlooked that I Kings 18:19ff. does not describe a cult-political controversy but a theological one." The narrative "already extends this trial of strength far beyond the original occasion... it is concerned with the proof that Yahweh alone is the true God and Baal no more than a powerless puppet." It presupposes Jehu's revolution AND exilic monotheism. "The theory should finally be abandoned."

## Evidence

- The narrative is not a cult legend — it doesn't end with cult foundation but with a confession by the people
- The Yahweh altar Elijah builds is destroyed by Yahweh's fire (v.38) — not preserved as a cult site
- The king does not appear in the original narrative (vv.21-40) — no "head and state action"
- The key verse for Alt's theory (v.30b, "he restored the destroyed altar of Yahweh there") is "probably a gloss"
- The narrative's rationalist polemic against idols (v.27) presupposes exilic monotheism

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Albertz's literary-critical argument against the historicity of the Carmel narrative is strong. Alt's territorial-historical reconstruction is indeed speculative.

## Stakes

If the Carmel narrative is fiction, the entire popular image of Elijah as the champion of Yahwistic monotheism against Baal rests on a late literary construct. The real Elijah was a rainmaker who got into a dispute with the king over a drought, not a theologian who proved Yahweh's superiority over Baal.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Albrecht Alt and scholars who follow his territorial-historical theory read the Carmel narrative as describing a genuine 9th-century cult-political dispute over jurisdiction of the Carmel sanctuary.

**Alternative reading:** The narrative may preserve authentic traditions about Elijah as a rainmaker/thaumaturge that were later expanded into a theological confrontation. The core event (a drought, a confrontation, rain returning) may be historical even if the theological framing is later.

## Assessment

Albertz's deconstruction of the Carmel narrative is the strongest section of §3.5. His literary-critical case is crisp and convincing: no king, no cult foundation, anti-idol polemic that presupposes exilic monotheism. Alt's theory was always speculative — it filled gaps in the evidence with a territorial-legal framework that the text doesn't support. Albertz's alternative reading (the real Elijah was a rainmaker, not a theologian) is more parsimonious.
