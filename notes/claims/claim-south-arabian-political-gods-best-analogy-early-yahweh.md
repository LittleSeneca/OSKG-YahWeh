# fleming-ybi-7.2: South Arabian political gods (Almaqah, 'Amm) are the best analogies for early Yahweh

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 7 — The Early Character of the God Yahweh]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 7

## The Claim

"In the end, the best analogies for thinking about Yahweh before Israel come from South Arabia" (p. 294). "The power of Almaqah and 'Amm derived from their complete identification with the people they unified and protected. The peoples of Saba' and Qataban were their family, 'the descendants of Almaqah' and 'the descendants of 'Amm'" (p. 295).

## Evidence

- Robin (2018) documented the pattern: each major South Arabian kingdom had a "great god" who bound communities together as "descendants" (wld) of that deity
- 'Amm of Qataban: the "Descendants of 'Amm" (wld 'm) are "a vast coalition of communes constituted by Qataban and led by its king" (p. 261)
- The divine name 'Amm is cognate with Hebrew 'ām ("people") — the god's very name means "Forefather" or "People" (p. 262)
- Almaqah of Saba' follows the same pattern: the "Descendants of Almaqah" assembled annually at Marib
- Kamosh and Moab show the same pattern in the Mesha inscription: slaughter devoted "to Kamosh and to Moab" (line 12), and the Bible's "people of Kemosh" ('am kĕmôš, Num 21:29; Jer 48:46) (pp. 263-264)
- The South Arabian evidence shows that the terminology of divine descent (wld 'm, wld 'lmqh) is old — "visible in the earliest documentation" — indicating "the kings latch onto conceptions that already exist" (p. 262)
- "The 'Descendants of 'Amm' would then have been such before kings" (p. 262) — the type predates monarchy

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The South Arabian evidence is genuinely illuminating and has been almost entirely neglected in Yahweh-origins scholarship. The structural parallels (people defined by divine descent, god and people joined in ritual, kinship terminology) are striking. But South Arabia is geographically distant and chronologically later (first millennium BCE) — the analogy may illuminate the pattern without proving historical connection.

## Stakes

This is Fleming's positive model for what Yahweh was before Israel. If correct, Yahweh was a "political god" in the South Arabian sense — a deity whose identity was inseparable from the people he unified, before any king claimed him.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** This is Fleming's own synthesis. No one has applied the South Arabian model to Yahweh before. The standard alternatives are: Yahweh as El-epithet (Cross), Yahweh as southern storm god (van der Toorn, Smith, Römer), or Yahweh as local highland storm god (Berlin hypothesis).

**Alternative reading:** The South Arabian pattern may reflect a different historical development — city-states coalescing into kingdoms under unifying deities. This may not apply to the mobile pastoralist Shasu setting that Fleming reconstructs for early Yahweh.

## Assessment

This is the most creative contribution in the chapter. Fleming's knowledge of Mari and his willingness to range beyond the Levantine evidence pays off here. The South Arabian analogies are genuinely more apt than the Ugaritic ones for understanding a god defined by identification with a people. But the geographical and cultural distance is real, and the analogies are suggestive rather than probative.
