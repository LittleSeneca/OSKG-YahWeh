# lewis-ocg-9.2: Judean kings were infused with divinity but never deified

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 9 — King and Judge]] — Theodore Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"Ideologically, while Judean kings were not considered to be divine (nowhere do they ever receive cult), they nonetheless were infused with divine qualities." The language of divine sonship (Ps 2:7: "You are my son; today I have begotten you"; 2 Sam 7:14: "I will be a father to him") and incomparability (Hezekiah, Josiah) creates a category of quasi-divine kingship without crossing into deification.

## Evidence

- Divine vocabulary: the king is called ʾĕlōhîm in Ps 45:7 ("Your throne, O God, is forever")
- He sits on "Yahweh's throne" (1 Chr 29:23)
- He is anointed with holy oil
- But: no Judean king ever receives sacrifice, prayer, or worship directed to him
- The "infused with divinity" concept distinguishes Israelite kingship from Egyptian (where the king IS a god) and Mesopotamian (where the king is the god's representative but can be deified in rare cases)
- Comparative context: Egyptian pharaohs received cult; Mesopotamian kings could be deified (Naram-Sin); Israelite kings occupy a genuinely intermediate position

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH. This is a well-established distinction in the scholarship on Israelite kingship.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite religion was fundamentally different from its neighbors or just one variant. The "infused but not deified" model is genuinely distinctive — not unique in every feature, but distinctive in configuration.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Minimalists might argue the "divine sonship" language is late theological fiction. Maximalists might argue it reflects genuine Davidic ideology. Some scholars (e.g., Mettinger) see stronger Egyptian influence than Lewis allows.

**Alternative reading:** The divine sonship language may reflect actual deification in the pre-exilic period, later suppressed by Deuteronomistic editors.

## Assessment

Lewis's middle-ground position is persuasive. The evidence for divine LANGUAGE about the king is strong; the evidence for divine CULT of the king is absent. That distinction is the key.
