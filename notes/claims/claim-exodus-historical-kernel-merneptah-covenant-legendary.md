# kaufmann-ri-6.8: Historical Exodus and Covenant Kernel Beneath Legendary Embellishment

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

There is a historical core to the Exodus and Sinai traditions. The tribes, several thousand strong, escaped from Egypt during the reign of Merneptah (late 13th century BCE). The route led from northern Egypt into the desert of the isthmus, not to the southern Sinai peninsula. The covenant at Sinai involved a ceremony with twelve pillars, sacrifice, blood-sprinkling, and the proclamation of laws — the core of which was the exclusive worship of YHWH. The "book of the covenant" (Exod. 20:19-23:33) and the stone tablets represent genuine early traditions.

## Evidence

Kaufmann uses the Merneptah Stele (which mentions "Israel" as a people in Canaan circa 1208 BCE) to date the Exodus to the late 13th century. He identifies Raamses with Tanis in the northern delta and argues the tribes escaped through the isthmus toward Lake Sirbonis. He speculates that an Egyptian pursuing force was lost in the treacherous swamps — a natural event interpreted as divine deliverance. He reconstructs the covenant ceremony from Exodus 24.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Some sort of escape from Egypt by a group that later became part of Israel is plausible. But the covenant ceremony Kaufmann reconstructs is drawn from a text composed centuries later — we have no independent access to what happened at "Sinai." The Merneptah Stele provides a terminus ante quem for Israel in Canaan, not a date for the Exodus itself.

## Stakes

Whether the covenant at Sinai — the founding moment of Kaufmann's monotheistic revolution — has any recoverable historical content, or whether it's entirely a later theological construct.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Minimalists would deny any historical Exodus. But even among scholars who accept some historical kernel, the Sinai covenant is usually seen as a later theological framework imposed on diverse traditions, not a recoverable historical event. The law collections in the Torah reflect the concerns of the monarchy period, not the desert.

**Alternative reading:** A small group of escaped slaves brought the story of deliverance by YHWH into Canaan, where it merged with traditions of other groups. The covenant idea developed later — perhaps modeled on Hittite suzerainty treaties — as a way of conceptualizing the relationship between YHWH and Israel. The Sinai setting may reflect memories of a southern mountain sanctuary where YHWH was originally worshipped, not a historical legislative event.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reconstruction of the Exodus is actually reasonably sober by critical standards — he accepts a small-scale historical event, naturalistic explanations for the "miracles," and a limited route. His problem is the covenant. He takes Exodus 24 as a basically reliable memory of a historical ceremony, but everything we know about how covenant texts functioned in the ANE suggests the Sinai covenant narrative is a literary-theological construct, not a report. Kaufmann is right that SOMETHING happened — but what happened was probably much simpler than what Exodus 24 describes.
