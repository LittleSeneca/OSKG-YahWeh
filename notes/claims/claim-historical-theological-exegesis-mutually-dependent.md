# schmid-ht-1.3: Historical and theological exegesis are mutually dependent

**Source:** [[Schmid — §1 — Is There a Theology of the Hebrew Bible]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §1

## The Claim

"Historical-critical exegesis renders itself historically implausible when it devalues the theological dimension of the biblical texts; theological interpretation of biblical texts deteriorates into Docetism when it prioritizes the theological 'y-coefficient' before the historical 'x-coefficient' of textual interpretation."

## Evidence

- The two approaches "exist strangely separate from one another" in current scholarship
- Historical scholars "view [theological approaches] as subjective, confessionally determined, and unscholarly"
- Theological interpreters "show little concern for the results of historical exegesis, which are viewed as arbitrary, artificial, and irrelevant"
- "The charges of arbitrariness raised by both sides have some basis in reality"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is a diagnosis of the field's dysfunction, not an empirical claim. The mutual suspicion between historical and theological approaches is well-documented.

## Stakes

Schmid's project aims to heal this divide by doing theology that is rigorously historical AND history that takes theological content seriously. If he succeeds, he's solved a structural problem in the discipline. If he fails, he's produced work that satisfies neither camp.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Hard historicists would say theological content is just more historical data — it doesn't require a separate "theological" approach. Hard theologians would say Schmid's historical approach is just history of religion dressed as theology.

**Alternative reading:** The separation is functional and appropriate. Historical and theological inquiry serve different purposes and communities. Trying to unite them produces confused scholarship that serves neither purpose well.

## Assessment

Schmid's diagnosis is correct, and his proposed solution — letting historical conclusions drive theological description — is the right direction. The acid test is whether his "theology" produces insights that purely historical inquiry wouldn't. If Schmid's theology just redescribes the history in theological vocabulary, he's failed. If it illuminates patterns, logics, and meanings that historical description misses, he's succeeded.
