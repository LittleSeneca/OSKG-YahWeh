# keel-ggi-p2.6: The Astarte Plaques depict a goddess — indigenous domestic piety surviving continuously from MB IIB through the Iron Age

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II, Excursus (Claim 6)

## The Claim

"Decisive help is provided by the iconographic context in which one finds similar images of the naked figures that are depicted on terra-cotta... The 'naked woman' on the 'Astarte plaques' is a 'naked goddess.'" And: "The modern question about whether a figure represents a goddess or a woman, posing strict alternatives, is too short-sighted. The plaques portray the goddess as a woman, and conversely they identify the female worshipers above the plaques with the goddess." This is the synthetic treatment of the Excursus within Part II, tying the Astarte Plaque tradition to the larger argument about the official/private split in LB religion and the continuous tradition from MB IIB metal figurines through LB terra-cotta plaques to Iron II Judean Pillar Figurines.

## Evidence

- **Rebuttal of Tadmor's "concubine" thesis (1982):** (a) Typology — most plaques are long oval, not rectangular beds; same shape as qudshu plaques Tadmor accepts as goddesses. (b) Context — vast majority from living quarters, not graves; ~80% of LB graves contained no plaques; only two from dozens of Lachish graves. (c) Iconography — Tell Qasile shrine plaque (Iron I, 11th c.) shows two standing female figures inside shrine facade with pilasters and cornice; "to represent naked female figures either in front of or actually inside a sanctuary shows clearly that they are to be interpreted as (twin?) goddesses."
- **Shrine models and cultic stands:** Naked female figures appear in duplicate or multiples on LB and early Iron Age shrine models, clay towers, and cultic stands. Pella cultic stand (Iron I/IIA transition): two mold-made figures "standing on feline heads" flanking window — combining "Astarte plaque" posture with lion attribute of qudshu type.
- **Gravidae flasks:** Small pitchers shaped like female body, filled with oil to prevent stretch marks during pregnancy, found LB II through Iron IIA. Tell Qasile vessel with holes in breasts — "presentation of the breasts... could be understood as an offering of, or the pressing out of, the mother's milk."
- **Gender association:** Smaller figure alongside goddess on shrine plaques always female. "No evidence of males in this posture has been found." Tall Munbaqa mold inscribed *sinnistu(m)* "woman" — "identifying the goddess as an exemplary woman."
- **Continuity argument:** The naked goddess tradition runs continuously from MB IIB metal figurines, through LB "Astarte plaques," into Iron Age I cultic stands and shrine models, eventually into Iron Age II Judean Pillar Figurines.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High that plaques represent goddesses — the typological, archaeological, and iconographic arguments form a convergent case. Medium on specific identity of the goddess (Asherah? Anat? Astarte?) — Keel & Uehlinger are appropriately cautious.

## Stakes

This Excursus is directly relevant to the debate over Judean Pillar Figurines. Dever argues JPFs represent Asherah and are evidence of persistent goddess worship despite official Yahwistic monolatry. Keel & Uehlinger provide the *prehistory* — showing the "naked goddess" tradition runs continuously from MB IIB through Iron II, making it harder to argue Asherah worship was a late "pagan corruption." It was the indigenous default. The Excursus also anticipates Dever's domestic-vs-official religion framework by nearly a decade. And the shift from expensive metal figurines to cheap terra-cotta plaques represents the shift from temple religion to "private piety" and "the little cultic centers that were set up in each home."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Tadmor (1982), H. Weippert (1988, 1990), Hadley (1989) argue for "concubine" interpretation. Pritchard (1943) coined "Astarte plaques" but corpus is outdated.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the plaques are goddesses, specific function could vary — some votive offerings, some healing/fertility rituals (Gravidae flasks), some mortuary (minority). Interpretation doesn't need to be all-or-nothing.

## Assessment

Keel & Uehlinger's rebuttal of Tadmor is devastating and largely persuasive. The three-pronged argument leaves little room for the "concubine" thesis. The Tell Qasile shrine plaque is the smoking gun: naked female figures inside a shrine. The Excursus provides the deep history showing that Dever's JPFs are not a late Iron Age innovation but the latest phase of a tradition stretching back 700+ years. Key differences from Dever: Keel & Uehlinger are more cautious about naming the goddess; more attuned to the city/hill-country distribution gap; and show that the official/private split long predates Israel.
