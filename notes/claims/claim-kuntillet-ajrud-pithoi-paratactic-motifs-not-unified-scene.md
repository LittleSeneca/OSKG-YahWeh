# keel-ggi-5.2: The Kuntillet Ajrud Pithoi Paintings Are Paratactic — Not a Unified Scene

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part V — Iron Age IIB — Baal El Yahweh and His Asherah]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part V

## The Claim

"Even a quick glance at illuss. 219-221 shows that the various paintings on each side of the jars do not form a coherent composition, but are rather made up largely of a set of motifs that are positioned paratactically either next to one another or above one another." The famous pithoi from Kuntillet Ajrud — which scholars have used to argue that Yahweh and Asherah were depicted together — are not a single coherent scene. Different artists painted different motifs at different times. The inscription "Yahweh... and his asherah" runs across a Bes figure, but inscription and painting are unrelated — they happened to be on the same jar.

## Evidence

- Three different artists identified by P. Beck (1982): different brush styles, colors (red on Pithos A, red+black on Pithos B), and skill levels
- "A significant time period must have elapsed between the time when [some motifs] were painted... and when [others were] later added to the vessel. During this time period, some of the older drawings would have faded"
- The motifs include: chariot horses, boar, lioness, suckling cow with calf, caprids at lotus tree, Bes figures, female lyre player, archer, five male worshipers
- The Egyptian solar/astral motifs that dominate Israelite glyptic art are "completely absent from both the pithoi and the wall paintings at Kuntillet Ajrud" (Beck 1982, 44)
- The artists were likely northern Syrian or Phoenician, not Israelite

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** Beck's analysis is art-historical and has not been seriously challenged. The different hands are visible in the published photographs.

## Stakes

Every interpretation that reads the pithoi as a unified theological statement ("Here is Yahweh and his Asherah depicted together!") collapses if the images are random, accumulated over time, and unrelated to the inscriptions written across them.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1984), Margalit (1990), Coogan (1987a) — all of whom treat the inscriptions and paintings as related. Margalit even describes the paintings as a "graphic commentary on the word *'šrh*."

**Alternative reading:** Even if the paintings were done by different artists at different times, the inscriptions were intentionally placed on a vessel that was used in a Yahwistic cultic context — the vessel itself might have been chosen because its imagery was relevant to the religious message.

## Assessment

Beck's analysis is rigorous and the paratactic nature of the paintings is hard to dispute. The fact that Egyptian solar motifs — the dominant iconographic language of Iron Age IIB Israel — are completely absent from Kuntillet Ajrud suggests this was a provincial outpost, not a major cult center. The pithoi are fascinating but they are not a Rosetta Stone for Israelite religion. They are the doodles of travelers at a caravanserai in the desert.
