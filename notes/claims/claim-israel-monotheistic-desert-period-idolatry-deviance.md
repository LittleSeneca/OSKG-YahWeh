# kaufmann-ri-6.4: Israel Was Monotheistic from the Desert Period

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

The Torah portrays the desert period as monotheistic. The golden calf was either dedicated to YHWH (not a foreign god) or a fetishistic god-image. The Baal Peor apostasy was partial and punished. "The people as a whole never once suggest exchanging their God for another." National sin presupposes a monotheistic people — idolatry is "a national sin" precisely because Israel is the covenant people of YHWH. "By making Israel enter a covenant with the one God, [Moses] made it a monotheistic people."

## Evidence

Kaufmann's key argument is that the stories of desert idolatry are internally inconsistent with the claim that Israel was polytheistic — they depict apostasy as aberration, not norm. The golden calf narrative (Exod. 32) says only 3,000 out of 600,000 were punished. The Baal Peor narrative (Num. 25) commands judges to execute only "those of your men who joined Baal Peor," implying partial participation. The narrative speaks of national sin because the covenant makes the whole people answerable, not because all sinned.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** Kaufmann is taking legendary narratives composed centuries after the events and reading them as historical reportage. The desert stories are not eyewitness accounts — they're theological narratives produced by later writers who WERE monotheists and who projected their monotheism back onto the founding period. The very numbers in the narratives (600,000, 3,000) are patently legendary.

## Stakes

Whether the desert period was actually monotheistic or whether this is a later theological retrojection. If Kaufmann is right, the critical model (monotheism emerged gradually during the monarchy) is wrong. If the critical model is right, Kaufmann is reading theology as history.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire critical tradition. The desert narratives were composed and edited during the monarchy and exile. Their monotheistic character reflects the theology of their AUTHORS, not the religion of the historical desert period. The archaeological evidence from the settlement period (1200-1000 BCE) shows continuity with Canaanite religious practice, not a monotheistic revolution.

**Alternative reading:** There WAS a distinctive religious identity that formed in the pre-monarchic period — perhaps a loose confederation bound by covenant to YHWH. But this was likely henotheistic (YHWH as patron god of the confederation, other gods acknowledged but not worshipped) rather than monotheistic. The desert narratives preserve a memory of this formative period but reinterpret it through later monotheistic lenses.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reading of the narratives as evidence of early monotheism is methodologically circular. He takes the stories' own theological assumptions (Israel is a monotheistic people, therefore apostasy is aberration) as evidence for the historical reality those assumptions describe. But those assumptions are exactly what needs to be tested. The narratives could equally well reflect a LATER community's attempt to understand how their ancestors could have fallen into idolatry if the covenant was always monotheistic.
