# lewis-ocg-5.3: Masseboth (Standing Stones) as Likely El Representations

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 5 — Iconography of Divinity — El]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"The etymological origin of cultic stones known as betyls or baetyls (from bēt-ʾel, 'the house of God/El') predisposes us to look for ways in which sacred stones were envisioned as physical representations of the deity El." The Arad stela "occupied the place where the deity resided and was manifest" (Bloch-Smith). Masseboth are associated with El in biblical tradition at Bethel (Gen 28:18, 22; 31:13; 35:14) and at Shechem (El Berith temple).

## Evidence

- Six sites with masseboth: Shechem (huge 6.5 ft stela next to Temple 1, likely El Berith temple), Bull Site (large worked stone with bronze bull), Hazor Area B (curved basalt stone as focal point, with bronze figurine in buried jar), Hazor Area A and M (multiple installations, some LB heirlooms), Arad (single red-painted limestone stela in temple niche), Tel Dan (multiple installations in gate complex), Khirbet Ataruz (1.5m stone in cultic niche with bull-decorated jar)
- Biblical texts associate masseboth with El (Gen 28, 31, 35), Baal (2 Kgs 3:2; 10:26-27), and Yahweh (Isa 19:19-20; Hos 3:4; 10:1-2)
- The word "betyl" (from bēt-ʾel) etymologically connects sacred stones to El
- The Arad stela is in the holy of holies niche — the clearest case of a massebah as divine symbol
- Lewis pushes back against Mettinger's overstatement: standing stones had multiple functions (tombstones, boundary markers, commemorative markers, tribal markers, surrogate heirs) — not all are divine symbols
- Even cultic masseboth may mark a theophanic encounter rather than the deity's ongoing presence

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Medium-high that some masseboth represented El; medium on specific site identifications — no inscription accompanies any massebah identifying the deity. The evidence is circumstantial.

## Stakes

If masseboth represent El worship, then El devotion persisted into the Iron Age in material form — not just in textual traditions. The small scale of masseboth sites (Bull Site, "ruin cults" at Hazor) aligns with El's profile as a family/clan deity rather than a national one.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mettinger overstates the case: "cultic masseboth must be regarded as aniconic representations of the divine." Lewis pushes back: many masseboth are not cultic. Korpel argues masseboth could represent ancestor cults. The masseboth could be generic cult foci not tied to a specific deity.

**Alternative reading:** The masseboth could represent ancestor cults (Korpel), or they could be generic cult foci not tied to a specific deity. The word "betyl" may have become a generic term.

## Assessment

Lewis's treatment is admirably cautious. He resists the temptation to claim every massebah as El. The cumulative picture — El's biblical association with stones, the etymological connection (betyl = bêt-ʾel), the small-scale clan-oriented cult sites — is suggestive but not probative. This is iconographic evidence at its limit: we can see that SOMETHING was worshipped at these sites, but we can't see WHO.
