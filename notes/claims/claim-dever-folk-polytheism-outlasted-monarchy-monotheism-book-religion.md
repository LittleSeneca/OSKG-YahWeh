# dever-dghw-6-9.7: Folk polytheism persisted through monarchy; monotheism was a book religion

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapters VI-IX — Asherah, Women, Monotheism]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter VIII

## The Claim

The biblical narrative of Israel's journey from pure monotheism to syncretistic corruption and back is exactly backward. "Israelite religion was everywhere and always polytheistic in practice until the very end of the monarchy — and for most people, even beyond." Monotheism was the invention of a small circle of reformers who wrote the Bible. It only became the "religion of Israel" because they won the battle of the books.

## Evidence

- The archaeological evidence of polytheistic practice (figurines, multiple temples, inscriptions to other deities) continues UNABATED through the entire monarchy. Josiah's reform didn't stop it.
- Even Josiah's reform was limited: "we have no archaeological evidence whatsoever that Josiah's reform had any lasting effect on folk religion"
- The prophets were a tiny minority — "the 'Yahweh-alone' movement was a minority party until the very end"
- The exile created monotheism not because everyone became monotheistic but because the elite who went into exile wrote the Bible: "Monotheism was a 'book religion' — the religion of a book, written by the few for the few"
- "The real religions of ancient Israel persisted long after the canon was closed, and they have continued even into modern times in various guises"
- **The irony:** "The very fact that the biblical writers condemn folk religion so vehemently is the best proof that it was widespread. You don't rail against things that aren't happening."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Consensus across Smith, Römer, and Dever; archaeological evidence of persisting polytheistic practice is robust; the absence of archaeological trace for Josiah's reform is telling.

## Stakes

This inverts the biblical narrative: pure monotheism was not the beginning that Israel fell away from; it was the late construction imposed on a polytheistic population. The Bible is not a history of Israelite religion but the propaganda of the winners.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Yehezkel Kaufmann (Israel was fundamentally monotheistic from the start; idolatry was vestigial fetishism, not genuine polytheistic conviction), Jeffrey Tigay (onomastic evidence shows minimal polytheism in personal names), conservative biblical scholars.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets represent genuine popular consciousness; monotheism was widely held among the people, not just the elite. The exile completed a process already underway. The archaeological evidence shows deviant practice, not normative religion.

## Assessment

This is the strongest of Dever's three synthetic claims in Ch VI-IX because it deploys the same archaeological data that makes his Asherah case but within the consensus framework shared by Smith and Römer. The observation that Josiah's reform left no archaeological trace is particularly damaging to the Deuteronomistic narrative. The weakest point: "book religion written by the few for the few" may overstate the elite/popular divide — the production of texts was elite by definition in the ancient world, but that doesn't mean the theology had no popular resonance.
