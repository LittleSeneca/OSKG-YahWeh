# kaufmann-ri-9.10: The rift was social, not religious — prophecy born from social cleavage

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"The ancient narrative sources count one religious-cultic sin against the people: straying after foreign gods. Violations of other religious laws are not mentioned there, nor in the psalms, and only incidentally in the latter prophets." The real gap between ideal and reality was in "the relationships between man and man" — social exploitation, class division, oppression of the poor. "In the Torah, prophets, wisdom and psalm literature the distinction [between 'righteous' and 'wicked'] is a socio-moral, not a religious one." The Torah's social legislation was largely "utopian" and "edifying literature" — not enforceable law. As the monarchy developed, exploitation grew, the rift widened, and "out of this rift, classical prophecy was born."

## Evidence

- The Torah prescribes no penalties for violation of its social laws — they were "moral sanctions and exhortations"
- "The Torah provided no real legal recourse for the oppressed"
- The Torah's social background was tribal/patriarchal — it became obsolete under the monarchy, saying nothing of royal officialdom
- The Torah forbids usury, yet creditors took children as slaves (2 Kings 4:1)
- Sabbath emancipation of slaves was enacted as a NOVELTY in Zedekiah's time and immediately reversed (Jer. 34:8 ff.)
- Even the Restoration community took debtors' children into slavery (Neh. 5:1 ff.)
- "Sabbath and festival, sacrifice and sacred dues, matters of purity and impurity were all part of popular life and popular piety"
- The one sin consistently condemned is "straying after foreign gods" — which Kaufmann reads as limited cultic deviance, not polytheism

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Right that social exploitation was a major prophetic concern, but the wedge between 'religious' and 'social' is artificial — ancient Israelites wouldn't have recognized the distinction.

## Stakes

Whether classical prophecy was primarily a social movement (Kaufmann's view) or a religious reformation (the critical view). This determines how we read the prophetic corpus.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the prophets as combatting genuine polytheistic practice. The Deuteronomistic History presents the fall of both kingdoms as punishment for idolatry — not social injustice. Kaufmann has to argue that this framing is retrospective theodicy, not historical reality.

**Alternative reading:** The prophets addressed BOTH social exploitation and religious syncretism. These were connected: worship of Baal and Asherah involved agricultural fertility rites that had social dimensions (land tenure, economic exploitation). You can't separate the religious from the social in ancient Israel — they were the same thing.

## Assessment

Kaufmann makes an important point that has been underappreciated: the social legislation of the Torah was indeed aspirational rather than enforceable, and social exploitation WAS a major prophetic concern. But he drives an artificial wedge between "religious" and "social" that ancient Israelites wouldn't have recognized. The prophets' condemnation of idolatry and their condemnation of social injustice are two sides of the same coin — covenant violation. Kaufmann's attempt to make social injustice the primary rift while minimizing the religious rift is a consequence of his need to maintain that popular religion was essentially monotheistic.
