# heiser-ur-9.1: Divine foreknowledge does not require predestination — God can foreknow events that never happen

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 2 — The Households of God]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 9

## The Claim

Divine foreknowledge does not logically require predestination. God can foreknow an event without predestining it — and 1 Samuel 23 provides a biblical case study. God tells David that Saul WILL come to Keilah and the people WILL turn David over. David leaves Keilah. Neither event happens. Therefore: "That which never happens can be foreknown by God, but it is not predestined, since it never happened."

## Evidence

**1 Samuel 23:7-13:**
- Saul learns David is in Keilah and plans to besiege the city
- David inquires of Yahweh: "Will Saul come down?" Yahweh: "He will come down."
- David: "Will the men of Keilah surrender me?" Yahweh: "They will surrender you."
- David and his men leave Keilah
- Saul learns David has escaped and abandons the siege
- Saul never comes to Keilah. The men never surrender David.
- Yet God foreknew both events under the CONDITION that David remained in Keilah

**The logical distinction:**
- Foreknowledge: God knows what WILL happen under specific conditions
- Predestination: God CAUSES it to happen regardless of conditions
- 1 Samuel 23 shows foreknowledge of contingent outcomes that human free action prevents
- "That which never happens can be foreknown by God, but it is not predestined, since it never happened."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The 1 Samuel 23 example is exegetically strong. God foreknows contingent events that David's free action prevents. This is a coherent logical distinction regardless of one's theological commitments.

## Stakes

This is a crucial move for Heiser's larger project. If foreknowledge requires predestination, then God predestined the *elohim* to rebel, which makes God the author of evil. Heiser needs to explain how the *elohim* could freely rebel without God predestining their rebellion. Separating foreknowledge from predestination solves this: God foreknew the rebellion was possible but did not predestine it. The 1 Samuel 23 case demonstrates that the Bible itself distinguishes between foreknowledge and predestination.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Theological determinists (Calvinists) who maintain that foreknowledge entails predestination — if God knows X will happen, X must happen, and God's knowledge is causative. Open theists who maintain that God cannot foreknow future free choices — the 1 Samuel 23 example, on their reading, shows God's limited knowledge, not contingent foreknowledge.

**Alternative reading:** 1 Samuel 23 describes God's knowledge of Saul's intentions (which were real) and the likely outcome if David stayed. This is divine wisdom about probabilities, not foreknowledge of counterfactuals. Or: God's statements were conditional ("if you stay, Saul will come") even though the condition is implicit.

## Assessment

This is Heiser's most philosophically careful argument outside of his main divine-council thesis. Whether the 1 Samuel 23 reading is exegetically sound is debatable — the text can be read as God reporting current intentions and probable outcomes, not foreknowing counterfactuals — but the logical distinction between foreknowledge and predestination is philosophically valid regardless. Heiser's theological motivation (explaining *elohim* rebellion without divine culpability) is transparent but doesn't invalidate the argument.
