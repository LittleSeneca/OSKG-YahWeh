# keel-ggi-10.2: The standard reconstruction of Israelite religion is structurally flawed because it relies on two corpora (Ugaritic texts and the Hebrew Bible) that are both geographically and chronologically distant from Iron Age Palestine

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch X — Summary and Conclusion]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter X

## The Claim

"The one-sided orientation toward the world of hearing (and reading) has led to the situation that the religious history of Palestine (ca. 1800-500) has been reconstructed predominantly on the basis of two lexical corpora: the texts of Ugarit (especially the mythology) and the Hebrew Bible, and this situation continues." "Ugarit is about 400 km (240 miles) from Jerusalem... The production of the Ugaritic texts ended about 1200, thus about the mid-point of the period that concerns us here, and at a time when not a single biblical text had yet been written."

## Evidence

- The Ugaritic textual corpus (14th-13th c. BCE) is 400 km north of Jerusalem and ends around 1200 — making it contemporaneous with the Late Bronze Age, not the Iron Age when Israelite religion actually developed
- The Hebrew Bible was "recopied, adapted, and purged over the centuries" — even preexilic fragments are embedded in post-exilic redactions
- "Trying to make sense of the symbol system of ninth- or seventh-century Palestine with the aid of texts from Ugarit is extremely problematic. Frequently, these can offer nothing more than 'parallels'"
- The biblical texts cannot produce consensus reconstructions "given the contemporary views about the uniqueness and age of these texts"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The geographic and chronological gaps are indisputable. The question is whether those gaps matter — and their entire book is the argument that they do.

## Stakes

If accepted, this critique undermines the methodology of Smith, Cross, Day, and the entire Ugaritic-studies-to-Hebrew-Bible pipeline. It does not mean Ugaritic texts are irrelevant, but it means they cannot be the PRIMARY evidence for Iron Age Israelite religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Everyone who built their career on Ugaritic-Hebrew Bible comparisons. Smith (who acknowledges the missing "puzzle pieces"), Cross, Clifford, Miller, Coogan. They would argue that Ugarit preserves the closest available West Semitic mythology and that the conceptual continuity across the Late Bronze/Iron Age transition justifies the comparison.

**Alternative reading:** The authors overstate the problem. Ugaritic religion and Israelite religion share a common Canaanite heritage. The geographic distance is real but the cultural distance may be less than they claim. And we simply do not have Iron Age Canaanite mythological texts — Ugarit is the best we have got.

## Assessment

This is the book's most aggressive methodological claim, repeated here with full force. It is partly a turf war (iconographers vs. text scholars) but the critique is legitimate. The problem is that without Ugaritic texts, we lose the only detailed mythological narratives from the West Semitic world. The iconography can show us WHICH symbols clustered together but not the STORIES told about them. The ideal approach — as Keel & Uehlinger themselves practice — uses both, with iconography as the primary chronological anchor and texts as supplementary.
