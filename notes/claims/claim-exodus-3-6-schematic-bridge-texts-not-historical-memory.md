# fleming-ybi-3.2: Exodus 6:2-3 and Exodus 3 are schematic bridge texts, not historical memory

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 3 — The Midianite Hypothesis — Moses and the Priest]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 3

## The Claim

"No matter the literary-historical system, there is no good case that Exodus 3 and 6 represent such ancient religious realities" (Fleming, p. 107). "Both texts make Moses central even to knowing the name by which this god must be approached" (p. 127). "The texts contradict the assumption in Genesis that Israel's ancestors knew Yahweh by name, but with Moses' privileged authority at stake, it appears that the writer of this section in Exodus has added the revelation to its already powerful claim" (p. 107).

## Evidence

- Exodus 6:2-8 is Priestly (P), widely considered a relatively late composition that self-consciously connects Genesis ancestors with Moses (pp. 107-108)
- The text's language and concepts are thoroughly Priestly: El Shadday, covenant, land promise, "I am Yahweh"
- The declaration "by my name Yahweh I was not known to them" (6:3) serves to make the exodus moment uniquely transformative: "The name goes with the deliverance, and the deliverance will confirm the name" (p. 109)
- Exodus 3:1-4:18 is an insertion linking Genesis promises and the Joshua conquest — the strongest links to the ancestors appear precisely in the section where the divine name is revealed (3:6-17)
- In European scholarship, this text is widely considered post-Priestly or post-Deuteronomistic — a bridge text (Schmid 2010, Berner 2010)
- "Neither text offers a matrix that preserves fixed ancient traditions for religious origins with roots in historical realities" (p. 117)
- The texts address "perceived narrative problems" — how to reconcile ancestor stories where Yahweh is known with the need to make Moses the mediator of something new

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Fleming's literary analysis is strong and aligns with broad European scholarship on the post-exilic dating of key Pentateuchal bridge texts. The schematic function of these passages is well-established.

## Stakes

If Exodus 3 and 6 don't preserve ancient memory, the entire Midianite Hypothesis loses its biblical anchor. The claim that "Israel didn't always know Yahweh" depends entirely on these texts. Without them, there's no biblical basis for Israel having "acquired" Yahweh from anyone.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Documentary Hypothesis defenders who treat Exodus 3 (E) and 6 (P) as preserving genuinely old traditions from monarchic-period sources. Also, scholars like Römer who accept later dating but still find historical memory embedded in the texts.

**Alternative reading:** Even if the texts as we have them are late, they could preserve older traditions. The very oddness of the claim that the ancestors didn't know Yahweh argues for its antiquity — a late writer wouldn't invent a problem they'd then need to solve.

## Assessment

Fleming's case is strong. The schematic character of these texts is visible on their face — they're doing theological bridge-work. The alternative reading (oddness = antiquity) has some force, but Fleming's literary analysis shows the "oddness" is exactly what you'd expect from a writer trying to connect two originally separate narrative complexes.
