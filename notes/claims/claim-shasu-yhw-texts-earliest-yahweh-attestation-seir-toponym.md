# lewis-ocg-6.4: The Shasu texts provide the earliest possible attestation of Yahweh — toponyms tied to Seir

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 6 — Origin of Yahweh]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 6

## The Claim

Egyptian geographical lists from Amenophis III and Ramses II refer to "the land of the Shasu of Yhw" (t3 š3sw yhw). "The Shasu are tantalizing for historians of Israelite religion due to their mention in conjunction with the term yh(w)/yhw3, which seems to be a toponym rather than a theonym." But "in the ancient Near East many anthroponyms could serve as toponyms" (Astour). The connection to Seir (š3sw sʿrr) ties these texts to archaic biblical poetry where Yahweh marches from Seir/Edom.

## Evidence

Papyrus Harris I and Papyrus Anastasi VI both connect Shasu with Seir and Edom. Redford argues the skepticism about equating sʿrr with Seir is "wholly unwarranted." Schneider's Book of the Dead name ("My lord is the shepherd of Yah") provides a possible theophoric attestation from the same period. Ben-Yosef et al. connect Shasu to copper production at Faynan.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The toponym vs. theonym ambiguity prevents higher confidence; the convergence with biblical poetry is the strongest argument for the connection.

## Stakes

If the Shasu texts reference Yahweh, the deity predates Israel by centuries and originated among nomadic peoples south/southeast of the Dead Sea. This would be the strongest evidence for southern origins.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Weippert, Astour, Ahlström, and de Moor have argued sʿrr is not Seir, and Yhw should be located in Lebanon/Syria. These are minority positions.

**Alternative reading:** The toponym "Yhw" may refer to a place, not a deity — and the place may not be in the south. The convergence with biblical poetry could be coincidence or literary retrojection.

## Assessment

The Shasu texts are genuinely exciting and Lewis handles them well — not overclaiming, but recognizing their significance. The convergence with biblical poetry (Yahweh from Seir/Edom/Teman) is the strongest argument. If the toponym and the poetry both point south, that's unlikely to be coincidence.
