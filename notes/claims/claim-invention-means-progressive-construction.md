# romer-inv-intro.4: "Invention" means progressive construction — a sedimentary process over centuries, not fabrication

**Source:** [[Romer — Introduction]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Introduction

## The Claim

"When we speak, then, of 'the invention of God,' we should not imagine either that a group of Bedouins met one day and huddled around an oasis to create a god for themselves, or that some scribes, much later, invented Yahweh out of whole cloth." Rather, "invention" means "a progressive construction arising out of a particular tradition... a series of sedimentary strata gradually laid down over the course of time, which is then sometimes disrupted by historical events."

## Evidence

- **The key metaphor:** Sedimentary geology. Layers accumulate. Sometimes earthquakes disrupt the layers and something new emerges. The conception of God was "continually revised in the light of particular, changing social and historical contexts."
- **Collective invention:** Not one person's project, but a communal process over centuries.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that this is a useful framing. It captures the gradual, cumulative nature of theological development without the pejorative connotations of "fabrication" or "fiction." It's also honest about the role of historical contingency.

## Stakes

This framing leaves room for divine involvement (God could work through the geological processes of history) without requiring it. Whether you see God's hand in the strata or just the strata is up to you. Romer the historian doesn't answer. But he makes it clear that the strata ARE there — the development happened.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional theologians who see "invention" as a provocative and misleading term regardless of Romer's clarification. Scholars who prefer different metaphors (evolution, emergence, development) without the connotations of human artifice.

## Assessment

This is a helpful and honest clarification. The sedimentary metaphor captures the gradual, cumulative, contingency-shaped nature of theological development without reductionism. Romer anticipates and disarms the obvious objection (that "invention" implies conscious fabrication) before readers can make it. The remaining tension: even with the clarification, "invention" is a loaded word, and Romer chose it knowing that.
