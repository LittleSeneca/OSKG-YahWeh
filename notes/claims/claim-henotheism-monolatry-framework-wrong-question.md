# kaufmann-ri-1.4: The henotheism/monolatry framework is the wrong question

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"It is commonly assumed that the religion of YHWH began as henotheism or monolatry, recognizing him as sole legitimate god in Israel, but acknowledging the existence of other national gods... This view is founded on the tacit assumption that the pagan gods were conceived of identically by both Israelite and pagan." Kaufmann rejects this framework entirely: "We cannot ask whether it was during the preprophetic or prophetic age that the religion of YHWH came to deny the reality of the foreign gods. The Bible nowhere denies the existence of the gods; it ignores them."

## Evidence

Kaufmann's evidence is the entire survey — the Bible's consistent characterization of paganism as fetishism rather than mythological polytheism. The "other gods" are always idols — manufactured objects — not living deities from the Canaanite or Mesopotamian pantheons.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** MEDIUM on the *critique* of the henotheism framework (it really does oversimplify); LOW on Kaufmann's *positive* claim that Israel never passed through a polytheistic/henotheistic stage. Biblical texts like Judges 11:24, Deuteronomy 32:8-9, and Psalm 82 preserve traces of an earlier worldview.

## Stakes

The entire developmental narrative. If Kaufmann is right, Israelite religion was *sui generis* from the beginning — not one more example of the henotheism-to-monotheism trajectory. Israel didn't *evolve* to monotheism; it *began* with a radically different conception of deity.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The henotheism/monolatry framework is standard in critical scholarship. Smith explicitly traces this development. Römer documents the emergence of monolatry in the 9th-8th centuries BCE. The Mesha Stele shows Moabites conceived of Chemosh as a national god who could be angered — the implication is early Israel likely thought similarly.

**Alternative reading:** The henotheism framework captures something real — early Israel DID acknowledge the existence of other national gods. Judges 11:24 (Jephthah acknowledging Chemosh), Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (YHWH receiving Israel as Elyon's portion), and Psalm 82 preserve traces of an earlier worldview. The Bible's later characterization of foreign gods as mere idols reflects the theological position of its final editors.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is right that the henotheism/monolatry framework can be reductive — it imposes Greek-derived categories (henotheism was coined by Max Müller) onto Israelite religion. His point that the Bible *ignores* rather than *denies* other gods is genuinely interesting. But he overreaches. Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (in its original form, LXX/DSS) describing Elyon apportioning the nations to the "sons of God" and YHWH receiving Israel is devastating to Kaufmann's thesis. Someone in Israel's tradition knew a polytheistic framework.
