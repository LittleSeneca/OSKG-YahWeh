# kaufmann-ri-2.4: Pagan gods are subject to necessity/fate

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch II — Pagan Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter II

## The Claim

"Necessity dominates the universe and the gods who are part of it. Birth, procreation, growth, youth, age, death, and the like — are innate properties of the world-stuff." Even the highest gods cannot escape predestined decrees. The fabulous wonderland of myth and magic is bound to necessity.

## Evidence

- Babylonian astrology: stars follow fixed laws determining even divine actions — "immutable laws are operative" alongside gods
- Hindu rita: the world order — gods are "lords of rita, but also its servants, guardians, members of its household"
- Persian asha: cosmic order to which even Ormazd is subject — he merely "has knowledge of what has been foreordained"
- Greek ananke/moira: Zeus doesn't decide Achilles' fate — he "merely weighs the fates of the two in the balance to learn what is destined." Zeus must swallow Metis to forestall prophecy — "even Zeus may not avoid what is destined"
- Roman fatum: similar notions throughout pagan world
- Fixed cosmic cycles: "the world is destined to pass through various predetermined stages before its destruction or renewal. The gods have no control over this 'natural' process"
- Buddhist karma: "the self-operating law of moral causality and retribution which binds both gods and men"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann has identified a real cross-cultural pattern — the subjection of even high gods to impersonal cosmic order is genuinely widespread.

## Stakes

If Israelite religion subjects Yahweh to NO higher order — no fate, no necessity, no cosmic law Yahweh didn't create — this IS a genuine difference. But the Bible depicts Yahweh acting within constraints: he "repents" (Gen 6:6, Exod 32:14), is persuaded by human argument (Abraham, Moses), is bound by covenant promises. None are exactly "fate" in the pagan sense, but they complicate the claim of absolute, unconstrained sovereignty.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars of continuity point to Isaiah 45:7 ("I form light and create darkness") as evidence Israel DID develop radical divine sovereignty — but the question is WHEN. If late (Deutero-Isaiah, 6th century), early Israel may have shared pagan assumptions about cosmic order. [[claim-deut-32-8-9-sons-of-el]] suggests Elyon, not Yahweh, apportioned nations in earlier theology.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's silence about fate/necessity could reflect genre, not theology. Israelite literature is historical narrative and prophecy — not theogonic myth or philosophical speculation. Greek tragedy explores fate because tragedy requires it; Hebrew narrative explores covenant because covenant narrative requires it.

## Assessment

A real and important comparative observation. Pagan religions consistently subordinate gods to impersonal cosmic order. Israel consistently presents Yahweh as the source of order rather than its subject. Whether this reflects actual Israelite belief from the beginning or the theological achievement of later scribes is the question.
