# smith-obm-1.8: Genesis 1 achieves "monotheistic poetics" by eliminating conflict with cosmic enemies and depersonalizing traditional deities

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch1 — Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"The literary order of Genesis 1 contributes to a monotheistic vision. The text manifests a 'monotheistic poetics,' which alters the perception of reality with its created order." Genesis 1 prepares the audience for cosmic conflict (verse 2's *tehom*, darkness, wind), then subverts that expectation — God speaks and it is done, without battle. The *tanninim* appear not as pre-creation enemies but as created beings within the order (v. 21). Sun and moon are depersonalized as "greater light" and "lesser light" (v. 16) — traditional divine titles used without divine status.

## Evidence

- **Genesis 1:2** sets up expectations of conflict: *tehom* ("the deep" = Tiamat/Thmt), darkness, wind — standard chaos-battle opening. "The audience... is prepared for a cosmic conflict by the opening references to enemies in verse 2."
- **But no conflict occurs:** "In this passage the lack of any conflict, or even any personification of the cosmic oceans or waters, heightens the picture of a powerful God who but speaks and the divine will is accomplished."
- ***Tanninim* in verse 21 (day 5):** within the created order — not at the beginning as pre-creation foes. "By omission, [the narrative] transforms them into another part of creation."
- **Sun and moon depersonalized:** "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (1:16). These are traditional titles: "great light" (*nyr rbt*) for Ugaritic sun-goddess (CAT 1.161.19), "light of the heavens" (*nrt shmm*) for Ugaritic moon-god (1.24.16, 31) — but in Genesis they are "no longer divinities."
- "Here, ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left in Genesis 1's 'monotheistic poetics.'"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The reading of Genesis 1 as subverting chaos-battle expectations is well-established. The "monotheistic poetics" formulation is Smith's but the underlying observation is broadly accepted.

## Stakes

This is arguably the most important claim in the chapter for Smith's overall thesis. It shows that monotheism is not primarily a philosophical proposition ("only one god exists") but a literary-theological strategy that reorganizes traditional mythic materials to eliminate middle ground between Creator and creature. "Monotheistic poetics" is a more precise analytical tool than "monotheism" as a belief-stage.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Genesis 1 as dependent on P's monotheistic theology rather than as a creative literary transformation. Also those who see the lack of conflict as simply reflecting P's non-mythological Priestly style rather than a deliberate subversion.

**Alternative reading:** Genesis 1's treatment of *tanninim* and celestial bodies may be less about "monotheistic poetics" and more about the Priestly author's concern with order, separation, and taxonomy. The *tanninim* are classified as creatures of day 5 because that's where sea creatures belong in the Priestly scheme — the theological implication may be secondary to the classificatory impulse.

## Assessment

Smith's reading is powerful and persuasive. The phrase "monotheistic poetics" captures something real — Genesis 1 doesn't just assert monotheism, it performs it through narrative structure. The audience-knows-the-tradition point is crucial: the text's silences are meaningful precisely because the audience knows the chaos-conflict stories. My one reservation: Smith may overstate how much the Priestly author was consciously subverting specific Ugaritic traditions rather than drawing on generalized ANE chaoskampf motifs.
