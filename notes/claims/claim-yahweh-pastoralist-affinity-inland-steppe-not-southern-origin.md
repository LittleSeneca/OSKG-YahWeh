# fleming-ybi-4.5: The southern geography reflects pastoralist affinity, not historical origin

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 4 — The Old Poetry]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"This poetic motif, however ancient, makes Yahweh at home in the land of pastoralists, the very population that in still earlier times include a group called Yhw3" (Fleming, p. 179). "The old poetry draws our eye not just to the south, but behind the geography, to a deep affinity for the pastoralists of the inland steppe" (p. 179).

## Evidence

- Seir and Edom in Judg 5:4 match the two Egyptian terms for Shasu regions in Papyrus Anastasi VI and Papyrus Harris I (p. 178)
- "Seir takes priority and Edom is open space (śādeh)" — not a kingdom but pastoralist terrain (p. 178)
- By the early first millennium, the inland east was occupied by Aramean Damascus, Ammon, and Moab — "the south was the terrain left open for life on the move, life in tents" (p. 179)
- This explains why the pastoralist association shifted southward over time
- The prose kinship traditions (Ch 3) and the poetic southern theophany (Ch 4) converge on the same insight: Israel had deep affinities with pastoralist populations

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is Fleming's most synthetic and original claim in the chapter. It's elegant but requires accepting multiple prior arguments (the Shasu reading from Ch 2, the kinship traditions from Ch 3). The claim that Seir/Edom in the 12th-century Egyptian texts are "pastoralist regions, not political entities" is well-supported. But whether the biblical poetry preserves this specific understanding is less clear.

## Stakes

This is the positive payoff of Fleming's deconstruction. He's not just saying the Midianite Hypothesis is wrong — he's offering an alternative explanation for why the southern geography matters. Yahweh was a god of pastoralists, and the poetry preserves that association even after the geography shifted.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The Midianite Hypothesis camp would say Fleming is essentially restating their position in different terms: Yahweh came from southern pastoralists. The difference is that Fleming denies "came from" — Yahweh was always among pastoralist groups in the inland Levant, some of whom became Israel.

**Alternative reading:** The southern geography is a late theological development with no historical basis — the Berlin hypothesis position (Pfeiffer, Müller).

## Assessment

This is where Fleming's argument becomes most constructive and most vulnerable. He's offering an alternative that explains the data without the Midianite Hypothesis, and it's genuinely attractive. But it's also the sketchiest part of the chapter — more a direction for future research than a settled conclusion.
