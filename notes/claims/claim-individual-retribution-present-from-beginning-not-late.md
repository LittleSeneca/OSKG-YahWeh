# kaufmann-ri-9.8: Individual retribution is present from the beginning, not a late discovery

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"It is a widespread opinion that the doctrine of individual retribution is a late development of Israelite religion... This opinion is a compound of errors. The notion of individual retribution is to be reckoned among the ideas that Israel inherited from the ancient cultures. It is found, alongside the idea of collective punishment, in Egypt and Mesopotamia as well."

## Evidence

- The first mention of retribution (Adam and Eve) is individual
- Noah and his generation receive strictly individual requital
- Lot's family members are requited variously according to their deserts
- The Egyptian midwives, the wilderness generation, Caleb, Joshua, Zelophehad, David are all rewarded or punished individually
- Various Torah laws (sin and guilt offerings, vows, moral injunctions) reflect individual requital
- Collective retribution exists alongside — primarily in family, tribal, and national frameworks
- The punishment of children for fathers was eventually abolished (Deut. 24:16; 2 Kings 14:6) — but both customs coexisted
- "The people of Israel constitute a special sphere of collective responsibility" based on covenant — not natural, but artificial
- Multiple auxiliary assumptions explain apparent injustices: God tests loyalty, God is patient, God waits until the measure of sin is full

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann is simply right about this — individual retribution appears in the earliest biblical material; the critical claim that Ezekiel 'invented' it is nonsense.

## Stakes

A subordinate but revealing point. The critical claim that Ezekiel "discovered" individual responsibility is part of a larger narrative about the evolution of moral consciousness. If Kaufmann can show that individual retribution was present from the beginning, he undermines one pillar of the evolutionary model.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The "Ezekiel as discoverer of individual responsibility" claim is largely a straw man at this point — most scholars acknowledge that individual and collective responsibility coexisted throughout. Kaufmann is fighting a 19th-century battle here.

**Alternative reading:** The issue isn't whether individual retribution existed in early Israel — it's that the EXILE created a crisis where collective punishment (the nation destroyed for accumulated sin) no longer made sense, and individual accountability became theologically dominant. Ezekiel 18 isn't inventing individual responsibility; it's making it the PRIMARY framework in a new situation.

## Assessment

A solid corrective to a simplistic evolutionary scheme, but Kaufmann overstates his case. The coexistence of individual and collective retribution is well established. The more interesting question is what happens to this doctrine under the pressure of theodicy — which Kaufmann addresses in his treatment of Job.
