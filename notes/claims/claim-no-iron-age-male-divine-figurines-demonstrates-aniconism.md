# lewis-ocg-7.2: No provenanced Iron Age male divine figurines exist — the absence demands explanation

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 7 — Iconography of Divinity — Yahweh]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 7

## The Claim

Dever's statement that "no representations of a male deity in terra cotta, metal, or stone have ever been found in clear Iron Age contexts" has "become the starting point for all scholars wrestling with the iconic/aniconic debate." The two exceptions Dever noted (Hazor bronze, Gezer stick figure) have been eliminated. The absence is particularly striking given the abundance of female figurines and the wealth of Late Bronze Age male figurines.

## Evidence

All the striding/smiting male figurines (Megiddo, Tell Balâṭah) are Late Bronze or transitional LB/Iron I. The contrast with female figurines (hundreds of Judean pillar figurines) is stark. The economic argument (precious metals were melted down) can't explain the absence in terra-cotta. "That we have female divine figures in terra-cotta but not male ones suggests some degree of ideological motivation driving the latter's absence." Albertz and Schmitt suggest this is a broader trend — aniconism for major national deities across the southern Levant.

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is an established archaeological fact, not a matter of interpretation; the contrast with female figurines eliminates preservation bias as an alternative explanation.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite aniconism was real practice or just elite rhetoric. The archaeological silence on male figurines is the strongest evidence that aniconism affected actual cult, not just theology.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Uehlinger argues his catalogue of data is "definitely sufficient to make the hypothesis [of an anthropomorphic divine image of Yahweh] more reasonable and probable than its opposite." But he also admits "we are still unable to identify beyond a doubt a Yahweh image as such."

**Alternative reading:** Wooden images — which the image-ban texts frequently mention — wouldn't survive archaeologically. The absence could be a preservation bias rather than evidence of aniconism. But this doesn't explain the terra-cotta gap.

## Assessment

This is one of the most important archaeological facts about Israelite religion, and Lewis handles it well. The contrast with female figurines is the key point — if all divine images were simply not preserved, we'd expect female ones to be missing too. They're not. Something specific was going on with male deity representation.
