# heiser-ur-29.1: The "two powers in heaven" controversy reflected the divine council tradition — not heresy

**Source:** [[Heiser — Part 6 — Thus Says the Lord]] — Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), Chapter 29

## The Claim

Heiser engages the "two powers" controversy in early Judaism — the idea that some Jews believed in a second divine figure alongside Yahweh (later branded heretical). Heiser argues this wasn't heresy — it was a recognition of the divine council structure. "The second power was the Angel of Yahweh, the visible Yahweh who could interact with humans while the invisible Yahweh remained in heaven." This aligns with Sommer's fluidity model: one God, multiple manifestations.

## Evidence

- Alan Segal's *Two Powers in Heaven* (1977) documented that early rabbinic Judaism condemned belief in a second divine power as heresy
- The Angel of Yahweh in the Hebrew Bible speaks AS Yahweh, is addressed AS Yahweh, and receives worship due to Yahweh — yet is distinct from the invisible Yahweh in heaven
- This visible/invisible distinction is not a New Testament innovation — it is present throughout the Hebrew Bible
- The second power is not a separate deity but a localized manifestation of the one God — consistent with ANE divine fluidity patterns

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The "two powers" controversy is well-documented in early Judaism (Alan Segal's work). Heiser's reading that this reflects the visible/invisible Yahweh distinction from the divine council tradition is plausible.

## Stakes

If Heiser is right, early Judaism's "two powers" beliefs were not proto-Christian heresy but authentic Israelite theology rooted in the divine council tradition. The Angel of Yahweh was not a created angel but a visible manifestation of Yahweh himself — what later Christian theology would call a theophany or Christophany.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Rabbinic Judaism which branded "two powers" as heresy. Scholars who see the Angel of Yahweh as a distinct created being, not a manifestation of Yahweh himself. Unitarians who reject any plurality within the Godhead.

**Alternative reading:** The Angel of Yahweh is a high-ranking created angel who speaks on God's behalf as a messenger. The "two powers" heresy was a genuine departure from monotheism, not authentic Israelite theology. The rabbis were right to condemn it.

## Assessment

Heiser's "two powers" reading is one of his strongest contributions. It bridges the Hebrew Bible's Angel of Yahweh theology with early Judaism's "two powers" controversy and Sommer's fluidity model. The connection to Sommer is particularly productive: both scholars, working independently from different disciplines (biblical theology vs. comparative religion), converge on the same pattern — one God, multiple localized manifestations. This convergence strengthens both arguments.
