# albertz-hir-5.4: The canonization of the Torah was a Persian-backed compromise between Deuteronomistic (lay) and Priestly parties

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 5 — Post-Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"The Torah, created in the fifth century probably under Persian pressure, is a compromise between the adherents of Deuteronomistic theology and those of priestly theology, which was to secure the religious identity and legal basis of the community of Judah along the lines of the two dominant groups."

## Evidence

- Ezra's mission (398 BCE in Albertz's preferred dating) involved Persian imperial authorization (Frei's "Reichsautorisation" model) — making Jewish law binding as imperial law for all Judaeans in Transeuphratene
- Parallel cases: the Xanthus stele, the Egyptian legal codification under Darius I, the Elephantine Passover Letter — all show Persian practice of authorizing local legal traditions
- The Pentateuch is literarily composite: KD (a lay/Deuteronomistic composition — Gen 12 through Deuteronomy, connected to the council of elders) was followed by KP (a priestly composition/revision — connected to the priestly college)
- Blum's traditio-historical model: the lay theologians wrote first, the priests responded, producing a "tense, agitated dialogue" that became the Torah
- "The juxtaposition, opposition and also interweaving of the two Pentateuchal compositions can be sufficiently explained from the juxtaposition of two rival groups of tradents who had different, indeed contrary, views of what could make up and preserve the identity of Israel"
- Ezra's role was not to INTRODUCE the Torah (it was already known, v.25) but to secure its PROPER IMPLEMENTATION with Persian state backing

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Frei's imperial authorization model is influential but not universally accepted; the Pentateuch's internal tensions as product of two-party compromise is increasingly consensus.

## Stakes

This completely reframes the origin of the Bible. The Torah wasn't a purely internal religious development — it was shaped by imperial politics. The Pentateuch's internal tensions (Deuteronomistic vs. Priestly) aren't accidents of redaction but the PRODUCT of the canonization process itself: a deliberately unresolved dialogue between rival theological parties, both of whom had to be included for the document to function as a unifying charter.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date Ezra earlier (458) and see him as introducing a substantially new law. Those who reject the imperial authorization model. Minimalists who date the Pentateuch much later (Hellenistic period).

**Alternative reading:** The Pentateuch's composition was a purely internal Jewish development, and Persian involvement was limited to post-hoc recognition. Or: Ezra's law was a specific legal corpus (P, or the Holiness Code), not the full Pentateuch.

## Assessment

This is one of the most important claims in the entire two-volume work. The "two-party compromise" model elegantly explains the Pentateuch's internal tensions without resorting to purely literary explanations. The Persian imperial authorization model gives a plausible political mechanism. However, the reconstruction is highly inferential — we have no direct evidence of the negotiation process. The strength of the claim is its explanatory power, not its direct attestation.
