# sommer-bog-app.1: Monotheism was well established among biblical authors before the exile

**Source:** [[Sommer — The Bodies of God]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Appendix

## The Claim

"I believe the documents preserved in the Hebrew Bible, when taken as a whole, are monotheistic."

Sommer counts himself among scholars who argue "monotheism was well established in ancient Israel considerably earlier than the exile, even though some Israelites (perhaps even most Israelites) were not monotheists."

This puts Sommer in an interesting position: he REJECTS the polytheism → monotheism narrative for the biblical authors themselves, while accepting that popular religion was polytheistic. The biblical writers were monotheists from early on — but their monotheism was of the FLUID variety, not the abstract, transcendent variety of later Judaism.

## Evidence

Sommer's evidence for this claim is distributed throughout the book. Key components:
- J and E sources are monotheistic (only Yhwh is truly God) but fluid (Yhwh has multiple bodies/manifestations)
- P and D are monotheistic and anti-fluid
- The biblical documents, despite their internal theological diversity, are unified in affirming that Yhwh alone is God
- Popular Israelite religion included worship of other deities, but the biblical AUTHORS were monotheists

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Depends heavily on how one defines "monotheism." If monotheism means "only Yhwh is truly God, other gods are nonentities or subordinate beings," then Sommer's claim is defensible. If monotheism means "denial that other gods exist at all," then even many biblical texts fall short. The distinction between authorial theology and popular religion is crucial but hard to verify independently.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right that the biblical authors were early monotheists (of the fluid variety), then the standard critical narrative — polytheism → monolatry → monotheism, with monotheism emerging only in the exile — is wrong about the AUTHORS even if right about the POPULATION. The Bible is not a record of Israel's gradual discovery of monotheism. It's a record of monotheists arguing about what KIND of monotheism is correct.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Smith, Römer, Dever (who date monotheism later); scholars who argue that J and E are not monotheistic but monolatrous or henotheistic.

**Alternative reading:** The biblical authors were monolatrists (Yhwh alone is worthy of Israel's worship), not monotheists (Yhwh is the only god that exists). True monotheism — the denial that other gods exist — emerges only in Deutero-Isaiah during the exile. Sommer conflates monolatry with monotheism.

## Assessment

Sommer's claim about early monotheism among the biblical authors is a minority position in current scholarship but not an indefensible one. It depends on defining monotheism as "only Yhwh is truly God" rather than "no other gods exist." The former definition fits much of the Hebrew Bible; the latter fits only Deutero-Isaiah and a few other texts. Whether the former counts as "monotheism" is a semantic question with theological stakes. Sommer's real contribution is not the dating of monotheism but his demonstration that the monotheism that DID exist was more theologically interesting — more fluid, more embodied — than the standard narrative allows.
