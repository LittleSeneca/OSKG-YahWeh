# kaufmann-ri-10.5: Consolation prophecies are native to covenant theology, not mythological imports

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"Both the reproof and the consolation of the prophets spring from the same ground — the perpetuity of the kingdom of God." There is "no need... to look for a special source of Israel's prophecies of consolation (e.g., the Gunkel-Gressmann view that it stems from popular mythological-eschatological beliefs)." The prophets denounce Israel for profaning the covenant; "the denunciation cannot... be an end in itself; it aims at arousing the people to repentance so that the glory of the divine kingdom may be restored."

## Evidence

- Early Israelite eschatology is rooted in "faith in the perpetuity of Israel as the kingdom of God, of the covenant and its concretizations: the land, the people, national independence, and the monarchy"
- YHWH's kingdom "is not something to be achieved in the future as a result of YHWH's triumph over forces of evil in the end of days, but has already come into being through his covenant with Israel"
- "Through chastisement and suffering, Israel's sin will be expiated, and then it will return to its former state, or be even more glorious than in time past"
- The prophets share this faith: "They, too, regard the land, the people, the temple, Jerusalem, and the monarchy as the visible symbols — in the future, no less than in the past — of God's kingdom"
- Destruction "does not come from demonic sources whose defeat is a condition of YHWH's kingship. YHWH himself brings it on to expunge sin, and thereby to reconstitute his kingdom"
- "He remains king during the period of chastisement"
- "To separate the religious-moral from the national-messianic elements of prophecy reflects a modern prejudice"
- Prophecies of ingathering "do not necessarily imply a date after 586. From the times of Tiglath-pileser an ever growing Israelite dispersion existed"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The theological coherence argument is convincing — in a covenant framework, judgment and restoration aren't opposites; but this doesn't prove all consolation oracles are historically from the named prophet.

## Stakes

Whether the prophetic books are theologically unified (doom + consolation = single message) or theologically composite (doom = original prophet, consolation = later additions). The critical view often treats consolation oracles as secondary precisely because they seem to undermine the urgency of repentance.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The Gunkel-Gressmann school, which traced Israelite eschatology to borrowed mythological themes (the combat myth, the dying and rising god, the New Year festival). More generally, scholars who see consolation oracles as exilic or post-exilic additions to pre-exilic doom oracles.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that consolation *theologically* follows from covenant logic, but wrong that this means all consolation oracles are *historically* from the named prophets. A post-exilic writer could generate consolation oracles from the same theological logic — they wouldn't need to borrow from mythology. The question is not whether consolation is theologically coherent with doom (it is), but whether particular consolation passages come from the 8th-century prophet or from later tradents.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's theological argument is elegant and genuinely insightful. He correctly identifies that in a covenant framework, judgment and restoration aren't opposites — they're two movements of the same divine action. Punishment is purgative, not destructive; the kingdom persists through judgment. This is a better reading of the prophetic logic than the fragmentation approach. However, Kaufmann's rejection of *any* mythological influence is too sweeping. The imagery of the "day of YHWH" as cosmic battle, the language of creation's transformation, the vision of universal peace — these have genuine parallels in ancient Near Eastern mythology even if their *theological function* in Israel is different.
