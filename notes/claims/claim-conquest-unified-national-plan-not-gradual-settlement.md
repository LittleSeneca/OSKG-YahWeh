# kaufmann-ri-7.1: The Conquest Was a Unified National Plan

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VII — The Conquest and Settlement]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VII

## The Claim

"It is the accepted view that Canaan was conquered gradually, in several unrelated stages, by individual tribes or tribal bands. The evidence, however, argues for the unified conquest of Canaan by a confederation of tribes that fought to carry out a national plan of conquest." The consciousness of tribal unity cannot be explained as a late development — the centrifugal forces of post-conquest geography would have dissolved any nascent unity. The survival of twelve-tribe consciousness through centuries of division "must have had its roots in the period before the entry into Canaan."

## Evidence

- **Ideal boundaries predate settlement:** The far-flung northern boundary and the exclusion of Transjordan in the ideal promised land "prove that these limits... were already fixed before the entry into Canaan." No post-conquest political reality produced these boundaries.

- **Judges 1 presupposes a plan:** The chapter records which populations tribes *failed* to dispossess — implying the author "regarded the tribes as following a prearranged plan, each fighting for its own allotted territory." The sum of conquered + unpossessed land equals the complete land of Canaan.

- **Absence of inter-tribal territorial disputes:** "The period of the judges, filled with internecine strife, fails to produce a single boundary dispute or even boundary agreement." The land was divided by lot before conquest — "the settlement followed a prearranged national plan."

- **Archaeology excursus (footnote):** Kaufmann acknowledges the Jericho dating debates but insists his argument doesn't depend on treating the biblical stories as straightforward reports — the "general considerations" prove historical substratum even if individual elements are legendary.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann correctly identifies that the biblical texts *present* the conquest as a unified national project. The ideal boundaries argument is genuinely good — the limits of the promised land don't match any historical Israelite state. But archaeological evidence favors gradual, largely peaceful settlement in the highlands with continuity of material culture. The absence of boundary disputes could be an argument from silence.

## Stakes

The entire nature of early Israel. If the conquest was a unified national-religious project, the "kingdom of God" concept emerges from a real political-military experience. If settlement was gradual and piecemeal, the unity is a retrojected ideal.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The "accepted view" Kaufmann opposes is the dominant critical consensus — the conquest/settlement models of Alt, Noth, and (later) the "peasant revolt" model of Mendenhall and Gottwald. Archaeology supports gradual, largely peaceful settlement in the highlands with continuity of material culture. Fleming (Ch 6) describes the "people of Yahweh" as a collaborative, non-state grouping that coalesced gradually. Cross (Parts II-III) sees the league as a contingent military alliance that developed into an amphictyony.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite unity was REAL but emerged through the settlement process itself — through shared worship at central sanctuaries, common enemies, and the integrative work of the YHWH cult — not through a prearranged plan. The twelve-tribe scheme is an ideal construct (probably late) that retrojects unity onto earlier periods.

## Assessment

Kaufmann correctly identifies that the biblical texts *present* the conquest as a unified national project, and the question is whether this presentation reflects historical reality or theological idealization. His argument that centrifugal forces would have dissolved unity without a pre-conquest foundation has some force — but it assumes that later political fragmentation is the "natural" state and unity requires special explanation. The opposite model is equally plausible: tribal societies often maintain strong kinship-based identity across political fragmentation. Kaufmann's "ideal boundaries" argument is actually quite good — the limits of the promised land don't match any historical Israelite state, so they must predate the settlement. But this doesn't prove a unified *conquest*; it proves a unified *vision* that shaped how the conquest was later narrated.
