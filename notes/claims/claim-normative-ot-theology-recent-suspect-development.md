# schmid-ht-1.4: Normative OT theology is a historically recent and theologically suspect development

**Source:** [[Schmid — §1 — Is There a Theology of the Hebrew Bible]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §1

## The Claim

"In terms of the history of scholarship, the notion of 'Old Testament theology' as an explicitly normative discipline is comparatively new. Its roots lie in the Enlightenment, and it first gained significant ground...under the influence of neo-orthodox theology at the beginning of the twentieth century."

## Evidence

- "Premature demands for normativity with regard to the interpretation of the Bible should...be met with healthy skepticism, especially in Protestant theology"
- "Protestantism reads the Bible as gospel and not as demand. Reading the Bible as gospel means...letting its content and declarations address the audience and, when appropriate, win them over, even if this may not be accorded canonical authority"
- "It would be theologically (and also historically) incorrect to base its ability to persuade on its canonicity, rather than on the nature of its content"
- Canonicity originates in liturgical use — "the prestige of the canonical books refers to their recognition as writings worthy of liturgical use and not as specific content that is now declared normative"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The historical claim (normative OT theology is recent) is well-supported by the history of scholarship Schmid details in Part B. The theological claim (that normativity arises from content, not canonicity) is a Protestant theological judgment, not a historical finding.

## Stakes

This is Schmid's liberation move. If canonicity is a reception-history phenomenon, not an intrinsic property of the texts, then a historical-descriptive theology is legitimate. The texts' authority rests on their persuasive power, not on a declaration. This frees Schmid to do critical theology without needing to defend normative claims.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Catholic and Orthodox traditions that locate authority in the Church's declaration of canonicity. Conservative Protestants who hold to verbal inspiration. Barthians who locate authority in the Word's self-attestation.

**Alternative reading:** Schmid's functional theory of canonicity is itself a normative theological claim — it's a Protestant (specifically liberal Protestant) theology of Scripture. Calling it "historical" obscures its confessional character.

## Assessment

This is elegant theology — but it IS theology, not just historical description. Schmid is making a normative claim about how canonicity works while claiming not to do normative theology. He's right that his approach is more historically defensible than inspiration theories, but he should be more honest that he's doing Protestant theology here, not neutral description.
