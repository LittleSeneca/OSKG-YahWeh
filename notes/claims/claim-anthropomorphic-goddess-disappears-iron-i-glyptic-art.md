# keel-ggi-5.3: The Iron Age I divine iconographic repertoire is exclusively masculine, domineering, and triumphant — the anthropomorphic goddess virtually disappears from glyptic art

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

"By comparison with the repertoire of deities in the Late Bronze Age, that of Iron Age I is reduced markedly. Ptah, whose presence is noted at many different sites during the Late Bronze Age, disappears completely." More dramatically: "As the Egyptians departed, their goddesses, such as Hathor, and also the Egyptian versions of goddesses, such as the lady Anat, disappeared from Palestinian iconography. Images of female deities made using costly materials (metals) are entirely absent in Iron Age I." And most starkly: "If one sets aside consideration of the terracottas for the moment, anthropomorphic figures in Iron Age I show deities that are exclusively masculine, domineering, and triumphant."

The Philistine "Ashdoda" — a seated goddess figure fused with her throne — was popular during the 12th century at Ashdod, Ekron, and other coastal cities, but "after this time period the 'Ashdoda' type disappears. It apparently did not survive the move to accommodate Philistine symbolism to the indigenous system." The one fragment of a nursing mother goddess from Tell Qasile Str. X (late 11th century) is the exception that proves the rule.

## Evidence

- Complete absence of Hathor, Anat, Astarte, and Qudshu from Iron Age I glyptic art — all major LB figures
- "Even goddess depictions (reduced to being of one piece with a throne) that were introduced by the Philistines from Mycenaean contexts disappeared totally at the end of Iron Age I"
- The Ashdoda type: found at Ashdod, Ekron, Aphek — 12th century only; absent from later strata
- The only surviving female divine images are on "inexpensive terracottas... only the simplest forms of the 'naked woman,' without attributes" — used in "personal piety" settings, primarily by women
- By contrast, male deities proliferate: Amun-Re, Seth-Baal, Reshef, the "Lord of Crocodiles," the archer, the enthroned god

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is the chapter's central empirical finding, documented across every site and medium surveyed. The contrast with Late Bronze Age (Ch IV) — where goddesses were abundant and diverse — is stark and undeniable.

## Stakes

If the anthropomorphic goddess disappears from the official/elite iconographic record during Iron Age I — BEFORE the emergence of Israel as a state — then the absence of a divine consort for Yahweh in Iron Age II may not result from "Yahweh-alone" polemics suppressing her, but from a prior, broader Canaanite cultural shift away from anthropomorphic goddess imagery. This would significantly complicate Dever's argument that the Judean Pillar Figurines represent the popular survival of Asherah worship suppressed by orthodox Yahwism. If the goddess was ALREADY depersonalized in Iron Age I, the JPFs of Iron II may represent something different from simple continuity.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever argues that goddess figurines show CONTINUITY of Asherah worship from Bronze Age through Iron II. Keel and Uehlinger's claim that anthropomorphic goddess imagery "disappeared" depends on privileging glyptic art (seals, metalwork) over terracotta figurines. They acknowledge this tension: "We now find images of the female deity only on inexpensive terracottas" — but they treat terracottas as a separate, lower-status phenomenon.

**Alternative reading:** The goddess did not disappear; she migrated from elite media (seals, metal) to popular media (terracotta). This could reflect changes in production and patronage rather than changes in belief. The "disappearance" is an artifact of Keel and Uehlinger's seal-centric methodology.

## Assessment

This is the most important finding in the chapter, and also the most debatable. Keel and Uehlinger are right about the glyptic record — the goddess IS gone from seals. But their dismissal of terracottas as merely "personal piety" may underestimate their significance. In a period when seal ownership may have been narrowing (fewer literate elites), terracottas may have been the PRIMARY medium of popular religious expression. The "disappearance" may be a class phenomenon, not a religious one.
