# keel-ggi-p2.7: The LB witnessed a radical split between "official" male warrior religion and "private" goddess piety

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

"During this era, which witnessed the increasing suppression of the female god(s) within Late Bronze Age 'official' religion, as represented in the great temples, in the Egyptian city-state and colonial power structure, and in products manufactured in the workshops that were to spread these ideas, there was concurrently a huge increase in the production of inexpensive terra-cotta plaques depicting a 'naked goddess,' clearly showing that the traditions connected with the Middle Bronze Age goddess continued to hold sway." The split: official religion (costly materials, monumental scale, male warrior/political deities, Egyptian style) vs. private religion (cheap terra-cotta, domestic contexts, goddess figurines, indigenous Canaanite style).

## Evidence (synthesized from entire Part II)

- Costly metal figurines of goddesses virtually cease production; metal is now used for warrior and enthroned male deities (Baal, Reshef, the pharaoh)
- Monumental art (stelae, temple decoration) is dominated by Egyptian royal theology: Amun, Re-harakhty, Ptah, the divine pharaoh in battle/victory scenes
- Seal amulets (the most common image-bearing artifacts) are "almost exclusively male" — goddesses appear "only in the form of a fetish (Hathor) or as part of a king's name (Ma'at)"
- Meanwhile, "inexpensive terra-cotta plaques depicting a 'naked goddess'" proliferate — produced by the thousands, found overwhelmingly in domestic contexts
- Male deities "are hardly represented in terra-cotta" — terra-cotta is the medium of the goddess
- The goddess's iconography shifts: she is "shown as the mistress of the city (Beth-Shean) or as a warrior deity... in a role that corresponds more closely to the 'masculine' attributes that would be associated with a power-woman"
- "The change from expensive to easily affordable material carried with it the assumption that the goddess had now entered the realm of what some call a 'private piety' and that the goddess was more approachable than ever before in the little cultic centers that were set up in each home"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High that a split existed. Evidence across four cities (Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, Beth-Shean) converges on the same pattern: official = costly + Egyptian + male; private = cheap + indigenous + goddess. Medium on whether this represents deliberate "suppression" vs. natural consequence of Egyptian colonial administration.

## Stakes

This is the archaeological precedent for everything that follows. The split between official Yahwism (male, aniconic, Jerusalem-centered, text-based) and popular religion (goddess-inclusive, iconic, domestic, practice-based) that Dever documents for Iron Age II Judah has its roots in the LB colonial situation. Egyptian colonialism didn't just suppress goddesses — it created the very category of "official religion" as distinct from "popular religion" by imposing a state cult that was indifferent to domestic practice. Moreover, if this split was already in place during the LB, then the emergence of Israel in Iron Age I did not create the conditions for goddess suppression — it inherited them. Yahweh's rise to exclusive worship occurred in a landscape where the goddess had already been relegated to the domestic sphere for centuries.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize textual evidence (Smith 1990, de Moor 1990) tend to treat LB Canaanite religion as a unified pantheon. The Amarna letters and Ugaritic texts give a picture of an integrated divine family — but Keel & Uehlinger's material evidence shows that what was actually practiced varied enormously by city, region, and social class.

**Alternative reading:** Keel & Uehlinger may overstate the split. The Anat stele at Beth-Shean shows a goddess in costly monumental form. The qudshu goddess appears on gold leaf and Egyptian-style stelae. The goddess wasn't *only* in terra-cotta. It was more about *what kind* of goddess was acceptable in official contexts — warrior goddess (Anat) and Egyptianized city-mistress could appear on monuments; nurturing naked fertility goddess was pushed into domestic terra-cotta.

## Assessment

The split is real and important, but Keel & Uehlinger's own evidence shows it's not absolute. The Beth-Shean Anat stele and the Lachish gold-leaf goddess-on-horse show that goddesses could still appear in costly media when they adopted "masculine" traits. This suggests the split was partly about *what kind* of goddess was acceptable in official contexts. The nurturing, naked, fertility-focused goddess was pushed into domestic terra-cotta; the warrior goddess (Anat) and Egyptianized city-mistress could still appear on monuments. This nuance matters for understanding Asherah in the Iron Age: she straddles the official/private divide, appearing as both a fertility symbol (asherim poles) and a divine consort (Kuntillet Ajrud).
