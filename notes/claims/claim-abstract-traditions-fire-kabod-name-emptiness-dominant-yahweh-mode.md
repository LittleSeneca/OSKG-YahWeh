# lewis-ocg-7.4: Abstract traditions (fire, kābôd, Name, sacred emptiness) are the dominant mode of representing Yahweh

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 7 — Iconography of Divinity — Yahweh]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 7

## The Claim

The abstract traditions — Yahweh represented by fire (burning bush, Sinai theophany, Elijah on Carmel), by radiance/glory (_kābôd_ in P and Ezekiel), by his Name (_šēm_ in Deuteronomy/DtrH), and by sacred emptiness (invisible enthronement on cherubim) — represent "the dominant mode of representing Yahweh." These traditions are not late theological inventions but have deep roots in Israelite religion.

## Evidence

Fire traditions: Moses' burning bush (Exod 3), Sinai theophany (Exod 19), pillar of fire (Exod 13:21), Elijah's Carmel contest (1 Kgs 18), fiery anthropomorphisms (Yahweh's mouth breathes fire, nostrils snort smoke). Kābôd traditions: P's tabernacle narrative (Exod 40:34-38), Ezekiel's elaborate vision (Ezek 1), Isaiah's temple vision (Isa 6). Name theology: Deuteronomy's insistence that Yahweh causes his Name to dwell (_šakkēn šēm_) in the chosen place (Deut 12:5, 11; 14:23; 16:2, 6, 11), replacing the notion of Yahweh himself dwelling there. The Name "resides" in a way that avoids embodied limitation. Sacred emptiness: Yahweh "enthroned upon the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4; 2 Sam 6:2) but invisibly — the cherubim throne is empty. The Ayn Dara temple in Syria has gigantic footprints leading into the inner sanctum — a physical representation of an invisible deity.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The four modes are attested across multiple independent biblical traditions; the Ayn Dara comparator shows invisible enthronement was a known ancient Near Eastern concept, not just Israelite theological spin.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite aniconism was a genuine innovation or just retroactive theological spin. Lewis argues it was real, ancient, and multifaceted — not a single Deuteronomistic party line but multiple independent traditions converging on the idea that Yahweh shouldn't be depicted physically.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The "statue in the Temple" school (Niehr, Uehlinger) sees these abstract traditions as late apologetics covering up earlier iconic practice. Stavrakopoulou emphasizes the radical anthropomorphism of the texts and sees the aniconic traditions as one voice among many.

**Alternative reading:** The abstract traditions weren't dominant in practice — they dominated the SURVIVING texts because those texts were edited by aniconic parties. Popular religion may have been more iconic than our sources admit.

## Assessment

Lewis's treatment of the abstract traditions is the most valuable part of this chapter. The four modes (fire, kābôd, Name, emptiness) form a coherent typology that shows aniconism wasn't one thing — it was multiple creative solutions to the problem of representing an invisible god. The Ayn Dara footprints are a particularly striking comparator: Israel wasn't alone in imagining invisible deity, but it developed the idea further than anyone else.
