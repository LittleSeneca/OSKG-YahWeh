# dever-dghw-6-9.5: Asherah was a real goddess, Yahweh's consort — the full cumulative archaeological case

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapters VI-IX — Asherah, Women, Monotheism]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter VI

## The Claim

The biblical writers' ambivalence about the asherah reflects their embarrassment about a "Hebrew Goddess" they couldn't deny but wanted to forget. "Originally the biblical writers were embarrassed by the notion of a 'Hebrew Goddess,' but they could not condemn her and her cult without tacitly, but covertly, acknowledging her existence." By the time the canonical texts were finalized, "the old Mother Goddess had been driven underground and all but forgotten." But archaeology has brought her back. The final redactors "did not know who 'Asherah' had been, or whether she had existed at all" — only archaeology can answer the questions the biblical text raises but cannot resolve.

## Evidence

**1. Biblical language itself:**
- Hebrew *'asherah* occurs over 40 times
- The consonantal text can be read EITHER as "the asherah" (symbol) OR "Asherah" (goddess) — the ambiguity is deliberate
- In at least several passages, the term MUST refer to a goddess:
  - 1 Kings 15:13: "an abominable image made for Asherah" — you can't make an image FOR an image
  - 1 Kings 18:19: "the 400 prophets of Baal and the 400 prophets of Asherah" — if Baal is a deity, so is Asherah
  - 2 Kings 23:4: listed alongside Baal and "the host of heaven" — both deities
- The ancient versions (LXX, Vulgate) understood *'asherim* as connected with trees/sacred groves but also occasionally with Astarte — preserving memory of a female deity

**2. Ugaritic background:**
- 14th-13th century texts portray Asherah (*Aṯiratu*) as El's consort
- "Lady Asherah of the Sea," "Mother of the Gods," "Creatress of the Gods"
- She had seventy divine sons
- When El and Yahweh merged, Asherah came with the package

**3. Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions** (c. 800 BCE): "Yahweh of Samaria/Teman and his Asherah"

**4. Khirbet el-Qom inscription** (c. 700 BCE): Burial inscription invoking blessing "by Yahweh and his Asherah"

**5. 3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines:** Mass-produced nude female figurines found in domestic contexts throughout Judah. "Found in almost every excavated 8th-6th century house in Judah." These are almost certainly Asherah representations.

**6. Cult stands and iconography:** The Taanach stand shows a nude female, lions, cherubim, a tree of life, and a young bull — "an encyclopedia of Canaanite-Israelite religion."

**7. Standing stones at Arad:** Two *massebot* in the holy of holies — probably representing Yahweh and Asherah as a divine couple.

**8. Persistence of the symbol:** Even after Josiah's reform removed the asherah from the Jerusalem temple (2 Kings 23:6-7), the figurines continued in domestic contexts. Folk religion outlasted official religion.

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The cumulative evidence from seven converging categories is overwhelming; the inscriptions are unambiguous about the pairing; Dever's archaeological case is stronger than Smith's text-based skepticism.

## Stakes

"Did God have a wife?" Dever's archaeological answer is yes. This directly challenges both traditional Jewish and Christian theology. If the biblical God absorbed a goddess consort from Canaanite religion, the claim that Israel's religion was uniquely revealed and fundamentally different from its neighbors becomes difficult to sustain.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mark S. Smith (argues for symbol-only reading at Kuntillet Ajrud), Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger (argue "his asherah" = cultic symbol, stylized tree, not goddess consort), Yehezkel Kaufmann (Israel was non-pagan from the beginning, Asherah was vestigial fetishism).

**Alternative reading:** "His asherah" refers to a wooden cult object symbolizing Yahweh's presence, not a goddess consort. The figurines represent apotropaic or healing amulets, not named deities. The Arad massebot may both represent Yahweh (or Yahweh and his asherah-symbol).

## Assessment

This is Dever's strongest chapter and the most complete presentation of the archaeological case for Asherah as goddess consort. The seven converging data categories make the case cumulative rather than dependent on any single artifact. However, Dever does not fully engage Keel/Uehlinger's specific counter-argument about paratactic composition on the Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi, which remains the strongest challenge to the "divine couple" reading. The goddess interpretation is more parsimonious overall, but the symbol interpretation is not ruled out by the evidence Dever presents.
