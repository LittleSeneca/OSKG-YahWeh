# albertz-hir-4.10: Deutero-Isaiah was the first to formulate consistent monotheism — as an internal Judahite development, not borrowed from Persia or Babylon

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 4 — Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"First of all — probably for the first time in the history of Israelite religion — it arrived at the formulation of a consistent monotheism." He explicitly rejects external stimulus theories (Persian, Babylonian): "some tendencies to monolatry or 'monotheism' can be recognized in the late Babylonian period, but these either go quite different ways theologically... or are chronologically later." Instead, monotheism was "an inner-Judahite theological development."

## Evidence

- The universal claims of the hymns (Yahweh as creator of the world, lord of history: Isa 40.12-17, 18-24, 25-26) were radicalized: Yahweh creates not only light and salvation but also darkness and disaster (45.7 — contra Gen 1.2)
- The dispute with the nations' gods is framed as a legal dispute, not a battle — Israel is only a witness
- The explicit denial formula: "I am Yahweh and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (45.5, etc.) — this goes beyond monolatry ("worship only Yahweh") to ontological denial of other gods' existence
- Albertz argues this was a consequence of the group's core message: if Yahweh could raise up a foreign king (Cyrus) as his anointed to save Israel, then Yahweh's power must be truly universal — and if truly universal, then other gods cannot have any real power
- External stimuli (Persian Zoroastrianism, Babylonian identification theology) are improbable: the former is chronologically problematic, the latter goes in different theological directions

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The "first consistent monotheism" claim depends on definitions. The relative chronology with Persian influence is debated. And whether Deutero-Isaiah represents "consistent" monotheism (vs. rhetorical monolatry) is disputed.

## Stakes

This is one of the most important claims in the entire book. If Albertz is right, monotheism emerges not from philosophical speculation but from the specific historical situation of a marginal community trying to make sense of world politics. And critically, it emerges in a situation of political powerlessness, not imperial ambition — which has implications for how monotheism relates to violence and intolerance.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Persian influence as decisive (Zoroastrian dualism → Jewish monotheism). Scholars who date "consistent" monotheism later (Third Isaiah, or even Hellenistic period). Mark S. Smith (Origins of Biblical Monotheism) sees a longer, more gradual process — Deutero-Isaiah is a stage, not the point of origin.

**Alternative reading:** Monotheism was a gradual development from pre-exilic monolatry through exilic rhetoric to post-exilic consolidation. Deutero-Isaiah is a rhetorical high point but not a conceptual breakthrough. Or: monotheism was always implicit in Yahwism and the exile simply made it explicit.
