# kaufmann-ri-9.9: Job demonstrates the triumph of faith over wisdom — Israelite theodicy's distinctive contribution

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

Job is "religious to its very core. It comes from the realm of revelation, not wisdom; this is the distinctive Israelite feature of the book." The legend's Job is a righteous man who withstands trial; the dialogue's Job is a sage whose mind leads him to deny the moral order. "With wonderful subtlety the poet lets Job the sage finish the work of Satan and deal Job the righteous man the final blow: to deprive him of his dearest treasure, faith in divine justice." Yet even in despair, Job clings to his righteousness. "His final word is this great oath. Though his world has collapsed, he clings to the one value that is left him, his righteousness." God's answer is not a rational solution but a theophany — the very fact of revelation restores faith. "I had heard of thee only a report, but now my eye beholds thee" (42:5 f.).

## Evidence

- Two elements combine: prose framework (ancient legend) and poetic dialogue (wisdom)
- The Satan of the legend tests whether a righteous man can withstand trials — he does
- The dialogue's Job is a sage driven by logic to deny moral providence: "the wicked man prospers and goes unpunished"
- Yet Job never curses God, never draws impious conclusions — he remains "profoundly moral and religious" throughout
- Job's companions argue the conventional wisdom dogma: "Job has suffered, ergo he is wicked"
- Elihu (a transition figure, possibly the poet's reflex) argues from providence in nature and human moral consciousness
- YHWH's speech from the whirlwind is the decisive answer — not its content, but the fact of revelation itself
- "I had heard of thee only a report, but now my eye beholds thee" (42:5 f.) — this is Job's last word
- The book is pre-exilic — nothing stands in the way of this dating
- "Pagan wisdom asks and answers; it presumes itself capable of solving the problem and proceeds to pass a negative verdict concerning the gods. Israelite wisdom asks but does not answer; it knows that man is dust and ashes"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's structural reading (the two Jobs, Elihu as transition) is genuinely illuminating, but his conclusion that it demonstrates 'conquest of pagan wisdom' domesticates a more disturbing book.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite theodicy represents a genuine advance over pagan wrestling with suffering. Kaufmann's answer: yes, because it refuses to blame God or posit an evil principle, and because the final resolution is faith in God's presence, not a rational solution.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the whirlwind speeches as non-answers — God browbeats Job with displays of power rather than addressing the justice question. The book may be subverting theodicy rather than resolving it. Kaufmann's reading of the theophany as "the answer" is one interpretive tradition among several.

**Alternative reading:** The book of Job may be DEEPLY subversive of the retribution doctrine. God never says Job was wrong about the lack of moral order — he just says Job doesn't understand creation. The epilogue restores Job's fortunes but the theological problem remains unresolved. The book may be wisdom literature's critique OF wisdom literature, not a religious resolution.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's structural reading (the two Jobs, Elihu as transition) is elegant and illuminating — this is genuinely helpful exegesis. But his conclusion that the book demonstrates the "conquest of pagan wisdom" by Israelite faith is too neat. The book is more disturbing than Kaufmann allows — God never actually answers the question, and the happy ending doesn't retroactively justify the suffering. Kaufmann domesticates Job into his system rather than letting it trouble his system. That said, his observation that Israelite theodicy is distinctive in REFUSING to blame God or posit an evil principle IS important — even if Job pushes that refusal to its breaking point.
