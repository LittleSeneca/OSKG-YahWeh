# kaufmann-ri-1.6: The golden calf story depicts fetishism, not mythological polytheism

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"In this portrayal of Israel's prototypal sin we have a classic representation of the biblical view of idolatry. The sin is not that the people represent YHWH in the figure of an ox." Rather, "They do not give their allegiance to a living god, one of the gods of the nations or of their own ancient pantheon, but to an anonymous image, just now fashioned out of their own trinkets. In this calf, this idol that was not the image of a god, but a god itself, the Bible embodies its conception of Israelite idolatry as fetishism."

## Evidence

- **The golden calf (Exod 32):** The people demand a "god who will go before them." Aaron makes a "molten calf." The people cry "This is your god, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt." Kaufmann's key interpretive move: they're not worshipping a known deity (Baal, El) through an image — they're worshipping the image itself as a newly created god.
- **Nebuchadnezzar's image (Dan 3):** A late story showing the same conception: "idolatry is the worship of an anonymous idol, an idol that represents no god at all, but is itself to be worshiped."
- **Continuity across periods:** "Even as late as Persian and Hellenistic times the Jewish attitude toward paganism was determined by the belief that the pagans worshiped idol-fetishes."

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** LOW on the historical claim (that Israelite idolatry was actually fetishism); MEDIUM on the literary claim (that this is how the Bible *portrays* Israelite idolatry). The golden calf narrative is widely recognized as polemic against Jeroboam's calves at Bethel and Dan.

## Stakes

The nature of Israelite "apostasy." If Kaufmann is right, when Israelites "worshipped idols" they weren't reverting to Canaanite polytheism — they were engaging in a degraded, contentless fetishism. This allows Kaufmann to accept the Bible's picture of constant apostasy while denying that this apostasy involved genuine polytheistic belief.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The golden calf narrative is widely recognized as a polemic against Jeroboam's calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28-29), which were almost certainly YHWH cult images — not "anonymous idols." Dever argues that archaeological evidence shows Israelites DID worship Canaanite deities (Baal, Asherah) alongside YHWH. Römer documents that the "calves" of the northern kingdom were likely YHWH images, not foreign gods.

**Alternative reading:** The golden calf story is a polemical construct designed to delegitimize the northern kingdom's calf iconography. The historical reality: Jeroboam's calves were YHWH images, and the people who worshipped before them understood themselves to be worshipping YHWH (the god who "brought you up out of the land of Egypt"), not anonymous fetishes.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's method in microcosm — and it reveals the problem. He reads Exodus 32 as a transparent window onto Israelite religious practice, when it's clearly a theological polemic with a specific political agenda (delegitimizing the northern cult). The narrative's portrayal of idolatry as fetishism is evidence of how the *author* wanted to characterize apostasy, not evidence of what apostasy actually looked like. Kaufmann's willingness to treat the Bible's own categories as historical data is both his greatest strength and his greatest weakness.
