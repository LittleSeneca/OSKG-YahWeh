# keel-ggi-3.2: At Megiddo, female deity figurines dramatically outnumber male ones (16:6), and the weather god appears primarily in association with fertility, not battle

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch III — MB Temples Tel Kitan and Megiddo]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter III

## The Claim

"Unlike at Nahariyah, Gezer, and the settlement of Tel Kitan, male deities are here as well, but they are by far in the minority. Of the 22 figures, 16 are female deities and only six are male (Negbi 1976, 131)." On the weather god: "Where he does appear, his relationship with fertility is accentuated, not his aggressive nature; that aspect will become more important during the Late Bronze Age. He does in fact raise his right hand but he holds no weapon, so that the gesture depicts more likely a victory rather than a battle. In his other hand, stretched out in front of himself, the deity holds a branch or a blossom."

## Evidence

- Megiddo sacral area (Area BB): open-air sanctuary in late MB IIA (ca. 1800), restructured in early MB IIB (Strata XII–XI) into a broadroom temple (5.5 × 3 m) with massebahs and altar podium; replaced near end of MB IIB (Stratum X) by a rectangular temple with thick walls, north-facing entrance, and southern niche
- A mold and 21 metal figurines found in MB levels of the sacral area, plus one from Area CC = 22 total
- Three naked female sheet-bronze figures (1800–1700) similar to Nahariyah and Gezer types (Loud 1948, Negbi 1976)
- Later MB IIB (1700–1500): cast female figures wearing high pointed head covering, holding both breasts
- Male figures in "Anatolian posture" with weapons appear as early as MB IIA (Loud 1948, Negbi 1976); a limestone casting mold from Stratum XIIIB (1800–1750) shows mass production of weapon-wielding gods
- The cylinder seal from Megiddo: weather god with club, axe, and rope tied around a reclining bull; goddess facing him, pushing clothing aside with one hand; three bull heads emphasizing connection
- On scarabs: weather god holds branch/blossom, not weapon — victory gesture, not battle posture

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The figurine count is a direct archaeological datum from Negbi's authoritative 1976 corpus. The observation about fertility emphasis on scarabs is an interpretive claim about iconographic emphasis, but it is well-supported by the visual evidence they reference.

## Stakes

This establishes the MB IIB baseline: the goddess is numerically dominant in temple deposits, and even the weather god (who will later become the aggressive warrior Baal of the Ugaritic texts) is depicted primarily as a fertility/vegetation deity. This challenges the assumption — common in text-based reconstructions — that the Canaanite pantheon was always headed by an aggressive male warrior god. The MB IIB evidence suggests a different religious configuration: goddess-centered, with the male deity in a subordinate and fertility-oriented role.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who privilege the Ugaritic textual corpus (Late Bronze, 14th–13th c.) and project its theology backward onto MB IIB Palestine would find this picture unfamiliar. The Ugaritic Baal cycle emphasizes Baal's kingship and warrior aspects; Keel and Uehlinger are suggesting this emphasis is a Late Bronze development, not the original Canaanite pattern.

**Alternative reading:** The figurine count may reflect differential preservation or deposition practices rather than actual cultic emphasis. Female figurines might have been deposited more frequently as votives, while male deity worship took other forms (e.g., massebahs, animal sacrifice) that leave no figurine trace. The scarab evidence about the weather god's peaceful aspect could reflect Egyptian artistic conventions (which avoided depicting foreign gods as threatening) rather than Canaanite theology.

## Assessment

The 16:6 ratio is striking and hard to explain away. Combined with the evidence from Chapter II (the "Naked Goddess" on seals and the goddess on the Lachish ewer, the Gezer and Nahariyah figurines), it builds a cumulative case that MB IIB religion was goddess-prominent. The observation about the weather god's changing iconography (fertility in MB, warrior in LB) is a classic Keel-Uehlinger move: tracking a single deity's iconographic evolution across periods to show how religious concepts shifted. This directly supports their "seismograph" methodology from Chapter I.
