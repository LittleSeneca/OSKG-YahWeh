# keel-ggi-4.5: Jerusalem Temple Iconography is Gender-Neutral and Royal — the Goddess Structurally Absent

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part IV — Iron Age IIA]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part IV

## The Claim

"All the decoration in the Solomonic temple is predicated upon the idea that this is the place of divine order and blessing, but the motifs are not gender specific or else are associated with a 'male' royal god." The biblical description of Solomon's Temple (1 Kings 6-7) describes cherubs, palm trees, open flowers, lions, and oxen — motifs well-attested in Iron IIA Syro-Phoenician royal iconography. What is absent is equally telling: NO doves, NO caprids at trees, NO suckling animals, NO "Mistress of the Animals" — all motifs associated with the goddess in Bronze Age and Iron I iconography.

## Evidence

- The Temple was "built in the tradition of the Syrian long-room style" with "privileged trade relations and technological ties with the Syro-Phoenicians"
- The Holy of Holies (dĕbîr): an enclosed, windowless wooden cube ~10m per side — "an adaptation of an Egyptian tradition about a chapel of the gods"
- Two gold-covered wooden cherubs (1 Kgs 6:23-28) with inner wings held horizontally to form the throne for the invisibly present Yahweh
- "The cherub throne, not the ancient Israelite ark... became 'the new symbol for the presence of God'"
- Cherub thrones are attested archaeologically: LB ivories from Megiddo, Iron I terra-cotta fragments from Megiddo, and the sarcophagus of King Ahiram of Byblos (~1000 BCE)
- Phoenicia maintained "widespread and fairly continuous use" of (sometimes empty) cherub thrones into the Hellenistic Period
- The palm trees, flowers, lions, and oxen are standard Syro-Phoenician royal decorative motifs — associated with kingship, not fertility goddesses
- "The tree no longer represents the goddess but stands for the kingdom"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the iconographic parallels. MEDIUM for the biblical description reflecting 10th-century reality (vs. later retrojection).

## Stakes

If the Temple's iconography matches the Iron IIA archaeological pattern, the Jerusalem cult was aniconic from the beginning — and the goddess was structurally excluded from its decorative program. This is the architectural correlate of the broader aniconic trend.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Temple descriptions to Josiah or the exile. Also those who argue for Asherah's presence in the First Temple (based on 2 Kgs 21:7 — Manasseh's installation — and 2 Kgs 23:6 — Josiah's removal).

**Alternative reading:** The Temple descriptions could be a post-exilic retrojection that happens to preserve accurate Iron Age iconographic details. Manasseh's asherah (7th century) may represent a late introduction of the goddess into a Temple that was previously goddess-free.

## Assessment

The iconographic parallels between the Temple description and the Iron IIA archaeological record are independent of the Solomon question. Whether the Temple was built in the 10th, 9th, or even 8th century, its decorative program matches what we find archaeologically. Even in the Temple, the goddess is present only through what she has become: royal blessing symbols that no longer carry her name.
