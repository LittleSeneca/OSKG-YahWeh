# romer-inv-9.3: The Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions prove Yhwh and Asherah were paired

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 9 — Yhwh and His Asherah]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 9: Yhwh and His Asherah

## The Claim

The 8th-century inscriptions from Kuntillet Ajrud ("Yhwh of Samaria/Teman and his Asherah") and Khirbet el-Qom ("Yhwh and his Asherah") are unambiguous evidence that Asherah was paired with Yhwh. The possessive suffix on *'šrth* is most naturally read as referring to the goddess, not a cult object.

## Evidence

- **Kuntillet Ajrud (c. 800 BCE):** "Yhwh of Samaria and his Asherah" / "Yhwh of Teman and his Asherah"
- **Khirbet el-Qom (c. 700 BCE):** A burial inscription invoking blessing "by Yhwh and his Asherah" — found in the heartland of Judah
- **Kuntillet Ajrud was not a fringe site:** It was a caravanserai/way-station on a major trade route. "The religious practices of Kuntillet Ajrud probably do not constitute practices peripheral to Judean culture."
- **The pottery was Judean; the script uses Hebrew *matres lectionis* (not Phoenician)**

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High that the inscriptions pair Yhwh with Asherah. Medium-high that this represents a divine couple rather than a deity + hypostasis.

## Stakes

This is the single most explosive question in the field — did Yahweh have a wife? Römer says the inscriptions answer yes. If correct, ancient Israelite religion included a divine couple, and the biblical prohibition on other gods was a reform program, not a description of historical reality.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mark S. Smith (see [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]]) reads the same inscriptions and concludes they refer to the cult symbol, not the goddess. Smith argues the pronominal suffix on a divine name is unusual in Hebrew and that "his asherah" (something belonging to Yahweh) makes better sense than "his Asherah" (requiring an implied "his consort, Asherah").

**Scholars who agree with Römer:** The majority — Dever, Day, Olyan, Zevit, Ackerman, Hadley, Keel/Uehlinger.

**Alternative reading:** Smith's position: the grammar favors "his asherah" (the symbol), and the possessive suffix on a personal name, while possible in Ugaritic, is unusual in Hebrew. McCarter's hypostasis theory offers a middle ground: the asherah was a personified divine attribute of Yahweh, not a separate goddess. See [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]] for the full counter-argument.

## Edges

**Depends on:**
- [[claim-asherah-el-consort-transferred-to-yahweh]] (romer-inv-9.1) — The consort reading of the inscriptions is much stronger if Asherah was already a goddess (El's consort) rather than merely a symbol
- [[claim-yhwh-represented-by-standing-stones-massebot]] (romer-inv-8.1) — The Arad temple's two steles (Yhwh + another deity, probably Asherah) provide material evidence for a divine couple in an official Judahite sanctuary, supporting the consort reading of the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions

**Supports:**
- [[claim-josiah-reform-removed-asherah]] (romer-inv-9.4) — If the inscriptions prove the divine couple, Josiah's reform was actively suppressing a genuine consort goddess

**Contradicts:**
- [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]] (smith-ehg-3.2) — Römer and Smith read the SAME inscription and reach OPPOSITE conclusions. These two claims cannot both be correct.

**Challenged by:**
- [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-cult-object-symbolized-goddess]] (day-ygc-2.3) — Day partially challenges Römer by arguing the suffix indicates a cult object (agreeing with Smith on grammar) but that the object SYMBOLIZED the goddess (agreeing with Römer on consort). This is a hybrid position that disagrees with both polar positions.

## Assessment

The inscriptions do pair Yhwh with Asherah — that's not in dispute. The debate is what "his Asherah" means. Römer reads it as the goddess. Smith reads it as the symbol. Day offers a hybrid: it's the symbol grammatically, but it represents the goddess functionally. After reading all three scholars, the cumulative case favors the goddess reading: the inscriptions, the Ugaritic background, the OT polemic, and the archaeological evidence (3,000+ figurines) converge. Smith's skepticism — while methodologically admirable — increasingly looks like it's fighting the weight of the evidence.
