# cross-cmhe-ii-iii.4: The Ritual Conquest was practiced by early Israel

**Source:** [[Cross — Parts II-III — Divine Warrior and League]] — Frank Moore Cross, *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), Part II, Chapter 5

## The Claim

Cross argues that the Israelite league (the pre-monarchic tribal confederation) reenacted the Divine Warrior's victory in ritual form — possibly at Gilgal or Shiloh. The battles narrated in Judges reflect both historical skirmishes AND ritual patterns. The "conquest" narrative in Joshua blends both — it is not pure history but "epic," where the ritual reenactment of Divine Warrior victory has been narrativized as military campaign.

## Evidence

- The Divine Warrior pattern in ANE religion typically included ritual reenactment: the god's victory over chaos was celebrated liturgically as a present reality. Israelite religion would have inherited this pattern.
- The "holy war" ideology in Judges and Joshua follows a stereotyped pattern: divine summons → muster of tribal levy → divine intervention (panic/rout) → herem (devotion to destruction). This pattern suggests ritual standardization, not ad hoc military reporting.
- Gilgal, Shiloh, and possibly Shechem functioned as league sanctuaries where tribal gatherings included covenant renewal and Divine Warrior liturgy.
- Psalm 24 (the "lift up your heads, O gates" liturgy) preserves the entrance ritual for the Divine Warrior — this was performed at the Jerusalem temple but likely originated in the league's sanctuary rituals.
- The Song of Deborah (Judges 5) blends mythic theophany (Yahweh marching from Seir) with historical battle (the defeat of Sisera) — exactly the blend of ritual pattern and historical event Cross's thesis predicts.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The Ritual Conquest theory is creative and consistent with Cross's broader framework, but speculative — no direct archaeological or textual evidence confirms ritual conquest reenactments.

## Stakes

If Cross is right, the Joshua conquest narrative is not bad history but a different GENRE altogether — it's liturgy narrativized. This means historical and theological readings of Joshua are both misfiring: historians err by treating it as failed military history, and theologians err by treating it as literal divine command. Cross's reading opens a third way: Joshua is epic, blending mythic pattern with historical memory through ritual reenactment.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Most archaeologists and historians. The "conquest model" (Albright) has been largely abandoned in favor of the "gradual settlement" or "peasant revolt" models. [[claim-bible-ideal-religion-never-existed-israelites]] — Dever's archaeology-based skepticism extends to any reconstruction of league ritual practice. [[claim-biblical-pure-yahwism-deuteronomistic-propaganda]] — the Deuteronomic history's conquest narrative is later theological retrojection, not ritual memory.

**Alternative reading:** The conquest narrative in Joshua is either (a) late Deuteronomistic fiction with no historical basis, (b) exaggerated folk memory of gradual settlement, or (c) deliberate theological propaganda for Josiah's expansionist program. Cross's Ritual Conquest theory is an unnecessary middle position.

## Assessment

The Ritual Conquest is the most speculative of Cross's claims. It's elegant — it explains the blend of mythic and historical elements in Judges and Joshua better than either pure-literary or pure-historical readings. But it lacks direct evidence. Cross himself rated it lower confidence than his other claims, and subsequent archaeology has not provided support. It remains a creative hypothesis that illuminates the texts without being provable.
