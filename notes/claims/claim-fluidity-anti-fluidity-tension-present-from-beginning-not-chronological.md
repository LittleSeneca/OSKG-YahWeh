# sommer-bog-5-6.1: The fluidity/anti-fluidity tension was present from the beginning

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapters 5-6 + Appendix]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapters 5-6

## The Claim

The tension between fluid and anti-fluid theologies was not a tidy chronological development (fluid early → anti-fluid late). It was present from early Israelite religion onward. Some very early texts show anti-fluid tendencies; some later texts preserve fluidity. The Bible is an anthology that preserves THE DEBATE, not a single resolution. The editorial process that produced the Pentateuch deliberately preserved BOTH J/E (fluid) and P/D (anti-fluid) — creating the canon's inherent theological tension as a feature, not a bug.

This complicates the standard Sommer narrative (Chapters 1-3) that presents fluidity as early and anti-fluidity as late. The fuller picture (Chapters 5-6) shows that the two theologies coexisted, overlapped, and competed throughout Israel's history.

## Evidence

- **Psalms:** Some Psalms combine fluid and anti-fluid language in the same poem — the same text moves between embodied and transcendent divine language without resolution
- **Pentateuchal editing:** The redactors who combined J/E and P/D sources into the Torah deliberately preserved both theologies rather than harmonizing them
- **Early anti-fluid texts:** Some very early texts already show resistance to divine embodiment — anti-fluid impulses are not a late invention
- **Late fluid texts:** Some post-exilic texts preserve fluid language — fluidity was not simply superseded
- **The canon as anthology:** The Hebrew Bible preserves contradictory theological traditions side by side because its editors valued preservation over harmonization

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Multiple biblical texts show both fluid and anti-fluid language coexisting; the evidence of deliberate canonical preservation of contradictory traditions is strong.

## Stakes

If the tension was present from the beginning, then neither fluidity nor anti-fluidity can claim historical or theological priority. The "evolutionary" model (fluid → anti-fluid) is too simple. The Bible preserves a live debate, not a resolved development. This means later theological choices (rabbinic anti-fluidity, kabbalistic fluidity, Christian incarnation) are all legitimate developments of possibilities already present in the canon — none is more "biblical" than the others.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the canon as a chronological development (J/E early → D → P late) might see anti-fluidity as a late innovation that superseded earlier fluidity. Sommer's own Chapters 1-3 could be read this way.

**Alternative reading:** The chronological-developmental reading treats anti-fluid texts as corrections of earlier fluid texts. Sommer argues this imposes a modern preference for linear development onto an anthology that preserves multiple voices simultaneously.

## Assessment

This is Sommer's self-correction. Chapters 1-3 present a clean arc: ANE fluidity → J/E preserve → P/D reject. Chapters 5-6 complicate that arc: the tension was always there, the canon preserves the debate, and the "development" is more like an ongoing argument than a resolved progression. This makes Sommer's model more historically plausible — real theological change is messy, contested, and non-linear — but also harder to summarize. The clean arc of Chapters 1-3 is pedagogically useful; the messy reality of Chapters 5-6 is historically accurate.
