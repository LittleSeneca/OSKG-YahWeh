# smith-obm-intro.2: "Canaanite" is a modern scholarly construct — "West Semitic" is preferable

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Introduction

## The Claim

"'Canaanite' is a misleading term that often clouds analysis." Smith largely avoids it, preferring "West Semitic." Yet he defends Ugaritic relevance: "These specific points of contact between Ugaritic and Israelite religion need not be understood as pointing to a single or 'same' religion, but they do point to a larger religious tradition shared broadly by West Semitic peoples."

## Evidence

- Rainey (1960s): Ugaritic is not Canaanite — CAT 4.967 lists a Canaanite alongside an Egyptian and an Ashdodite; an Akkadian text from Ugarit distinguishes "sons of Ugarit" from "sons of Canaan."
- Lemche goes further: "Canaanite" never appears as self-designation; it's always an external label. But Smith pushes back: "It seems odd to suppose that foreign courts from Egypt to Hatti to Mesopotamia used the term 'Canaanite,' but the people whom others designated with this term never knew themselves by it."
- Zadok: Ugaritic may be the "westernmost dialect of 'Amorite' type" — not straightforwardly Canaanite.
- The "Canaanite shift" (ā > ō) may be a SOUTH Canaanite feature, not present in Ugaritic or even north Canaanite, per Albright.
- Yet specific names and terms (Mount Sapan in Psalm 48:3, Leviathan, Tannin) are common to Ugaritic and biblical texts.
- Keel and Uehlinger correctly note Ugaritic texts "are not primary sources for the religious history of Canaan and Israel," but Smith argues they remain essential as background.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that "Canaanite" is problematic. MEDIUM-HIGH that "West Semitic" is better. The specific textual parallels are too precise to dismiss.

## Stakes

If Ugaritic texts are irrelevant to Israelite religion (as Hillers, Keel/Uehlinger, Lemche argue to varying degrees), a major comparative corpus is lost and Smith's entire project is undermined. If they ARE relevant, Smith has the best available data for reconstructing Israel's polytheistic background.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Lemche (minimalist: "Canaanite" is a Persian-period construct), Hillers (Ugaritic ≠ Canaanite, therefore irrelevant to Israel), Keel and Uehlinger (Ugaritic texts are not primary sources for Canaan/Israel).

**Alternative reading:** Even if "Canaanite" is problematic as a category, Ugaritic texts might map to a NORTHERN coastal culture with limited relevance to highland Israel. Smith's counter is the specific textual parallels — but those could reflect shared ANE stock rather than direct cultural continuity.
