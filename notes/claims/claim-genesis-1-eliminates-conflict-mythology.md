# smith-obm-9.1: Genesis 1 creates a "monotheistic poetics" — eliminates conflict mythology and depersonalizes cosmic enemies

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch9 — Formation of Monotheistic Theologies]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"Genesis 1 creates a 'monotheistic poetics'... the lack of any conflict, or even any personification of the cosmic oceans or waters, is designed to heighten the picture of a powerful God who but speaks and the divine will is accomplished." The old divine enemies are "creatures like other creatures rendered in this story," enclosed within the created order and transformed by omission.

## Evidence

- The cosmic waters (*tehom*) in Genesis 1:2 are not personified, not addressed, not fought — they simply exist as raw material for divine ordering. Contrast with the Baal Cycle where Yamm (Sea) is a personal divine antagonist defeated in combat.
- The *tanninim* ("sea monsters") in Genesis 1:21 are mentioned only in passing as creatures God made — "not only is the conflict role eliminated in Genesis 1; even the old role of cosmic forces as domesticated has been muted by downplaying them, even depersonalizing them."
- Sun and moon are called only "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (Gen 1:16). These titles are not necessarily polemical — "great light" (*nrt rbt*) appears as a title for the Ugaritic sun-goddess Shapshu (CAT 1.161.19) — but Genesis 1 strips them of divine status: "like the sea creatures, they are located within the created order."
- The key move: "Here ambiguity between Creator and creatures is resolved; there is no middle ground left in Genesis 1's 'monotheistic poetics.' Such a depiction drains power from any old forces of opposition and leaves God as the only power in the universe."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The contrast between Genesis 1 and the combat myths (Baal Cycle, Enuma Elish, Psalm 74, Isaiah 51) is indisputable. Smith's reading of the depersonalization strategy is well-supported.

## Stakes

If Genesis 1 deliberately mutes conflict mythology rather than being ignorant of it, the Priestly writer knew the old traditions and chose to reconfigure them. This makes Genesis 1 a theologically motivated act of literary transformation, not a naive or primitive account.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read Genesis 1 as preserving vestiges of conflict mythology (the *tehom* as Tiamat, the *tanninim* as Rahab/Leviathan). Those who see Genesis 1 as independent of, rather than responding to, West Semitic combat traditions.

**Alternative reading:** The demythologization could be less polemical than Smith claims. The Priestly writer may simply have operated in a different genre (liturgical/calendrical) where cosmic combat was irrelevant, rather than actively suppressing it.

## Assessment

Smith is right that Genesis 1 is doing something deliberate here. But "monotheistic poetics" is vague — it describes the effect (only God has power) without specifying the mechanism. The real insight is the *enclosure* strategy: the old enemies aren't defeated or banished but absorbed into the created order. That's genuinely innovative compared to the combat model.
