# day-ygc-4.2: The OT dragon/sea conflict derives from Baal-Yam, not Marduk-Tiamat

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 4 — Yahwehs Appropriation of Baal Imagery]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"Since the discovery of the Ugaritic texts from 1929 onwards, it has become generally accepted that the Old Testament's references to a divine conflict with a dragon and the sea are an echo of Canaanite rather than Babylonian mythology" (pp. 99-100). Gunkel's late 19th-century Babylonian theory is superseded.

## Evidence

1. **Name correspondence:** OT Leviathan (*liwyātān*) = Ugaritic Litan (*ltn*), the seven-headed dragon defeated by Baal and Anat (KTU² 1.5.I.1-3; 1.3.III.40-42). OT describes Leviathan as "twisting serpent" (*nāḥāš bārîaḥ*) — almost identical to Ugaritic (p. 100)
2. **Also called Rahab in OT** (Ps 89:11; Job 26:12-13) — not attested extra-biblically but presumably alternative name
3. **Multiple heads:** Ps 74:14 refers to Leviathan's multiple heads — at Ugarit he had seven heads
4. **Creation context:** OT associates dragon conflict with creation (Ps 74:12-17; 89:10-15; Job 26:12-13). Ugaritic Baal-Yam conflict is not explicitly a creation account, but the Mari text (Zimri-Lim era) has Adad declaring "I gave you the weapons with which I fought against Sea (*tâmtum*)" — possibly connecting these traditions (p. 100)
5. **Tehom ≠ Tiamat directly:** Gen 1:2's *tehôm* "deep" lacks the feminine ending that Tiamat has. The word *thm* occurs in Ugaritic, so "it is more natural to assume a Canaanite prototype" (p. 102)
6. **Behemoth = Ugaritic Arsh:** The name means "great ox" — Ugaritic texts twice mention a mythical ox-like creature alongside Leviathan called Arsh or "El's calf Atik" (KTU² 1.3.III.40-44; 1.6.VI.51-53). Behemoth dwells in a river (Job 40:23), and in the second Ugaritic allusion Arsh is "in the sea" (pp. 103-104)
7. **NOT natural animals:** Behemoth and Leviathan in Job 40-41 are not hippopotamus/crocodile — Leviathan breathes fire (Job 41:10-13), and both are presented as unconquerable by humans (p. 103)

**Three modes of OT reuse:**
- **Creation** (Ps 74, 89, 104; Job 26, 38)
- **Historicization** (Rahab = Egypt in Isa 30:7; Pharaoh = dragon in Ezek 29:3-5; Assyrians = chaos waters in Isa 17:12-14)
- **Eschatologization** (Isa 27:1; Daniel 7; Revelation 12-13)

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is the scholarly consensus since Ugarit. The name correspondences (Leviathan/Litan) are precise. The shift from Gunkel's Babylonian theory to the Canaanite theory is one of the most important discoveries of 20th-century biblical scholarship.

## Stakes

The Chaoskampf is one of the most pervasive mythological motifs in the OT. If it's Baal's victory over Yam, not Marduk's over Tiamat, then Israel's theological imagination was shaped by Canaan, not Mesopotamia. Genesis 1 is a demythologization of the same tradition — the chaotic sea becomes the tame *tehôm* of creation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain Babylonian influence (Gunkel's original position, now minority). Those who argue the Mari *tâmtum* text complicates the neat Babylonia-vs-Canaan dichotomy.

**Alternative reading:** The Chaoskampf is a common ancient Near Eastern motif not specifically traceable to either Canaanite or Babylonian origin; both are local expressions of a shared cultural pattern.

## Assessment

Day's expertise on this topic is peerless — his 1985 monograph is the standard reference. The Leviathan/Litan name correspondence alone is close to decisive. The Behemoth/Arsh connection is less secure but still plausible. The "demythologization" thesis (Genesis 1 tames the battle into a "job of work") is elegant and widely accepted. The three modes of reuse (creation, historicization, eschatologization) provide a sophisticated model for how mythological material was transformed, not just borrowed.
