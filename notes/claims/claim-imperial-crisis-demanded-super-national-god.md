# smith-obm-8.7: Imperial crisis made national theology obsolete and demanded a super-national god

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch8 — Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 8

## The Claim

"The rise of the Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian empires issued in a serious religious reflection on Yahweh's power over the nations. The loss of identity as a nation changed Israel's understanding of the national god. Looming empires made the model of a national god obsolete. Moreover, the rise of supra-national empires suggested the model of the super-national god." "As Judah's situation on the mundane level deteriorated in history, the cosmic status of its deity soared in its literature."

## Evidence

- Tigay: "The need to emphasize the monotheistic idea in this period was probably due to the increased exposure of Israel to the triumphant Assyrian and Babylonian empires, which attributed their victories, including victories over Israel, to their gods."
- The traditional theology (Deut 32:8-9) affirmed that all nations had their own patron-gods, with Yahweh as Israel's — but this model PRESUMED rough equality among nations.
- "First in the face of the great empires and then in exile, Israel stands at the bottom of its political power, and it exalts its deity inversely as ruler of the whole universe."
- The reduction of Judean kingship (especially after Josiah) changed the "parallel or mirroring worldview" of the royal psalms.
- Mesopotamian precedents: "the figures of Assur and Marduk assumed such proportions, the super-gods whose patronage of empires matches their manifestation as the sum-total of all the other deities." Mesopotamian authors were "exploring the nature of all divinity in relation to a single major god" (as discussed in Ch5).
- "The timing of the emergence of Israelite monotheism in the late Iron Age and exilic period fits Karl Jaspers's the 'Axial Age'...'the separation between transcendent and mundane spheres of reality.'"

**Against the Persian/Zoroastrian model:**
- Some scholars locate monotheism in the Persian period, crediting Zoroastrian dualism as a model.
- Smith's reservations: (1) Zoroastrian dualism (Ahura Mazda vs. Angra Mainyu) "does not truly resemble biblical monotheism" — a principle of evil only appears in latest biblical works. (2) Biblical monotheistic language "appears to represent...developments of older language exalting the national god." (3) Proponents of the Persian model "rarely, if ever, address these issues."
- Concession: Persian tradition may have "reinforced monotheistic rhetoric" or influenced some presentations (Oppenheim: the Persian king's "eyes and ears" as model for "the satan" in Job 1-2). "Yet it was not Judean monotheism's main progenitor."

**The two-corollary synthesis:**
- Factor 1 (social): Weakening family lineages → divine family metaphor erodes → individual deity becomes intelligible
- Factor 2 (historical): Rise of empires → national god model obsolete → super-national god emerges
- Together: "These two features constitute the larger landscape of monotheistic discourse and help to explain its intelligibility in this time period."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the imperial crisis argument; MEDIUM on the Axial Age framing; HIGH on the rejection of Persian primacy. The inverse correlation is a genuinely elegant insight.

## Stakes

If monotheism is a response to imperial crisis (Assyria/Babylon), then it's POLITICAL theology — a way of asserting Yahweh's sovereignty when Israel had no political sovereignty. The Axial Age framing situates Israelite monotheism within a broader world-historical pattern without making it derivative.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Persian-period advocates (who date the key texts later). Scholars who see monotheism as a purely internal Israelite development. Those who credit Zoroastrian influence more heavily.

**Alternative reading:** The Mesopotamian precedent (Assur, Marduk as sum-total of all gods) might be the REAL model — Israelite monotheism as imitation of imperial theology rather than reaction against it. Smith notes this but doesn't develop it fully here (it's in Ch5).

## Assessment

This is the strongest of Smith's explanatory sections. The inverse correlation (political power ↓ → cosmic status ↑) is a genuinely elegant insight, and the Mesopotamian parallels (Marduk in Enuma Elish, Assur) show that Israel wasn't unique — imperial theology tended toward monotheism-like formulations across the ANE. The rejection of Persian primacy is well-argued: the Zoroastrian model doesn't fit the actual FORM of Israelite monotheistic statements, which are clearly developments of older Yahwistic rhetoric. The "reinforced but not progenitor" position is sensible.
