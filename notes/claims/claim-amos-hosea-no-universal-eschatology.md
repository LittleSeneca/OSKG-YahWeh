# kaufmann-ri-11.9: Neither Amos nor Second Hosea had universal eschatology — it was born only after them

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XI — Amos and Hosea]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XI

## The Claim

Amos knows nothing of "the new eschatology, and the new concept of universal history." The consolation prophecy in 9:8b-15, if authentic, is "typically antique" — the remnant consists of righteous survivors (like Elijah's remnant in 1 Kings 19:18), not those chastened into repentance. "Of this, the prophecies of Amos know nothing. There is no historical connection between the judgment of Israel and that of the nations." Similarly, Second Hosea "has not a word for the nations. YHWH is, of course, the ruler and judge of all men, but the realm of his grace is Israel. Even his eschatological visions, such as we have of them, do not embrace the nations. Universal prophetic eschatology was born only after him."

## Evidence

- Amos 9:12: restored Israel repossesses nations formerly part of the Davidic empire — nothing about nations turning from idolatry
- No historical connection between judgment of Israel and judgment of nations in Amos
- The conqueror of Israel has no role in Amos's vision of salvation
- Hosea's messianic vision: God will make the people "dwell in tents" (12:10; cf. 11:11 "their houses") — restored to simplicity in the land, not a transformed world order
- Hosea 11:8ff: divine soliloquy about grace for Ephraim — entirely national
- "Universal prophetic eschatology was born only after him"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** This is an accurate reading of the texts. Whatever universalist impulses exist in Amos (9:7) and Hosea, neither envisions the nations participating in eschatological salvation. That development belongs to Isaiah, Jeremiah, and especially Second Isaiah.

## Stakes

The developmental trajectory of Israelite religion. If Amos and Hosea are pre-universalist, they represent an earlier stage of theological development. This is actually consistent with the critical model (earlier = more particularistic, later = more universalistic) — which creates an interesting tension with Kaufmann's larger thesis that monotheism was fully present from the beginning.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No serious disagreement. Even scholars who see universalist seeds in Amos 9:7 acknowledge that full universal eschatology emerges later.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of explicit universal eschatology doesn't mean Amos and Hosea REJECTED it — they may simply not have addressed the question. Their national focus was appropriate to their audience and mission.

## Assessment

This claim is correct and largely uncontroversial. The interesting question is WHY Kaufmann emphasizes it: he wants to show that classical prophecy developed over time (contra the flattening tendency of some critical scholarship) while simultaneously arguing that monotheism itself was static (contra the developmental model). There's a tension here — if eschatology develops, why can't monotheism develop too?
