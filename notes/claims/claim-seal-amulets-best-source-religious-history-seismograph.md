# keel-ggi-1.3: Seal amulets are the single best source for reconstructing religious history — they function as a "seismograph" for religious change

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch I — Starting Point and Points of Departure]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter I

## The Claim

"Not only because of their sheer number but also because of their importance, seal amulets far outshine all other kinds of image-bearing artifacts in value, being even more valuable than inscriptional evidence. Since they are preserved in relatively comparable quantities for all periods, they can virtually serve as the standard by which religious history is documented, particularly because they are more or less public artifacts and can thus serve as a sensitive seismograph to detect subtle shifts in religious history."

## Evidence

- The Fribourg corpus: approximately 8,500 stamp seals from legal excavations in Palestine/Israel
- Seals survive in comparable quantities across all periods (unlike temples, bronze statues, or texts)
- Seals are "more or less public artifacts" — worn, traded, used by broad populations — not just elite productions
- Different media tell different stories: cylinder seals allow complex "constellations," stamp seals simplify but can still combine motifs, isolated statues lose their iconographic context

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The quantitative argument is strong. The corpus size (8,500) dwarfs every other artifact category for religious imagery.

## Stakes

If seals are as representative as they claim, then shifts in seal iconography ARE shifts in popular religious mentality — not just elite theological fashion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the numbers, but one could question representativeness. Seals were owned by the literate/elite class, not the entire population. Dever's figurines (3,000+ Judean Pillar Figurines) might be MORE representative of popular religion than seals.

**Alternative reading:** Seals reflect the iconographic choices of seal-cutters and their elite patrons — they may lag behind or diverge from popular belief. The "public" character they claim could be overstated.
