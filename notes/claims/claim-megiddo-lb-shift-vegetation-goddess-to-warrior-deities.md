# keel-ggi-p2.3: Megiddo witnessed a decisive LB shift from vegetation goddess to warrior deities

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

"There was a decisive change in what was depicted on metal figures during the transition from the Middle Bronze Age to the Late Bronze Age. Warrior gods, such as Baal and Reshef, virtually replace the naked goddesses." On the human level: "Military themes replace those of loyalty and erotic contact between the sexes." And "the main concern of the prince is not his encounter with the goddess but rather a triumph over his enemies."

## Evidence

- **Goddess pendants replaced:** Silver/bronze leaf pendants depicting slender goddess replaced by gold pendants in inverted-pear shape with puncture holes marking head, breasts, navel, genitalia — overall shape imitating female genitalia with pubic hair along edge. Connect to "Branch Goddess" iconography. "None of these gold pendants survived beyond the Late Bronze Age to be found in an undisputed Iron Age context."
- **Cheap terra-cotta replaces expensive metal for goddesses:** "The Late Bronze Age witnessed the introduction of cheap, molded clay figures for the first time and these are found in great quantities." Dominant type: goddess holding/presenting breasts — same as MB IIB, but now in clay instead of metal.
- **Enthroned youthful god:** Bronze figure (25+ cm high) covered in gold leaf from LB temple, holding a blossom — connected to weather god. Taanach letters confirm Baal-Hadad as "the dominant deity in the area surrounding the broad central plain of Jezreel during the Late Bronze Age."
- **Reshef figurine:** Bronze figure (13 cm high) from Grave 4 holding handheld weapon and shield — "typical of portrayals of Reshef."
- **Warrior imagery on painted sherds:** "Warriors with handheld weapons and small shields" from end of LB IIB, nearly identical to Reshef bronze except human warriors wear beards.
- **Chariot motif:** Introduced from Egypt — pharaoh in chariot triumphing over enemies becomes "an important motif within the religious system of Egypt" and appears on Palestinian seal amulets.
- **Victory celebration ivories:** Famous Megiddo ivory shows prince returning victorious, enthroned on cherub throne, winged solar disk above. Women now shown "either as servants who stand before the enthroned prince or are absent altogether."
- **Feast scene:** Another ivory shows only male celebrants. "Whereas women appear in depictions from the Middle Bronze Age both as sexual partners for men and with equal status, here they are shown either as servants... or are absent altogether."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The evidence is abundant and consistent across multiple media (metal figurines, ivories, painted pottery, seal amulets). The shift from MB goddess dominance to LB warrior dominance at Megiddo is one of the most clearly documented transitions in the book.

## Stakes

This shift — goddesses out, warrior gods in, women relegated to servant roles — is directly attributed to Egyptian colonialism and its military ideology. If this is correct, then the religion of pre-monarchic Israel (Iron Age I) inherited a landscape where male warrior gods dominated public religion and goddess worship had been pushed into cheap domestic terra-cottas. This makes sense of why the Hebrew Bible's polemics target goddess worship (Asherah, the Queen of Heaven) while Yahwism presents Yahweh as a warrior — both phenomena have LB roots.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Kempinski (1989a) argued for a triad of El, Baal, and a fertility goddess at Megiddo's temple. Keel & Uehlinger reject this: "hardly any of the precious metal figures of the goddess were still being manufactured in the Late Bronze Age."

**Alternative reading:** The shift could reflect the bias of surviving evidence — metal figurines and ivories were elite products, and the elite may have been more Egyptianized than the general population. Cheap terra-cotta goddesses found in large quantities suggest the goddess remained popular among common people.

## Assessment

This is compellingly argued. The convergence of metal figurines, painted pottery, seal amulets, and carved ivories all telling the same story is hard to dismiss as preservation bias. The most significant detail is the gold pendants: their disappearance from undisputed Iron Age contexts suggests a genuine rupture, not just elite fashion. Yahweh didn't invent the warrior-god archetype — he stepped into a role that Baal and Reshef were already playing.
