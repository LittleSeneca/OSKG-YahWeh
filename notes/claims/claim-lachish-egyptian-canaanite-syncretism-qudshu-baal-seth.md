# keel-ggi-p2.4: Lachish shows the most intense Egyptian-Canaanite syncretism — qudshu goddess, Baal-Seth, Hathor

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part II — Late Bronze Age]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part II

## The Claim

"Egyptian influence clearly predominates at Lachish and at other southern Palestinian sites." The qudshu-type goddess is "an Egyptian-style version of the Middle Bronze Age 'Branch Goddess'," and Baal-Seth represents a fusion where "the god who guaranteed the earth's fertility, when combined with Seth, has become a champion against all evil."

## Evidence

- **Qudshu-type goddess:** Naked female figurines with shoulder-length Hathor-style locks, holding papyrus or lotus stems in bent arms. "Iconographically, this is an Egyptian-style version of the Middle Bronze Age 'Branch Goddess'." Over 70 examples known — primarily from sites south of Carmel. The term "qudshu" comes from Egyptian New Kingdom stelae where inscription *qds* ("holiness") appears beside a goddess standing on a lion, flanked by Reshef and Min. "*Qds* is probably not the name of the goddess but an epithet indicating the dominant aspect of her sexual and erotic powers."
- **Goddess on a horse — warrior adaptation:** Gold-leaf piece from Lachish temple showing goddess standing on a *horse* rather than a lion, wearing atef-crown. "The goddess, whose role had been connected primarily with vegetation... is now drawn into the arena of war."
- **Hathor cult:** Six Hathor head pendants and Hathor head amulet with cow's horns and solar disk from Fosse Temple (13th c.); gold Hathor sistrum amulet from Tell el-Ajjul; Timnah shrine with Hathor capitals, Ramesses III relief showing pharaoh sacrificing to Hathor, and faience fragments with her title *nb.t mfk3.t* ("Mistress of the Turquoise").
- **Inscribed pitcher:** A pitcher from Fosse Temple bears inscription *mtn.sy [l'][rb]ty* — "Mattan [or: gift]: a present for my lady Elat [or: the goddess]." Inscription placed over caprid-flanked tree. "The venerated goddess is possibly to be identified as Asherah; in the mythological texts from Ugarit *rbt* 'lady' and *ilt* 'goddess' are epithets of Atirat/Asherah."
- **Revadim/Aphek goddess:** Molded naked goddess figure with palmetto tree flanked by caprids on each thigh near exposed genitalia, nursing children at each breast, wearing Q-shaped womb-symbol pendant. "Within one individual deity, this goddess combines the secret powers of mother earth."
- **Baal-Seth as serpent-conqueror:** Incised depiction from Egyptian-Canaanite temple on tell shows god holding great lance overhead — "a figure that combines the Canaanite Baal (who defeats the sea serpent *litanu*/Leviathan) and the Egyptian Seth (who conquers the Apophis serpent)." Scarab from Grave 120 shows Baal-Seth with bull's horns striking horned snake. "The god who could defeat such a creature is treated as a savior, pure and simple."
- **Reshef:** Cylinder seal from Fosse Temple shows god with shield and hand weapon. Multiple scarabs confirm Reshef's presence.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** High for the syncretism pattern — evidence is dense and converges across media. Medium-high for the specific identification of the Lachish pitcher's goddess as Asherah.

## Stakes

The Lachish evidence shows that southern Canaanite religion in the LB was a genuine fusion, not purely Egyptian or purely Canaanite. The qudshu goddess is a perfect example: her nakedness, frontal pose, and vegetation associations are Canaanite; her Hathor locks, lotus stems, and lion-mount are Egyptian. This syncretism is the religious context in which early Israel emerged in the southern hill country. The Baal-Seth "savior" figure is especially relevant: it shows a LB precedent for a god who conquers cosmic chaos, which Yahweh would later absorb. The inscribed pitcher calling the tree-goddess "my lady Elat" is one of the closest material links between LB iconography and the Asherah polemics of the Hebrew Bible.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Cross (1973), Maier (1986), and Hadley (1989) identify qudshu-type goddess with Atirat/Asherah — Keel & Uehlinger are more cautious. Day (1986) and Smith (1990) differ on Asherah identification. Ussishkin (1978) published Lachish temple incised god but interpretations vary.

**Alternative reading:** The syncretism could be interpreted as Egyptian religious imperialism rather than genuine fusion — Egyptians imposing their iconographic norms on indigenous deities.

## Assessment

The Lachish chapter provides the most direct pre-Israelite context for what would become Judah. The inscribed pitcher calling the tree-goddess "my lady Elat" is one of the closest material links between LB iconography and the Asherah polemics of the Hebrew Bible. The Baal-Seth fusion as "savior" from cosmic danger anticipates Yahweh's Chaoskampf mythology and supports the view that Yahweh absorbed Baal's functions, not just El's.
