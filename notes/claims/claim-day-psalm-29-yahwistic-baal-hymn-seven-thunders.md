# day-ygc-4.1: Psalm 29 is a Yahwistic adaptation of Canaanite Baal mythology

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 4 — Yahwehs Appropriation of Baal Imagery]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"There can thus be no doubt that Psalm 29 stands remarkably close to the circle of mythological ideas surrounding Baal as they are attested in the Ugaritic texts" (p. 98). Some scholars claim it's a Canaanite psalm taken over wholesale with Yahweh's name substituted for Baal. Day is more cautious: it's "an Israelite composition largely modelled on the language used by the Canaanites about Baal" (p. 98).

## Evidence

1. **Yahweh's voice = thunder = Baal's voice:** In Ps 29, Yahweh's "voice" (*qôl*) is the thunder — exactly as Baal's voice is thunder in Ugaritic texts (KTU² 1.4.VII.29-31) (p. 97)
2. **Kingship over cosmic waters:** Yahweh enthroned "over the flood" (v. 10) parallels Baal's enthronement "like the flood" in KTU² 1.101.1-3a (pp. 97-98)
3. **Seven thunders:** Day's original contribution — the sevenfold *qôl yahweh* (vv. 3a, 4a, 4b, 5, 7, 8, 9) corresponds to Baal's "seven lightnings... eight storehouses of thunder" in KTU² 1.101.3b-4. The Ugaritic 7/8 sequence is "automatic parallelism" — it means seven (pp. 97-98)
4. **Divine assembly:** "Sons of gods" (*benê ʼēlîm*) in v. 1 are the divine council — derived from sons of El at Ugarit (p. 97)
5. **Geographic clues:** The cedars of Lebanon and Sirion (vv. 5-6) are northwest Semitic, not Israelite heartland — consistent with a psalm originally from the Baal-worshipping region (p. 98)
6. **Against wholesale Canaanite origin:** v. 8's reference to "the wilderness of Kadesh" and Yahweh's Sinai theophany "would appear to militate against the view that Psalm 29 is nothing more than a Canaanite psalm with the simple substitution of Yahweh for Baal" (pp. 98-99)

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is one of the most widely accepted cases of direct Canaanite borrowing in the entire OT. The seven thunders parallel that Day himself discovered adds additional weight.

## Stakes

Psalm 29 is in the canonical Psalter. If it's essentially a Baal hymn with Yahweh's name inserted, the line between Yahwism and Baalism was thinner than the OT's own polemic suggests. Israel's worship language was Baal's worship language.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** S. Mittmann (original psalm had only fivefold *qôl yahweh*) and O. Loretz (six thunders, v. 7 secondary). Day argues the seven-thunder parallel to Ugaritic proves the sevenfold structure is original. Loretz also separates the psalm into three originally independent compositions — Day finds this "overly speculative" (p. 99).

**Alternative reading:** The psalm is an original Israelite composition that independently uses storm theophany imagery common across the ancient Near East, not specifically derived from Baal.

## Assessment

The seven thunders discovery is genuinely important — it means the psalm's structure IS the theology. You can't remove a *qôl yahweh* without breaking the Ugaritic pattern. This is Day's field (he wrote his 1985 monograph *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea* on this material), and his expertise shows. The cautious "modelled on" rather than "taken over wholesale" position is well-judged.
