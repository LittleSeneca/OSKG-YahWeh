# albertz-hir-2.1.5: Ancestor cult (teraphim, necromancy) was present in early family religion but progressively suppressed by official Yahweh religion

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §2.1 — Patriarchal Religion as Family Piety]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §2.1

## The Claim

Teraphim were likely figurines of deified ancestors, part of the basic equipment of early Israelite household cults. Ancestor veneration was a genuine element of early family piety, but it was already being "reduced to remnants" by the personal relationship to the God of the fathers, and later came under increasingly severe attack from official Yahweh religion. The largely negative evidence for ancestor cult in the OT is "at least in part the result of later dogmatic correction."

## Evidence

- Rachel's teraphim (Gen 31:19, 34f.) — small figurines called "my/your gods" ('ĕlōhaykā), compared to the ilāni (household gods) in Nuzi family documents
- Nuzi parallels: ilāni were part of ancestral heritage, and disinherited persons were denied access to household gods — suggesting the teraphim functioned to "secure the continuity of the family and solidarity between generations"
- In Nuzi, eṭemmu (spirits of the dead) are mentioned in partial parallel to ilāni — suggesting household gods = deified ancestors
- Teraphim appear alongside 'ōbôt and yiddĕ'ōnîm in II Kings 23:24 (Deuteronomistic polemic) — the latter are clearly spirits of the dead
- Necromancy (consulting the dead) was "probably widespread" down to the early post-exilic period (I Sam 28)
- Yet the patriarchal narratives show emotional solidarity with ancestors (being "gathered to his fathers," significance of ancestral tombs) without regular cult of the dead (no kispu ritual like Mesopotamia)

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The teraphim-as-ancestor-figurines theory is plausible but contested. The Nuzi parallels are suggestive but from a different culture and earlier period. The "dogmatic correction" argument is a classic appeal to silence.

## Stakes

If Albertz is right, Yahweh religion was not just adding to family piety but actively *suppressing* elements of it — ancestor cult, divination, image-use. The history of Israelite religion includes a repressive dimension: official religion fought to eliminate forms of family piety it considered incompatible.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Loretz argued for a MUCH stronger ancestor cult (full Canaanite-style cult of the dead), which Albertz rejects as overreaching. Other scholars (Bloch-Smith, Lewis) have documented Judahite burial practices suggesting more robust ancestor veneration than Albertz allows. The minimalist position would be that teraphim were simply household idols without specific ancestor reference.

**Alternative reading:** The evidence for ancestor cult might be *stronger* than Albertz says. Bloch-Smith's *Judahite Burial Practices and Beliefs about the Dead* (1992) — cited in Albertz's bibliography — documents extensive provisions for the dead in Judahite tombs (food, vessels, lamps) suggesting active ongoing care for the dead, not mere emotional solidarity. Albertz might be underplaying the evidence that contradicts his "already reduced to remnants" claim.

## Assessment

This section feels like Albertz hedging. He wants to acknowledge the ancestor cult evidence (because it's there) but minimize its significance (because it complicates his picture of family piety as primarily oriented toward the personal father-god). The most honest move would be to say: we don't have enough evidence to know how extensive ancestor cult was in early Israelite families, and the biblical texts have been shaped by later polemic against it. The Nuzi parallels are interesting but don't prove the teraphim were ancestors specifically.
