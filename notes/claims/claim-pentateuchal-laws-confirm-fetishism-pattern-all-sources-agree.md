# kaufmann-ri-1.8: The Pentateuchal laws confirm the fetishism pattern — all sources agree

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"The Pentateuch also represents pagan religion as mere fetishism, and again there is no difference between sources; all agree in their view of idolatry."

## Evidence

- **No law against belief or myth-telling:** "There is no law in the Pentateuch interdicting the belief in pagan gods or the telling of their myths." The only name-related prohibition is against using their names in oaths (Exod 23:13). The standing prohibition concerns "making" and worshipping other gods.
- **Images of YHWH treated as "other gods":** "Images of the YHWH cult are assumed to be the product of foreign influence, and a cult involving them is regarded as no worship of YHWH at all, but of 'other gods'."
- **Deuteronomy especially clear:** The gods of the nations are "the handiwork of man," "wood and stone." The folly of idolatry is "that its gods 'see not, and hear not, and eat not, and smell not'."
- **All sources agree:** "There is no difference between sources" — whether J, E, P, or D. This uniformity is itself evidence, for Kaufmann, that the fetishism view is not a late development but an original feature of Israelite religion.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the descriptive claim (the laws really do focus on manufacture and worship of objects); LOW on the inference that agreement across sources proves antiquity. Uniformity can reflect the work of redactors who harmonized the sources' views.

## Stakes

For Kaufmann, the agreement of all Pentateuchal sources on the fetishism view is evidence that this view is ancient and original — not a late editorial imposition. If the fetishism view were a late development, we'd expect to find traces of an earlier, more mythologically-engaged view in some sources. The uniformity suggests the fetishism view is foundational.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Source critics note that the Pentateuchal sources have all been edited by later hands — the "agreement" Kaufmann observes might reflect the work of redactors. The Documentary Hypothesis doesn't claim the sources had *different* theologies of idolatry; it claims they were composed at different times. Moreover, the fact that laws prohibit "making" rather than "believing in" other gods might reflect the practical nature of ancient Near Eastern law codes.

**Alternative reading:** The Pentateuchal focus on "making" rather than "believing" reflects the nature of ancient law as behavioral regulation, not theological treatise. Ancient Near Eastern law codes don't typically legislate belief — they legislate practice. The absence of a law against "believing in" pagan gods doesn't tell us Israelites didn't believe in them; it tells us the law regulated behavior, not thought.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's observation about the Pentateuchal laws is descriptively accurate — they really do focus on the manufacture and worship of cult objects. But his inference that this reflects original Israelite theology rather than the practical nature of ancient law is questionable. "You shall not make for yourself a graven image" is a behavioral prohibition, not a theological statement about the nature of pagan gods. The legal focus on practice over belief is what we'd expect from any ancient law code, regardless of the underlying theology.
