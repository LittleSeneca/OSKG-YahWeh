# dever-dghw-intro.1: Dever's personal journey gives him a unique vantage point — he knows what's at stake

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife? Archaeology and Folk Religion in Ancient Israel* (2005), Introduction

## The Claim

Dever's biography matters for understanding his book. He was raised fundamentalist ("My father was a fire-breathing fundamentalist preacher, sometime tent evangelist"), became a liberal Protestant minister, studied at Harvard under G. Ernest Wright, lost his faith through archaeology, converted to Reform Judaism, and now describes himself as "not a theist... essentially a secular humanist." He knows the Bible from the inside and knows what it costs to lose faith in it. Dever is explicit about his biases in a way that many scholars are not — he tells you where he stands and lets you evaluate his evidence accordingly.

## Evidence

- "I was reared in a deeply religious family in small towns in the South and Midwest. My father was a fire-breathing fundamentalist preacher, sometime tent evangelist, for a while a missionary in Jamaica, from whom I inherited a lifelong love of the Bible."
- "Finally I went to Harvard to study Old Testament theology... only to discover that while I had the necessary dogmatic temperament, I really had no talent for that discipline, and little patience. Indeed, theology by now seemed to me a dead end. What more could be learned from endless reinterpretation of the same texts? So I turned to archaeology."
- "The Hebrew Bible finally became real for me, indeed more 'credible,' because I dealt constantly with the tangible evidence."
- "I became a nominal Jew. Today I am somewhat active in the Reform community, but I am not observant, in fact not a theist."
- "To my parents, long gone, I am grateful for inculcating in me a deep respect for the Bible and an awareness of the awesome power of religion (even though they would be horrified to see how I have turned out)."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that this is his genuine self-description. The significance for evaluating his work is that he is not a neutral observer — but neither is anyone else. Dever is explicit about his biases in a way that Smith and Römer are not.

## Stakes

Dever's personal trajectory mirrors the faith deconstruction process itself. He started where many believers start, discovered that the evidence didn't support what he'd been taught, and reconstructed a different relationship to the tradition. His book is, in part, an account of what he found in the ground that made belief impossible — and what he found that still had value.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives might argue Dever's loss of faith predetermines his conclusions. Dever would respond that his conclusions drove his loss of faith, not the reverse — and that he's being more honest about his biases than scholars who pretend neutrality.

**Alternative reading:** His personal journey is irrelevant to evaluating his archaeological arguments — the evidence stands or falls on its own merits regardless of the scholar's biography.

## Assessment

Dever's candor about his personal journey is one of the book's strengths. He doesn't pretend to objectivity. He tells you where he stands and lets you evaluate his evidence accordingly. This is methodologically superior to the "objective scholar" pose.
