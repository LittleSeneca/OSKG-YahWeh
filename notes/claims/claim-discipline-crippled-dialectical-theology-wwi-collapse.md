# albertz-hir-intro.1: The "history of Israelite religion" as a discipline was crippled by dialectical theology after WWI, and subsequent post-WWII histories were methodologically regressive

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — Introduction — History, Method, Dividing Lines]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §1.1

## The Claim

The discipline flourished in the 19th century (Gabler, de Wette, Vatke, Wellhausen, Smend) but was gutted by the post-WWI rise of Barthian "dialectical theology," which rejected historicism for revelation. The few post-WWII histories of religion (Ringgren 1963, Schmidt 1968/1982, Fohrer 1969) were "largely uninteresting resumes" that "mark more of a step back in method and system."

## Evidence

- Traces five intellectual sources for the historical approach: rationalism, Herder, Hegel (→ Vatke), comparative ANE research, and the history-of-religions school
- Vatke (1835) was the first to achieve a genuinely historical account, using Hegel's dialectic to break the "Mosaic" paradigm and produce a developmental scheme (pre-prophetic → prophetic → post-prophetic)
- Wellhausen stripped Vatke's philosophical superstructure and produced a genuinely dynamic interplay of political and religious history
- The Wellhausen school (Smend Sr, 1893) made the discipline dominant — it displaced OT theology entirely
- Then WWI: Eissfeldt (1926) tried to rescue it by proposing a clean methodological separation — history of religion = knowledge, OT theology = faith
- Post-WWII histories failed: Ringgren organized material dogmatically, Schmidt's "Faith of the Old Testament" was apologetic and ignored popular piety, Fohrer's "impulse" theory was history of ideas in disguise

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The factual genealogy of who wrote what when is publicly verifiable; the evaluative claim that post-WWII histories were regressive is more debatable.

## Stakes

If this narrative is correct, the revival of history-of-religions research is not merely filling a gap but recovering a lost methodological richness. The "history of religion vs. OT theology" dispute is not just academic turf warfare — it's about whether Israelite religion is understood historically (as a human, social, contested process) or theologically (as revelation to be appropriated).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire OT theology guild — von Rad, Eichrodt, Childs, and their successors would argue that history-of-religions approaches miss the theological *kerygma* — what the texts actually claim to *be* (revelation).

**Alternative reading:** One could argue that Albertz is fighting the last war. The collapse of *Religionsgeschichte* after WWI wasn't just theological fashion — it reflected genuine dissatisfaction with historicism's failure to say anything normative. Post-WWII "histories" integrated Ugaritic and archaeological data that Wellhausen never had.

## Assessment

Albertz is convincing on the diagnosis (the discipline collapsed, what followed was less rigorous) but less so on the prescription (that *his* new approach will fix it). The proof is in the execution. The question is whether his "internal religious pluralism" framework actually produces better history, or just a different set of blind spots.
