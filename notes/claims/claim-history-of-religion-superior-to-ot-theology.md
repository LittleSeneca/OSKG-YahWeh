# albertz-hir-intro.3: The "history of Israelite religion" is the more meaningful comprehensive Old Testament discipline, superior to "Old Testament theology"

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — Introduction — History, Method, Dividing Lines]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §1.2

## The Claim

"I cannot disguise the fact that in the present situation I regard the history of religion as the more meaningful comprehensive Old Testament discipline." Albertz gives seven reasons, pivoting on: it matches the historical structure of the OT, takes seriously that religious statements can't be separated from their background, doesn't force contradictory statements into abstraction, describes a dialogical process that mirrors contemporary ecumenical learning, sees continuity in the *people* of Israel (not ideas to be appropriated by Christians), openly dispenses with any claim to absoluteness, and facilitates interreligious dialogue.

## Evidence

- Traces the Eissfeldt compromise (1926): history of religion = knowledge, OT theology = faith → neither side is satisfied today
- Shows OT theology has drifted toward the historical: Eichrodt already located it within empirical historical scholarship; von Rad conceded "re-telling remains the most legitimate form"; Preuss returned to Eichrodt's view that OT theology "remains an undertaking with a historical orientation"
- Meanwhile, history of religion has become more theological: can't be purely objective post-Auschwitz, must pursue theological interests, must evaluate wrong decisions
- The two disciplines are converging; the question is which does the job better

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is a normative argument about disciplinary priorities, not a factual claim that can be verified; the force depends on shared presuppositions.

## Stakes

If Albertz is right, the entire OT theology enterprise is a category mistake — it tries to do systematically what can only be done historically. If he's wrong, his history of religion will lack the normative dimension that makes the OT matter to religious communities.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The entire OT theology tradition. Childs would argue the canonical approach does what Albertz claims only history can do — and does it better, because it takes the text's own theological claims seriously. Von Rad would argue that "re-telling" is historical in form but theological in substance.

**Alternative reading:** Albertz's argument is clever because it makes history of religion *appear* more theological than the theologies. But this is a rhetorical move: he's defined "theological" in a way that favors his method (dialogical, non-absolutist, open to Judaism) and then claimed OT theology fails on those terms.

## Assessment

This is the most contestable section of the Introduction. Albertz is right that OT theology has a structural problem (no agreed center, no agreed method). But his alternative — a history of religion that *evaluates* wrong decisions and pursues *theological interests* — reintroduces the very normative dimension he criticized OT theology for. The difference is that his norms emerge from the historical process itself rather than from church dogma. That's elegant but fragile: what if the historical process produced "wrong decisions" that Albertz evaluates differently than the tradents did? Whose evaluation wins?
