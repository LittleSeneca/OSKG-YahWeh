# kaufmann-ri-12.2: The temple mount vision (Isaiah 2:2-4) marks the beginning of prophetic eschatology and universalism — the first vision of the end of idolatry

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XII — Isaiah Micah Habakkuk]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XII

## The Claim

The vision of the end of days in chapter 2 is "the first to envisage the end of idolatry, and thus marks the beginning of prophetic universalism." Going beyond Amos's "day of YHWH" as national judgment, Isaiah envisions a universal doomsday. Idolatry is conceived as arising from human pride — "the wisdom through which man is creative and can control nature has become his stumbling block. Trusting in his power he makes himself gods; in adoring them, he worships himself." The vision is further unprecedented in that "Zion is depicted not as the seat of the Davidic dynasty, not as the capital of an Israelite empire, but as the center of God's universal kingdom." The cultic aspect of the temple is completely ignored; it is not a site of worship for all mankind (contrast 56:7) but "the place from which justice and law go out to all men." Isaiah is the first prophet to affirm that all men will worship the one God at the end of days.

## Evidence

- "This sublime vision, unparalleled in the earlier literature, ushers in a new phase in the history of Israelite religion"
- The vision of universal peace — "nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more" — has no antecedent in earlier Israelite literature
- The rationale: warfare ends because "the lust for power and domination has come to an end"; this is the first adumbration of "an international morality"
- The vision rounds out the legend of primeval monotheism with an eschatological monotheism of all humanity
- The prophecy of Isaiah 19:23-25 (Egypt, Assyria, and Israel as a triad of YHWH's peoples) is presented as the peak of this universalism — "nowhere else is YHWH represented as calling a foreign nation 'my people'"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Medium-high on the observation; low on the dating. Isaiah 2:2-4 IS unprecedented in its scope — Kaufmann is right that nothing in Amos or Hosea matches it. But the claim that this is genuinely 8th-century Isaiah depends on Kaufmann's broader dating framework, which many scholars reject. Micah 4:1-4 contains the identical oracle; the direction of borrowing (Isaiah → Micah) is contested.

## Stakes

When universalism entered Israelite religion. If Kaufmann is right, universal eschatology is an 8th-century development, predating the exile by 200+ years. The exile didn't produce universalism — it was already there.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars who date 2:2-4 as post-exilic. Schmidt and others see the universal pilgrimage motif as reflecting the post-exilic temple's international profile. Smith (Origins Ch 10) sees Second Isaiah as the real moment of universalistic breakthrough.

**Alternative reading:** The vision could reflect Isaiah's genuine thought but in a later formulation. The core idea (Zion as moral-spiritual center, not military capital) fits Isaiah's known theology. But the precise formulation may have been shaped by later tradents. Or, alternatively, the vision genuinely IS Isaiah's — it's not as anomalous as critics claim, since universalistic impulses appear in the 8th century (the J source, Amos's oracles against the nations).

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reading of the vision's CONTENT is excellent — his observation that the temple is purely a seat of torah/justice, not cult, and that Zion is not a Davidic capital but a universal teaching center, is precise and important. Whether it's 8th century is harder. I'm inclined to think the core vision IS early — the absence of any cultic dimension, any Davidic messianism, any national revenge, makes it hard to place in the post-exilic period where those themes dominate. It fits Isaiah better than any later prophet. But "fits better" is not the same as "proven."
