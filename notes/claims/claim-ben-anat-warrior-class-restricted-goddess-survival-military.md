# keel-ggi-5.7: The Ben-Anat warrior class provides epigraphic evidence for the continuing significance of goddess traditions — but ONLY within a specific, socially restricted military stratum

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch V — The Hidden God Victorious Gods Fertility Iron I]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter V

## The Claim

"About two dozen arrowheads from Lebanon and el-Hadr near Bethlehem, dated to the eleventh century, are inscribed with their owner's names." The pieces from el-Hadr "apparently belonged to members of the warrior class Ben-Anat." Arrowhead no. 5 bears the inscription 'bdlb't / bn 'nt; nos. 1-4 carry hṣ 'bdlb't, "arrow of 'Abdlabi'at." The personal name 'Abdlabiat — attested at Ugarit (KTU 4.63 III 38) — means "servant of the 'lioness,'" where lb't is "an epithet of the goddess."

But the authors immediately narrow the claim: "Whoever wants to use this evidence to show the continuing significance of one or more goddesses in early Iron Age Palestine must immediately narrow the focus and recognize that this evidence can be linked to traditions that are at home only within specific social classes. Even in such cases, the themes seem to be a carryover, by and large, from what has come down by inheritance from the Late Bronze Age (family names or personal names!)."

## Evidence

- Arrowheads from el-Hadr (near Bethlehem): inscribed with Ben-Anat names, 11th century
- Arrowhead no. 5: 'bdlb't bn 'nt — "Servant of the Lioness, son of Anat"
- Arrowheads nos. 1-4: hṣ 'bdlb't — "arrow of 'Abdlabi'at"
- Ugaritic parallel: 'Abdlabiat as archer's name (KTU 4.63 III 38)
- Dagger blade from Giloh watchtower: stamped with stylized tree — "just like the inscribed names, [these stampings] are to be regarded as ways to identify the owner"
- Conoids combining archer + suckling mother animal: Gezer, Tell el-Far'ah south
- Biblical references: Ben-Anat in Judg 3:31 (Shamgar ben-Anat), Judg 5:6
- Akkadian labbatu as Ishtar epithet

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH for the existence of a Ben-Anat warrior class with Anat-devotion. MEDIUM for the conoid interpretation — the archer+suckling-animal combination could represent other things.

## Stakes

This provides a nuanced alternative to both "the goddess disappeared" (Keel and Uehlinger's own glyptic argument) and "the goddess survived robustly" (Dever's figurine argument). The goddess DID survive — but in a specific, socially narrow context (military aristocracy with Late Bronze cultural inheritance), not as a widely worshipped public deity. This could explain why Anat appears in biblical personal names (Shamgar ben-Anat) and place names (Beth-Anat) even though she has no temple or cult in the biblical narrative.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the biblical references to Anat as purely literary or vestigial might resist the Ben-Anat warrior class interpretation. The arrowhead inscriptions could be elite fashion rather than evidence of active devotion.

**Alternative reading:** The archer + suckling animal conoids may simply juxtapose two popular motifs (aggression + fertility) without any connection to Anat or the Ben-Anat class. The authors acknowledge this possibility: paratactic combination rather than theological statement.

## Assessment

This is a brilliant piece of detective work that exemplifies the best of Keel and Uehlinger's method — combining glyptic, epigraphic, onomastic, and biblical evidence to reconstruct a specific social-religious phenomenon. The Ben-Anat class provides a model for how goddess devotion could persist within specific social niches even as public iconography shifted away from anthropomorphic goddesses. This has implications for understanding how Asherah worship may have survived in similar niches (women's domestic religion?) during Iron II.
