# keel-ggi-7.1: Neo-Babylonian glyptic was supplanted by Achaemenid royal-hero imagery, but local populations adapted both traditions to express their own religious identity

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VII — Iron Age III]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VII

## The Claim

The shift from Assyrian to Babylonian to Persian domination is visible in seal iconography during Iron Age III — but what locals *did* with those seals tells a more interesting story. Neo-Babylonian conoids (faceted hard-stone seals) depicted a male worshiper before the cultic symbols of Marduk and Nabu (spade and stylus), often under astral symbols (star, winged sun, crescent moon). Under the Persians, Achaemenid royal-hero imagery (king fighting hybrid creatures, "Lord of the Animals") entered circulation via Samaria and Tell Keisan. But Judeans bought imported seals and then *erased the images*, replacing them with names. A faceted conoid from el-Jib has only a Yahwistic name inscription (l'*nyhw bn hryhw*) where the astral cultic scene would normally be. The *lpqd yhd* ("Inspector of Judah/Yehud") seal portrays a worshiper before the Aramean moon god — a Persian official of Judah used a moon-god seal. Judeans weren't ignorant of Babylonian and Persian religious imagery — they deliberately chose not to reproduce it on their personal seals, in some cases actively erasing it.

## Evidence

- **Neo-Babylonian conoids** (§217): Faceted hard-stone seals depicting a male worshiper before the cultic symbols of Marduk and Nabu (spade and stylus), often under astral symbols (star, winged sun, crescent moon). A bulla from Tell el-Ajjul shows the worshiper before the cultic pedestal of the moon god Sin instead. "Nothing with comparable decoration has appeared thus far in the Judean heartland."
- **The `lpqd yhd` seal** (§217): A faceted conoid with the legend *lpqd yhd* — "Inspector of Judah/Yehud" — portrays a worshiper before the Aramean moon god in a crescent (illus. 358c). A *Persian* official of Judah used a moon-god seal.
- **Aniconic Hebrew adaptation** (§217): A faceted conoid from el-Jib has *only* a Yahwistic name inscription (l'*nyhw bn hryhw*, illus. 359) where the astral cultic scene would normally be. "It can hardly be by accident that the name of the female or male owner appears in place of the astral cultic scene on these and similar commercially acquired Hebrew name seals."
- **Achaemenid royal-hero imagery** (§218): Under the Persians (late 6th century), bullae from Samaria and Tell Keisan show the Persian king fighting hybrid creatures (lion-griffin, winged bull) or as "Lord of the Animals" subduing two beasts. A winged sun disk emphasizes divine legitimation. Syro-Phoenician carvers adapted the motif, replacing hybrid creatures with natural lions. On a scarab from Tell es-Safi, the hero "lacks any royal or Persian characteristics" — the motif has been localized.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The glyptic evidence is stratigraphically and stylistically well-dated. The contrast between imported cultic imagery and local aniconic adaptation is a strong independent witness to the direction of Judean religion.

## Stakes

This is a material-culture confirmation of the aniconic trajectory. Judeans weren't ignorant of Babylonian and Persian religious imagery — they deliberately chose *not* to reproduce it on their personal seals, in some cases actively erasing it. This parallels the literary evidence for aniconic polemic (Deutero-Isaiah's mockery of idols) but precedes it in some cases.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Stern (1982) treats the material more as evidence of Persian administrative presence than of local theological choices. Keel & Uehlinger's reading is more sensitive to the *selection* and *adaptation* of motifs.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of Babylonian cultic seals in Judah could reflect simple lack of access or trade routes rather than theological rejection. But the el-Jib seal — where the image was explicitly omitted — argues against this.

## Assessment

The el-Jib seal is the smoking gun. Someone bought a blank faceted conoid (the prestige form of the era) and had it inscribed with a Hebrew name instead of the standard Babylonian cultic scene. That's a choice. Combined with the broader pattern of aniconic Hebrew name seals already established in Iron Age IIC, the trajectory is clear: Judean religious identity was being expressed through the *absence* of images.
