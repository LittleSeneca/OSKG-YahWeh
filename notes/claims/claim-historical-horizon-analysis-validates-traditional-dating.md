# kaufmann-ri-10.4: Historical horizon analysis validates traditional dating of the prophetic books

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

"When the historical purview of each of the prophets is examined, it proves to belong to a definite setting and to reflect the events of one period only. The historical horizon of each prophet is always a natural one." This supports "the tradition that classical prophecy came to an end at the beginning of Second Temple times."

## Evidence (book-by-book survey)

- **Amos:** Dates from Jeroboam II, before Tiglath-pileser III (before 745). Foretells exile but never names Assyria. "Amos' prophecies are a faithful document of the Aramaean-early Assyrian period"
- **Hosea:** Couples Egypt with Assyria, foresees destruction, but gives "no indication that Assyria would be Ephraim's scourge." Not one verse was reworked
- **Isaiah (1-39):** Bulk reflects 8th century — Aram, Egypt, Assyria locked in battle. Assyria is "the rod of YHWH's wrath," the *final* enemy. "That it in turn was to fall before and be succeeded by Babylonia is nowhere indicated"
- **Micah:** Assyrian menace — fall of Samaria, deliverance of Judah. Only one later adaptation: "and you shall come to Babylon" (4:10)
- **Zephaniah:** Reign of Josiah — "clearly Assyrian; there is no allusion to Babylonia or the Chaldeans"
- **Nahum:** Prophesies at the collapse of Assyria. "For him the Assyrian domination is the last; there is no hint of a new heathen empire"
- **Habakkuk:** Rise of the Chaldeans. "The prophet comforts himself that their empire will speedily collapse; he knows nothing of Persia which was to follow"
- **Jeremiah:** Chapters 1-19 never mention Babylonia (before 605). After 605, Babylonia identified as conqueror. Chapters 50-51 foresee Babylon's collapse — but "not a word concerning Persia, its successor"
- **Ezekiel:** Reign of Nebuchadnezzar. Chapter 21 concerns Babylon's fall — "but nowhere is there a hint that Persia would have a hand in this"
- **Isaiah 40-66:** Rise of the Persian Empire. Also the background of Haggai, Zechariah 1-8, Malachi. "No allusion to its destruction by yet another heathen empire is to be found"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's most empirically grounded argument — the survey of twelve prophetic corpora showing consistent, limited historical horizons is genuinely remarkable.

## Stakes

Whether the prophetic books are reliable historical documents from the periods they claim, or retrospective compositions from later periods. If Kaufmann's horizon analysis is correct, the books contain authentic 8th-5th century material that wasn't substantially rewritten.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date portions of the prophetic corpus (especially Isaiah 24-27, Zechariah 9-14, Joel) to the Persian or Hellenistic periods. These texts have "undefined" historical backgrounds, which Kaufmann acknowledges but treats as neutral rather than evidence of lateness.

**Alternative reading:** The limited historical horizons prove that the *original* oracles are early — but not that the *final form* of the books is early. Redactors could have preserved the original horizons while adding material that didn't contradict them (e.g., universalizing expansions, liturgical frameworks). Moreover, a Persian-period author could deliberately restrict a work's horizon to the Chaldean period for literary verisimilitude.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann at his most persuasive. He does what good historical criticism should do: he tests the texts against a clear prediction (if the books were heavily revised, historical horizons would be extended) and finds the prediction falsified. The pattern is consistent across twelve prophetic corpora — that's not coincidence. The silence about Persia in books that would naturally mention it if they were late is genuinely hard to explain on the redaction model. However, Kaufmann's conclusion that ALL the material in these books is early goes beyond what the horizon analysis proves. The horizons prove the *oracles* are early; they don't prove that every editorial framework, superscription, and arrangement is equally early.
