# day-ygc-1.6: Jeroboam's golden calves were Yahwistic images derived from El's bull symbolism

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 1 — Yahweh and El]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 1: Yahweh and El

## The Claim

"Jeroboam's golden calves derived from the old bull cult associated with El-Bethel at Bethel, which was traced back to Jacob" (p. 39). They were Yahwistic images (not Baal-worship, not mere pedestals), representing Yahweh in the form of a bull — a direct material violation of the second commandment that was mainstream in the Northern Kingdom for two centuries.

## Evidence

### Yahwistic, Not Baal
1. Jeroboam was trying to stop people from going to Jerusalem to worship *Yahweh* — wouldn't impose an alien god (p. 37)
2. Jeroboam's words: "Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" — it's Yahweh who is the God of the Exodus (p. 37)
3. Exodus 32 (polemicizing against Jeroboam's calves) connects them to Yahweh: "Tomorrow shall be a feast to the LORD" (Exod 32:5) (p. 37)
4. Jeroboam's own son has a Yahwistic name (Abijah, 1 Kgs 14:1)
5. Samaria ostracon 41: personal name *ʻglyw* — "calf of Yahweh" or "Yahweh is a calf" (p. 38)
6. **Jehu's revolution** (the trump card): "wiped out Baal from Israel" (2 Kgs 10:28) but did NOT remove the golden calves (2 Kgs 10:29) — clearly not perceived as Baal symbols (p. 37)
7. Elijah, fierce anti-Baal prophet, never condemns the calves (p. 37)

### El-Derived, Not Baal-Derived
1. Bull symbolism at Ugarit is *supremely* associated with El ("Bull El," *tr ʼil*) — not primarily Baal
2. One of the two calf sites was **Bethel** — literally "house of El." The god was called El-Bethel (Gen 35:7; cf. 31:13); Jer 48:13 refers to the deity "Bethel" (p. 38-39)
3. Jacob's god is *ʼabîr yaʻaqob* "the Mighty One of Jacob" (Gen 49:24) — *ʼabîr* is closely connected to *ʼabbîr* "bull." Jacob's god was likely originally "the Bull of Jacob" (p. 39)

### Images, Not Pedestals
1. The OT consistently represents them as images: "Behold your gods... who brought you up" (1 Kgs 12:28); "sacrificing to the calves he had made" (p. 41)
2. Hos 8:6: "A workman made it; it is not God" — pointless unless people DID consider the calf a god (p. 41)
3. El at Ugarit is called "Bull El" — the epithet is applied to EL HIMSELF, "not to his pedestal" (p. 41)
4. The Jacob-Bethel connection: if Jacob's god was "the Bull of Jacob," the bull was "more than a mere pedestal" (p. 41-42)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Yahwistic identification is decisive (Jehu's purge is the trump card). The El connection is persuasive through Bethel specifically but weaker at Dan. The images-vs-pedestals debate is somewhat a false binary — the animal could function as BOTH symbol and throne.

## Stakes

This establishes that ancient Israelites worshipped Yahweh in the form of a bull image — a direct material violation of the second commandment that was apparently mainstream in the Northern Kingdom for two centuries. It also reveals that El bull cult survived at Bethel specifically, the very place associated with Jacob's vision. The golden calf story in Exodus 32 is a retroactive polemic against northern Yahwism, not an account of an ancient apostasy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Those who think the calves represented Baal (Tobit 1:5 already attests this view; modern advocates include T.J. Meek, H.M. Barstad). Obbink/Albright pedestal theory (the calves were thrones for invisibly enthroned Yahweh, not images — citing Hadad-on-bull Syrian iconography). Eissfeldt: calves were on standards/poles. Lewy/Bailey: calves represented the moon-god Sin.

**Alternative reading:** The pedestal theory has genuine iconographic support from Syrian Hadad depictions. Day's objection that "Bull" is applied to El himself (not his pedestal) doesn't fully engage with how visual cult representation actually works in the ANE — a deity could be both represented by and enthroned upon the same animal.

## Assessment

Day's case is strongest on the Yahwistic identification (Jehu's purge is the trump card). The El connection is persuasive through Bethel specifically but weaker at Dan. The images-vs-pedestals debate is a false binary: in ANE worship, the animal could function as BOTH symbol and throne. The pedestal theory isn't as easily dismissed as Day suggests. But overall, Day's position represents the current scholarly majority and his arguments are well-reasoned.
