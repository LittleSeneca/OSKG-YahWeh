# stav-god-5-8.4: God had sex — with his consort Asherah (as attested by Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions) and metaphorically with Israel (as depicted in Hosea 1-3 and Ezekiel 16, 23) — because the deity was understood as a sexual, married being.

**Source:** [[Stavrakopoulou — Part II — Genitals]] — Francesca Stavrakopoulou, *God: An Anatomy* (2021), Chapter 8

## The Claim

Stavrakopoulou argues that God was not celibate. He had a wife (Asherah) and the relationship was understood in sexual terms. The prophetic metaphor of Israel as God's unfaithful wife (Hosea, Ezekiel) draws on the cultural reality of a married deity — the metaphor only works if the audience understands God as a sexual being with a consort.

## Evidence

**Epigraphic evidence — the divine couple:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud: "Yahweh and his Asherah" — paired as divine couple
- Khirbet el-Qom: similar pairing of Yahweh and Asherah

**Biblical evidence — the sexualized prophetic metaphor:**
- Hosea 1-3: Yahweh as betrayed husband, Israel/Gomer as unfaithful wife — the metaphor makes sense because the deity IS understood as a sexual being
- Ezekiel 16: Explicitly sexualized allegory of God's relationship with Jerusalem — "your breasts were formed, your hair had grown... I spread my cloak over you and covered your nakedness; I pledged myself to you and entered into a covenant with you, declares the Lord Yahweh, and you became mine"
- Ezekiel 23: The allegory of Oholah and Oholibah — two sisters as Samaria and Jerusalem, with explicit sexual imagery of God's relationship to both

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The Asherah pairing is strong epigraphic evidence; the sexualized prophetic imagery is textually explicit; whether God was understood to have literal sex is debated.

## Stakes

If God had a wife and sexual relationships, the entire theological architecture of divine transcendence, holiness-as-separation-from-the-physical, and God's "otherness" collapses. God is not above sex — he participates in it. The biblical God is not the celibate father-figure of later theology but a married, sexual deity whose relationship with his people is modeled on marriage, complete with its sexual dimensions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] (Smith — Asherah was a Yahwistic symbol, not a goddess consort). [[claim-his-asherah-cultic-symbol-stylized-tree-not-goddess-consort]] (Keel/Uehlinger — "his asherah" is a cultic symbol, a stylized tree, not a goddess). [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]] (Kaufmann — Israelite religion was non-mythological; God could not have a consort).

**Alternative reading:** The Asherah inscriptions refer to a cult object (a stylized tree or pole), not a goddess. The prophetic marriage metaphor is just that — metaphor — and does not imply God actually had sex or a wife. The Hosea/Ezekiel imagery uses marriage as an analogy for covenant faithfulness, not as a claim about divine sexuality.

## Assessment

Stavrakopoulou's strongest claim in Part II. The epigraphic evidence for "Yahweh and his Asherah" as a divine couple is solid, and the prophetic marriage metaphor is genuinely sexualized in ways that later theology has sanitized. But she pushes the evidence to its maximum reading — the inscriptions could refer to a cult object rather than a goddess (Smith, Keel), and the prophetic metaphor could be metaphor without implying literal divine sex. Stavrakopoulou chooses the most radical reading each time.
