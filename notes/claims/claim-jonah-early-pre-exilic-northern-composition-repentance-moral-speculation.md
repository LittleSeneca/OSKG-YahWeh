# kaufmann-ri-8.8: Jonah is an early pre-exilic northern composition on repentance

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"The critical opinion that Jonah is a late work... is a tissue of errors." Jonah was composed "during the dynasty of Jehu" in the north, before Tiglath-pileser III and before Nineveh became a glorious imperial capital. "The Nineveh of the book of Jonah is not the capital of a great pagan empire; its king is not the ruler of Assyria, but 'king of Nineveh.'" "Of the universalism of literary prophecy there is not a trace in Jonah." Rather, Jonah "belongs, then, to the early literature of moral speculation" — like Job and the Sodom story — dealing with "sin, punishment, repentance, forgiveness — justice versus mercy."

## Evidence

- Nineveh in Jonah is a legendary "great city" with its own king, not the capital of a world-conquering empire. "This fabulous principality could have been conceived only before the reign of Sennacherib, who made Nineveh a glorious imperial capital"
- The book's universalism is not classical prophetic universalism but "the early universalism of preclassical prophecy" — YHWH judges the nations for moral sins, not idolatry
- "Jonah does not call on the Ninevites to abandon their idols"
- The Ninevites' faith is "the pagan 'fear of God' that figures in many early narratives (Gen. 20:11; 39:9; 42:18)"
- The book's hero is Jonah son of Amittai (II Kings 14:25), a northern prophet under Jeroboam II (784-744 BCE)
- "The road to this idea was long" — tracing the development of repentance from early stories where it is absent (Flood, Sodom) through David's and Ahab's partial repentance to Jonah's full expression
- "The book of Jonah shows what heights the religious-moral consciousness of Ephraim reached in the days of Jeroboam II"

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann's arguments are creative but run counter to strong linguistic and thematic evidence for a postexilic date; his observation about Nineveh as a legendary city is genuinely interesting.

## Stakes

The dating of Jonah and the development of Israelite universalism. If Kaufmann is right that Jonah is early (8th century), it would mean that sophisticated moral reflection on repentance and divine mercy developed in the north centuries before the exile — supporting his thesis that Israel's religious genius was early and broadly distributed.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus dates Jonah to the postexilic period (5th-4th centuries BCE) based on linguistic features (Late Biblical Hebrew), the book's universalist/anti-particularist themes (seen as responding to postexilic exclusivism associated with Ezra-Nehemiah), and its narrative genre (a didactic novella, not prophetic oracle). Sasson's Anchor Bible commentary thoroughly documents the linguistic and thematic arguments for a late date.

**Alternative reading:** Jonah may USE an 8th-century prophet as its protagonist while being composed much later. Its engagement with questions of divine mercy toward gentiles makes most sense in a context where Jewish-gentile relations were a live issue — the postexilic period. The anti-particularist message reads as a critique of Ezra-Nehemiah's exclusivism.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's argument is ingenious and worth engaging — the observation that Jonah's Nineveh is a legendary city with its own king rather than an imperial capital IS genuinely interesting, and his analysis of the repentance theme is excellent. But the late dating of Jonah rests on multiple converging lines of evidence (linguistics, genre, theme) that Kaufmann doesn't adequately address. His alternative early dating requires accepting that Jonah was an anomaly preserved for centuries before being included in the Book of the Twelve — not impossible, but less parsimonious than the standard view.
