# albertz-hir-3.7.2: Assyrian Religious Practices Were Enthusiastically Adopted at the Family Level

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.7 — Family Piety Under the Late Monarchy]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.7

## The Claim

"The population of Judah did not fight against the alien religious customs and ideas of its occupiers, but adopted them voluntarily and almost enthusiastically." The real syncretism was not at the official level (the Jerusalem priesthood maintained theological separation) but at the level of private piety. Assyrian practices were attractive because they were more sophisticated and professionalized.

## Evidence

- Roof-top cult places (Zeph 1:5; 2 Kings 23:12; Jer 19:13; 32:29): family shrines moved to flat roofs following Assyrian/Babylonian practice where prayer and invocation ceremonies took place
- Astral elements: connected with Babylonian astral religion where gods were identified with stars. Rituals took place at night or sunrise
- Typical cultic actions: incense altars (qiṭṭēr) and libations (nāsak) — standard in Babylonian invocation rituals
- Professional manticism (hepatoscopy, necromancy) from Mesopotamia was more sophisticated than local Israelite equivalents and became popular
- Zephaniah's charges of "star worship" may be exaggerated — these cults were primarily concerned with healing sick family members, not theological astral worship

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Albertz is reading between the lines of polemical texts. The Assyrian influence on family cult is plausible but the specific mechanisms (roof-top transfer, mantic professionalization) are inferential.

## Stakes

Albertz's claim inverts the standard narrative of "pagan corruption": Assyrian practices were not imposed by an occupying power but voluntarily adopted because they were *better* — more sophisticated, more professional, more effective at addressing family needs (illness, infertility, financial distress). The Assyrian occupation created a "market" for religious services that local Yahwism couldn't meet.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Kaufmann reads the polemical texts as describing vestigial survivals, not widespread adoption ([[claim-popular-cult-independent-non-pagan-outside-silent-temple]]). The Deuteronomistic Historian presents these practices as foreign corruptions imposed by apostate kings.

**Alternative reading:** The evidence for roof-top cults and astral worship may indicate indigenous Israelite practices that the prophets polemically labeled "Assyrian," not actual Mesopotamian imports. Roof-top cults are attested across the ANE, not uniquely Assyrian.

## Assessment

The reconstruction of Assyrian religious influence on family practice is necessarily inferential from polemical sources. Albertz is at his most creative here — and his most speculative. The roof-top cult transfer is an interesting hypothesis but the evidence is thin. The core insight (family piety enthusiastically adopted Assyrian practices because they were more effective at meeting family needs) is powerful even if the specific mechanisms are underdetermined.
