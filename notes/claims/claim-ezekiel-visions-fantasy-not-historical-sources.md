# kaufmann-ri-13.6: Ezekiel's visions are fantasy — not historical sources for Israelite paganism

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XIII — The Prophecy of the Fall]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XIII

## The Claim

Ezekiel's visions (chaps 8-11, 16, 20, 23) are "pure fantasy," "shadows out of the past," "products of an exuberant imagination" with "no historical worth." Modern scholars who use them as data for Israelite syncretism are making a fundamental error.

## Evidence

- Ezekiel 8-11 is explicitly set in a vision — the prophet is transported by the spirit, not physically present
- The seventy elders in a walled-up chamber: "How, then, did they enter?" — logical impossibility signals fantasy
- The abominations are things Ezekiel "had heard in his youth" about Manasseh's time — "ghosts of that time now rise before his eyes"
- Jeremiah's silence about these same practices is decisive (see Claim 3)
- Ezekiel's historical surveys (chaps 16, 20, 23) contradict the rest of the biblical record: "The high places of Israel were not idolatrous (save during the time of Manasseh); child sacrifice was not normally practiced"
- Ezekiel ignores pious kings (Saul, David, Solomon, Asa, Jehoshaphat, Josiah) — his history is systematically distorted
- His indictment of Jerusalem for "doting upon" Chaldea is absurd: "During Ezekiel's time, there was only one man in Jerusalem who 'doted upon' the Chaldeans — Jeremiah"
- "Modern scholars... fail to notice the allegorical character of these surveys"
- Ezekiel minimizes Sodom's sin to magnify Jerusalem's (16:49 vs. Gen 18-19)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Medium-high on Ezekiel 8-11 as visionary rather than journalistic; medium on the historical surveys. Kaufmann's argument that Ezekiel's temple visions cannot be reconciled with Jeremiah's testimony is genuinely strong. His reading of the historical surveys as hyperbolic allegory rather than reportage is plausible but not certain.

## Stakes

A significant portion of the critical evidence for Israelite syncretism comes from Ezekiel's descriptions. If those are fantasy, the case for popular polytheism loses a major witness.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who use Ezekiel as a source for Israelite religious practice. Römer, Smith, and Dever all draw on Ezekiel to reconstruct popular religion, though none treat it uncritically.

**Alternative reading:** Ezekiel's visions are exaggerated and theologized, but they reflect real practices he knew about. The visionary frame doesn't make the CONTENT fictional — it's a literary device. Prophets regularly use visionary settings to comment on real conditions.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's best argument is the Jeremiah-Ezekiel contradiction. If Ezekiel 8 describes actual temple practice, Jeremiah's silence is indeed inexplicable. But Kaufmann overstates: Ezekiel's visions could reflect real practices that Jeremiah simply didn't emphasize (different rhetorical purposes) or that occurred in spaces Jeremiah didn't access. The logical impossibility of the walled-up chamber is actually a good point — it signals the visionary nature of the entire scene. I think Kaufmann is right that Ezekiel 8 shouldn't be read as journalistic reportage, but wrong that this means there was NO pagan practice in the temple. The visions may be Manasseh-haunted memory rather than current reality — which is Kaufmann's own position.
