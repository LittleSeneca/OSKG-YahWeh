# stav-god-5-8.1: Biblical translators have systematically obscured references to God's genitals — Hebrew terms that clearly refer to male genitalia (yārēk, motnayim) are translated with euphemisms, driven by a theological reflex to deny God a sexed body.

**Source:** [[Stavrakopoulou — Part II — Genitals]] — Francesca Stavrakopoulou, *God: An Anatomy* (2021), Chapter 5

## The Claim

Stavrakopoulou argues that biblical translators have covered up references to God's genitals through systematic euphemism. "This is not prudishness for its own sake; it is a theological reflex. To grant that God had genitals is to grant that God had a sexed body — a male body — and all the problematic implications that follow."

## Evidence

**The key Hebrew terms:**
- Hebrew *yārēk* consistently means "thigh, loins, genitals" (used of Abraham's servant placing his hand "under Abraham's thigh" = oath on the genitals in Genesis 24:2)
- When applied to God (Exodus 33:21-23, where God places Moses in the cleft of a rock and covers him with his hand as his glory passes by), translations render *yārēk*-related terms as "rock" or "cleft" rather than "loins" or "thigh"
- Ezekiel's vision of God: "a form that had the appearance of a man. Downward from what appeared to be his loins" (*motnayim* + *yārēk* — waist-to-genital region). Translations systematically obscure the anatomical precision

**The theological mechanism:**
- Translators are not being prudish — they are protecting a theology. A God with genitals is a God with a sex, and a sexed God is incompatible with the incorporeal, transcendent deity of later theology

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Hebrew terms consistently refer to the genital region; Stavrakopoulou's philological case is strong.

## Stakes

If God has genitals, God has a SEX. The God of the Bible is male — not metaphorically, but anatomically. This has profound implications for gender, sexuality, and the construction of religious authority. The male priesthood, male God-language, and patriarchal religious structures are built on a literal anatomical foundation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]] (Kaufmann — Israelite God is incorporeal and non-mythological). Translators and theologians who argue the euphemisms are appropriate because the language is metaphorical.

**Alternative reading:** The anatomical language is metaphorical or idiomatic — ancient Hebrew used body-part terms more freely than modern English, and translations that use euphemisms are culturally appropriate, not theologically motivated.

## Assessment

Stavrakopoulou's philological case is strong — the Hebrew terms do consistently refer to the genital region. The translation pattern she identifies is real. Her argument about theological motivation is plausible but harder to prove — translators may be following cultural conventions about anatomical language rather than actively suppressing theological implications. The distinction matters for assessing intent vs. effect.
