# keel-ggi-4.6: Syro-Phoenician Cultural Reorientation Drove the Aniconic Turn

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part IV — Iron Age IIA]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part IV

## The Claim

The reorientation of Israel's cultural ties from Egypt toward Phoenicia and northern Syria during Iron Age IIA brought a new iconographic system in which attribute animals and astral/royal symbols replaced anthropomorphic deities. The Egyptian solar iconography that had dominated the Late Bronze Age and persisted in Philistine production gives way to a Syro-Phoenician repertoire where the emphasis is on cherub thrones, guardian lions, and abstract royal symbols rather than anthropomorphic gods and goddesses.

## Evidence

- The Egyptian-influenced solar deity scarabs are Philistine-produced, not Israelite — they are found in Israel/Judah as imports, not local products
- The Taanach cultic stands use "a Syro-Phoenician tradition when it uses cherubs and lions together" — paralleling a small ivory box from Megiddo (end of LB)
- The crescent moon above bovines and the crescent moon on a staff (emblem of the Moon God of Haran) are "typical of northern Syria"
- "Even in the traditional, inexpensive terra-cotta production, goddesses appear less frequently. Clothed votive figures appear in place of the naked goddesses, portraying a woman with a tambourine"
- "The enthroned, clothed mother-with-child makes her appearance" — a figure type that "became an extremely popular figure in Egypt and throughout the Phoenician world during the second half of the first millennium"
- The cherub throne tradition is Phoenician — "where (sometimes empty) cherub thrones appear in widespread and fairly continuous use"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Egyptian-to-Syrian shift is clear in the iconographic record. Whether this shift CAUSED the aniconic turn or merely coincided with it is harder to establish.

## Stakes

If the aniconic turn was driven by Syro-Phoenician cultural influence, Israelite aniconism is not unique or revolutionary — it is an adaptation of a broader regional trend. The distinctive Israelite contribution is not the aniconic impulse itself, but its radicalization (eventually no images at all).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israelite aniconism as unique and revolutionary (the biblical model). Also those who see Egyptian influence continuing through Iron II.

**Alternative reading:** The Syro-Phoenician reorientation may have been primarily political and economic (Solomon's alliance with Hiram of Tyre), with the religious-iconographic effects being secondary and unintended.

## Assessment

The direction of causality is the key question. Did Israel adopt Syro-Phoenician iconography because it was already moving toward aniconism, or did Syro-Phoenician iconography cause the aniconic turn? Keel & Uehlinger's evidence favors the latter: the new political alliances brought new artistic conventions, and those conventions happened to be less anthropomorphic than the Egyptian conventions they replaced. Israelite aniconism may have been partly an accident of geopolitics.
