# keel-ggi-10.4: The external evidence confirms neither the view that there was a fully developed Mosaic monotheism nor the popular view that late and even very late dates for the literature are justified

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch X — Summary and Conclusion]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter X

## The Claim

"These few biblical correlations have shown that the external evidence confirms neither the view that there was a fully developed Mosaic monotheism nor the popular view that late and even very late dates for the literature are justified."

## Evidence

- Their correlations between iconography and biblical texts have shown that many biblical motifs (solar imagery in Psalms, cherub thrones in Isaiah, the "Lord of Heaven" concept) have Iron Age IIB iconographic parallels — they are not late Persian or Hellenistic inventions
- But they also show that these motifs were SHARED with neighboring cultures — they are not uniquely Israelite innovations from a Mosaic age
- The truth is between the two extremes: the biblical texts preserve genuine Iron Age religious concepts, but those concepts were part of a shared Levantine symbol system, not a unique revelation

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** This is an important hedge against both maximalist and minimalist positions, but their evidence for the "not too late" side is less systematically presented than their evidence for the "not uniquely Israelite" side.

## Stakes

This positions the book between the "biblical minimalists" (who date everything late) and the "albrightians" (who trust the biblical chronology). The iconographic evidence supports a middle path: the religious concepts are genuinely old (Iron IIB, 9th-8th c.) but they are not uniquely Israelite.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Both extremes. Maximalists would reject the shared-culture framing. Minimalists might push for even later dates than Keel & Uehlinger allow.

**Alternative reading:** The correlations between iconography and biblical texts could be coincidental rather than causal — similar motifs appearing independently in different cultures. Keel & Uehlinger reject this as anachronistic: "the purely aesthetic use of figurative elements is recent."

## Assessment

This is a reasonable position but it is stated rather than argued in the conclusion. The evidence for it is scattered across Chapters VI-IX. A more systematic treatment of biblical-iconographic correlations would strengthen the claim.
