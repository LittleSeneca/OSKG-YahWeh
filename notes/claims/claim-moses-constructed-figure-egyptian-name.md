# romer-inv-3.1: Moses is a constructed figure with an Egyptian name; no extra-biblical evidence exists for him

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 3 — Moses and the Midianites]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapter 3

## The Claim

"Moses is a figure of tradition, of whom, however, there are no historical traces" (quoting Assmann). He has no extra-biblical attestation in Egyptian or any other ancient texts. But his name is genuinely Egyptian and may preserve an archaic transcription that takes us back to the second millennium. Moses is "a 'construct' in which memory traces of various different historical events and figures are combined."

## Evidence

**The name:**
- "Moses" derives from the Egyptian root *msj* ("to engender, beget"), found in names like Ramses ("Ra has begot him")
- The Egyptian consonant "s" in "Moses" is rendered in Hebrew with the letter *sin* — whereas in first-millennium texts, this sound is normally expressed by *samek*. "The name Moses thus takes us perhaps back to the second millennium."
- This is philological evidence for antiquity, not for historicity. An old name doesn't mean an old person.

**Two possible Egyptian historical parallels — neither is a perfect match:**

| Candidate | Role | Parallel to Moses | Problem |
|-----------|------|-------------------|---------|
| **Beya** | Canaanite chancellor under Seti II (1203-1197 BCE) | Proclaimed child Siptah as pharaoh and essentially reigned himself. "The close relation between Moses and the Egyptian court described in Exodus 2 may well be a reflection of this situation." Hired Canaanite mercenaries (*'apiru*) and seized Egyptian silver and gold (compare Exodus 11:2). | A newly published document indicates Beya was EXECUTED in Egypt, "which would make any identification with Moses arbitrary." |
| **Ben-Ozen** | Semite from Transjordan under Ramses II, held post of "Carver Esquire" | Egyptian name contained element "m-s-s." "He intervened as a mediator in a conflict between some groups of Shasu who were performing forced labor and their Egyptian overseers." Parallel to Exodus 2: Moses defends Hebrew slave. | Partial parallel at best — no full narrative match. |

**The Exodus narrative as literary art:**
- The story of Moses fleeing Egypt is "fashioned with great artifice in the manner of a romance"
- Moses killing the Egyptian overseer REVERSES the standard Egyptian iconographic motif of pharaoh striking down an enemy — the biblical author "certainly knew this convention and reversed it; by doing so he affirmed the authority of Moses vis-a-vis the power of Egypt"
- "It is very difficult to reconstruct any historical event that might lie behind this treatment"

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The absence of extra-biblical attestation for Moses is simply a fact — no Egyptian or ANE text mentions him. The Egyptian etymology of the name is philologically sound (HIGH), but name antiquity doesn't equal person historicity. The Beya and Ben-Ozen parallels are interesting but impossible to verify (LOW).

## Stakes

The founder of Israelite religion — the man who spoke to God face to face, who received the Torah on Sinai, who led Israel out of Egypt — has no footprint outside the Bible. None. The best we can say is that his name might be genuinely old and that some aspects of his story might echo real Egyptian political figures. For a faith tradition that depends on Moses as the unique mediator of divine revelation, this is a serious problem.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives who maintain Mosaic authorship and historicity. Scholars who argue for a historical Moses behind the legend (the "kernel of truth" approach). Minimalists who say the entire Moses narrative is Persian-period fiction with no historical value at all.

**Alternative reading:** The Egyptian name doesn't need to indicate a historical person — it could be a literary choice by an author familiar with Egyptian naming conventions. The Beya and Ben-Ozen parallels could be coincidental rather than sources.

## Assessment

Romer is admirably honest here. He presents the Egyptian parallels (Beya, Ben-Ozen) that might support a historical Moses, then immediately acknowledges their limitations. He doesn't pretend the evidence is stronger than it is. His conclusion — Moses as a "construct" combining multiple "memory traces" — is reasonable but, as he himself notes, unprovable. This is the kind of historical reconstruction where you can't tell the difference between a genuine trace and a coincidental parallel.
