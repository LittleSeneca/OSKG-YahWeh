# dever-dghw-5.2: Eight categories of archaeological evidence collectively demonstrate widespread folk religion centered on Asherah

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter V

## The Claim

Taken together, eight categories of archaeological evidence demonstrate that folk religion was widespread, persistent, and centered on a female deity (Asherah) worshipped alongside Yhwh. The biblical picture of a monotheistic, aniconic, Jerusalem-centered Yahwism describes the religion of a tiny elite — not the lived religion of most Israelites.

Dever's eight categories, organized from public to private:

| # | Category | Key Evidence |
|---|----------|-------------|
| 1 | Local shrines / family religion | At least a dozen household shrines, 12th-7th c. — Megiddo Shrine 2048, Tel Rehov, Lachish Cult Room 49 |
| 2 | Standing stones (massebot) | Found at nearly every excavated Israelite site; Arad holy of holies had two |
| 3 | Altars | Large four-horned altars (Beer-Sheba, Arad), portable incense altars at domestic sites |
| 4 | Figurines (JPFs) | 3,000+ nude female figurines in domestic contexts, 8th-6th c. |
| 5 | Cult stands | Taanach stand: four tiers of deities, lions, cherubim, tree of life, bull |
| 6 | Inscriptions | Kuntillet Ajrud ("Yhwh and his Asherah"), Khirbet el-Qom (same formula) |
| 7 | Votive offerings | Miniature vessels, beads, amulets, jewelry at shrines |
| 8 | Tombs / mortuary practices | Bench tombs with food/drink offerings, lamps — ancestor cult evidence |

## Evidence

- **Convergence:** All eight categories point in the same direction — popular religion that is non-biblical, non-Jerusalem, often female-centered, and persistent across centuries
- **Chronological span:** 12th through 6th centuries BCE — the entire period of Israelite and Judahite monarchy
- **Geographical spread:** Throughout Israel and Judah, both urban and rural
- **The biblical counter-narrative:** The prophets and Deuteronomists condemn every one of these practices — which confirms they were widespread

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Each category is well-attested archaeologically; the convergence across independent categories makes the cumulative argument strong.

## Stakes

This is the evidentiary core of Dever's entire book. If these eight categories are valid, the biblical portrait of Israelite religion is not just idealized — it's actively misleading about what most Israelites actually believed and practiced. The cumulative case forces a choice: either the biblical writers described a minority elite religion (Dever's view), or the archaeological evidence is systematically misinterpreted (a difficult position given the quantity and variety).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Text-focused scholars who treat the biblical picture as fundamentally reliable for religious history. Smith's caution about Asherah treats the archaeological evidence as ambiguous — but Dever argues this is because Smith is a text scholar who doesn't adequately engage material evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The archaeological evidence reflects syncretistic folk practices that the biblical writers rightly condemned — they don't disprove the existence of an orthodox Yahwistic core.
