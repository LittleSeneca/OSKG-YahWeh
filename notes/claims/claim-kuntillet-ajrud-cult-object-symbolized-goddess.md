# day-ygc-2.3: "Yahweh and his Asherah" refers to the cult object that symbolized the goddess

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 2 — Yahweh and Asherah]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 2: Yahweh and Asherah

## The Claim

The translation "Yahweh and his Asherah" is correct, and the Asherah here is the wooden cult symbol (not the goddess directly). BUT this cult symbol "was clearly regarded as a symbol of the goddess Asherah" (p. 60), and the relationship between Yahweh and Asherah "comes near to being a certainty" as god-and-consort (p. 61).

This is a HYBRID position: Day agrees with Smith on the grammar (it's a cult object, not a goddess name) but agrees with Römer on the conclusion (the consort relationship is real).

## Evidence

### Cult Object, Not Goddess

1. **Pronominal suffix problem:** "In ancient Hebrew idiom personal names do not take pronominal suffixes" — so Asherah "could appropriately be 'the Asherah' but not simply the goddess 'Asherah'" (p. 52). The suffix *-h* on *ʼšrth* means "his," which can't attach to a personal name in Hebrew.
2. **Analogies for cult object as source of blessing:** Neo-Assyrian letters: "May Uruk and Eanna bless my lord"; Byblos inscription: "To our Lord and the image of Baal... May they bless"; Elephantine: swearing "by the temple and by Anat-Yahu" (AP 44.3); rabbinic: swearing by temple and altar; NT: swearing by gold of the temple (Mt 23:16-22) (p. 53)
3. **The drawings are Bes, not Yahweh/Asherah:** P. Beck showed the two similar figures on pithos A are the Egyptian god Bes — "they have nothing to do with the inscription" (p. 51-52). The stylized tree flanked by ibexes on the same pithos probably depicts the Asherah cult object mentioned in the inscription.

### Consort Relationship

1. The Asherah cult object "stood in a special relationship with Yahweh" — it was in Yahweh's sanctuary, paired with the *maṣṣēbâ* (male deity symbol) in Deut 16:21-22 (p. 60-61)
2. The cult object symbolized the goddess. If the symbol was Yahweh's, so was the goddess
3. In Canaanite religion, Asherah was El's consort. Yahweh was equated with El. Ergo: Asherah was Yahweh's consort (p. 61)
4. The pairing of Asherah with Baal in the OT is a Deuteronomistic polemical move (Olyan's theory): "guilt by association" to discredit her (p. 61-62). This is never attested in any extra-biblical text.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** High for the cult object reading of the inscriptions; medium-high for the consort conclusion. Day's own position on the grammar question is widely shared (Emerton, Tigay, Olyan, Keel/Uehlinger, Wiggins, Hadley). The consort conclusion follows logically but requires the chain: cult object → symbolized goddess → goddess was El's consort → Yahweh = El → therefore Yahweh's consort. Each link is individually plausible but the chain has four steps.

## Stakes

This is the single most explosive question in the field — did Yahweh have a wife? Day says yes, the evidence "comes near to being a certainty." If true, ancient Israelite religion was more polytheistic (or at least dytheistic) than the biblical text admits.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees on the grammar:** Those reading the goddess directly (Coogan, Dietrich/Loretz, Xella — arguing pronominal suffixes on personal names are attested in other Semitic languages, just not Hebrew). See [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (romer-inv-9.3) for the direct-goddess reading.

**Who disagrees on the consort:** Mark Smith (see [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]]) — the cult object was just a Yahwistic symbol without goddess significance.

**Alternative reading (Smith's):** The Asherah cult object absorbed attributes of the goddess but was functionally a Yahwistic symbol — like the bronze serpent (Nehushtan) — rather than evidence of a consort goddess. This preserves the archaeological data without the theological claim.

## Assessment

Day's grammar argument (pronominal suffix can't attach to personal names in Hebrew) is the mainstream position and well-supported. The analogies for cult objects as sources of blessing are effective. The consort conclusion is harder to evade than Day's critics admit — if the symbol of Asherah was paired with Yahweh in his sanctuary and the symbol represented the goddess, it's hard to see how the goddess herself wasn't being worshipped alongside him. Day's position is the most nuanced of the three: he splits the difference between Smith and Römer, agreeing with each on the points where the evidence favors them. Dever's archaeological evidence (3000+ female figurines) adds independent weight Day doesn't fully leverage.
