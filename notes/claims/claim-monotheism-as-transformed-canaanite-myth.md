# smith-obm-9.8: Biblical monotheism is transformed Canaanite myth, not rejected myth — monotheism IS Israel's foundational myth

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch9 — Formation of Monotheistic Theologies]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 9

## The Claim

"In short, biblical monotheism included transformations of Canaanite myth. Put differently, biblical monotheism constituted part of Israelite's own foundational myth comparable in some respects to Canaan's own myths."

## Evidence (synthesis of the chapter)

Three different social locations produced three different strategies:

| Social Location | Corpus | Strategy |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Priests** | Genesis 1 | Eliminated conflict, depersonalized enemies, enclosed them within creation, envisioned cosmos as sanctuary |
| **Wisdom scribes/Levites** | Proverbs 1-9 | Transformed goddess imagery into personified Wisdom, creating a female divine patron subordinate to Yahweh |
| **Apocalyptic visionaries** | Daniel 7 | Preserved the conflict structure but removed the human king, replacing him with the persecuted community |

- "Both Ugaritic and Israelite royal and scribal-priestly groups constituted the primary literary preservers of mythic material, and they were both in a position to use ancient mythic material to suit their own ends."
- "For scribal-priestly groups, this reuse of mythic material assumed a monotheistic cast."
- The chapter's final sentence sets up Ch10: "The last chapter is reserved for the portion of the Bible that contains the most and the boldest monotheistic statements and presentation of deity in order to convince its audience of Judean victory in the era of foreign empires; that portion is Isaiah 40-55, also called 'Second Isaiah.'"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the overall thesis. The evidence across three corpora supports the claim that monotheism was achieved through transformation, not invention. The social-location framework is productive and well-illustrated.

## Stakes

This is the chapter's master argument. If biblical monotheism IS transformed Canaanite myth, then the traditional theological claim of Israel's radical uniqueness collapses. Israel didn't reject myth — it rewrote it with a single protagonist. This is a profound challenge to both confessional and older critical narratives of Israelite religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize Israel's rejection of myth (Kaufmann's "revolution," Albright's "Mosaic monotheism"). Those who see the three corpora as too diverse to support a unified argument.

**Alternative reading:** The transformation thesis could be overstated. Some of what Smith calls "transformation" might be independent development — Israelite scribes could have arrived at similar concepts without deliberately reworking Canaanite material. The genetic relationship between, say, Asherah and Woman Wisdom is plausible but not proven.

## Assessment

This is the strongest synthesis in the chapter and one of the best in the whole book. The three-case-study structure is elegant, and each case illuminates a different social location and strategy. The weakness is that the three case studies receive very unequal treatment — Genesis 1 gets ~4 pages, Wisdom gets ~1 page, Daniel 7 gets barely a page — and the Wisdom and Daniel 7 sections feel rushed. Smith's heart is clearly in the Priestly material, and the other two corpora feel like afterthoughts. But the overall framework is compelling.
