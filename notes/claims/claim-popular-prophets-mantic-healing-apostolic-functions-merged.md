# kaufmann-ri-8.6: Popular prophets merged mantic, healing, and apostolic functions in a single institution

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"The Israelite popular prophet is at once a seer, a wonder-healer, and a militant apostle of YHWH. The characteristic intertwining of functions is evident." The popular prophet operates on multiple levels — accepting payment for mantic services while also serving as a messenger of God. "The mantic and the messenger are two sides of the same prophet." Moreover, "the boundary between mantic and apostolic prophecy was never sharply drawn. And it was from among the 'professional' prophets that the first martyrs of the one God arose."

## Evidence

- **Samuel:** Saul comes to him as a seer to find lost asses but encounters a messenger of YHWH who anoints him king
- **Ahiah:** Jeroboam's wife comes disguised with payment to inquire about her sick son; Ahiah foretells both the boy's death and the doom of the dynasty
- **Elisha:** Naaman comes with payment for healing; the cure converts him to YHWH worship. Hazael and Ben-Hadad consult Elisha as a clairvoyant "man of God"; Elisha weeps and announces Hazael's destiny
- **Elijah (II Kings 1):** Intercepts Ahaziah's embassy to Baal-zebub, asking "Is there no God in Israel that you send to inquire of Baal-zebub?" — defending the mantic function of YHWH prophecy
- "These stories shed light on the conception that Israelite prophecy had of its mantic and healing functions. These were not viewed as powers inherent in persons, spirits, or materials, but as effects of and witnesses to the favor and might of Israel's God"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Well-grounded in the biblical narratives — the Elijah and Elisha cycles in particular support this picture of multi-function prophecy.

## Stakes

The nature of pre-classical prophecy and its relationship to later literary prophecy. If the popular prophets were already functioning as apostolic messengers, the classical prophets (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah) represent continuity with, not a radical break from, earlier prophecy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars have drawn a sharper distinction between "professional" court prophets (who served the king for payment) and "free" prophets (who spoke independently). Kaufmann's portrait blurs this distinction. Critical scholars might question how much of the Elijah/Elisha cycle reflects 9th-century reality versus later legendary development.

**Alternative reading:** The Elijah/Elisha narratives reflect a convergence that wasn't always typical — many "professional" prophets were purely mantic and never functioned apostolically. The classical prophets represent a new phenomenon precisely because they broke with the professional/mantic model.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's observation is valuable and supported by the texts. The Elijah and Elisha narratives do depict prophets functioning across multiple registers — mantic, healing, and apostolic — without a sharp distinction. This challenges the common scholarly tendency to treat the classical prophets as an entirely new phenomenon. However, Kaufmann may be too quick to generalize from the Elisha cycle, which is legendary literature, to the historical reality of 9th-century prophecy.
