# keel-ggi-p1.7: The ruler in the fringed garment represents deified Canaanite kingship — a mediating figure between divine and human

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part I

## The Claim

"Politically, this led to the founding of the Hyksos Dynasty in Egypt. Religiously, the sovereigns appear as some sort of mediating figures who bridge the gap between the divine and earthly worlds. Perhaps they were deified after they died." The "ruler wearing the garment with thick fringes" is the dominant human figure in MB IIB glyptic art, appearing on both cylinder seals and scarabs of the jasper group. On cylinder seals, the fringed garment is worn by both worshipers and the worshiped — the same figure can appear as supplicant and as object of veneration. The ruler appears alone on numerous scarabs, sometimes with an enemy prostrate beneath his throne. Stone stelae fragments from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem portray the fringed-garment ruler in raised relief — "perhaps these stelae were used in the cultic worship of the person who is depicted."

## Evidence

- Jasper group cylinder seals (1720-1650 BCE): ruler in fringed garment as central figure (Collon 1986)
- Barqai scarab (~1650 BCE): only example where ruler wears both fringed garment AND tall Syrian cap
- Scarabs with ruler alone — interpreted as evidence of strong kingship and possible deification
- Unique scarab: enemy prostrate under ruler's throne
- Stelae fragments from Tell Beit Mirsim and Shechem: ruler in fringed garment in raised relief
- Male and female worshipers with branch/flower on steatite scarabs
- Dancers depicted on scarabs; oval plaque showing weather god on one side, three dancers on reverse — one dancer imitates weather god's posture
- Chronological context: emerges ~1700-1650 BCE, exactly when Canaanite city-state kings asserted independence from weakening Egyptian 13th Dynasty

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The iconographic type is well-defined; the link to political developments is plausible. The evidence for actual deification (cultic worship of the ruler's image) is more inferential — stelae fragments could be commemorative rather than cultic.

## Stakes

This is the iconographic evidence for divine or semi-divine kingship in MB IIB Canaan — a concept the Hebrew Bible explicitly rejects (the Davidic king is adopted as Yahweh's son, not deified; 1 Samuel 8 treats kingship as rebellion against divine rule). The biblical polemic against Canaanite kingship (Deuteronomy 17:14-20) may be reacting specifically against the kind of kingship depicted on these seals. The fact that worshipers carry branches/flowers — the same attributes held by deities — suggests a religious system where the boundary between human and divine was permeable.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the fringed-garment figure as simply a human worshiper or local ruler without divine claims. The interpretation as "deified" is Keel & Uehlinger's (and Schroer's), not universally shared.

**Alternative reading:** The fringed-garment figure might represent an ideal type — "the pious ruler" — rather than a specific deified individual. The stelae could commemorate royal patronage rather than cultic worship of the king.

## Assessment

The evidence for some form of exalted kingship is strong — the ruler appears as a central, independent figure on seals; receives homage; is depicted with a defeated enemy underfoot. Whether this amounts to full deification or "sacral kingship" is harder to determine. For the Truth Project, the important point is that MB IIB Canaan had a political-religious system where the king occupied a semi-divine mediating role. This is the ideological background against which Israelite kingship defined itself.
