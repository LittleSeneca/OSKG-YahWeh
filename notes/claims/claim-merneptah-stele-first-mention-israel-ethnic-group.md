# romer-inv-4.2: The Merneptah Stele is the first extra-biblical mention of Israel — as an ethnic group, not a kingdom

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 4 — How Yhwh Became Israel's God]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2014), Chapter 4

## The Claim

The Merneptah Stele is the oldest clearly attested reference to "Israel" outside the Bible. Israel is described as an ethnic group (not a city or territory), located in the mountains of Ephraim. The stele "in no way presupposes an 'exodus' or any kind of emigration of this group from the land of Egypt." By 1200 BCE, a group called "Israel" existed in the central highlands — but there is no indication they worshipped Yhwh at this point.

## Evidence

**The stele in detail:**
- **Date:** 1210-1205 BCE, reign of Pharaoh Merneptah
- **Physical description:** Granite stele, 3.18m high, 1.6m wide, 31cm thick — a monumental victory inscription
- **The key line:** "Israel is destroyed, its seed is no longer" — preceded by Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yenoam, all of which are city-states

**The determinative — the decisive evidence:**
- Israel is given the determinative for an ETHNIC GROUP (a man and a woman, plus three strokes for plural)
- The other names (Ashkelon, Gezer, Yenoam) are given determinatives for cities/territories
- "This does not mean that it is a group of nomads, but rather that it is the name of a definite group rather than of a region or locality."

**The wordplay on "seed":**
- The Egyptian term *pr.t* can mean both "seed/semen" and "wheat"
- "The Egyptians had the habit... of destroying the wheat fields in conquered territories"
- It may also refer to "cutting off the penises of the dead bodies of its enemies"
- The ambiguity is deliberate — "the scribe could have clarified what was meant by adding the sign consisting of three kernels of grain if 'wheat' was intended, or a phallus if what was meant was 'sperm.'"

**Geographic location:**
- Israel is listed between Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yenoam — placing it in the mountains of Ephraim, "the region where Saul will found his 'kingdom.'"

**A possible earlier mention?**
- A Berlin pedestal statue (possibly Ramses II era) has been claimed to contain the name Israel as *Ia-cha-ri* or *a-chi-ru*
- Romer is skeptical: "At best... we might have a quasi-contemporary variant; at the worst it is a completely different toponym."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The Merneptah Stele as the first extra-biblical mention of Israel is universally accepted. The determinative for "ethnic group" is unambiguous in the hieroglyphic writing. The geographic location in Ephraim is consistent with the sequence of place names in the inscription.

## Stakes

The earliest historical mention of Israel is not a story of liberation from Egypt. It's a story of destruction by Egypt. "Israel is destroyed, its seed is no longer." Pharaoh thought he had annihilated them. He was wrong — they survived and eventually produced the Bible — but the first historical mention of God's chosen people is a boast about wiping them out. The stele also tells us Israel existed as a distinct ethnic entity in Canaan by 1200 BCE, which means the Exodus (if it happened) must predate this, and the group called "Israel" was already in the land — not migrating toward it.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Almost no one on the basic facts. Some debate whether the Berlin pedestal provides an earlier reference. Some debate the significance of "seed" as wheat vs. semen.

**Alternative reading:** The Berlin pedestal might provide an earlier reference — but Romer and most scholars consider it inconclusive.

## Assessment

The Merneptah Stele analysis is the strongest section of Chapter 4 — detailed, precise, and grounded in universally accepted evidence. Romer's treatment of the determinative and the "seed" wordplay is excellent philological work. The stele is a fixed point in Israel's history: by 1200 BCE, a group called Israel existed in Ephraim, and Pharaoh considered them worth boasting about destroying. Everything else is inference from this anchor.
