# cross-cmhe-pre-i.5: Exodus 6:2-3 preserves historical memory of patriarchal El worship

**Source:** [[Cross — Preface + Part I — Canaanite Religion and El]] — Frank Moore Cross, *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Exodus 6:2-3 explicitly acknowledges that the patriarchs worshipped El, not Yahweh:

> "I am Yahweh. I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shadday, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them."

Cross reads this as preserving genuine historical memory: the Priestly writer distinguishes the patriarchal cultus (El Shadday) from the Mosaic cultus (Yahweh). "The formula is a theological statement. It identifies Yahweh with El Shadday, the patriarchal god, but it distinguishes the old cultus from the new." The Priestly writer did not invent this distinction — he inherited a tradition that knew El worship preceded Yahweh worship.

## Evidence

- The text is NOT an etiological invention — it's a theological harmonization that acknowledges difference before asserting identity. An inventor would simply assert identity without acknowledging prior difference.
- The Priestly source is typically associated with theological innovation — here it preserves an archaic memory. This suggests the tradition was too well-established to be erased.
- The name 'Ēl Šadday is genuinely archaic — it appears only in the patriarchal narratives and a few poetic texts (Genesis 49:25), never in monarchic-period prose. Its restriction to the patriarchal period is consistent with historical memory.
- The text's distinction between "appeared as" and "made known my name" uses different verbs (*wayyērā'* vs. *nôda'tî*) — the Priestly writer is making a careful theological distinction, not sloppily conflating two traditions.
- The broader Priestly source shows awareness of pre-Mosaic religious forms (altars before Sinai, circumcision before Moses, Sabbath before Sinai) — Ex 6:2-3 fits a pattern of P acknowledging pre-Mosaic religious realities.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The text is explicit and its distinction is carefully constructed — the question is whether this reflects genuine historical memory or a sophisticated theological retrojection.

## Stakes

If Cross is right, the Priestly writer (often considered the most "theological" source) preserves the single most important piece of historical evidence for El worship preceding Yahweh worship. The text is a confession that Israel's God had a prior identity — and that prior identity was El. This makes Ex 6:2-3 the Bible's own testimony to the convergence thesis.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-name-yahweh-revealed-moses-time-p-chronology-reliable]] — Lewis and others see P's chronology (name revealed at Moses's time) as reliable theological history rather than evidence of El → Yahweh convergence. [[claim-exodus-3-6-schematic-bridge-texts-not-historical-memory]] — Fleming argues Ex 3 and 6 are "bridge texts" that schematically connect patriarchal and Mosaic traditions without preserving historical memory.

**Alternative reading:** The text is a theological retrojection — the Priestly writer created the distinction to explain why the patriarchs didn't use the name Yahweh, not because they actually knew a different god. The distinction serves P's theological schema of progressive revelation, not historical memory.

## Assessment

The text is explicit but its interpretation depends on one's broader model. If you accept convergence, it's a smoking gun. If you reject convergence, it's a theological retrojection. The strongest argument for Cross's reading is that an inventor would not create a distinction they then need to explain away — the text reads like an inherited problem being solved, not a fabricated one.
