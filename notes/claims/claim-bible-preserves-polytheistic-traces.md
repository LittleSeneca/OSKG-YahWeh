# romer-inv-intro.1: The God of the Bible was not always "the only God" — the Bible preserves polytheistic traces

**Source:** [[Romer — Introduction]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Introduction

## The Claim

"It is now beyond doubt that the god of the Bible was not always 'unique,' the one-and-only God." The Bible itself preserves traces of a polytheistic past. The journey from one god among many to THE only God is "the basic, and theologically fundamental, enigma that this book attempts to illuminate."

## Evidence

- **Judges 11:24:** Jephthah treats Chemosh as a real god on equal footing with Yhwh: "Do you not possess that which Chemosh, your god, has given into your possession? And shall we not possess that which our God has given into our possession?" Each people has its own tutelary deity. Yhwh is Israel's; Chemosh is Moab's. Neither is said to be the only god.
- **Deuteronomy's warnings:** The audience is repeatedly exhorted not to follow other gods — "without it ever being asserted that these gods did not exist or were not real." You don't warn against something you think is imaginary.
- **Exodus 6:2-3:** Yhwh reveals himself to Moses as a previously unknown god — "he himself tells Moses that this is the first time he has manifested himself under his real name." This is a trace of the historical fact that Yhwh was not always Israel's god.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The biblical polytheistic traces are unambiguous. Even conservative scholars acknowledge that the Hebrew Bible acknowledges the existence of other deities in some passages. The debate is about what this means, not whether the passages exist.

## Stakes

This is the foundation Romer lays for the entire book. If the Bible itself admits that other gods were real to its earliest authors, then monotheism is not the original biblical position — it's a LATER development within the biblical tradition itself. This means you can't appeal to "what the Bible teaches" as a monolithic thing. The Bible teaches multiple things at different stages.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Heiser would say these passages reflect the "divine council" — created divine beings subordinate to Yhwh, not rival gods. Traditional theologians would say Deuteronomy's warnings are about the temptation of idolatry, not an acknowledgment that other gods exist. Sommer would say Jephthah's statement reflects popular belief, not the theology of the biblical authors.

**Alternative reading:** These passages could reflect rhetorical accommodation — the biblical authors speaking in terms their polytheistic audiences would understand, without endorsing the reality of other gods. Jephthah is a character in a story, not a mouthpiece for the narrator's theology.

## Assessment

Romer is right that the Bible preserves polytheistic traces. The cumulative weight of Judges 11:24, Deuteronomy's warnings, and Exodus 6:2-3 is hard to dismiss as mere rhetoric. But he's claiming more than the evidence strictly proves when he says "it is now beyond doubt." What's beyond doubt is that the Bible CONTAINS polytheistic-sounding passages. What's NOT beyond doubt is that the biblical authors themselves were polytheists. That depends on how you date and interpret each passage — exactly the work the rest of the book will do.
