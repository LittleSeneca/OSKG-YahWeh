# albertz-hir-3.1-3.2.6: The Succession Narrative is a mediating position that humanizes the king

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.1-3.2 — Formation of Monarchy and Legitimation Crisis]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.2

## The Claim

The rebel movements "compelled the monarchy to justify itself politically and theologically." The first literary works of Israel arose from this concern. The Succession Narrative (2 Sam 9-1 Kings 2) and History of David's Rise (1 Sam 16:14*-2 Sam 5:12) depict David "constrained by all kinds of human strengths and weaknesses in a way which is without parallel in the Near East." This was done "in order to bring him closer to the way of thinking among the small farmers in Israelite society."

## Evidence

- David is depicted as a tricky yet generous young man, a touching father — "all young men and fathers of families can identify with" him
- Political justification: David was innocent of Saul's downfall; Saul's own failings caused his demise
- These works mediate between sacral kingship ideology and traditional Yahwistic values by humanizing the king

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The literary observation is standard (the "Court History" is widely recognized as a masterpiece of characterization). But whether the humanization was specifically a "mediating position" responding to anti-monarchical pressure is Albertz's interpretation.

## Stakes

If the earliest Israelite literature was produced by the monarchy's need to justify itself to a skeptical tribal populace, then Israelite literature was born from political-theological crisis, not from royal self-glorification.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the Succession Narrative as political propaganda legitimating Solomon by showing David's other sons as unworthy, or as apologetic for David's scandals (Bathsheba, Absalom).

**Alternative reading:** The Succession Narrative could be political propaganda legitimating Solomon by showing David's other sons as unworthy, or could be apologetic for David's scandals (Bathsheba, Absalom). Its "humanizing" character may reflect literary artistry rather than theological mediation.

## Assessment

This feels like Albertz forcing his model onto a text that may have quite different purposes. The Succession Narrative IS remarkably humanizing for ANE royal literature, but there are multiple explanations: it could be apologetic (defending David/Solomon against scandals), propagandistic (showing David's other sons as unworthy), or simply literary artistry. Albertz's "mediating position" reading is creative but underdetermined by the evidence.
