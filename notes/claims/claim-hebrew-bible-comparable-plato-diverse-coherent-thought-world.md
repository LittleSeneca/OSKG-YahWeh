# schmid-ht-1.2: The Hebrew Bible is comparable to Plato's works — diverse but a coherent thought-world

**Source:** [[Schmid — §1 — Is There a Theology of the Hebrew Bible]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §1

## The Claim

"A theology of the Hebrew Bible is as important and necessary as is an account of Plato's philosophy. Plato's work also displays diversity because it was written at different times and addresses various topics. However, while continuing to respect its internal diversity, one can investigate the trajectories, developments, and types of logic involved in Plato's philosophy."

## Evidence

- Like Plato, the HB can be investigated without subsuming it into reception history, though the reception dimension is present
- "One can investigate the diverse (but not endlessly diverse) theology of the Hebrew Bible"
- The HB "even demonstrates overarching structural elements in its literary presentation, which indicate a certain degree of textual unity" (unlike Plato, Schmid claims)
- The texts speak of God "in manifold ways," which justifies calling it "theology"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The Plato analogy is rhetorically effective but question-begging. Plato was one author with a developing thought; the HB is dozens of authors over centuries. The "overarching structural elements" claim is doing a lot of work — Schmid will need to demonstrate these in Parts E-H.

## Stakes

The Plato analogy is Schmid's warrant for treating the HB as having a THEOLOGY (singular) rather than just theologies (plural). If the analogy fails, the book should be titled "Theologies of the Hebrew Bible" — which Schmid admits would be "in principle" defensible.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who emphasizes the HB's polyphony. Schmid himself acknowledges that the plural title "would provide a different emphasis" and "the unifying literary elements would then receive less value."

**Alternative reading:** The HB is more like a library than a single author's corpus. Treating it as a "thought-world" risks imposing unity where there is only proximity.

## Assessment

The Plato analogy is the weakest structural move in the introduction. It's doing too much work with too little justification. Schmid's better argument is his empirical one: the texts ARE interconnected through Fortschreibung, and those interconnections display theological logic. That's a stronger basis than a dubious analogy with a single-author philosophical corpus.
