# kaufmann-ri-6.3: Moses Was a Creative Genius — the Monotheistic Revolution Originated with a Single Person

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

The spiritual revolution that produced Israelite monotheism "must have been, like all similar events in history, the working of a creative genius and leader of men. Following the biblical saga, we call this pioneer creative spirit by the name Moses." Moses "must have been the first to conceive" the monotheistic idea. The birth of every original idea is "a mystery which defies attempts to account for it."

## Evidence

Kaufmann argues by elimination: since monotheism has no roots in paganism, and since it appears in Israel suddenly, a single creative mind must have originated it. He compares Moses to Mohammed — another figure who effected a rapid religious revolution among tribal peoples. The cultural environment (Egyptian technical magic, Hebrew kahin-seer traditions, Canaanite ecstatic prophecy) provided the FORMS through which the new idea expressed itself, but not the CONTENT. "He was sent; his God revealed himself to him and let him hear his voice. Intuitive insight took the shape of a prophetic vision."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-low

**Rationale:** This is not an explanation — it's a placeholder for one. "Creative genius" is what you say when you've ruled out evolutionary explanations but don't want to say "miracle." Kaufmann is one step away from saying "God revealed it to him." A historian who says "this can't be explained" about their central explanandum has stopped doing history.

## Stakes

The entire historical credibility of Kaufmann's project. If the origin of Israelite monotheism is a mystery that "defies attempts to account for it," then Kaufmann has abandoned historical explanation for something closer to theology.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Everyone doing historical-critical work on Israelite origins. Smith traces a gradual differentiation from Canaanite polytheism. Römer traces the step-by-step emergence of monotheism through the monarchy and exile. Even scholars sympathetic to Kaufmann's emphasis on Israelite distinctiveness (Tigay, Sommer) don't resort to "creative genius" as an explanatory category.

**Alternative reading:** The "revolution" was actually a gradual process that took centuries. What Kaufmann attributes to a single Mosaic moment was the accumulated work of prophets, priests, scribes, and reformers over generations. The biblical tradition compressed this long development into a founding moment and attached it to Moses. The comparison with Mohammed actually undermines Kaufmann: we HAVE contemporary sources for Mohammed and can trace the development of his ideas. For Moses, we have only legends written centuries later.

## Assessment

This is where the theological commitments become most visible. Kaufmann's "creative genius" is functionally equivalent to "divine revelation" — it's an uncaused cause that explains everything while explaining nothing. The chapter reads like a secularized version of traditional Jewish belief, with "creative genius" standing in for "God spoke to Moses." The structure is identical: Moses received something new, transmitted it to the people, and the people were transformed. Kaufmann's contribution is to translate this into historical-sounding language without actually providing a historical mechanism.
