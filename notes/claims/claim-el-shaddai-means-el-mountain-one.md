# day-ygc-1.5: El-Shaddai means "El, the Mountain One," not "God Almighty"

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 1 — Yahweh and El]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 1: Yahweh and El

## The Claim

"The most likely interpretation of the divine name El-Shaddai is 'El, the mountain one', with reference to El's dwelling place on a mountain" (p. 33). The traditional "God Almighty" (LXX *Pantokratōr*, Vulgate *omnipotens*) is a later misunderstanding. Every time the OT calls God "Shaddai" or "El-Shaddai," it's invoking a Canaanite epithet for El as the mountain-dwelling god.

## Evidence

### 1. Akkadian Etymology
- Akkadian *šadû* = "mountain," with derived forms *šaddāʼu/šadduʼa* = "mountain inhabitant" (p. 33)
- The *š* (shin) consonant matches Shaddai, unlike the competing "field" etymology (*śādeh* has *ś* = sin)

### 2. Hurrian Parallel
- Hurrian hymn: El described as "El, the one of the mountain" (*ʼIl paban-ḫi-wi-ni*) (p. 34, citing Cross)
- This is an independent witness to El's mountain association

### 3. Amorite Parallel
- *bēl šadê* "lord of the mountain" used of Amurru, whom Cross identifies as the Amorite El
- Amurru's consort is Asratum (Asherah) — El's consort (p. 34)
- Note: this depends on Cross's identification of Amurru as El, which is itself debated

### 4. Deir 'Alla Inscription (Strongest Evidence)
- Deir 'Alla I.5-6: "the Shaddayyin took their places as the assembly" — the *šdyn* are parallel with "gods" (*ʼlhn*)
- The divine assembly meets on a mountain — El, as supreme deity, would be Shaddai "par excellence" (p. 34)
- This is direct inscriptional evidence for Shaddai-related terminology in a divine council context

### 5. Parallel Usage with El
- Shaddai appears parallel with El "a remarkable number of times, especially in Job" (Num 24:4, 16; Job 8:3, 13:3, 15:25, etc.) (p. 34-35)
- This parallelism reinforces the identification of Shaddai as an El epithet

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Day himself hedges: "though we cannot be certain, a plausible case can be made" (p. 35). The Deir 'Alla evidence is the strongest point; the Amorite parallel depends on Cross's contested identification.

## Stakes

If correct, every time the OT calls God "Shaddai" (or "El-Shaddai"), it's invoking a Canaanite epithet for El as the mountain-dwelling god — not a statement about omnipotence. This changes how we read P's deliberate use of "El-Shaddai" for the patriarchal period: P is preserving the old El epithet, not describing a different attribute of God.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Those favoring "El of the field" etymology (M. Weippert, W. Wifall, O. Loretz). The traditional "Almighty" reading is confessional, not linguistic.

**Alternative reading:** Shaddai could simply be an archaic divine name whose etymology was lost early. Day's "mountain" derivation is the best linguistic proposal but not certain. The "field" etymology is phonologically weaker (wrong sibilant) but semantically plausible.

## Assessment

This is a "plausible but not proven" claim, and Day is appropriately cautious. The Deir 'Alla evidence is genuinely strong — the Shaddayyin as divine assembly members. The Amorite *bēl šadê* parallel is weaker (depends on Cross's chain of identifications). I'd say MEDIUM-HIGH, upgrading from Day's own caution because the Ugaritic El-mountain association is independently strong.
