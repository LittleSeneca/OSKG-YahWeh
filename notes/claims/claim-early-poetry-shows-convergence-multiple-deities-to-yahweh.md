# smith-ehg-1.3: Israel's early poetry shows convergence — multiple deities' imagery applied to Yahweh.

**Source:** [[Smith Chapter 1 — Deities in the Period of the Judges]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* (2nd ed., 2002), Chapter 1

## The Claim

The oldest stratum of biblical poetry (Genesis 49, Deuteronomy 33, Judges 5, Psalm 18, Psalm 29, Psalm 68) mixes imagery from El, Baal, and possibly Asherah and applies it all to Yahweh. This "convergence" happened early and was not polemical — it was creative theological synthesis.

## Evidence

- **Deuteronomy 33:26-27:** "Who rides through the heavens" = Baal storm language. "The eternal God" (*'ĕlōhê qedem*) = El language. Both in adjacent verses, both applied to Yahweh.
- **Psalm 18:14-16:** "Yahweh thundered... the Most High (*'elyôn*) uttered his voice... the channels of the sea (*'ăpîqê mayim*) were seen." Baal thundering + El's title + El's cosmic waters — all conflated.
- **Genesis 49:25-26:** A series of paired divine epithets including possible goddess language (*šādayim wārāḥam*, "Breasts-and-Womb," echoing Ugaritic titles of Asherah and Anat). The goddess language was NOT later applied to Yahweh — it was dropped.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that the imagery is shared. MEDIUM on the dating of these poems — Smith dates them to the early monarchy or pre-monarchic period, but this is contested. If they're later, the convergence might be later too.

## Stakes

The poetry that sounds most "monotheistic" to modern readers (Yahweh as cosmic king, thunderer, ancient one) is actually the most SYNCRETISTIC — it's built from pieces of other gods. The majestic language of the Psalms is borrowed from Baal hymns. This doesn't make it false, but it makes it derivative.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservatives argue shared language reflects common ANE idiom, not borrowing. Poetic imagery is like language — everyone in the region used similar metaphors for the divine without implying theological equivalence.

**Alternative reading:** The parallels are common ANE poetic convention. Psalm 29 using Baal-hymn structure no more proves borrowing than a modern worship song using a pop song structure proves the worship song worships the pop star. Form and content are different categories.

## Assessment

The parallels are too specific for "common idiom." When Psalm 29 uses the exact structure of a Baal hymn (sevenfold voice, progression from sea to mountains to temple, enthronement over the flood), "coincidence" is harder to believe than "borrowing." The convergence thesis is one of Smith's strongest arguments.
