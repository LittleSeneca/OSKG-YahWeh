# smith-obm-8.4: The prior context was a "national theology" where Yahweh was a son of Elyon, not the presider god

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch8 — Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 8

## The Claim

"In early Israel the god of Israel apparently belonged to the second tier of the pantheon; he was not the presider god but one of his sons." Before monotheistic rhetoric emerged, Israel operated with a "national theology" where each nation had its own patron god, Yahweh was Israel's, and a higher god (Elyon/El) presided over the arrangement.

## Evidence

**Psalm 82** — the key text:
- "God (`'ĕlōhîm`) stands in the assembly of El / Among the divinities He pronounces judgment" (Ps 82:1)
- The gods are "sons of Elyon" (v. 6) — Elyon is a title of El (cf. Gen 14:18-20)
- They are condemned to die "like humans" (v. 7)
- A prophetic voice calls on Yahweh to "arise" and "judge the world" and "inherit all the nations" (v. 8) — Yahweh is to *arrogate* the inheritance of all other gods
- "Psalm 82 preserves a tradition that casts the god of Israel not in the role of the presiding god of the pantheon but as one of his sons."

**Deuteronomy 32:8-9** (LXX + DSS version, not MT):
- "When the Most High (Elyon) allotted peoples for inheritance...He fixed the boundaries for peoples, according to the number of the divine sons: For Yahweh's portion is his people, Jacob His own inheritance."
- MT changed "divine sons" (`bĕnê 'ĕlōhîm`) to "sons of Israel" (`bĕnê yiśrā'ēl`) — "reflects a discomfort with this polytheistic theology"
- "Even if this text is quite mute about the god who presides over the whole arrangement, it does maintain a place for such a god who is not Yahweh."

**The 70-nation framework:**
- The notion of ~70 divine sons matches "the Ugaritic motif of the seventy sons of El and Athirat (CAT 1.4 VI 46)"
- "This older picture...presupposes the model of roughly equal national gods for all of the seventy nations of the world"

**Survival mechanism:**
- These texts survived only because they could be conformed to later monotheism: Elyon in Ps 82 could be read as Yahweh; MT "corrected" Deut 32:8
- "Analyzed not in terms of the later monotheism but in terms of the earlier national situation, these two passages offer an important witness to the old monarchic period theology of the national god."
- The surviving evidence is "only the tip of the iceberg" given later editing

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Deut 32:8-9 reading (LXX/DSS) is now widely accepted in scholarship. Psalm 82 is unambiguous.

## Stakes

If Yahweh was originally a second-tier god — a son of El/Elyon — then Israelite monotheism involved not just demoting other gods but *promoting* Yahweh to the top tier and then absorbing El's identity. This is the culmination of the "convergence" argument. The tripartite development is: (1) El is the high god, Yahweh is a national patron among many → (2) Yahweh absorbs El's identity and titles → (3) monotheistic rhetoric declares the other gods don't exist.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date these texts late and read them as already monotheistic. Those who question whether "Elyon" is El or just an epithet of Yahweh.

**Alternative reading:** Psalm 82 could be read as Yahweh already presiding and judging — the "assembly of El" could mean "divine assembly" generically. But the "sons of Elyon" in v. 6 makes the El identification strong.

## Assessment

This is where Smith ties the whole book together. The national theology model explains WHY monotheistic rhetoric was needed — the old model (each nation has its own god, presided over by El) became unintelligible when the nations collapsed. If Assyria and Babylon conquered Israel, the national god model failed. Monotheism was the theological response to imperial crisis. The tip-of-the-iceberg metaphor is also important: what survived was what could be reinterpreted monotheistically.
