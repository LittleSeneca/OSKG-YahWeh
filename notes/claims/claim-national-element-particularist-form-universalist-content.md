# kaufmann-ri-9.2: The national element was particularist in form, universalist in content

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch IX — Some Aspects of the Popular Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch IX

## The Claim

"Israelite religion was national in its forms and in the scope of its activity from its beginnings to the end of the Second Temple period. Ideally, however, YHWH was not a national, but a cosmic God; the area of his special grace was restricted to Israel, but his dominion extended everywhere." The covenant concept is not the matrix of monotheism; rather, "it was the monotheistic idea that precluded every conception but this." Israel's election is not ethnic superiority — Israel is "the fewest of all peoples" (Deut. 7:7) and the youngest nation. The relation is one of free choice, not natural kinship.

## Evidence

- Genesis makes Israel the youngest of peoples; the world existed long before Israel
- YHWH is not Israel's kinsman; Palestine is not his natural habitat; the cult is not the source of his vitality
- "The covenant has no pagan meaning; it does not unite the two parties in any natural or mysterious way"
- Israel is "the fewest of all peoples" — the disparity between exalted God and lowly nation is emphasized
- The greatest moments of glory are the Exodus and Wandering (signs and wonders), not the conquest
- Intermarriage prohibitions are religious/cultural, not racial — Egypt is favored for historical reasons, Ammon and Moab excluded for the same
- The third generation of settlers could join "the community of YHWH" (Deut. 23:8 f.)
- Gerim (resident aliens) are made equal to native-born in all Torah laws
- The Torah was given to non-Israelites too — they participated in the covenant (Deut. 29:10 ff.)
- The idea of purely religious conversion is foreshadowed in Naaman, who returns to his land but becomes a YHWH worshipper

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Descriptively sound — Israel combined national particularism with cosmic universalism in a distinctive way; but the 'theoretical universalism' may be evidence of monolatry, not monotheism.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite particularism (YHWH as the God of Israel specifically) is evidence that early Israelite religion was henotheism/monolatry rather than genuine monotheism. Kaufmann wants to say: Israel always believed YHWH was the ONLY God; the national focus was just the arena of his special self-revelation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Römer and Smith would argue that the particularist framework IS the original religion — YHWH was Israel's god among other gods of other nations — and universal monotheism developed later. The very "theoretical universalism" Kaufmann describes (Israel acknowledges YHWH rules the nations but doesn't expect them to convert) is monolatry, not monotheism.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann has genuinely identified something distinctive — Israelite religion combined national particularism with cosmic universalism in a way paganism didn't. But this combination may have developed over time rather than being original. The earliest material (Song of the Sea, Song of Deborah) is purely national; cosmic universalism grows over the monarchic period.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is at his most persuasive here. The observation that Israel's literature consistently emphasizes Israel's SMALLNESS and weakness — making YHWH's favor entirely gracious rather than merited by national greatness — IS distinctive and supports his claim that Israelite particularism was qualitatively different from pagan national religion. But his evidence cuts both ways: the very fact that this "theoretical universalism" is so abstract and non-missionary suggests it's a relatively late theological refinement, not the original popular conception.
