# albertz-hir-3.5.4: Jehu's Revolution Was the First Yahwistic Intolerance

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.5 — Ninth-Century Syncretism Dispute]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.5

## The Claim

Elisha instigated Jehu's coup. Jehu designated himself a "zealot for Yahweh" (qin'ātî lēYHWH, 2 Kings 10:16) and "completed his action with the destruction of the temple of Baal in Samaria and the murder of its priests and cultic followers." This was "the first time Yahweh religion showed its intolerance. Under the conditions of the state the exclusive relationship to God from the period before the formation of the state took on fanatical features and had cruel consequences."

## Evidence

- Elisha sent a disciple to anoint Jehu as rival king (2 Kings 9:1-13)
- Jehu murdered Joram, Jezebel, the entire Omride family (70 "sons of Ahab"), and all Baal worshippers
- The coalition included Rechabites (2 Kings 10:15-17, 23) — conservative Yahwists who rejected agriculture and city life
- Jehu's self-designation "zealot for Yahweh" (10:16) explicitly frames the coup as religious
- The black obelisk of Shalmaneser III depicts Jehu prostrating himself before the Assyrian king — "a glaring spotlight on the fundamental political and religious conflict"

**Consequences:**
- "Baal became the religious enemy" — from Hosea to the Deuteronomists, anything incompatible with "real" Yahwism was denounced as Baalism
- Political isolation: the North lost its diplomatic network, became vulnerable to Aram
- Jehu himself became an Assyrian vassal shortly after his "zeal for Yahweh" purge

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the basic events (Jehu's coup, Baal temple destruction). MEDIUM on the interpretation as "first intolerance" — the exclusivity was present from the Exodus group's beginnings (Albertz argued this himself in §2.2).

## Stakes

Jehu's revolution marks the moment when Yahwistic exclusivity became violent. This would shape Israelite religion for centuries: the prophetic tradition was forever associated with political violence, and "Baal" became the catch-all category for religious deviance.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Kaufmann reads the prophets and Deuteronomistic History as describing the suppression of foreign cults, not Israelite syncretism ([[claim-no-genuine-syncretism-yhwh-baal]]).

**Alternative reading:** Jehu's coup may have been primarily political, with the religious framing serving as retroactive legitimation. The "zeal for Yahweh" language may reflect Deuteronomistic theology, not Jehu's self-understanding.

## Assessment

Jehu's revolution is the first documented instance of religious violence in Israelite history, and Albertz's reading captures its tragic irony: the very exclusivity that protected Yahwism from absorption into the Canaanite pantheon now expressed itself as fanaticism. The black obelisk is a devastating historical artifact — Jehu prostrating himself before Shalmaneser III immediately after slaughtering Baal worshippers for Yahweh exposes the political reality beneath the religious rhetoric.
