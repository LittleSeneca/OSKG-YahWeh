# smith-obm-3.2: CAT 4.360 provides a direct social parallel matching the divine household structure exactly

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch3 — The Divine Family]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 3

## The Claim

The administrative text CAT 4.360 uses the same terms as the myths: 'adn (patriarch), b'l (family head), bn (sons), 'aḫt (daughters), 'aḫt (sisters), 'att (women), n'rm (retainers), ḫpt (free-men). "This text follows a standard in its listing of family members: patriarchs (once called 'adn), sons called heads (b'lm), male servants... followed by daughters and women."

## Evidence

- CAT 4.360 lists multiple families: "The sons of B'LN of BIRY: Three heads. And their master Bull, and his four daughters. YRḪM (together) with his two sons, two heads, and three retainers and one daughter..." — same structure as mythological divine household.
- In the first family, 'adn refers to patriarch; his three married sons are b'lm ("heads"). Identical usage in myths: El is 'adn (1.1 IV 17), Baal is b'l.
- Females listed in three classes (sisters, daughters, women) — same categories in myths: Anat as Baal's sister (1.4 IV 39) and El's daughter (1.3 IV 27); Pidray, Arsay, Tallay as Baal's women (1.3 IV 40) and daughters (1.3 I 24) and brides (1.3 VI 15).
- Royal letters use b'l for social superior and 'adn for the father of the writer. The king's titulary (7.63.4) calls the king "upright lord" (b'l ṣdq).
- "In ideological terms, the king is at once the patriarchal provider and protector; he is judge and father to the society, as well as the warrior who battles on its behalf."

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** This is a genuinely important empirical finding — documentary evidence, not just literary interpretation.

## Stakes

This grounds Smith's argument in documentary evidence, not just literary interpretation. If the pantheon reflects actual Ugaritic social structure, then changes in the pantheon (convergence, collapse) may reflect changes in social structure — which is exactly Smith's argument about the neo-Assyrian crisis.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Probably few on the factual claim; the terminology overlap is clear. Disagreement would be on whether this means the divine household WAS society's self-representation or merely used convenient social language.

**Alternative reading:** The shared terminology could reflect scribal convention (administrative scribes and myth-scribes using the same vocabulary) rather than a deep structural homology. The myths might use household language because that's the available vocabulary, not because the pantheon IS a household.

## Assessment

This is excellent evidence. The CAT 4.360 comparison is Smith's strongest empirical move in Part I. Even if one is skeptical about "the pantheon IS a household," it's undeniable that the Ugaritic texts consistently USE household language to describe divine relations. That's a fact about the texts, not an interpretation. The question is whether the language is structural or metaphorical. Smith's answer (structural) is well-supported by the systematic nature of the parallels.
