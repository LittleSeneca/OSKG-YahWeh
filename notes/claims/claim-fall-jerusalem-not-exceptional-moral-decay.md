# kaufmann-ri-13.1: The fall of Jerusalem was not caused by exceptional moral decay

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XIII — The Prophecy of the Fall]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XIII

## The Claim

"Jerusalem was not demoralized, did not suffer a failure of nerve, did not lack the will to fight. On the contrary, little Judah armed with an ardent faith went out boldly to battle the Babylonian Goliath." Judah was not exceptionally corrupt — "nothing suggests that the extent of Judah's corruption was abnormal; it was surely no more than that of its conqueror."

## Evidence

- The people withstood a long siege; the "wicked king" and officers were "faith and fortitude incarnate"
- Only Jeremiah raised a voice against resistance — "who cannot at all be counted among the 'sinners'"
- By the late 7th century, the dispossession of masses had been resolved — a new industrial class had consolidated
- Jeremiah's treatment proves the society was not morally bankrupt: he called for desertion to the enemy "and yet lived; surely nowhere in oriental antiquity but in 'sinful' Jerusalem could this have happened"
- Officers saved him, feared to shed blood, threw him in a pit rather than kill him
- The doctrine of Judah's sin was produced by "the inner necessity of religious faith, of theodicy" — not historical fact
- Even Lamentations, from the pen of Jeremiah's opponents, doesn't know of widespread idolatry

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann makes a genuinely interesting case that the prophetic rhetoric of sin has been mistaken for historical reportage. His observation that Judah was militarily resilient is striking. But he understates the biblical evidence for social dissolution in Jeremiah's own oracles (oppression of poor, corruption of priests and prophets).

## Stakes

Whether the exile was a punishment for genuine national sin (the biblical view) or a geopolitical event retrospectively theologized. If Kaufmann is right, the entire prophetic enterprise was not about moral reform of a uniquely corrupt nation but about interpreting geopolitical catastrophe through Torah categories.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The prophets themselves. Jeremiah's indictment (Jer 5-7) is comprehensive. Römer reads the prophetic denunciations as evidence of real social crisis. Albertz ties prophetic critique to specific social changes under the monarchy.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that Judah wasn't uniquely corrupt by ancient Near Eastern standards, and right that theodicy drives the biblical interpretation. But he overcorrects — there WAS real social disintegration under the late monarchy that the prophets responded to, even if their theological framing amplified it.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's strongest point is the treatment of Jeremiah by his enemies — if Jerusalem were truly a den of wickedness, the prophet who calls for surrender wouldn't have survived. This is a genuinely sharp observation. But his claim that morality actually IMPROVED since the 8th century prophets is speculative and contradicts the trajectory suggested by the biblical texts themselves.
