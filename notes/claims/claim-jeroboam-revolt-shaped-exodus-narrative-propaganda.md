# albertz-hir-3.3-3.4.4: Jeroboam's revolt shaped the Exodus narrative

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.3-3.4 — State Cults of Jerusalem and Bethel]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.4

## The Claim

"It is highly probable that the battle against Solomon's forced labour by Jeroboam and the northern tribes was fought with an appeal to the liberation of their forefathers from Egyptian forced labour, and that these old religious reminiscences took their first narrative form from the contemporary experiences of Jeroboam's revolt."

## Evidence

- Striking parallels between the Moses story (Ex 2:11-15; 5*) and Jeroboam's revolt (1 Kings 11-12): both are men from royal circles who show solidarity with oppressed countrymen; both strike an overseer and flee abroad; both return after the king's death; both negotiate with the successor, whose demands are heightened; both achieve liberation by departure from the oppressor's sphere of power
- Ex 5:16: the Israelite overseers accuse Pharaoh of having "sinned against his own people" — polemically identifying Solomon/Rehoboam with Pharaoh
- "The narrative Ex.5...3,4*,6-19 was once a propaganda text from the struggle against the forced labour imposed by Solomon or Rehoboam"
- Jeroboam's movement "was the first instance in which an individual prophet with no institutional ties intervened to destabilize an existing political rule" — Ahijah of Shiloh's oracle (1 Kings 11:29-39) supported the revolt

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** The parallels are real but could be literary convention rather than historical causation. The claim that Ex 5* was Jeroboam-era propaganda is creative but speculative.

## Stakes

This is a major claim about the formation of the Exodus tradition itself — that it was shaped (perhaps even created) by 10th-century anti-Solomonic politics. If true, the Exodus narrative is not ancient memory but political propaganda from the early monarchy. This would be a significant contribution to Pentateuchal source criticism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date the Exodus narrative earlier (pre-monarchic oral tradition) or later (exilic composition). Mainstream Pentateuchal criticism would place the composition of Exodus centuries later than Albertz proposes.

**Alternative reading:** The parallels could run the other direction — Jeroboam deliberately modeled his revolt on the Exodus tradition because it was already well-known. Or both narratives could draw on common literary tropes (the hero's flight and return) rather than one shaping the other.

## Edges

**Depends on:**
- [[claim-rebel-movements-anti-domination-yahwism-against-monarchy]] — Jeroboam's revolt is the culmination of the anti-monarchical resistance tradition; the Exodus narrative was its theological weapon
- [[claim-monarchy-social-transformations-contradicted-pre-state-yahwism]] — the forced labor (mas) that Jeroboam rebelled against is the specific social transformation the Exodus narrative polemicizes

**Supports:**
- [[claim-jeroboam-bethel-cult-conservative-archaizing-bull-pedestal]] — the Exodus-shaped revolt produced an Exodus-oriented cult at Bethel (the cult cry: "who brought you out of Egypt")

**Contradicts:**
- [[claim-exodus-group-small-declassed-mixed-forced-laborers]] — Albertz's own earlier claim that the Exodus tradition originated with a small historical group; here he argues it was shaped by 10th-century politics — the two claims are in tension
- [[claim-yahweh-liberation-origin-structural-contrast-family-piety]] — Albertz's earlier claim that liberation-origin predates the monarchy; if the Exodus narrative was shaped by Jeroboam's revolt, the liberation character may be a 10th-century retrojection

**Challenged by:**
- Mainstream Pentateuchal criticism dating Exodus to the exilic or post-exilic period
- The possibility that the literary parallels are conventional, not causal

**Primary sources:**
- Ex 2:11-15; 5*
- 1 Kings 11-12

## Assessment

This is Albertz's most speculative claim in this batch, and it creates a tension with his own earlier claims about the Exodus group's historical experience. If the Exodus narrative was shaped by 10th-century politics, how much of it preserves genuine pre-monarchic memory? Albertz seems to want both: the Exodus group's historical experience AND the narrative's political shaping under Jeroboam. The parallels between the Moses story and Jeroboam's revolt are real but the causal direction is underdetermined — they could be literary convention, Jeroboam modeling himself on Moses, or later redactors shaping both narratives.
