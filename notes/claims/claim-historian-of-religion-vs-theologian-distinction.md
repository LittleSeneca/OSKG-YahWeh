# lewis-ocg-1.1: The historian of religion's approach differs fundamentally from the theologian's

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 1 — Introductory Matters]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"My approach is that of a historian of religion rather than that of a theologian. Specifically, I write as a historian of the religion of ancient Israel as it is understood within its ancient Near Eastern context." Lewis emphasizes his training in "Near Eastern languages and civilizations (not theology, as close readers will see)."

## Evidence

Lewis doesn't argue this point with evidence; he asserts it as his disciplinary identity and methodological positioning. The evidence is in the book's execution: its heavy reliance on epigraphic data, iconographic analysis, archaeological reports, and comparative ancient Near Eastern texts alongside (not subordinate to) the Hebrew Bible.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that this is Lewis's actual approach. The question is whether the distinction is as clean as he claims — every scholar makes interpretive choices shaped by unstated philosophical commitments.

## Stakes

Whether the volume can claim an Archimedean point outside confessional commitments. If Lewis smuggles in theological assumptions without acknowledging them, his "historian of religion" stance is partly rhetorical.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Confessional scholars (Heiser, Waltke, Provan) would argue that bracketing theology distorts the subject matter — ancient Israelites understood their religion *theologically*. More theoretically sophisticated scholars (Asad, McCutcheon) might argue Lewis's stance is still too Protestant in its assumptions about belief over practice.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis is making a pragmatic claim, not a philosophical one. He means: "I will not assume the truth of biblical claims, and I will use the full toolkit of historical-critical scholarship."

## Assessment

Lewis is on solid ground for his target audience (scholars of ancient Near Eastern religion, not seminarians). The real test is whether he maintains this stance consistently — especially in Chapter 10 on holiness, where the temptation to slip into theological description is strongest. His insistence is more useful as a signal of method than as a claim about disciplinary purity.
