# sommer-bog-5-6.2: The kabbalistic *sefirot* are a structural rediscovery of ANE fluidity within monotheism

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapters 5-6 + Appendix]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 6

## The Claim

The kabbalistic doctrine of the ten *sefirot* — emanations/manifestations of the infinite God (*Ein Sof*) — represents a rediscovery of the ancient fluidity model. The *sefirot* are distinct from each other and from *Ein Sof*, yet they ARE God. Each *sefirah* is a particular aspect of divinity: Wisdom, Understanding, Mercy, Judgment, etc. They interact with each other, flow into each other, and form a dynamic system. This is structurally identical to the ANE conception of gods with overlapping selves and multiple local manifestations — but kabbalah is rigorously monotheistic. The *sefirot* are not "gods" but divine modalities within the one God.

"The kabbalists rediscovered the old idea, though probably without realizing what they were doing."

Kabbalah was controversial precisely because it revived a theology that mainstream rabbinic Judaism had suppressed. Maimonides' principle of divine incorporeality ("God has no body, no form") had become orthodoxy. The *sefirot* — divine bodies, divine modalities, divine multiplicity within unity — challenged that orthodoxy by recovering the fluidity model the rabbis had rejected.

## Evidence

- **Structural parallel:** ANE gods had multiple local manifestations with partial independence (Ishtar of Arbela/Nineveh, Baal of Saphon/Ugarit). The *sefirot* have distinct identities (Ḥokhmah, Binah, Ḥesed, Gevurah) while being manifestations of the one *Ein Sof*.
- **Interaction:** ANE divine manifestations interacted with each other — the *sefirot* flow into each other and form a dynamic system of mutual influence.
- **Unity-in-multiplicity:** Both models maintain that the multiple manifestations ARE the one deity, not separate beings.
- **Historical gap:** Kabbalah emerges in medieval Europe, over a millennium after the ANE fluidity model was suppressed by P, D, and the rabbis. The recovery was almost certainly independent, not a direct borrowing.
- **Controversy as evidence:** The rabbinic opposition to kabbalah confirms that it was reviving a theology the mainstream had rejected — the "scandal" of kabbalah was its challenge to anti-fluid orthodoxy.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The structural parallel is compelling, but whether kabbalists were consciously recovering ANE patterns is uncertain (Sommer says probably not); the claim is strong as a typological comparison, weaker as a historical-genealogical one.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right, Jewish mysticism is not an alien import but a recovery of Israel's own suppressed theological inheritance. Kabbalah becomes less exotic and more biblical — a legitimate development of possibilities present in J/E but rejected by P/D. This reframes the history of Jewish theology: anti-fluid monotheism (Maimonides) and fluid monotheism (kabbalah) are both authentic, competing developments of Israel's biblical inheritance.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read kabbalah as primarily influenced by Neoplatonic emanationism rather than recovering internal biblical patterns. Historians who see the *sefirot* as a medieval innovation with no meaningful connection to ANE religion.

**Alternative reading:** The *sefirot* are a product of medieval Neoplatonism, not a rediscovery of ANE patterns. The structural parallels are coincidental — any system of divine emanations will resemble ANE fluidity because both are addressing similar theological problems (how the infinite relates to the finite).

## Assessment

This is Sommer's most creative move — and his most speculative. The structural parallel between ANE fluidity and kabbalistic *sefirot* is genuinely striking, but the historical connection is unproven. Sommer's argument works better as a typology (both are solutions to the same theological problem) than as a genealogy (kabbalah recovers ANE patterns). The claim is valuable for the Truth Project because it shows that the "primitive" fluidity model is not merely a stage to be outgrown — it's a live theological option that re-emerges whenever monotheists try to account for divine presence in a world that God also transcends.
