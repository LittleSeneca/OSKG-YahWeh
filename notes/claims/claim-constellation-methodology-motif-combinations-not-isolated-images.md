# keel-ggi-1.4: The methodology requires analyzing "constellations" of motifs, not isolated images — Assmann's iconicity theory adapted to Levantine material

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch I — Starting Point and Points of Departure]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter I

## The Claim

Building on Jan Assmann, they argue that iconographic interpretation must focus on "constellations" — recurring combinations of motifs that represent stable complexes of ideas — rather than hunting for specific myths behind images. "Mythic texts are nothing other than icons (or else constellations) that have been presented in story form." The battle-against-the-dragon constellation appears with different divine names (Baal/Yamm, Marduk/Tiamat, Yahweh/Leviathan) but the underlying ICON is constant.

## Evidence

- Assmann's distinction between "myth" (narrative) and "constellation" (pre-narrative complex of ideas)
- The "iconic constancy" principle: "The myth owes its 'iconic constancy' to a few basic constellations"
- Examples: divine couple = fertility; serpent/dragon combat = order vs. chaos; nursing mother = divine provision
- Different image-bearing media have different capacities

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Assmann's theory is well-established in Egyptology. The adaptation to Levantine material is original but methodologically sound.

## Stakes

This is the theoretical heart of the book. Without the constellation method, they're just cataloguing images. With it, they can make claims about religious CONCEPTS from visual patterns.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Text-focused scholars might argue that this method over-interprets visual evidence — that a winged sun disk on a seal doesn't necessarily mean the owner "worshipped" a solar deity.

**Alternative reading:** Some images may be purely conventional — seal-cutters reproducing standard patterns without theological investment.
