# keel-ggi-10.6: The deuteronomistic suppression of Canaanite religion is structurally parallel to Christian anti-Judaism — and both traditions need to confront this

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch X — Summary and Conclusion]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter X

## The Claim

"Judean religion encountered a situation in the seventh and sixth centuries before Christ that was comparable to Christian anti-Judaism... It believed that it could establish its own identity only by outlawing everything Canaanite and by calling for the destruction of such items as the holy stones... that their ancestors had once erected." "Part of the tragic history of the Judeo-Christian history of guilt is the fact that the people who later became the victims of the holocaust reported in a central passage in their own religious writings that a major preoccupation of theirs was the elimination of other groups in order to ensure the purity and sanctity of their own religious community."

## Evidence

- Deuteronomy 7:2-6, 16, 24-26 — the command to destroy Canaanite cult objects
- The "holy stones" (masseboth) that the ancestors erected (Gen 28:18, 22) became "Canaanite" and were outlawed (Deut 7:5; 12:5)
- The pattern: a religious community defines its identity by vilifying and destroying what came before, then later becomes the victim of the same dynamic
- The comparison to Christian anti-Judaism (1 Thess 2:15; John 8:42-44) and the Holocaust

## Confidence

**Rating:** debatable

**Rationale:** This is a moral-theological claim, not a historical one. As historical analysis, the comparison between deuteronomistic suppression of Canaanite religion and Christian anti-Judaism is provocative but debatable. As a theological confession from within the tradition, it is powerful.

## Stakes

This is the ethical payoff of the entire book. If the iconographic evidence shows that Canaanite religion was not a foreign corruption but the ROOT SYSTEM of Israelite religion, then the deuteronomistic polemic against "Canaanite" practices was a self-mutilation — a tradition cutting off its own roots and calling them weeds. The authors are calling for the tradition to acknowledge this.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditionalists who maintain the deuteronomistic distinction between "pure" Yahwism and "corrupt" Canaanite religion. Also Jewish scholars who might object to the comparison between deuteronomistic polemic and Christian anti-Judaism as unfair or anachronistic.

**Alternative reading:** The deuteronomists were not suppressing their own roots — they were purifying a tradition that had genuinely been corrupted by syncretism. The "Canaanite" practices they opposed were not ancestral Israelite religion but foreign imports from Phoenicia and Assyria.

## Assessment

This conclusion is remarkably honest for scholars working in a Catholic university context (Fribourg). They are not just describing the evidence — they are drawing moral consequences from it. The comparison to the Holocaust is handled with care (they call it "relatively speaking, a less harmful fall into sin") but it is still striking. This is what distinguishes Keel & Uehlinger from purely descriptive scholarship: they have a theological stake in the outcome.
