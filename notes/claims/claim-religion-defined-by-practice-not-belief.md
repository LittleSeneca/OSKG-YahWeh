# dever-dghw-1.1: Religion must be defined by what people DO, not what they believe

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter I — Defining Religion]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter I

## The Claim

Most works on Israelite religion fail even to define "religion." Dever adopts Hans Penner's functional definition: "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." This focuses on PRACTICE — the observable, material, archaeological dimension of religion — rather than theology or belief. The definition is deliberately minimalist and behavioral: it says nothing about God, faith, salvation, or morality. It simply describes what people DO in relation to superhuman beings. For an archaeologist, this is the only workable definition — you can dig up practices; you cannot dig up beliefs.

## Evidence

- **Penner's definition:** "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." Simple, functional, focused on practice. No theology required.
- **Survey of definitions:** Dever surveys definitions from Tylor, Frazer, Durkheim, Evans-Pritchard, Douglas, Geertz, James, Marx, Eliade, and others. Notes that Zevit is the rare exception among biblical scholars who attempts a definition at all.
- **The methodological necessity:** If religion is defined by belief, it is invisible to archaeology. If defined by practice — sacrifices, offerings, figurines, standing stones, altars, tomb deposits — it is directly observable in the material record.
- **Theological definitions fail:** Definitions that require belief in a specific god or creed exclude most of what people actually did in the ancient world and are useless for comparative study.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that this is a useful operational definition for an archaeological study. MEDIUM that it captures all dimensions of Israelite religion (it deliberately excludes theology).

## Stakes

If Dever is right about the definition, then most scholarship on Israelite religion has been studying the wrong thing — the theology of a literate elite rather than the practiced religion of the population. The definitional question is not academic; it determines what counts as evidence and whose religion gets studied.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Theologians and text-based scholars who define religion primarily by belief, creed, and canonical texts. They would argue that practice without belief is empty ritual, and that the Hebrew Bible's theological content IS the religion.

**Alternative reading:** A definition of religion should include both practice AND belief, recognizing that material remains and texts provide complementary access to different dimensions of religious life.
