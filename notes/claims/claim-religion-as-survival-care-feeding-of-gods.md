# dever-dghw-intro.3: Religion in ancient Israel was about survival — "the care and feeding of the gods"

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife? Archaeology and Folk Religion in Ancient Israel* (2005), Introduction

## The Claim

Ancient Israelite religion was not about theology, morality, or "spirituality" in the modern sense. It was about survival. In a marginal economy with 30-year life expectancy, unpredictable rainfall, and constant threat of famine, religion addressed the most basic human needs: food, health, fertility, protection from disaster. "The 'care and feeding of the gods' — sacrifices, offerings, rituals — was the practical strategy." Dever adopts Hans Penner's definition: "Religion is a verbal and nonverbal structure of interaction with superhuman being(s)." Simple, functional, focused on PRACTICE.

## Evidence

**The three ultimate concerns:**
1. **Survival:** "They faced the ultimate threat: extinction... Israelites could not comfort themselves with the much later (and non-Semitic) notion of the 'immortality of the soul.' When the body died, that was the end. Religion thus had first of all to deal with the problem of survival, in the most brutal, elementary sense."
2. **Aligning with the universe:** "It was essential to 'personalize' the numinous powers that ultimately ruled the universe and to 'get on their side.'" Even the gods could kill you for no reason. "Living in antiquity was being 'religious.'"
3. **Placating the deities:** Sacrifices as gifts — "give back to the gods a token portion of what they had graciously given." This was the practical mechanism. It "worked — or so it appeared when things were going well."

**Dever on the brutality of ancient religion:**
"Ancient religion was, as the anthropologist Eilberg-Schwartz puts it, rather messy. It was in fact 'savage' — a brutal, often bloody, life-and-death struggle, the outcome of which was by no means certain. The modern, idealistic, romantic portrait of ancient Israelite religion is a comfortable delusion, but one that obscures the reality."

"Such a pragmatic definition of religion may seem to us primitive, even debased, as though religion were simply 'magic.' But that's precisely what religion is, or at least was, however much that may offend modern sensibilities."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that ancient religion was pragmatic and survival-oriented (this is the anthropological consensus). MEDIUM that Dever's characterization captures ISRAELITE religion specifically (the Bible does contain moral and theological dimensions that Dever minimizes for rhetorical effect).

## Stakes

If ancient Israelite religion was primarily about survival — making it rain, having children, not dying of disease — then the Bible's moral and theological content was the concern of a tiny literate elite, not the lived religion of most people. The God of the Bible was, for most Israelites, a god you placated with sacrifices to keep your family alive, not a god you loved with all your heart and soul.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who emphasize the theological distinctiveness of Israelite religion (Kaufmann) would reject this characterization as ignoring what made Israel unique.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible itself contains both strands — the pragmatic/sacrificial and the moral/theological. Dever emphasizes the former because it's what archaeology reveals; the latter is what texts reveal. Both are real; the question is which was more representative of MOST people's experience.

## Assessment

Dever overstates for effect. The Bible contains genuine moral and theological reflection alongside the sacrificial system. But he's right that the material evidence — figurines, altars, cult stands — points toward pragmatic, survival-oriented religion. This is a useful corrective to the overly theological readings of Israelite religion that dominate biblical scholarship.
