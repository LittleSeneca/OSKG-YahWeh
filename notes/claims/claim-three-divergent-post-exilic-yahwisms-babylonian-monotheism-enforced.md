# keel-ggi-7.4: The exile produced three divergent Judean Yahwisms — Babylonian monotheism, Egyptian syncretism, and Palestinian popular religion — and the returning Babylonian elite imposed exclusivity by force, with goddess worship as the primary target

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VII — Iron Age III]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VII, §222, 227

## The Claim

"The complexity of the religious history of Judah during Iron Age III is increased by the need to take into account the religious traditions of the groups that were dislocated when they were exiled to Babylon and Egypt, as well as for those who remained in Judah. The three groups lived in different political, social, and cultural contexts, which affected the Judean-Jewish religious concepts in different ways." The exile produced not one but three trajectories: (1) Babylonian Diaspora — monotheism, with Deutero-Isaiah's mocking polemic against graven images; (2) Egyptian Diaspora (Elephantine) — syncretism, worshipping Yahweh alongside a female deity Anat; (3) Those who remained in Judah — popular religion, continuing Iron Age IIC practices including figurines. The returning Babylonian elite then enforced monotheism by political means — Haggai excluded non-exiles from temple restoration, Zechariah personified evil as a woman, and the Chronicler erased all traces of goddesses. The decisive target was not Baal but the goddess.

## Evidence

**The three groups** (§222):
1. **Babylonian Diaspora — Monotheism**: "The credal tenet of monotheism (the monotheism of Deutero-Isaiah is hardly to be taken as independent from the Achaemenian worship of Ahuramazda), and the concomitant, mocking polemic against the production of so-called graven images... is stated explicitly for the first time." The exile gave the elite "an opportunity to collect and reformulate the religious traditions of the motherland" under "priestly" and "deuteronomistic" schools.
2. **Egyptian Diaspora (Elephantine) — Syncretism**: The Jewish military colony at Elephantine "demonstrated that their version of the religion had 'syncretistic' tendencies. Certainly they followed the Yahwistic El-traditions of Iron Age IIC, but, apparently following the lead of some colonists from Phoenicia concerning the partner/consort issue, they recognized and acknowledged alongside Yahweh a female deity, Anat." This is "merely a variant form of the 'Late-Canaanite' religion that was common throughout the Levant and that we encounter in Hurvat Qitmit."
3. **Those who remained in Judah — Popular Religion**: The returnees' exclusion of the non-exiled from temple restoration (Hag 2:10–14) and the conflicts over leadership "allow us to surmise that the Jewish women and men who had remained in Palestine had continued to follow the popular religious ideas and practices from Iron Age IIC."

**The enforcement of orthodoxy** (§227):
- **Zechariah's seventh vision** (Zech 5:5–11): Personifies "everything 'evil' in the form of a woman(!)" transported in a closed vessel to Babylon — "lumps foreign cults and goddess worship together."
- **Haggai's exclusion** (Hag 2:10–14): The leadership around Zerubbabel and high priest Joshua excluded those who had not been exiled from helping restore the temple. "They particularly had their sights set on stopping all worship of the goddess."
- **The Chronicler's erasure**: In the 4th century, the Chronicler "sought to eliminate all traces of the goddesses (Astarte and most especially Asherah)... from its own 'worldview.'" Tellingly, the Chronicler was less zealous about Baal or Dagan — "the decisive question at the time of the Chronicler was no longer about foreign cults and gods. The dangerous issues concerned the existence of the goddess."
- **Wisdom as the goddess's vestige**: "Only in postexilic theological speculation about wisdom was it possible, within a monotheistic-Yahwistic viewpoint about God, to think explicitly about personified wisdom as a way to show an explicitly female dimension of God. Patterned on contemporary worship of Isis, through the use of some metaphorical footprints, the heritage of the 'Canaanite' Asherah lived on."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The three-group framework is well-established in biblical scholarship (Albertz, Römer agree on the basic division). The Elephantine papyri are unambiguous about Anat-Yahu. The Haggai/Zechariah/Chronicler evidence for goddess-targeted polemic is textually clear.

## Stakes

This reframes the monotheism question. It wasn't a gradual evolution — it was a *political victory* by one faction (the Babylonian returnees) over two others (the Egyptian syncretists and the Palestinian popular religionists). Monotheism was enforced, not naturally emergent. And the *primary target* of that enforcement was the goddess. The authors explicitly note that Baal and Dagan were less threatening than Asherah/Astarte — the real battle was over whether Yahweh had a female counterpart.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some scholars (e.g., Smith) emphasize Persian imperial authorization of local cults as the mechanism — the Torah was compiled under Persian auspices as the "law of the God of Heaven" for Yehud. In this reading, monotheism was as much Persian policy as Jewish theology. Keel & Uehlinger hint at this (the Ahuramazda parallel) but don't develop it.

**Alternative reading:** The Chronicler's erasure of Asherah could reflect the passage of time rather than active suppression — the goddess had simply faded from memory.

## Assessment

The three-group model is the single most important framework for understanding Iron Age III religion. It explains why the biblical texts are so internally conflicted about the goddess: they were written by the winners of a factional fight. The Elephantine evidence is especially valuable because it's extrabiblical — it shows what Yahwism looked like when the deuteronomistic editors *weren't* in control. Keel & Uehlinger's insight that the goddess (not Baal) was the primary target of the orthodox campaign is profound and underappreciated in the broader literature.
