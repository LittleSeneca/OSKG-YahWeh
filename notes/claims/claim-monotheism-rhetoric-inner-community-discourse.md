# smith-obm-8.3: Monotheistic statements are RHETORICAL — they serve inner-community discourse, not a new religious stage

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch8 — Emergence of Monotheistic Rhetoric]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 8

## The Claim

This is the chapter's core thesis: "Monotheistic statements do not herald a new age of religion but explain Yahwistic monolatry in absolute terms. As rhetoric, monotheism reinforced Israel's exclusive relationship with its deity. Monotheism is a kind of inner community discourse establishing a distance from outsiders."

## Evidence

- "Because of the post-biblical importance of monotheism, the relative rarity of its expression in the Bible is quite striking." The few instances are "spread over the whole of the so-called Deuteronomistic History" — they are NOT concentrated in a "monotheistic revolution" moment.
- "Israelites continued to worship deities other than Yahweh both before and possibly after the exile." The ruling priestly groups maintained "a Yahwistic monolatry expressed in its rhetoric of monotheism, but such a historical conclusion does not justify claims for an entirely 'monotheistic culture.'"
- "Because of the relative rarity of monotheistic claims and the ongoing presence of polytheism in ancient Israel, no one can confirm a clear evolution from monarchic monolatry...to a new stage of religion called monotheism."
- Monotheism "uses the language of Yahweh's exceptional divine status beyond and in all reality ('there are no other deities but the Lord') to absolutize Yahweh's claim on Israel and to express Israel's ultimate fidelity to Yahweh."
- Seitz quote (which Smith fully endorses): "This is not a sublime monotheism capable of differentiation from a more concrete henotheism — rather it is henotheism of a particularly potent stripe. The other elohim that continue to claim allegiance from humanity have detachable names and detachable existences — to the degree that YHWH insists that they do not exist at all."
- Monotheistic statements "attempted to persuade Judeans still unconvinced of this perspective." They may represent "the efforts of a minority of 'monotheists' to persuade a majority of Judeans who held Yahweh as the head of a larger group of divinities."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is Smith's finest conceptual move in the entire book. The distinction between "new stage of religion" and "rhetoric serving monolatry" fundamentally reframes the entire debate.

## Stakes

If monotheism is rhetoric, not a new stage, then:
1. The "evolution" model (polytheism → henotheism → monotheism) is wrong
2. Monotheism and monolatry coexisted — monotheism is what monolatry SOUNDS LIKE when pushed to rhetorical extremity
3. The question shifts from "when did Israelites become monotheists?" to "under what conditions did Judeans START USING monotheistic rhetoric?" — a much more productive question

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Anyone who sees monotheism as a genuine theological breakthrough or a new conceptual stage (evolutionary models). Scholars who take the monotheistic statements at face value as describing Israelite belief.

**Alternative reading:** The fact that monotheistic language is RARE doesn't mean it's "just rhetoric" — minority positions can be genuine innovations. And "rhetoric" implies insincerity in a way Smith may not intend.

## Assessment

This is the best version of Smith's argument. The Seitz quote is the pithiest formulation: monotheism is "henotheism of a particularly potent stripe." Smith is NOT saying monotheism is fake — he's saying it's a *way of talking* that serves and reinforces an exclusive relationship, not a *way of thinking* that replaces an older worldview. The minority-persuasion model (monotheists trying to convince a polytheistic majority) is also productive — it makes the rhetoric *functional*, not decorative.
