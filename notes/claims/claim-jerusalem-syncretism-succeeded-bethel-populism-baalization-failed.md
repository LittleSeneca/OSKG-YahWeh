# albertz-hir-3.3-3.4.6: Jerusalem's syncretism succeeded; Bethel's populism led to Baalization and failure

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.3-3.4 — State Cults of Jerusalem and Bethel]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.4

## The Claim

"Whereas the El syncretism of Jerusalem, more strictly controlled by the state, succeeded, the El syncretism of Bethel, which was close to the people, ended in complete disaster." The bull's ambiguous symbolism — power AND fertility — made it vulnerable. "At least in the view of the people... the theological differentiation between Yahweh and his cultic pedestal was lost and the bull image was identified directly with Yahweh." Hosea saw it as "a 'Baalized' Yahweh cult, which had forgotten its historical origins."

## Evidence

- Jerusalem's syncretism was controlled: Yahweh's identity was maintained through theology (Sabaoth, creator, king), and the cult was hidden behind walls and priestly mediation
- Bethel's syncretism was populist: the image was visible, accessible, and the bull's fertility associations (Baal, El's sexual potency) overwhelmed its Exodus associations
- Hosea's polemic (Hos 8:6; 10:5; 13:2) shows the bull had become identified with Yahweh directly
- Hosea's verdict provided the legitimation for Josiah's destruction of Bethel (2 Kings 23:15-18)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The contrast between state-controlled and populist syncretism is analytically sharp. But was Bethel really more "Baalized" than Jerusalem, or did the Deuteronomistic polemic (which was Jerusalem-centered) simply succeed in discrediting the Northern cult while protecting its own?

## Stakes

This is Albertz's explanation for why the Northern Kingdom's religion was condemned and the Southern Kingdom's was (relatively) preserved — not because the North was more syncretistic but because its syncretism was populist and thus uncontrolled. The winners (Jerusalem) wrote the history.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see Jerusalem as equally syncretistic (Asherah, Shemesh, Zedek in the temple). The Deuteronomistic editors were Jerusalem-based — their condemnation of Bethel may reflect political bias, not theological assessment.

**Alternative reading:** The "failure" of Bethel may be an artifact of the southern/Jerusalem perspective that dominates the OT. From a Northern perspective, Bethel was the legitimate Yahwistic sanctuary preserving Exodus traditions, and Jerusalem was the syncretistic innovation. The difference is that Jerusalem's party won the editorial war.

## Assessment

The contrast is analytically sharp but normatively loaded. Albertz essentially says: Jerusalem's syncretism succeeded because it was elitist (controlled by priests and hidden from the people); Bethel's failed because it was democratic (visible and accessible). This is a politically uncomfortable argument — it suggests that theological orthodoxy requires elite control and priestly mediation. The alternative reading — that Bethel's "failure" is an artifact of Jerusalem's editorial victory — is more plausible: we simply don't have Northern texts to compare. The winners wrote the history, and the winners were from Jerusalem.
