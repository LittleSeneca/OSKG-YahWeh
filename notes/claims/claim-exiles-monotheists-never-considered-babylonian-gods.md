# kaufmann-ri-13.8: The exiles were monotheists who never considered worshipping Babylonian gods

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XIII — The Prophecy of the Fall]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XIII

## The Claim

Despite Ezekiel's rhetoric, the exiles never entertained genuine pagan belief. "The notion that Israelite monotheism was threatened by a popular belief in the superiority of Babylonia's gods over YHWH... finds no support in the Bible." Ezekiel 20:32-44 is "monumental testimony to the real state of affairs."

## Evidence

- Ezekiel 20:32: The exiles who wish to "be like the nations" don't say "Let us accept Marduk, Ishtar, Adad." They say "let us serve wood and stone"
- "This is not faith in new gods, it is no faith at all. This is the desperate counsel of men whose spirit is broken"
- "Ezekiel never issues a demand that they remove idols from their midst; he never upbraids them for entering pagan temples, or celebrating pagan festivals"
- "He has not one word of polemic against mythological beliefs"
- The exiles "raised a generation loyal to YHWH, out of whom came those who returned to Zion"
- "No exilic prophet fights the idea that Jerusalem's fall is due to the pagan gods"
- "Neither side believed that any but YHWH would carry out the destruction"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is a genuinely interesting reading of Ezekiel 20, and the observation about the absence of polemic against Babylonian mythology is striking. But it's an argument from silence, and the archaeological/epigraphic evidence for syncretism at Elephantine and elsewhere complicates the picture.

## Stakes

Whether the exile was a theological crisis (Marduk defeated YHWH?) or a crisis of faith in the covenant (why did YHWH abandon us?). Kaufmann says it was the latter — the exiles never doubted YHWH's power, only his favor.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the exile as generating genuine theological crisis. Römer argues the exile forced monotheism to develop in response to the apparent defeat of the national god. Smith sees Second Isaiah's monotheism as a creative response to this crisis.

**Alternative reading:** The absence of explicit polemic against Babylonian gods could reflect the editors' priorities, not the exiles' beliefs. And the exiles who DID assimilate wouldn't appear in the biblical record — survivorship bias. Kaufmann's reading of Ezekiel 20 is perceptive but the text could also be interpreted as Ezekiel's polemical CARICATURE of the exiles' position.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's reading of Ezekiel 20:32-44 is genuinely illuminating. The exiles don't say "Marduk is stronger" — they say "let's be like everyone else and worship wood and stone." That IS a different thing. But I suspect the reality was messier than either Kaufmann or the critical consensus allows: some exiles assimilated fully (and vanished from the record), some maintained YHWH worship alongside Babylonian practice, and some (the ones who produced the biblical texts) maintained exclusive YHWH worship. Kaufmann takes the last group as representative of the whole, which is the same move he makes throughout the book.
