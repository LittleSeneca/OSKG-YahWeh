# fleming-ybi-summary.4: Early Yahweh was a people-god like South Arabian Almaqah and 'Amm

**Source:** [[Fleming — Book Summary]] — Daniel E. Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021)

## The Claim

Fleming proposes that the earliest character of Yahweh is best understood through analogy with South Arabian political gods: Almaqah (god of Saba) and 'Amm (god of Qataban). These gods bound communities through kinship — the "Descendants of 'Amm" and "Descendants of Almaqah" were designations for entire peoples. This is a different model of divinity from the Canaanite storm god (Baal), the patriarchal deity (El), or the warrior god (Yahweh's later profile). In this model, Yahweh was originally defined by his identification with a specific community (the people of Yahweh), not by attributes (storm, war, fertility) or kinship relations (father, king, husband). The Moabite "people of Kemosh" (Num 21:29) provides a closer parallel in the Levant. Yahweh only later acquired the trappings of Baal (storm warrior), merged with El (patriarchal father), and became the sole God of Israel.

## Evidence

- South Arabian inscriptions designate entire peoples as "Descendants of Almaqah" (Saba) and "Descendants of 'Amm" (Qataban) — the god defines the community
- The pattern is one where gods bind communities through kinship, with the king latching onto pre-existing divine-community relationships
- Numbers 21:29 refers to Moab as the "people of Kemosh" — the same pattern closer to Israel
- The "people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh) in Judges 5:13 follows this structural pattern: a community defined by its god, without reference to a state or monarchy
- This community-binding god model contrasts with the Baal-type storm/warrior god and the El-type patriarchal/family god

## Confidence

**Rating:** low-medium

**Rationale:** This is the most exploratory argument in the book. The South Arabian analogies are under-studied in Yahweh-origins scholarship, making the comparison suggestive but not yet well-tested. The geographical and chronological distance between South Arabia and early Israel requires bridging arguments that Fleming acknowledges need development.

## Stakes

If Yahweh was originally a people-binding god (like Almaqah and 'Amm), then the search for Yahweh's origins has been looking for the wrong kind of deity. Scholars have tried to find a storm god, a mountain god, a warrior god, or a patriarch — but Yahweh's earliest profile may have been none of these. He was a god defined by communal identification, and only later acquired the attributes (storm, war, kingship) that scholars have used to trace his origins. This reframes the entire debate: the question is not "what kind of god was Yahweh?" but "who were the people of Yahweh, and how did their community-binding god become the sole God of Israel?"

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The storm-god model (Römer, Smith, Cross — Yahweh as southern mountain storm god), the warrior-god model (Lewis — Yahweh as divine warrior from the southern periphery), and the El-convergence model (Smith — Yahweh absorbed El's patriarchal profile) all imply a different early character for Yahweh than the community-binding god Fleming proposes.

**Alternative reading:** Yahweh was always a storm/warrior god from the southern deserts; his communal identification with Israel is a secondary development of the monarchy period. Fleming's response: the "people of Yahweh" predates Israel and the monarchy — the communal identification came first, the attributes came later.
