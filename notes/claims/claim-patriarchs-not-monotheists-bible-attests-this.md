# kaufmann-ri-6.2: The Patriarchs Were NOT Monotheists — and the Bible Attests to This

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VI — The Origins of Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VI

## The Claim

Despite the Bible's surface presentation of Abraham as a monotheist, the patriarchal narratives contain "indirect" testimony that Israelite monotheism is post-patriarchal. Three pieces of evidence: (1) No apostolic prophecy in Genesis — no patriarch is "sent" with a mission; (2) No battle with idolatry — the patriarchs never reproach their neighbors for idolatry, and there is "no religious contrast between the patriarchs and their surroundings"; (3) The name YHWH was unknown to the patriarchs (Exod. 3:13ff; 6:2f), confirmed by the absence of the YHWH element in early Hebrew names.

## Evidence

The three points above are genuinely textual observations. Kaufmann also notes that the people — the "real bearers of historical monotheism" — did not inherit their faith from the patriarchs; even biblical tradition doesn't claim the Israelites knew or worshipped YHWH in Egypt. The people had to be taught the name of YHWH by Moses. The patriarchal narratives consistently use El names (El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam, El Roi), not YHWH names.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is one of Kaufmann's most honest and valuable moves. He sees what the text actually says and doesn't force it into his larger thesis. The absence of YHWH in patriarchal names, the Exod. 6:3 statement that the patriarchs knew God as El Shaddai not YHWH, and the lack of anti-idolatry polemic in Genesis are all real features of the text that critical scholarship also notes.

## Stakes

This claim partially undermines Kaufmann's "absolute difference from the beginning" thesis from the Introduction. If the patriarchs weren't monotheists, then Israelite religion didn't begin as something "absolutely different." It BECAME different — with Moses. Kaufmann is forced to admit there was a "before" that looked continuous with its environment. This concession actually aligns him more with the evolutionary model than he'd like to admit — he just compresses the evolution into a single generation and a single person.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional Jewish and Christian readers who take Genesis at face value — that Abraham was already a monotheist. Also, scholars who argue the patriarchal narratives contain genuine memories of El worship that WAS proto-Israelite religion (see [[claim-el-original-god-israel-yahweh-imported-later]], [[claim-yahweh-el-originally-distinct-deities]]).

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the patriarchs worshipped El, not YHWH — but wrong that this was "not yet monotheism." The question is whether El worship in the patriarchal period was polytheistic (El as head of a pantheon) or already tending toward monolatry/henotheism. Cross and Day argue that El was the original high god of Israel, and that YHWH was later identified WITH El — meaning the patriarchal "El" was continuous with later Israelite religion, not a different religion entirely.

## Assessment

This is the most intellectually honest moment in the chapter. Kaufmann sees the textual evidence that the patriarchs didn't know YHWH and didn't fight idolatry, and he admits it rather than explaining it away. The problem is that this admission creates a tension he never fully resolves: if Israelite religion was a radical NOVUM with no antecedents, what do we do with the patriarchal El worship that clearly IS an antecedent? Kaufmann's answer ("it wasn't monotheism yet") is descriptive but doesn't explain the continuity — why did Moses' God get identified as the "God of the fathers" if there was no genuine continuity?
