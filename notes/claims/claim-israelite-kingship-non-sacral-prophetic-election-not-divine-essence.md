# kaufmann-ri-8.1: Israelite kingship was fundamentally different from pagan sacral kingship — grounded in prophetic election, not divine essence

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"The Israelite conception rejected all the divine aspects of kingship and based itself exclusively on the idea of divine election." In pagan cultures, the king was endowed with divine qualities — Pharaoh is a god, Mesopotamian kings are sons of the gods, consorts of Ishtar. In Israel, "there are linguistic vestiges of the idea that the king is a 'son' of God (Pss. 2:7; 89:27 f.)," but "these are purely figurative." The Israelite king "does not incorporate any divine essence; he does not control the destiny of the cosmos through the cult; he is but the bearer of God's grace, appointed to office by his messenger-prophet."

## Evidence

- Pagan kings in Egypt and Mesopotamia were gods, sons of gods, or consorts of goddesses who maintained cosmic order through the cult
- Israelite kings are never deified; even at the height of David's career he asks "Who am I Lord YHWH, and what is my house?" (II Sam 7:18)
- Israel's monarchy is grounded in "apostolic prophecy," not priesthood — the king succeeds the prophet-judge, not the priest
- Israelite kings performed altar service but "never bore the official title 'priest'"; their priestly function "was but a by-role"
- The ideal future king has "no priestly features" (Isa 11:1 ff.) — he is a just judge, not a cultic mediator
- Even in fierce antagonism toward Saul, Samuel "never again suggests that the monarchy itself is a sin"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The contrast with Egyptian/Mesopotamian sacral kingship is generally valid, but Kaufmann understates the sacral dimensions that DID exist (royal psalms, divine sonship language).

## Stakes

If Kaufmann is right that Israelite kingship was categorically non-sacral, this supports his broader thesis of Israel's absolute distinctiveness. If the distinction is one of degree rather than kind, then Israelite kingship developed within a shared ancient Near Eastern paradigm.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars of the "royal psalms" and temple ideology argue that Israelite kingship had genuine sacral dimensions. The "Myth and Ritual School" (Mowinckel, Engnell) argued that Israelite kingship was deeply sacral, with the king playing a central role in an annual enthronement festival.

**Alternative reading:** Israelite kingship shared structural features with other ancient Near Eastern monarchies (divine sonship language, temple building as royal duty) while rejecting the most extreme claims (the king as literal god). The difference is real but not absolute — a modification of Near Eastern royal ideology, not a complete rejection.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is right that no Israelite king claimed to BE a god — this is a genuine distinction from Egypt. But his claim that Israelite kingship was entirely non-sacral overstates the case. The Davidic covenant's language of eternal dynasty, the royal psalms' elevation of the king, and the king's role as temple builder/patron all suggest significant sacral dimensions. Kaufmann's argument serves his larger thesis of absolute difference, but the data supports a more nuanced picture: Israelite kingship demythologized the ANE royal ideology without wholly abandoning its sacral character.
