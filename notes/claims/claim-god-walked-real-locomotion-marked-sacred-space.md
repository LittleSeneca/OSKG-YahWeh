# stav-god-2-4.2: God's walking was real, not metaphorical — his locomotion through the landscape transformed it into sacred space, and standing stones (massebot) marked the places where he had stood.

**Source:** [[Stavrakopoulou — Part I — Feet and Legs]] — Francesca Stavrakopoulou, *God: An Anatomy* (2021), Chapter 3

## The Claim

Stavrakopoulou argues that God's locomotion was real and physical, not metaphorical. "When the biblical story moves to Jerusalem, God's feet are there, too." God walks, strides, treads, and stamps. His movement through the landscape transforms it. Where his feet touch, the ground becomes holy. Standing stones (*massebot*) marked the places where God had stood — they are material memories of divine presence.

## Evidence

**God walking:**
- Genesis 3:8: God "walking in the garden at the breezy time of the day"
- Genesis 17:1, 22: God "appeared" to Abraham — the Hebrew verb suggests visible, bodily manifestation, often including locomotion
- Genesis 18: Abraham walks alongside God toward Sodom
- Genesis 28: Jacob's dream at Bethel — "Yahweh is in this standing-place!" — God was literally standing there

**The massebah connection:**
- Genesis 28: Jacob's stone pillow becomes a *massebah*
- Genesis 31:13: "I am the god of Bethel, whom you anointed there in the massebah" — God identifies with the standing stone
- The stone IS the standing place — God identifies with it

**The maqom wordplay:**
- "A sacred location at which these stones were placed is a *maqom*, a term commonly translated 'place,' but which more precisely means 'a standing place.'"
- The very word for sacred space derives from standing

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The biblical evidence for God walking is extensive; the maqom/massebah connection is philologically sound.

## Stakes

If God walked — really walked — then sacred space is not abstract or symbolic. It is where God's feet touched the ground. The massebah is not a symbol of God; it IS the place where God stood. This is a radically material religion: stones, footprints, dust made holy by physical contact with the deity's body.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** [[claim-israelite-religion-non-mythological-fundamentally-unique]] (Kaufmann) and the theological tradition that reads divine locomotion as metaphor.

**Alternative reading:** The walking language is anthropomorphic metaphor — God's "presence" moves, but God does not have literal feet that touch literal ground.

## Assessment

Stavrakopoulou is at her strongest when she catalogs textual data. The walking language is pervasive and resists purely metaphorical reading. The maqom/massebah connection is a genuine philological insight.
