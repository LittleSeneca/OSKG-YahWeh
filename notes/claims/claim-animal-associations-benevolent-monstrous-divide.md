# smith-obm-1.4: Animal associations follow the benevolent/monstrous divide — domesticated for deities, snake-dragons for cosmic enemies

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Ch1 — Anthropomorphic Deities and Divine Monsters]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"Benevolent deities are often rendered anthropomorphically, whereas destructive divinities appear as monstrous in character. Moreover, theriomorphic representations reflect the dichotomy between deities and cosmic enemies." Domesticated species (bull, calf, bird, cow) are emblematic of deities; undomesticated species (snake, serpent) are emblematic of monsters.

## Evidence

- **El as Bull:** Bears the title "Bull" (*tr*) extensively across the Baal Cycle (CAT 1.1 III 26, IV 12, V 22; 1.2 I 16, 33, 36, III 16-21; 1.3 IV 54, V 10, 35; 1.4 I 4, II 10, III 31, IV 39, 47; 1.6 IV 10, VI 26). Personal name *'iltr* ("El is Bull," 4.607.32).
- **Baal as Calf:** Presented as a bull-calf (1.5 V 17-21; 1.10 II-III, esp. III 33-37). Amiet identifies the bull as the storm-god's "attribute animal" in Syrian glyptic.
- **Anat as Bird:** Textual — title "flyer of flyers, she who soars" (*d'i d'it rhpt*, 1.108.8), hovering among birds over Aqhat (1.18 IV). Iconographic — a drinking mug from Ugarit depicts a bird (Anat) behind Athirat at El's abode. Tessier: "On Syrian seals the weather god is very often associated with a winged and armed goddess."
- **Biblical connection:** The golden calf of Exodus 32 and Jeroboam's bulls at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12) may reflect El's bull iconography — Bethel ("house of El") was originally an old El cult-site (Genesis 28:10-22).
- **Cosmic enemies as snake-dragons:** CAT 1.3 III 40-42 — "I fought the Twisty Serpent, the Potentate with seven Heads." CAT 1.5 I 1-3 — Mot reminds Baal: "[Y]ou killed Litan, the Fleeing Serpent, annihilated the Twisty Serpent, the Potentate with seven heads."
- Glyptic from Ugarit attests to the anthropomorphic warrior-god with a snake in either hand.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The textual and iconographic evidence converges strongly.

## Stakes

This claim grounds the conceptual division between deity and monster in concrete, visual imagery that worshippers would have encountered in cult statues and seals. It also connects Ugaritic iconography to biblical traditions (golden calf), showing continuity of divine representation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars debate whether the golden calf reflects El or Baal iconography. Smith notes the divide: "Many scholars are inclined to see El's rather than Baal's iconography behind the famous 'golden calf'" but "this iconography has been traced back to Baal as well."

**Alternative reading:** The bull iconography may have been shared by multiple West Semitic deities, making it difficult to assign specific biblical images to one god. The "calf" could be a generic symbol of divine strength.

## Assessment

Smith handles the golden calf debate judiciously — he presents both positions and leans toward El (via Bethel) without overclaiming. The Anat-as-bird evidence is particularly striking because it combines explicit texts, iconography, and one specific artifact (the drinking mug). The seven-headed serpent as shared Ugaritic-biblical imagery (Psalm 74:13-14, Revelation 12:3) is one of the most concrete links between the two corpora.
