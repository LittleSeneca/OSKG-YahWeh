# fleming-ybi-6.4: 'Am kĕmôš (Num 21:29) provides the Moabite parallel to 'am Yhwh

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 6 — The People of Yahweh]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 6

## The Claim

"In what Levine (2000: 123-25) calls the 'Heshbon Ballad' of Num 21:27-30... a poetic fragment recalling Heshbon's defeat sets in parallel 'Moab' and 'the people of Kemosh' ('am kĕmôš, v. 29)" (p. 211). "'The people of Kemosh' therefore appear an old traditional category, with another named god entering the Bible without complaint of illegitimacy or idolatry, caught up in memory of military defeat" (p. 211).

## Evidence

- Num 21:28-29a presents Sihon and Heshbon belonging to Moab before the text is aligned with Amorite conquest — "neither this nor any other biblical text explains who defeated them or when" (p. 211)
- Jeremiah 48:45-46 preserves a variant of the same text in an oracle against Moab
- The 'am kĕmôš construction is grammatically identical to 'am Yhwh
- Kamosh appears in the 9th-century Mesha inscription as Moab's god — and Mesha devotes slaughter "to Kamosh and to Moab" (line 12), precisely pairing god and people
- "'The people of Yahweh' should likewise be old and traditional, not a later monarchic or post-monarchic construction" (p. 211)
- The broader South Arabian evidence (developed in Ch 7) confirms this pattern: "descendants of 'Amm" / "descendants of Almaqah" = people defined by divine descent

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The 'am kĕmôš parallel is a genuinely important piece of evidence that has been underutilized in Yahweh-origins scholarship. The grammatical identity and the Mesha inscription's parallel pairing ("to Kamosh and to Moab") make a strong case.

## Stakes

If 'am kĕmôš is an old traditional category in Moab, and 'am Yhwh uses the identical construction in Israel, the pattern is regional, not uniquely Israelite. This supports Fleming's argument that 'am Yhwh represents a genuine political-religious type, not a late theological coinage.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Those who date the Heshbon Ballad late — if it's post-exilic, it could be imitating Israelite usage rather than representing independent Moabite tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The 'am kĕmôš phrase could be an Israelite/Judahite coinage applied to Moab — not evidence of actual Moabite usage.

## Assessment

The combination of the Heshbon Ballad with the Mesha inscription's "to Kamosh and to Moab" makes the alternative reading unlikely. Two independent sources (biblical poem + Moabite inscription) both pair Kamosh with his people in parallel. The pattern is real.
