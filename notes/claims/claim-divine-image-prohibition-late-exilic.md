# romer-inv-8.3: The prohibition on divine images is late — 6th century BCE at the earliest

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 8 — Statue of Yhwh]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 8: The Statue of Yhwh in Judah

## The Claim

The Decalogue's prohibition on graven images is not Mosaic. It was formulated during the exile (6th century) to distinguish Yahwism from Babylonian idolatry, then projected backward into Israel's foundational narrative. The aniconic ideal triumphed only gradually — it was never the original Israelite position.

## Evidence

- **No ancient Near Eastern tradition of aniconism:** "Already in the second millennium at Mari, betyles and statues of gods coexist" — aniconism was an Israelite innovation, not an inheritance
- **The golden calf story** (Exodus 32) was composed to condemn northern bull worship retroactively — the story exists BECAUSE bull worship was real, not because it was an ancient apostasy
- **Isaiah 19:19** (c. 350-300 BCE) still envisions a *masseba* for Yhwh in Egypt — the prohibition was not absolute even in the Persian period, centuries after the Decalogue supposedly existed
- **The Masseba evidence** (see [[claim-yhwh-represented-by-standing-stones-massebot]]) proves pre-exilic Israel used cult stones as divine representations
- **The seals and coins evidence** (see [[claim-seals-coins-depict-yhwh-anthropomorphic]]) proves visual representations persisted into the Persian period

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Well-established in critical scholarship. The convergence of archaeological evidence (massebot, seals, coins) with the lateness of the polemical texts (golden calf story, Deuteronomic prohibitions) makes the exilic dating persuasive.

## Stakes

The second commandment — "you shall not make for yourself a graven image" — was not given to Moses on Sinai. It was invented by priests in Babylon who wanted to distinguish their religion from the statues they saw around them. The invisible, transcendent God is a late construction — earlier Israelites could SEE their God, in stone and on seals and coins. This fundamentally challenges the traditional claim that aniconism was original to Israelite religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditionalists who maintain the Decalogue is genuinely Mosaic. Some scholars date the aniconic impulse earlier (to Hezekiah's reform in the late 8th century) while accepting it wasn't primordial.

**Alternative reading:** Aniconism may have been a genuine early impulse (Hezekiah's reform was pre-exilic) that was radicalized during the exile, rather than invented there. The Decalogue in some form could pre-date the exile, with the exilic editors strengthening the image prohibition. The complete "invention" model may overstate the case.

## Assessment

Römer's case for the lateness of the prohibition is well-grounded. The strongest point is the convergence: archaeological evidence proves Israel used images; the polemical texts (Exod 32, Deuteronomic prohibitions) are therefore reactions to existing practice, not restorations of lost purity; the Yehud coin shows visual representation persisted into the Persian period. The question isn't whether the prohibition is late (it is) but whether there was an EARLIER aniconic impulse that was radicalized by the exile — Hezekiah's reform suggests the aniconic instinct existed before Babylon, even if it wasn't the universal prohibition the Decalogue later claimed.
