# romer-inv-7.1: Multiple Yahwistic sanctuaries existed in Judah beyond Jerusalem

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 7 — Cult of Yhwh in Judah]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 7

## The Claim

The Deuteronomistic ideal of one temple in Jerusalem was not the historical reality. Multiple Yahwistic sanctuaries existed in Judah at Arad, Lachish, Beer-Sheba, and elsewhere. "These high places were probably typically 'Israelite' or 'Judean,' but they had finally to cede their places to the Temple of Jerusalem."

## Evidence

- **Arad:** A Yahwistic temple with one or two standing stones (*maṣṣēbôt*) in the holy of holies — possibly representing Yhwh and Asherah. The stones were deliberately hidden (buried) to protect them from destruction — possibly during Hezekiah's reform or the Assyrian invasion.
- **Lachish:** An Assyrian relief from Sennacherib's palace depicts soldiers carrying an extremely large incense burner from Lachish — too large for private use, almost certainly from a Yahwistic temple.
- **Beer-Sheba:** Remains of a large four-horned altar — a Yahwistic sanctuary.
- **High places (*bāmôt*):** "These high places were in most cases Yahwistic sanctuaries." The editors of Kings confirm this while complaining that "the high places did not disappear; the people still offered sacrifices and perfumes there" (2 Kings 12:14). Only two kings (Hezekiah and Josiah) attempted to eliminate them — proving they were the NORM.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Archaeological evidence is unambiguous at Arad, Beer-Sheba. HIGH that the high places were Yahwistic (the biblical texts themselves say so).

## Stakes

If the Jerusalem temple was not the exclusive sanctuary, then the Deuteronomistic history's central claim — that proper worship of Yhwh was always and only in Jerusalem — is false. The high places were not pagan intrusions; they were indigenous Yahwistic worship. Centralization was not restoration but suppression.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional readings that take the Deuteronomistic history at face value. The biblical editors themselves frame the high places as tolerated-but-wrong.

**Alternative reading:** The high places were syncretistic sites that mixed Yahwism with Canaanite practices, and the Deuteronomists were correct to condemn them.

## Assessment

The Jerusalem-centered, exclusive Yahwism of the Bible was not the original religion of Judah. It was imposed by reforming kings — especially Josiah — who destroyed their own people's traditional sanctuaries. What the Bible presents as "restoring pure religion" was actually "suppressing traditional religion."
