# kaufmann-ri-11.2: Amos's actual innovation was making everyday social morality decisive for national destiny

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XI — Amos and Hosea]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XI

## The Claim

Before Amos, "national doom and exile are, as a rule, threatened only for idolatry" — or for "particularly heinous sins which the whole society has committed" (Flood, Sodom, Canaanite dispossession). "Violations of everyday social morality — perversion of justice, bribe-taking, exploitation of the poor, and the like — are never mentioned. These are regarded as 'venial sins,' subject to the regular process of God's judgment and his individual providence." Amos is "the first to evaluate social morality as a factor in national destiny." He makes it plain that the ruling class in particular was involved, "and in the everyday moral sins. He never speaks of murder, but only of exploitation, oppression, and perversion of justice."

## Evidence

- Amos 1:3-2:3: the nations are judged for "particular enormities, in line with the early view"
- Amos 2:6ff: Israel and Judah judged "for selling the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" — a category shift
- Amos's "almost complete silence regarding idolatry, the chief offense which the early literature held crucial for the destiny of the people"
- Contrast with Nathan (rebukes David's adultery/murder but doesn't make it national), Elijah (rebukes Ahab's judicial murder but doesn't make it national)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** This is genuinely one of Kaufmann's sharpest observations in the chapter. The distinction between heinous sins (murder, sexual atrocity) and everyday social sins (exploitation, bribery, price-gouging) as triggers for NATIONAL judgment IS a real shift in Amos. Kaufmann is correct that prior literature treats these differently.

## Stakes

Whether Amos is merely applying inherited covenant law or transforming the very framework within which covenant violation is understood. If Kaufmann is right, Amos took an existing ethical tradition and made it politically existential.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the covenant lawsuit (*rîb*) form as already present in earlier traditions. If the Decalogue and Covenant Code are pre-Amos (which Kaufmann believes), then the moral demands aren't new — but Kaufmann's point is about their ELEVATION to national-decisive status, not their content.

**Alternative reading:** Amos may not be the first — he's simply the first whose oracles survive. The Elijah-Elisha cycles, even if later redacted, may reflect earlier prophetic concern with social morality. The absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.

## Assessment

This is the strongest claim in the chapter and genuinely advances our understanding of Amos. Kaufmann correctly identifies a structural shift: prior literature treats social sins as individually adjudicated; Amos treats them as national-existential. Even if one rejects Kaufmann's larger thesis about early monotheism, this observation about Amos's framework stands on its own.
