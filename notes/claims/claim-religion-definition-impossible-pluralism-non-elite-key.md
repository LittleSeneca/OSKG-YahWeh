# lewis-ocg-3.1: Defining Religion Is Impossible; Pluralism and Non-Elite Focus Are Key Developments

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 3 — Methodology]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 3

## The Claim

"No field ever moves forward because a good number of people agree on the definition of some central concept" (quoting Catherine Bell). Lewis identifies two "notable developments": Albertz's "internal religious pluralism" (royal vs. household religion; Zevit's plural "religions") and the post-Annales turn toward non-elite communities — "kitchen religion" in Saul Bellow's phrase — that had been "slighted in the past in favor of the religion of the privileged." He also cites the failure of earlier scholars to find a single essence of sacrifice — quoting Evans-Pritchard (14+ motivations), Bourdillon ("any general theory of sacrifice is bound to fail"), Hendel, and Milgrom.

## Evidence

- Lewis cites Albertz (1978, 1994), Zevit (2001 — note the plural "Religions"), Stavrakopoulou and Barton (2010 — "Religious Diversity"), and the feminist archaeological work of Meyers
- He quotes Bell dismissing definition-hunting as unproductive
- Evans-Pritchard documented 14+ motivations for sacrifice; Bourdillon argued general theories of sacrifice "are bound to fail"
- The plural "Religions" in Zevit's title is deliberate — Israelite religion was never singular

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** Pluralism and non-elite focus are well-established as major developments; defining religion is genuinely contested but Lewis's point that lack of consensus on definition does not stall the field is sound.

## Stakes

If Lewis is right, then any book on "Israelite religion" (singular) is already misleading. His own subtitle uses the singular but he insists it's used "collectively." The real question is whether his own book succeeds at representing pluralism or defaults to synthesizing elite Yahwism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue for a working definition of religion (e.g., Dever's "belief in and response to a supernatural power"). Confessional scholars who see Israelite religion as fundamentally unified despite surface diversity.

**Alternative reading:** Useful working definitions exist; the problem is insisting on ONE definition for ALL purposes. Dever's definition may be too thin, but operational definitions are necessary for historical work.

## Assessment

Lewis's pluralism is methodologically sound and aligns with the best recent work (Smith, Stavrakopoulou, Dever). The tension between this commitment and his focus on El and Yahweh (two elite male deities) will be a recurring theme throughout the book.
