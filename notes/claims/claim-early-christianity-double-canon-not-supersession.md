# schmid-ht-10-12-13.3: Early Christianity had no "Old Testament" — just "the Law and the Prophets" — and established a double-canon, not a supersession

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§10, 12, 13 — Canon Pluriformity and Relativity]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §12

## The Claim

"Earliest Christianity...were familiar neither with an Old Testament nor a New Testament. There was not even a stable collection of literature." NT references to "the Law and the Prophets" reflect the still-fluid two-part canon. Critically: "the writings of the New Testament were neither joined to the Hebrew Bible — in something like a fourth part of the canon... — nor made superior to the Hebrew Bible as the normative interpretive authority. Christianity instead came up with a double canon in which neither part is superior or inferior to the other."

## Evidence

- Luke 16:16, 29, 31; 24:27, 44; Acts 26:22; 28:23 — all use "Moses and the prophets" or "law of Moses, prophets, and psalms"
- NT writings were occasional writings, not a planned canon; Gospels established liturgically around 100 CE
- Marcion (2nd century) was the first to propose dismissing the OT — early Christianity REJECTED this
- "The New Testament canon that was developed preserved the theological polyphony of early Christianity"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The NT references and Marcion controversy are well-documented.

## Stakes

Schmid is arguing that Christianity's double-canon is a deliberate theological decision to maintain polyphony, not to establish supersession. This has implications for how Christian theology should relate to the HB — not as "old" (superseded) but as part of an ongoing conversation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Supersessionist theologians who read the NT as declaring the OT "old" and obsolete (Hebrews 8:13). Scholars who argue the double-canon structure implicitly subordinates the OT — the NT interprets the OT, establishing a de facto hierarchy.

**Alternative reading:** The double-canon structure inevitably creates a hermeneutical hierarchy. The NT's placement after the OT and its explicit interpretation of OT texts (fulfillment citations) makes it the interpretive lens through which the OT is read, even if this wasn't the original intention.

## Assessment

Schmid's reading of the double-canon as deliberately preserving polyphony is an important theological argument. It means the HB's theological voices are not "fulfilled" by the NT — they continue to speak alongside it.
