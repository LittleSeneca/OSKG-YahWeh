# keel-ggi-excursus.5: The Astarte plaques are a city phenomenon — absent from early Israelite hill country settlements

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Excursus — Astarte Plaques]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Excursus §§57-60

## The Claim

"It is especially noteworthy that all the plaques that have been found to date come only from the cities and their environs; none have been found in the small, early Iron Age settlements in the hill country." Representative pieces come from Tel Zeror, Tell Beit Mirsim, Gezer, Lachish, Taanach — all urban sites. The early Iron Age hill country settlements (proto-Israelite) have produced NO Astarte plaques. This distribution contrasts with the qudshu-type plaques, which have a wider distribution.

## Evidence

- All known findspots are urban: Tel Zeror, Tell Beit Mirsim, Gezer, Lachish, Taanach
- Zero examples from early Iron Age hill country (proto-Israelite) settlements
- Distribution contrasts with qudshu-type plaques which have wider geographic range
- This is the archaeologically documented distribution pattern — not an argument from silence

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The distribution data is archaeological fact. The absence from hill country sites could theoretically be overturned by new finds, but the pattern is currently clear.

## Stakes

THIS IS THE CRITICAL FINDING FOR THE TRUTH PROJECT. If the "Astarte plaques" are a Canaanite city phenomenon and NOT found in proto-Israelite settlements, then they cannot be used as evidence for popular Israelite goddess worship. Dever's argument that goddess figurines represent widespread folk religion suppressed by Jerusalem orthodoxy depends on continuity between these LB/Iron I plaques and later Iron II figurines. Keel & Uehlinger show that continuity is broken — the plaques disappear from the archaeological record precisely in the areas and periods where Israelite settlement emerges.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever implicitly, by treating the figurine evidence as continuous from LB through Iron II. Scholars who assume cultural continuity between Canaanite cities and Israelite villages.

**Alternative reading:** The absence from hill country sites could reflect different deposition practices (hill country villagers might have discarded plaques differently) rather than absence of use. Or the plaques might have been used in hill country but in perishable materials (wood, cloth) that didn't survive.

## Assessment

The absence is striking and significant. Combined with the finding that the plaques are primarily a women's piety phenomenon (Claim 3) and that they're non-royal, everyday goddesses (Claim 2), the picture that emerges is: these figurines represent a specifically Canaanite URBAN women's religious practice that did NOT transfer to the Israelite hill country. The goddess worship that Dever documents in Iron II (Judean Pillar Figurines) is a DIFFERENT phenomenon — later, different type, different distribution — not a survival of the Astarte plaque tradition.
