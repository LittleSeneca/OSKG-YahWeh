# sommer-bog-3.4: P and D are anti-fluid, not anti-corporeal per se

**Source:** [[Sommer — Chapter 3 — Rejection of Fluidity]] — Benjamin Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009), Chapter 3

## The Claim

The Priestly (P) and Deuteronomistic (D) sources rejected divine fluidity — the idea that God has multiple simultaneous bodies in different locations. But they did NOT reject divine corporeality. God still has a body; it's just located exclusively in heaven, accessible only through hypostatic substitutes (Name, Glory).

## Evidence

**The Historical Arc (from Chapter 3):**

| Source | Date | Theology | Body Count |
|--------|------|----------|------------|
| **J/E** | 10th-8th c. | Fluid monotheism | Many bodies, one God |
| **D** | 7th c. | Anti-fluid monotheism | No body on earth; only the Name |
| **P** | 6th-5th c. | Anti-fluid monotheism | No material body on earth; only the Glory |
| **Later Judaism** | Post-biblical | Radical anti-fluid | NO body at all |

**D's theology:** God dwells in heaven. He still has a body (Deuteronomy uses anthropomorphic language about God's hand, arm, etc.), but that body is confined to the heavenly realm.

**P's theology:** God has "form" (*ṣelem*) and "shape" (*dĕmût*) — Genesis 1:26. P's God has a body, but it's made of light/energy, not flesh, and humans can't see it directly.

**The key insight:** The anti-fluid revolution was NOT the rejection of divine embodiment. It was the rejection of MULTIPLE embodiments. The fully transcendent, bodiless God of later Judaism and Christianity is a further development beyond both D and P.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** Both D and P texts contain language that assumes God has a body (heavenly dwelling, form, shape); what they reject is the multiplicity and accessibility of that body in multiple locations.

## Stakes

This distinction matters enormously for the history of theology. Traditional narratives say Israel moved from anthropomorphic polytheism to transcendent monotheism. Sommer says: Israel moved from fluid corporeal monotheism (J/E) to anti-fluid corporeal monotheism (D/P) to anti-corporeal monotheism (later Judaism/Christianity). The rejection of the body came LAST, not first.

## Disagreement

**Alternative reading:** P and D are anti-corporeal — the Name and Glory are not just substitutes for local bodies but substitutes for ANY divine body. D's heaven is a location, not a body location; P's *kavod* is not a body but a phenomenon.

## Assessment

This is Sommer's most important conceptual clarification. The field has conflated "anti-fluid" with "anti-corporeal" — assuming that when D and P rejected multiple divine bodies, they rejected divine embodiment entirely. Sommer shows that's not what the texts say. D's God still has hands and arms; P's God still has form and shape. What changed was the LOCATION and ACCESSIBILITY of the body, not its existence.
