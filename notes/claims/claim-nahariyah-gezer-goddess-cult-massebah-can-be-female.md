# keel-ggi-p1.4: The Nahariyah and Gezer cult installations were dedicated to a goddess — massebot could represent female deities

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part I

## The Claim

"Stelae made of stone are not necessarily images of male deities... The figure on this stele... is represented more realistically by means of sculpted genital lips" (Tel Kitan stele). At Nahariyah, a steatite mold for mass-producing naked goddess figurines, 19 goddess figurines (or fragments) found in a vessel hoard, dove figurines in equal quantities, lion and vulture amulets — all suggesting a goddess cult. At Gezer, the famous row of ten monoliths has been traditionally interpreted as phallic symbols or male memorials. But a bronze figurine from a Gezer cache is "iconographically very similar to those made from the steatite mold at Nahariyah" — suggesting "the massebahs might also portray female deities." At Tel Kitan, one stele was shaped "in very rough fashion, in the form of a naked woman" with sculpted genital lips — directly confirming the possibility.

## Evidence

- **Nahariyah:** Steatite mold for mass-producing naked goddess figurines (22 cm high); 19 goddess figurines/fragments in vessel hoard — some solid-cast, some sheet-metal with pendant holes; dove figures in equal quantities to goddess figures; lion amulet and vulture amulet — both in goddess sphere
- **Gezer:** 10 monoliths up to 3.25 m, dated ~1600 BCE; bronze female figurine from treasure cache — naked, tall head covering, hands on thighs (same type as Nahariyah mold); gold sheet figures (largest 16 cm) — naked except head covering, genitalia "shown in larger scale," narrowed at bottom possibly "made to imitate the form of wooden or stone figures that were stuck into the ground"; Macalister found 7 similar naked female figures at Gezer, one appearing pregnant
- **Tel Kitan:** Stele shaped as naked woman among row of massebot — sculpted genital lips, stump arms, 45 cm high; two lead figures from Tell el-Ajjul — goddess with hands on breasts, high horned head covering

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The convergence of evidence across three sites with different media is compelling. The Tel Kitan stele is the clincher — an unambiguous female massebah.

## Stakes

This directly challenges a long-standing assumption in biblical archaeology that massebot represent male deities (or are phallic symbols). If massebot can represent goddesses, then every Iron Age Israelite site with standing stones must be re-evaluated. The two massebot in the Arad temple's holy of holies — which Dever identifies as "Yhwh and another deity" (almost certainly Asherah) — could both potentially represent female deities. The biblical prohibition of massebot (Deut 16:22; Lev 26:1) takes on new meaning if massebot were associated with goddess worship specifically.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain that massebot are exclusively male/phallic symbols. Kempinski's attempt to date the Gezer installation to MB IIA — Keel & Uehlinger reject this based on Giveon's demonstration of later contexts.

**Alternative reading:** The female stele at Tel Kitan might be exceptional — one goddess among many male stelae. The Gezer monoliths, being abstract/aniconic, might not be gendered at all. The presence of a female figurine in a cache doesn't prove the monoliths represent goddesses.

## Assessment

The Tel Kitan stele is genuinely important evidence that should change how archaeologists interpret massebot. It's a single example, but it proves possibility — once you know massebot *can* represent goddesses, you can't assume they always represent gods. The Nahariyah mold is equally important because it shows mass production of goddess figurines for a cult site — this is organized, institutional goddess worship, not private folk practice.
