# day-ygc-5.4: The "Queen of Heaven" in Jeremiah is most likely Astarte (possibly in syncretism with Ishtar)

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 5 — Yahweh and Astarte and Anat]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"Although certainty is not possible, the best case can be made for the Queen of Heaven being Astarte" (p. 150). Possibly "Astarte in syncretism with her Mesopotamian equivalent Ishtar" (p. 150).

## Evidence

**Eliminating alternatives:**

| Candidate | Proponents | Day's Refutation |
|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| Shapash (sun goddess) | Dahood | "Gained no support." Based on dubious translation of Ugaritic *knm* as "cakes" (p. 146-147) |
| Asherah | Koch, Keel/Uehlinger | Nothing in first-millennium texts "associates her particularly with the heavens at all" (p. 147). Astarte, not Asherah, is the West Semitic equivalent of Ishtar |
| Anat | Vincent, Albright, Porten, Cogan | The Deuteronomist "never once polemicizes against her cult elsewhere" — Anat seems less prominent in first-millennium Palestine (p. 148) |
| Ishtar | Schrader, Rudolph, Weinfeld | The Mesopotamian goddess IS called "Queen of Heaven" (*šarrat šamê*) and *kawwānîm* (cakes) is an Akkadian loanword (*kamānu*) attested for Ishtar offerings. BUT the text makes clear this was "deep-seated among both the ordinary people and rulers of Judah" — "a popular folk custom... not some recently implanted cult from Assyria" (p. 149) |

**For Astarte:**

1. Called "Lady of Heaven" in New Kingdom Egypt (alongside Anat, Ishtar, Qudshu)
2. **Most frequently associated with heavens in first millennium:** Eshmunazar inscription (KAI 14): *ʻštrt šmm ʼdrm* "Astarte of the Highest Heavens" (p. 150)
3. **Aphrodite Ourania = Astarte:** Two Delos inscriptions by Ascalonites address "the heavenly Aphrodite, Astarte of Palestine." Herodotus (1.105) says the most ancient temple of Aphrodite Ourania was in Ascalon — indicating Palestinian origin (p. 150)
4. **Cake offerings attested:** Kition tariff (4th c. BCE) mentions cakes baked for the "Holy Queen" (though different Hebrew word)
5. **Deuteronomistic polemic:** The Deuteronomist (to whom Jer 7 and 44 belong) explicitly polemicizes against Astarte elsewhere (1 Kgs 11:5, 33; 2 Kgs 23:13) — but never against Anat or Shapash
6. **Plus Ishtar syncretism:** The Akkadian loanword for "cakes" (*kawwānîm*) suggests some Mesopotamian influence. Ackerman's view: "not simply Astarte, but Astarte in syncretism with her Mesopotamian equivalent Ishtar" (p. 150)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Day's elimination of alternatives is methodical and persuasive; the Astarte conclusion isn't certain but is the best-supported, and the Ishtar syncretism nuance is appropriately cautious.

## Stakes

Jeremiah 44 is the most detailed description of popular goddess worship in the OT. Identifying the goddess tells us which Canaanite deity had the deepest hold on ordinary Judean religious practice — and it was most likely Astarte, with cakes, incense, and drink offerings provided primarily by women.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Ishtar proponents (Schrader, Rudolph, Weinfeld, Held). Asherah partisans (Koch, Keel/Uehlinger). Anat advocates (Vincent, Albright, Porten, Cogan).

**Alternative reading:** The Queen of Heaven was Ishtar imported from Assyria, based on the Akkadian cake-offering vocabulary and the Mesopotamian title "Queen of Heaven."

## Assessment

Day's case is strongest on two points: (1) Astarte is the only candidate the Deuteronomist actually polemicizes against elsewhere, and (2) the Aphrodite Ourania/Astarte identification is well-attested in the first millennium. The cake-offering vocabulary being Akkadian is the strongest counter-point for Ishtar, and Day handles this by adopting Ackerman's "syncretism" solution — a reasonable compromise.
