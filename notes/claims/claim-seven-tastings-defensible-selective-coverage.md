# lewis-ocg-1.5: Seven 'tastings' represent defensible selective coverage centered on El and Yahweh

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 1 — Introductory Matters]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 1

## The Claim

The seven chapters cover: (1) El worship, (2) El iconography, (3) Yahweh's origin, (4) Yahweh iconography, (5) Yahweh as warrior and family god, (6) Yahweh as king and judge, (7) Yahweh as holy one. This "distillate" captures core elements while leaving much aside (goddesses, calendrical festivals, wisdom literature, incantation, etc.).

## Evidence

Lewis justifies each chapter's inclusion: El worship is fundamental because "El" appears in the name *Israel*; iconography is essential because ancients thought about gods visually, not just textually; Yahweh's origin is the central historical question; characterization of Yahweh covers the major metaphors through which Israelites understood their god.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH that these are important topics. MEDIUM that they constitute a *representative* selection — notably absent are goddess worship (especially Asherah, which gets only passing mention in this roadmap), ancestor cult, divination, and the full range of popular religious practice.

## Stakes

If Lewis's selection skews too heavily toward elite, textual, and theological concerns, the book misrepresents Israelite religion as more systematic and less embodied than it was.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever would say archaeology of popular practice is missing. Stavrakopoulou would say the physical body of God is underrepresented. Albertz would say family and personal piety need more space. Zevit would want more material culture.

**Alternative reading:** Lewis isn't claiming this is a *balanced* coverage — he's claiming it's a *deep* coverage of selected topics. A single author can't do everything, and Lewis's topics are genuinely central (Yahweh, El, iconography). The missing topics are covered by other scholars.

## Assessment

Lewis's roadmap is honest about what's included and excluded. The real criterion is whether what he DOES cover is done with sufficient depth and rigor to justify the omissions. For a reference work, this is defensible. But readers should supplement with Dever (for archaeology of popular religion), Stavrakopoulou (for embodiment), and Albertz (for social history).
