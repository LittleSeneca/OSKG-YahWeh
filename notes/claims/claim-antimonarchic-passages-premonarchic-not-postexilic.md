# kaufmann-ri-8.2: The antimonarchic passages in I Samuel are premonarchic in origin, not postexilic retrojections

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch VIII — The Monarchy]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch VIII

## The Claim

"Classical criticism regards the antimonarchic version as a reflex of the 'theocratic ideal' of the postexilic age... The fact is, however, that later ages never were hostile toward the institution of kingship." The antimonarchic passages "preserve the mood of a specific occasion" — the crisis when the ancient prophetic kingdom of God gave way to a secular monarchy — and "remained forever linked with that occasion, without further ideological issue."

## Evidence

- Later biblical histories portray kings as sinning but "the monarchy itself is never regarded as a sin to be atoned for or repudiated"
- The prophets opposed individual kings but raised new dynasties — "their opposition takes the form of raising new dynasties to replace the old"
- The Chronicler's "extravagant idealization of the Davidic dynasty" was produced in Second Temple times — hardly antimonarchic
- Hosea 13:10f. shows a "telling divergence" — Hosea's Israel asks for "king and officials" (*sarim*), reflecting later bureaucracy, whereas I Samuel 8 has *sarim* only as "servants" (*'abadim*), reflecting earlier conditions
- "The origin of this hostility must be sought in the premonarchic period"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Plausible and worth taking seriously, but the dating of I Samuel 7-8, 10:17-27, and 12 remains contested — many scholars see Deuteronomistic editing.

## Stakes

The dating of the Deuteronomistic History's attitude toward kingship. If Kaufmann is right, the antimonarchic strain is genuinely ancient and not a retrojection of postexilic priestly ideology.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Critical scholars date the "theocratic" version of Saul's election (I Sam 8; 10:17-27; 12) to the Deuteronomistic History (late monarchic/exilic) or later. The negative portrayal of monarchy as a rejection of YHWH's kingship is seen as reflecting the experience of monarchy's failure.

**Alternative reading:** Both the promonarchic and antimonarchic strands in I Samuel reflect genuine tensions present from the monarchy's beginning — the tension between charismatic leadership and institutional authority. The Deuteronomistic editors preserved both because both captured aspects of their experience with monarchy.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's observation that later Israel never repudiated monarchy *as an institution* is correct and important. Even in the postexilic period, messianic hope centered on a Davidic king. The antimonarchic strand in I Samuel is anomalous within the broader biblical corpus and needs explanation. Kaufmann's explanation — that it preserves genuinely premonarchic sentiment — is reasonable, though the text as we have it has certainly been shaped by later editors.
