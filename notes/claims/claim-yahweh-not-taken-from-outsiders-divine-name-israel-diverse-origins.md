# fleming-ybi-1.1: Yahweh was not "taken from outsiders" — the divine name reveals Israel's diverse origins, not foreign borrowing

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 1 — Introduction]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 1

## The Claim

"Fleming argues in contrast that Yahweh was not taken from outsiders. Rather, this divine name is evidence for the diverse background of Israel itself" (p. 3). "It is time to reassemble all the pieces of this familiar puzzle in a fresh framework, not seeking a god foreign to Israel but one that belonged to a political landscape in which Israel played a relatively small role" (p. 27).

## Evidence

- The Egyptian Yhwʒ of Shasu-land (14th c.) is a people-name, not a divine name — a Shasu subgroup defined by kinship (p. 42)
- The Song of Deborah's battle narrative has a "people of Yahweh" ('am Yhwh, Judg 5:13) without reference to Israel — Israel is added only in the opening hymn (vv. 2-11), per Milstein's "revision through introduction" (p. 27)
- The Mesha inscription (mid-9th c.) refers to "the men of Gad" occupying land "since forever" without connection to Israel (p. 27)
- Groups like Ephraim, Benjamin, and Amalek appear as participants with "the people of Yahweh" in Judg 5:14 without clear relationship to Israel (p. 27)

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The exegetical observation about Judges 5 (Israel only in the frame, not the core) is strong and testable. The Mesha evidence for Gad is solid. The Egyptian Yhwʒ-as-people-name reading is Fleming's distinctive contribution (developed in Ch 2) — if it holds, the entire framework shifts. But the reconstruction depends heavily on his reading of the Egyptian material and Judges 5.

## Stakes

If true, the entire 150-year paradigm collapses. Yahweh worship didn't need to be "imported" from Midian or the deep south. Yahweh was already present in the inland Levantine highlands among Shasu-related groups that eventually became part of Israel. This changes how we understand Israel's formation: not a people who adopted a foreign god, but a coalition that coalesced around (or alongside) an already-present deity.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Essentially everyone who holds the Midianite Hypothesis — Römer (2015), Blenkinsopp (2008), Smith (2001, 2012), van der Toorn (1999), and the "Berlin hypothesis" scholars (Levin, Pfeiffer, Müller) who reject southern origins but for different reasons (late dating of all biblical texts). The Berlin school argues Yahweh was always a local highland deity; Fleming shares their skepticism of the south but rejects their late-dating methodology.

**Alternative reading:** The standard Midianite reading: the Egyptian Yhwʒ IS a place-name derived from the divine name (the region named after the god worshipped there), and Judges 5's southern geography (Seir, Edom) confirms Yahweh's southern origins independent of the prose narratives about Moses.

## Assessment

Fleming's reframing is elegant and overdue. The "borrowing from foreigners" model has always had a colonial-era flavor — as if Israel needed to be explained by contact with exotic others rather than by the complex internal dynamics of the region. His reading of Judges 5's redaction history (Israel only in the frame) is the strongest single piece of evidence, and it doesn't depend on his Egyptian interpretation. The weakest link is how much weight the entire argument places on the Yhwʒ-as-people-name reading — if specialists reject that, the foundation cracks.
