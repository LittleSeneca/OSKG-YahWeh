# kaufmann-ri-10.6: Classical prophecy created the idea of universal history

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch X — The Literature and the Age]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch X

## The Claim

The early religion knew only two periods (primeval monotheism → Babel → nations with idolatry; then YHWH reveals himself to Israel alone). "Israel alone was henceforth the subject of history." What happens to the nations "has no divine meaning, and is therefore not history in the biblical sense." Classical prophecy broke this framework: "A reconstituted, universal history first appears with Isaiah's vision of the ultimate return of the nations to YHWH in the future (Isa. 2). At the culmination of history mankind is reunited, all sharing the divine grace which heretofore had been the portion of Israel alone."

## Evidence

- The early religion's view: "prior to the tower of Babel, a primeval monotheism reigned among men; subsequently the nations came into existence with idolatry as their 'portion'"
- "The nations, deprived of the supreme human value — knowledge of God — stood outside"
- The new eschatology of the Aramaean wars "did conceive of a universal 'day of YHWH,' a judgment of all nations, but it was left for classical prophecy to give full expression to this new idea"
- Isaiah 2's vision of nations streaming to Zion is the breakthrough
- "With Habakkuk and Jeremiah, the idea that Israel's historical mission is to combat the idolatry of men is born. Idolatry has no right to existence; the ideal is one humanity under God"
- This is "the background of the prophecies of the Second Isaiah"
- Apocalyptic takes the final step: Daniel's four monarchies succeeded by the kingdom of God; idolatry becomes the sin for which nations are punished; the heathen realm becomes "the kingdom of Belial, of Satan"
- "This view is at once the climax of universalism and particularistic intolerance"
- "Again we see that there is no promiscuous mixing of realms, as current opinion would have it"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The three-stage schema captures real ideological differences, but Isaiah 2/Micah 4 may be late insertions, undermining the 8th-century dating of the breakthrough.

## Stakes

Whether the prophetic vision of universal history was a genuine innovation (Kaufmann's view) or the natural outgrowth of an evolving monotheism (the critical view). Kaufmann wants to show that the *idea* of universal history appears at a specific stage — classical prophecy — and doesn't belong to either the earlier or later stages.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see universalism developing more gradually. Smith traces universalistic language emerging in Second Isaiah as a response to the exile, not present in the 8th-century prophets. Römer sees Israel's universal mission as a late development. The critical view would place Isaiah 2's Zion pilgrimage vision later than the 8th century.

**Alternative reading:** The "new historiosophy" may not be as unified or as early as Kaufmann presents it. Isaiah 2:2-4 (paralleled in Micah 4:1-3) is widely regarded as a late insertion — its universalism is anomalous in 8th-century prophecy. If that's correct, the breakthrough Kaufmann attributes to Isaiah son of Amoz may actually belong to the exilic or post-exilic period.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's three-stage schema is illuminating as an *ideological* typology, even if the *chronological* assignment of particular texts is contested. He's identified real differences in how the three bodies of literature conceive of history. But his insistence on sharp boundaries ("no promiscuous mixing") may reflect his systematic mind more than the messier reality of literary development. The Zion pilgrimage vision (Isa 2/Mic 4) doesn't fit neatly — it's universalist in a way that looks more like Second Isaiah than 8th-century prophecy. Kaufmann's dating of it to Isaiah son of Amoz is crucial to his schema but debatable.
