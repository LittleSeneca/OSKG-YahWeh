# keel-ggi-3.3: The falcon-headed figure on MB IIB scarabs represents a deliberate syncretism — the Canaanite royal/fertility god identified with Egyptian Horus

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch III — MB Temples Tel Kitan and Megiddo]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter III

## The Claim

"The self-confident Canaanites who lived at the end of the eighteenth century and during the seventeenth and sixteenth centuries associated their royal god, their fertility god, with the chief Egyptian royal god, so they placed a branch or blossom scepter into the hand of their own royal god, their own fertility god." "We are encountering a merging of attributes from the Egyptian royal god with those of the Canaanite royal god."

## Evidence

- The falcon-headed figure appears "much more frequently than the weather god on Middle Bronze Age IIB scarabs"
- German scholars identified it as Canaanite Horon; English-speaking scholars as Egyptian Re — both sides missed the iconographic tradition
- The "jasper group" of seals (1720–1650): green jasper and similar hard stones, produced in Byblos or south of it, characterized by slender elongated figures, males worshiping and being worshiped, rudimentary hieroglyphs
- On Old Syrian cylinder seals, the falcon-headed figure wears the Egyptian double crown — clearly the royal god, probably Horus
- On scarabs, the figure appears WITHOUT the double crown but WITH a branch or blossom scepter — a non-Egyptian element adapted from the Canaanite weather god
- The Hyksos-era scarab from Tell el-Dab'a shows a pharaoh holding a club AND a branch — "quite unlike what the Egyptians would have included"
- The falcon-headed figure victorious over the crocodile: crocodile is an Egyptian motif, but Egyptians were hesitant to depict it as conquered; in Canaan, Baal's victory over Litanu/Leviathan may have encouraged this portrayal
- Horus falcon with outstretched wings on jasper-group scarabs and gold jewelry from Byblos and Tell el-Ajjul

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The iconographic argument — tracing motifs across cylinder seals and scarabs, noting the addition of the branch to the falcon-headed figure — is methodologically sound. But the identity of the Canaanite deity being syncretized remains somewhat speculative: is it the weather god? A distinct royal god? Horon? The authors do not pin this down definitively.

## Stakes

This is the earliest documented case of what will become a recurring pattern in Israelite religion: the selective appropriation of Egyptian royal/divine iconography to express Canaanite (and later Israelite) theological concepts. The falcon-headed figure prefigures the later absorption of solar imagery into Yahwism (Chapters VII–VIII). If Canaanite kings in MB IIB could identify their royal god with Horus, then the later identification of Yahweh with solar attributes (winged disk, falcon, uraeus) is not an innovation but a continuation of a well-established Canaanite practice of iconographic syncretism.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The German vs. English-speaking scholarly divide they mention is real. Scholars who emphasize the Egyptian borrowing (Re identification) would resist the idea that the Canaanite element is primary. Scholars who emphasize the Canaanite distinctiveness (Horon identification) would resist the idea that Egyptian identification was deliberate rather than superficial decoration.

**Alternative reading:** The falcon-headed figure might not represent a "merging" of deities at all. Seal-cutters might have simply combined visually impressive motifs from different traditions without any theological intent. The branch could be a misunderstood Egyptian motif rather than a deliberate Canaanite addition. The Hyksos period was one of intense cultural mixing; what looks like "syncretism" might just be eclectic borrowing.

## Assessment

This is the most complex and original argument in the chapter. The authors deftly navigate the German/English scholarly divide by moving to a third position: the figure is BOTH Egyptian and Canaanite, deliberately so. Their use of the jasper group as a coherent corpus is methodologically sophisticated — by identifying a specific workshop tradition, they can track how a motif was modified as it moved from cylinder seals (elite, Syrian-influenced) to scarabs (broader, Palestinian). The Tell el-Dab'a scarab with pharaoh + branch is a compelling piece of evidence for the Hyksos context. My main reservation: the argument assumes that the falcon-headed figure represents a SINGLE deity that was being syncretized, when it could represent different deities in different contexts.
