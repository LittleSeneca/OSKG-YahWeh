# dever-dghw-intro.6: Dever's methodology — archaeology as primary source, "descriptive" not "prescriptive"

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife? Archaeology and Folk Religion in Ancient Israel* (2005), Introduction

## The Claim

"As an archaeologist and an anthropologist, and thus a historian, I shall argue that philosophy and theology are distractions. These disciplines may well be legitimate and interesting in their own right; but they get us nowhere in the inquiry into ancient folk religion." Text-based scholars treat the Bible as the primary source and archaeology as supplementary. Dever reverses this: for folk religion, archaeology is primary and texts are secondary. His goal is "thick description" (Geertz) — understanding what people actually did, not what scribes said they should do.

## Evidence

**His eight-point phenomenological method:**
1. Observing society directly, in action rather than theory
2. Using typical case studies, not necessarily "reconstruction"
3. Understanding society "from within" — what people say about themselves
4. Emphasizing individual creativity over large-scale typologies
5. Aiming at Geertz's "thick description," not necessarily explanation
6. Using organic models (social systems like biological systems)
7. Inductive methods: particular → general, not deductive
8. Not eschewing ideology, but inferring the irretrievable from the observable

**Key methodological claims:**
- "This is a book about ordinary people... not about the extraordinary few who wrote and edited the Hebrew Bible"
- "I shall not regard it here as Revelation" — theological commitments distort historical analysis
- "Archaeologists are poorly equipped to be paleo-psychologists" — we can reconstruct practice, not belief
- "Folk religion" = what people actually DID, not what scribes said they should do
- "Book religion" = the canonical theology of the Hebrew Bible — a late, elite construction

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH that this is Dever's method. MEDIUM on whether archaeology CAN deliver what Dever claims for it (especially on religion, where practice is observable but belief is inferred).

## Stakes

If Dever is right about archaeology's primacy, then text-based scholars (Smith, Römer) are missing most of the evidence. The debate about Israelite religion can't be settled from texts alone — you need the figurines, the cult stands, the inscriptions from the ground. This is Dever's core methodological argument.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Text-based scholars (Smith, Römer, Day) would argue that texts provide access to beliefs, concepts, and narratives that material culture cannot — artifacts show what people did, not what they thought. And biblical scholars would note that even Dever needs texts to interpret his artifacts (the Bible provides the cultural vocabulary).

**Alternative reading:** Archaeology and texts are complementary, not competitive. The best scholarship (Keel/Uehlinger, Zevit, Albertz) integrates both. Dever's either/or framing is rhetorically effective but methodologically overstated.

## Assessment

Dever's method has real strengths — especially his insistence that texts are elite products and artifacts reveal popular practice. But his confidence that artifacts can be read straightforwardly as evidence of religion is sometimes overstated. Figurines could be toys, cult stands could be furniture, and the absence of evidence (no male figurines in Israel) could mean many things besides aniconism. Dever tends to read artifacts in the direction his argument requires — which is exactly the critique he levels at text-based scholars.
