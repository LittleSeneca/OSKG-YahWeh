# kaufmann-ri-intro.4: Biblical "idolatry" was not genuine mythological polytheism

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Introduction]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Introduction

## The Claim

"Despite appearances, Israel was not a polytheistic people. Careful examination of the biblical testimony to Israel's 'idolatry' yields an altogether new assessment of this idolatry. It was not genuine, mythological polytheism; foreign elements did not play a substantial, creative role in the formation of the popular culture." When Israelites "worshipped idols," they weren't importing the full pagan mythological apparatus — they were engaging in a degraded, fetishistic practice that didn't involve actual belief in the myths, theogonies, or divine biographies.

## Evidence

Kaufmann signals his signature argument — that when Israelites "worshipped idols," they weren't importing the full pagan mythological apparatus. They were engaging in a degraded, fetishistic practice that didn't involve actual belief in the myths, theogonies, or divine biographies that characterized genuine paganism. The evidence for this is the book's entire argument, previewed in the introduction.

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** Requires Kaufmann to argue that the archaeological and biblical evidence for polytheistic practice in Israel represents something fundamentally different from the same practices in neighboring cultures. That's a heavy evidentiary burden.

## Stakes

If Kaufmann is right, the entire "Israel was polytheistic" narrative collapses. Israel's idolatry was a superficial, contentless imitation of pagan forms — not evidence of genuine polytheistic belief.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever documents thousands of figurines and argues they represent genuine popular religion. Smith traces the mythological content Israelites shared with their neighbors. Römer documents the epigraphic evidence for Yahweh's pairing with Asherah.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is onto something REAL but overstates it. Israelite religion WAS distinctive in its aniconic tendency, its rejection of theogony, and its eventual monotheism. But the distinction wasn't present "from the very beginning" — it developed over time, and the biblical texts retroject later norms onto earlier periods.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's most interesting move. His claim that biblical polemic consistently misrepresents paganism as fetishism IS an observation worth taking seriously — the prophets do seem to mock idols as mere wood and stone rather than engaging with pagan theology. But the question is whether this reflects Israelite ignorance of paganism (Kaufmann's claim) or deliberate polemical strategy (the critical consensus). The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, which pair Yahweh with Asherah in a blessing formula, are particularly hard for Kaufmann to explain away as "not genuine polytheism."
