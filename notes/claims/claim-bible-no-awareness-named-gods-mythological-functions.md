# kaufmann-ri-1.9: The Bible shows no awareness of the connection between named gods and their mythological functions

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"Had we only the Bible, we should know nothing of the real nature of the 'gods of the nations'." Named gods like Baal, Ashtoreth, Chemosh, Milcom, Bel, Nebo, and Amon are "mere names" stripped of mythological content. "No god is ever styled according to his function or place in the pantheon, as so often occurs in the literatures of Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Canaan."

## Evidence

- **No functional epithets:** Gods are identified only by their nations — "Ashtoreth, god of the Sidonians," "Milcom, the abomination of the Ammonites," "Chemosh, the abomination of Moab." Never as "Baal, rider of the clouds" or "Ashtoreth, goddess of love and war."
- **No sexual differentiation:** "Nor is the sexual differentiation of the gods ever alluded to; gods and goddesses are both comprised under the masculine rubric *elohim*... there being, in fact, no word in biblical Hebrew for 'goddess'."
- **Astral deities not connected to the "host of heaven":** Ashtoreth (Ishtar), Bel, Marduk, and Nebo are known from pagan sources to have been astral deities, "yet not once does the Bible connect them with the worship of the 'host of heaven'." The "host" and the "idols" are always treated as distinct categories.
- **The "queen of heaven" is not identified with Ashtoreth:** Jeremiah 44:17ff mentions the "queen of heaven" (apparently the moon), but "is never identified in the Bible with Ashtoreth or any other deity that the Bible knows by name."
- **Ezekiel doesn't link sun worship to Shamash:** Ezekiel sees elders bowing eastward to the sun (Ezek 8:16) but "fails to link this solar cult with that of the 'idols of Israel'... Nor does he give any hint that this deified sun bears any of the personal mythological traits of the Assyro-Babylonian Shamash."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the descriptive observations — Kaufmann has identified a genuine textual phenomenon. The Bible really does treat named pagan gods in a remarkably flat, de-mythologized way.

## Stakes

One of Kaufmann's most striking observations, deserving more scholarly attention. The biblical treatment of named pagan gods really IS different from how pagan texts treat their own gods — and this difference requires explanation.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars might argue that the Bible's flattening of named gods reflects the monotheistic framework — once you believe YHWH is the only real God, other "gods" are ontologically demoted to mere names. Additionally, some biblical texts DO preserve functional/mythological characterizations — Deuteronomy 32:8-9, Psalm 82, and Ugaritic parallels suggest the biblical authors inherited and transformed mythological categories they certainly knew.

**Alternative reading:** The Bible's de-mythologized treatment of named pagan gods reflects a deliberate theological strategy: deny pagan deities any substantive reality by refusing to describe them in terms that would acknowledge their mythological identity. Calling Ashtoreth "goddess of love and war" would grant her a domain, a function, a reality. Calling her "god of the Sidonians" reduces her to a national marker.

## Assessment

This is one of Kaufmann's best and most underappreciated observations. The Bible's treatment of named pagan gods really is strangely flat. There IS something genuinely distinctive about how the biblical texts handle foreign deities — they're reduced to national identifiers with no mythological content. But Kaufmann's explanation (genuine ignorance) is less persuasive than the alternative (theological delegitimation through demythologization). The fact that the Bible knows the *names* of these gods at all presupposes contact with cultures that worshipped them.
