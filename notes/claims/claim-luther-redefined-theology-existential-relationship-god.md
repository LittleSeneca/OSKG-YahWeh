# schmid-ht-2-4.3: Luther redefined theology as existential relationship with God

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§2-4 — Concept of Theology from Plato to Gabler]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §3

## The Claim

"The Reformation introduced an influential modification to the concept...This semantic shift involved a change from defining 'theology' in terms of the doctrine of God to defining it in terms of the relationship of the person with God." Luther: "The proper subject of theology is man, sinful, guilty, and abandoned, and God the justifier and savior of sinful man." And: "true theology is practical, therefore speculative theology belongs in hell with the devil."

## Evidence

- Luther, *Lectures on Psalm 2, 45, and 51* (1532): theology's subject is sinful humanity and justifying God
- Luther, *Table Talks*: "true theology is practical"
- Luther: "God and faith belong in one heap"
- Luther "continued to hold firmly to the notion of theology despite his rejection of Aristotelian-influenced scholasticism"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Luther quotations are directly cited and well-known. The Reformation's existential turn is standard intellectual history.

## Stakes

This Luther move is the ROOT of the later confusion between "theology" and "religion." By defining theology as the relationship with God rather than doctrine about God, Luther made theology overlap with what later thinkers would call "religion." Schmid traces the current terminological muddle to this Reformation moment.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes the Reformation's existential turn. The question is whether Schmid's genealogy (connecting Luther's move to the later theology/religion confusion) is correct or overdetermined.

**Alternative reading:** Luther's redefinition was a recovery of biblical theology, not a confusion. The modern theology/religion problem traces to the Enlightenment, not the Reformation.

## Assessment

This is a genuinely illuminating historical observation. The theology/religion distinction that plagues modern scholarship (is theology normative? historical? descriptive?) traces back to Luther's decision to keep the term "theology" while redefining its content to be practically indistinguishable from what would later be called "religion." Schmid's genealogy explains why the confusion exists — it's not just sloppy thinking, it's built into the term's history.
