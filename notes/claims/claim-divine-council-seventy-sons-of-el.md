# day-ygc-1.3: The OT's divine council derives from the seventy sons of El

**Source:** [[Day — Chapter 1 — Yahweh and El]] — John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* (2000), Chapter 1: Yahweh and El

## The Claim

The "sons of God" in the OT (Gen 6:2; Job 1:6; Ps 29:1; Deut 32:8) are directly inherited from the Ugaritic concept of the seventy sons of El and his consort Asherah. "That this is certain can be established from the fact that both were seventy in number" (p. 24).

## Evidence

### 1. Ugaritic Precedent
At Ugarit: "the seventy sons of Asherah (Athirat)" (*šbʻm. bn. ʼaṯrt*, KTU² 1.4.VI.46). Since Asherah was El's consort, these are El's sons (p. 24). The concept predates Israel by centuries.

### 2. Deut 32:8 — The Text-Critical Smoking Gun
The original reading: "When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance... he fixed the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the **sons of God**" (*bene ʼelohim*). This is supported by:
- 4QDeut (Qumran) — the oldest Hebrew witness
- LXX (Greek translation)
- Symmachus, Old Latin, Syro-Hexaplaric manuscript
- Against MT's "sons of Israel" (*bene yiśraʼel*) — a deliberate scribal alteration driven by discomfort with polytheistic overtones (p. 24)

### 3. Genesis 10
Lists precisely seventy nations, corresponding to seventy guardian deities — the same number as Ugarit's sons of El.

### 4. Later Jewish Tradition Confirms
- 1 Enoch explicitly speaks of seventy guardian angels of the nations
- Targum Pseudo-Jonathan does the same
- The tradition survived even after the MT was altered (p. 25)

### 5. Name Pattern
The OT NEVER calls them "sons of Yahweh" — always "sons of God" with words containing *ʼl* (*bene haʼelohim, bene ʼelohim, bene ʼelim*). This "finds a ready explanation in their origin in the sons of the Canaanite god El" (p. 25).

### Replies to Critics
- **Whybray:** Claims "seventy" is a conventional large number. Day: No — Genesis 10 lists precisely seventy nations. This is specific, not generic.
- **Block:** Suggests the seventy gods are a back-projection from the seventy nations of Gen 10. Day: But seventy sons of El are "already attested prior to Deut 32:8, as the Ugaritic texts prove" — so Block has the direction of influence reversed (p. 25)

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The convergence of Ugaritic numbers, Deut 32:8 textual criticism, MT alteration confirming embarrassment, and later Jewish tradition makes this nearly unassailable.

## Stakes

This directly connects Israel's worldview to Canaanite mythology. The divine council is not a Hebrew innovation — it's inherited furniture. The MT scribes' alteration is the smoking gun: you don't censor what doesn't bother you. The seventy nations of Genesis 10 correspond to seventy guardian deities — Israel's understanding of its own place among the nations is built on polytheistic foundations.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** D.I. Block (*The Gods of the Nations*, 1988) — the only named counter-voice, whom Day dispatches efficiently. [[claim-]] Heiser (not mentioned here but in the broader debate) agrees about the "sons of God" reading but interprets them as created beings, not rival deities.

**Alternative reading:** Heiser's view: these are legitimate divine beings but subordinate, created, and part of Yahweh's bureaucracy — not a polytheistic pantheon in the Canaanite sense. The number 70 is shared but the *ontology* differs.

## Assessment

Day is clearly right about the derivation. The MT alteration alone proves the original was embarrassing. Where Day overstates slightly is the implication that this *necessarily* proves Israelite polytheism — Heiser's alternative (subordinate created beings) is theologically distinct but textually possible. The shared number cannot be coincidence — Ugarit and Israel are drawing from the same conceptual reservoir.
