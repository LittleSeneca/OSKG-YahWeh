# albertz-hir-4.11: Deutero-Isaiah dissolved the fusion of divine and political power that characterized pre-exilic kingship theology

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 4 — Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"The fusion of divine and political power which had happened in pre-exilic kingship theology had to be dissolved." The Deutero-Isaiah group "turned [kingship and Zion theology] into a critical attitude to domination."

## Evidence

- Cyrus is Yahweh's anointed (45.1) and executes Yahweh's will — BUT only temporarily, and he's a FOREIGN king. The Davidic line is NOT restored.
- After Babylon falls, Cyrus disappears from view entirely. "For the time after that, the Davidic king was not just temporarily displaced from his position by Cyrus, but no longer had any place at all in its view of the future."
- Yahweh himself will lead the exiles home and assume royal rule on Zion (40.9-11; 52.7-12)
- The title "king" is used only as "king of Jacob/Israel" (41.21; 44.6; 43.15) — NOT "king of the gods" or "king of the nations" with their imperial connotations
- Three things are excluded: (1) increase in Israel's political power, (2) military subjection of other nations by Israel, (3) Israel's rule over the world
- The controversy with nations is a LEGAL dispute, not a battle — Israel participates only as witness
- The Davidic covenant is transferred to Israel (55.3ff.): Israel becomes "crown pretender" (nagid) and "commander" (mesawwe) of nations — but these are moral/religious offices, not political authority
- The servant songs (especially 52.13-53.12) offer the most radical reinterpretation: Israel brings salvation precisely through its suffering and helplessness, not through power

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The textual evidence for the dissolution of political power is strong. The interpretation of the servant songs as collective/Israel-referring is contested but well-argued.

## Stakes

This is perhaps the most politically significant claim in the chapter. It means that at the very moment Israelite religion formulated monotheism, it ALSO renounced the fusion of divine and political power. The universal God is not imperial. "Given the later campaigns of conquest under Christian auspices which were justified by the absolutist claim of monotheism, it is all the more important to point to the sociological fact that Israel made the breakthrough to monotheism in a situation of absolute political helplessness."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the servant songs as referring to an individual (prophet, king, messianic figure) rather than collective Israel. Scholars who see Deutero-Isaiah as more nationalistic and less universalist than Albertz does. Those who argue the political quietism reflects imperial co-optation, not principled renunciation.

**Alternative reading:** Deutero-Isaiah's universalism is rhetorical cover for disappointed nationalism — when Israel couldn't have a real empire, it invented a spiritual one. The renunciation of political power was necessity dressed as virtue.
