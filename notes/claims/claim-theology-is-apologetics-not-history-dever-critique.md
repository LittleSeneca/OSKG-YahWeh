# dever-dghw-1-2.2: Theology is apologetics, not history

**Source:** [[Dever — Introduction and Chapters I-II — Methodology]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Introduction and Chapters I-II

## The Claim

"Theology... remains essentially an enterprise of apologetics, and moreover seems to conceive of religion merely in terms of ideas rather than of practice." Theological approaches to the Hebrew Bible — represented by the Old Testament Theology school (Eichrodt, von Rad, G. Ernest Wright) — are "prescriptive rather than descriptive." They ask what the Bible *should* mean for modern faith communities, not what ancient Israelites actually believed and did. This makes theology a dead end for historical inquiry. Dever's survey of the "History of the History" traces how each successive school (History of Religions, Myth and Ritual, OT Theology, Sociological approaches, Recent revival) moved incrementally toward integrating material evidence — but theologians remained trapped in texts.

## Evidence

**Dever's survey of scholarly approaches and his critiques:**

| School | Key Figures | Dever's Critique |
|--------|------------|-----------------|
| History of Religions | Wellhausen, Gunkel | Too broad, too diachronic, ignores material evidence |
| Myth and Ritual | Hooke, Engnell | Too theoretical, imposes patterns |
| Old Testament Theology | Eichrodt, von Rad, Wright | Apologetics, not history; "prescriptive rather than descriptive" |
| Sociological approaches | Gottwald, Mendenhall | Better, but still too dependent on texts |
| Recent revival | Smith, Zevit, Keel/Uehlinger, Albertz | Much better, but still under-utilizing archaeology |

**Dever's sharpest criticism is reserved for theologians:**
"Theology... remains essentially an enterprise of apologetics, and moreover seems to conceive of religion merely in terms of ideas rather than of practice."

**His strongest praise goes to:**
- Zevit (*The Religions of Ancient Israel*, 2001) — for his openness to archaeological evidence
- Keel/Uehlinger (*Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God*, 1998) — for their iconographic approach

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Dever's critique captures a real methodological tension but overstates the case — some theological scholarship is genuinely historical, and the boundary between "descriptive" and "prescriptive" is harder to draw than Dever implies.

## Stakes

If Dever is right, the entire tradition of Old Testament theology is, from a historical perspective, a category error. It asks questions about divine revelation and theological meaning that cannot be answered with historical methods — and it mistakes those questions for historical ones. For the Truth Project, this means that most of what has been written about "the religion of Israel" in theological terms is irrelevant to the historical question of what ancient Israelites actually believed and practiced.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Theologians and biblical scholars who see the theological interpretation of the Hebrew Bible as a legitimate intellectual discipline in its own right, not reducible to history. Collins, Brueggemann, and others would argue that theological readings can coexist with historical ones.

**Alternative reading:** The best theology is historically informed and the best history is theologically aware. The wall Dever builds between them is too high — scholars like von Rad were doing something different from history, but their work is not therefore worthless for historical understanding.

## Assessment

Dever's critique of theology is rhetorically effective but intellectually uneven. He's right that much OT Theology was prescriptive and confessionally motivated. But his blanket dismissal overlooks the genuinely historical work done by scholars working within theological frameworks — and his own work is hardly free of ideological commitments (he wants to dethrone patriarchal monotheism as much as any theologian wants to defend it). The truth is that everyone has commitments; the question is whether they're transparent about them. On that score, Dever practices what he preaches.
