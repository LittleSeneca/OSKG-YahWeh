# sommer-bog-2.1: J and E sources preserved the ANE fluidity model — Yhwh had multiple manifestations

**Source:** [[Sommer — The Bodies of God]] — Benjamin D. Sommer, *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009), Chapter 2

## The Claim

The oldest strands of biblical tradition (the J and E sources) portray Yhwh using the same fluidity model found in Mesopotamia and Canaan. Yhwh has multiple local manifestations, the *mal'akh* (angel/messenger) is not a separate being but a small-scale manifestation of Yhwh himself, and the line between Yhwh and his manifestations is deliberately blurred.

This is NOT polytheism. It's a way of understanding how the ONE God can be present in multiple places and forms. The "local Yhwhs" are not separate gods — they are manifestations of the same God. The *mal'akh* is not an angel in the later sense — it's Yhwh in a form humans can encounter without dying.

## Evidence

**Local Yhwh manifestations:**
- Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (8th century): "Yhwh of Samaria" and "Yhwh of Teman" — parallel to "Ishtar of Arbela" / "Ishtar of Nineveh"
- 2 Samuel 15:7: Absalom needs to go to Hebron to worship "Yhwh of Hebron" — because Yhwh of Hebron is distinct from Yhwh of Jerusalem. "In Jerusalem one can pray to Yhwh generally or to Yhwh of Jerusalem, but not to Yhwh of Hebron!"
- Psalm 99:2: "Yhwh in Zion" — a localized manifestation

**The *mal'akh* as avatar:**
- In J and E texts, the *mal'akh* (usually translated "angel" but literally "messenger") is NOT a separate being. It is "a small-scale manifestation of God's own presence" — something "very similar to an avatar in Indian religions."
- "The distinction between the messenger and God is murky." The *mal'akh* can speak AS God, be addressed AS God, and is treated AS God — not as a separate being.
- Genesis 16: Hagar encounters the *mal'akh* of Yhwh, then says "Have I really seen God and remained alive?" She saw the *mal'akh* — and calls it God.
- Genesis 22: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh calls to Abraham from heaven saying "you have not withheld your son from ME" — the *mal'akh* speaks as Yhwh, not as a messenger FOR Yhwh.
- Exodus 3: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appears in the burning bush, then "Yhwh saw that Moses turned aside to see, and God called to him from the bush." The *mal'akh* IS Yhwh, in a localized manifestation.
- Judges 6: The *mal'akh* of Yhwh appears to Gideon, then "Yhwh turned to him" — seamless transition from *mal'akh* to Yhwh.

**The fluid self:**
- In J and E, Yhwh can be in heaven AND on earth simultaneously. He can speak ABOUT himself in the third person.
- "It is possible that Yhwh temporarily overlaps with some heavenly beings who do God's bidding" — the distinction between God and the heavenly court is porous.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH that J and E preserve the fluidity model (the textual evidence is strong, especially the *mal'akh* passages). MEDIUM that this represents "original" Israelite theology rather than one strand among several. The dating of J and E is, as always, contested.

## Stakes

If Sommer is right about J and E, then the "polytheism → monotheism" narrative is too simple. The earliest Israelite theologians WERE monotheists — but their monotheism included multiple divine bodies, local manifestations, and a fluid self. This is not a stage BETWEEN polytheism and monotheism. It IS monotheism, just of a kind that later Judaism and Christianity rejected.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date J and E later (arguing the fluidity is not "original"), scholars who read the *mal'akh* as a distinct angelic being, scholars who see the local Yhwh manifestations as evidence of polytheism rather than fluid monotheism.

**Alternative reading:** The local Yhwh manifestations ARE evidence of polytheism — each "Yhwh of X" was originally a different god. The *mal'akh* IS a separate angelic being; the blurring in the text is literary, not theological. J and E are not monotheistic.

## Assessment

Sommer's reading of the *mal'akh* passages is compelling. The Genesis 16 and Exodus 3 examples are particularly difficult to read any other way: the text deliberately blurs the distinction between the *mal'akh* and Yhwh. Whether this represents "original" Israelite theology or one strand among several is harder to establish. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions are strong evidence that the local-Yhwh pattern Sommer describes was real, not just a literary convention.
