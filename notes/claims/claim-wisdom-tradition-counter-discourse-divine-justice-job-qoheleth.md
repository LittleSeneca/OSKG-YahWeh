# lewis-ocg-9.4: The wisdom tradition represents genuine counter-discourse questioning divine justice

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 9 — King and Judge]] — Theodore Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 9

## The Claim

The reflective side of Israelite religion "made space for direct challenges to the Almighty. One need not only imagine taking God to court, says Job; an actual legal case can be filed." Qoheleth "affirms that Yahweh is indeed just... but that lessens not the absurd and maddening ways in which divine retribution plays out."

## Evidence

- Job files a formal legal complaint against God (Job 31:35-37: "Oh, that I had one to hear me! Here is my signature! Let the Almighty answer me!")
- The divine speeches (Job 38-41) respond not with legal argument but with theophany — overwhelming Job with the majesty of creation
- Qoheleth observes that "the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong... but time and chance happen to them all" (Qoh 9:11)
- Both traditions AFFIRM divine justice while acknowledging its inscrutability
- Lewis contrasts this with prophetic counter-discourses (Nathan's parable, Elijah's indictment, Amos's oracles) which prosecute HUMAN injustice within a framework of assumed divine justice
- Wisdom counter-discourse questions DIVINE justice itself — a more radical move

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** VERY HIGH that wisdom literature questions divine justice. MEDIUM on whether it "affirms" it — Job's friends affirm it; Job himself is more ambiguous until the end.

## Stakes

Whether Israelite religion was intellectually honest. The presence of Job and Qoheleth in the canon shows that questioning God was not only permitted but preserved as Scripture. This is one of the most remarkable features of the Hebrew Bible.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Traditional readings that see Job as ultimately submissive and Qoheleth as a foil for orthodox wisdom. The debate is about tone and resolution, not about the existence of the questioning.

**Alternative reading:** Job and Qoheleth are late, marginal texts — they represent the exhaustion of wisdom, not its representative voice.

## Assessment

This section is excellent. Lewis's legal framing of Job ("filing a complaint") is sharper than the usual "theodicy" framing. The juxtaposition of prophetic counter-discourses (prosecuting human injustice) with wisdom counter-discourses (questioning divine justice) is a useful typology.
