# kaufmann-ri-11.8: Hosea was the first to condemn militarism and the power state as religious-moral sins

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XI — Amos and Hosea]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XI

## The Claim

"The first biblical author, indeed the first man in history, to condemn militarism as a religious-moral sin was Hosea." He is "the father of all Utopians who believe in the power of love to bring salvation to man. He is the first to repudiate the power state."

## Evidence

- Hosea 8:14: "Israel has forgotten its maker, and built palaces, and Judah has multiplied walled cities"
- Hosea 10:13f: "For you have trusted in your chariotry, in the multitude of your mighty men. Therefore tumult will arise among your people, and all your fortresses will be ravaged"
- Hosea 14:4 (the future confession): "We will not ride on horses nor say, 'Our god' to the work of our hands" — trust in chariotry is as idolatrous as trust in an idol
- Hosea 7:7: "All their kings have fallen, not one of them calls upon me" — the monarchy has failed
- Hosea 8:4: "They made kings, without asking me; officers, without my knowing" — kings made without prophetic sanction
- Hosea 13:10f: echoes Samuel's warning about monarchy — it was "born in sin and rebellion"
- Hosea 5:13: reliance on Assyria and Egypt for healing is breach of faith with YHWH

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann accurately characterizes Hosea's critique of militarism and politics. Whether Hosea was literally the "first man in history" to make this critique is unverifiable and somewhat triumphalist, but the observation that Hosea's anti-militarism is a distinctive contribution is sound.

## Stakes

The origins of the prophetic critique of state power. If Kaufmann is right, Hosea stands at the beginning of a tradition that runs through Isaiah's anti-Egyptian alliance polemic to Jeremiah's surrender advocacy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition generally sees Hosea's political critique as part of a broader 8th-century prophetic movement reacting to Assyrian pressure. Amos also critiques the ruling class, though not militarism per se. Isaiah's anti-alliance oracles are roughly contemporary.

**Alternative reading:** Hosea's anti-militarism is less a principled philosophical position than a theopolitical stance: reliance on military power is wrong BECAUSE it displaces reliance on YHWH, not because militarism is inherently evil. The distinction matters — Hosea isn't a pacifist; he's a theocrat.

## Assessment

Kaufmann is right to highlight this as a distinctive Hosean contribution, but his characterization of Hosea as "the father of all Utopians" strains the text. Hosea's vision is not utopian in the sense of imagining a transformed political order — it's a call to return to covenant faithfulness within the existing (monarchic) order, guided by prophetic word. Kaufmann's language here reflects his own theological commitments more than Hosea's.
