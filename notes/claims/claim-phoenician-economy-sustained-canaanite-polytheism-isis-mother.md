# keel-ggi-7.2: The Phoenician economy sustained "Canaanite" polytheism through Egyptian and Greek channels, with Isis-mother imagery dominating and Greek influence introducing a sharp gender-role separation

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part VII — Iron Age III]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part VII, §219

## The Claim

While Judah was stamping out goddess imagery, the Phoenician coast was doubling down on it — but with new sources and styles. "The Phoenicians and Punics are the actual heirs of the 'Canaanite' culture." Isis nursing the infant Horus ("Isis lactans") was the most commonly represented deity on scarabs from 'Atlit, Tel Megadim, and Lohame Ha-Geta'ot. She appears almost exclusively in her role as mother. Meanwhile, Greek influence introduced a sharp gender binary: for males, a high valuation of the body and physical-athletic power (Heracles, Zeus, nude gymnast); for females, deified motherhood. The goddess is no longer the naked, sexually-presented Astarte of the Bronze Age — she's Isis the mother.

## Evidence

- **Isis lactans dominates** (§219): "Clearly the most commonly represented deity was Isis, nursing the infant Horus." Scarabs from 'Atlit, Tel Megadim, and Lohame Ha-Geta'ot all show this motif (illuss. 363–364). "She is the goddess par excellence on the scarabs. It is no accident that she appears almost exclusively in her role as mother, obviously corresponding to a trend of the time."
- **Terra-cotta shift toward motherhood** (§219): Naked-goddess-with-breasts types persist (illus. 377), but new types gain popularity: a seated pregnant woman (illus. 365) and a goddess with a child. "The great majority of the terra-cotta figures from Iron Age III come from *favissae*, meaning that they are discarded cultic and votive statuettes."
- **Tinnit sign** (§219): Figurines from a shipwreck near Shave Ziyyon are stamped with the Tinnit sign — identifying the Phoenician-Punic goddess by name.
- **Male imagery shifts to Zeus and Heracles** (§219): A bearded, enthroned Zeus figure (Greek-Cypriot influenced, possibly descendant of El/Baalshamem) appears. Heracles fighting a lion or as war hero enters the repertoire (illuss. 367a–c from 'Atlit), even appearing in the conservative terra-cotta industry (illus. 368 from Lachish). The nude gymnast (illus. 369) shows "with absolute clarity that the increasing Greek influence resulted in the spread of a value system that recognized a clear separation of gender roles. For males, it encouraged a high valuation of the body and physical-athletic power; for females, it deified motherhood."
- **Bes as "Lord of the Lions"** (§219): A pictorial constellation combining Egyptian and Asiatic themes (illuss. 366a–b from 'Atlit and Ashkelon).

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Phoenician material is well-published and the iconographic shift is clear. The gender-role observation (warrior/athlete male vs. nursing mother female) is a significant interpretive insight.

## Stakes

This is the counter-narrative to Judah. The Phoenician coast preserves and evolves the Canaanite polytheistic heritage — but even here, the goddess is being reshaped. She's no longer the naked, sexually-presented Astarte of the Bronze Age. She's Isis the mother. The shift from erotic goddess to maternal goddess is a major religious development of Iron Age III that has implications for how we understand the eventual emergence of the Virgin Mary and the Wisdom traditions.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Stern (1982) catalogs the material but doesn't draw strong conclusions about gender ideology. Some scholars might argue the Isis-mother emphasis is simply Egyptian influence rather than a genuine shift in conceptions of the goddess.

**Alternative reading:** The emphasis on motherhood could reflect changing social conditions (population recovery after deportations?) rather than theological development.

## Assessment

The juxtaposition with Greek athletic male imagery is striking and probably deliberate. The authors are right to see a gender-role ideology being transmitted through imported imagery. This Phoenician gender binary — warrior male, mother female — is the exact opposite of what postexilic Judah was doing (where both the warrior goddess and the mother goddess were being erased, and Yahweh absorbed all functions).
