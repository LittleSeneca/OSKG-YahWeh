# romer-inv-6.3: The golden calves were legitimate Yhwh symbols

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 6 — Cult of Yhwh in Israel]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 6

## The Claim

Jeroboam's bulls at Dan and Bethel (1 Kings 12:28-31) were not foreign idols. They were legitimate representations of Yhwh using traditional El/Baal bull iconography. When El and Yhwh merged, Yhwh inherited El's bull imagery. The biblical polemic against the calves is a late Judean perspective that delegitimized northern Yahwism after Israel fell in 722 BCE. The Exodus 32 golden calf story was composed retroactively to condemn the northern bull cult — and to shift blame from Moses' priestly line (which served at Dan, per Judges 18:30) to Aaron's line (the Jerusalem priesthood).

## Evidence

- **Bull iconography for El is well-attested at Ugarit.** El is called "Bull El" (*ṯr 'il*) in the Ugaritic texts. When El and Yhwh merged, Yhwh inherited El's bull imagery.
- **Samaria ostracon 41:** The name *'glyw* ("Young bull is Yaw") confirms bull language was used for Yhwh in the north.
- **Bronze bull figurine:** A 12th-century bronze bull from the Ephraim hill country shows bull iconography predated Jeroboam by centuries.
- **Taanach cult stand (10th century):** Depicts bull imagery in an Israelite cultic context contemporary with Jeroboam.
- **The Exodus 32 polemic:** The narrative portrays Aaron — ancestor of the Jerusalem priesthood — making the golden calf. But Moses' grandson (Judges 18:30) actually serves at Dan. The story was composed to shift blame from Moses' line to Aaron's, while condemning the northern cult as idolatrous from its founding moment.
- **The biblical polemic itself:** "Here are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up from the land of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28) — the Deuteronomistic editor frames Jeroboam's declaration as apostasy, but it is almost certainly Yhwh who is meant.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The bull-Yhwh connection is well-evidenced (the ostracon, the figurines, the Taanach stand — three independent sources). The polemical reading of Exodus 32 is plausible but depends on late dating of the narrative. The shift-of-blame argument (Moses' line → Aaron's line) is clever but speculative.

## Stakes

One of the Bible's paradigmatic acts of idolatry — the golden calf — may have been legitimate Yahwism. The story we all know (Aaron making the calf while Moses is on Sinai) was written to condemn northern Israel's bull cult AFTER the north had already fallen. It's propaganda disguised as history. The "idolatry" was just different Yahwism — one that used traditional El imagery that southern Judeans later rejected.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see the bull cult as genuinely Canaanite/Baal worship imported into Israel. Conservatives who read Exodus 32 as historical memory of a real apostasy at Sinai.

**Alternative reading:** The bulls WERE Baal symbols, not Yhwh symbols. Jeroboam deliberately introduced Canaanite iconography to compete with Jerusalem's aniconic Yhwh cult. The Exodus 32 polemic, while theologically shaped, preserves a genuine memory of northern idolatry.

## Assessment

Römer's argument is strongest on the epigraphic evidence: the Samaria ostracon proves northerners used bull language for Yhwh. The bronze figurine and Taanach stand prove bull iconography was native Israelite, not imported. The Exodus 32 reading is more speculative but fits the pattern: every biblical polemic against northern religion is post-722 retrojection. If you accept that premise — and Römer has built it carefully across Chapters 1-5 — the calf argument follows naturally. The bulls were Yhwh symbols. The south made them "idolatry" after the north couldn't defend itself.
