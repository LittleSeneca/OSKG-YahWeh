# keel-ggi-5.1: Anthropomorphic Goddess Imagery Extremely Rare in Iron Age IIB — Completely Absent from Hebrew Glyptic Art

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part V — Iron Age IIB — Baal El Yahweh and His Asherah]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part V

## The Claim

"Contrary to what is often thought to be the case, terra-cottas from Iron Age IIB with images of a 'naked goddess' have not been found 'by the hundreds,' but are relatively rare by comparison with the so-called pillar figurines that appear in the following periods... In the glyptic art of Iron Age IIB from Israel and Judah, there is not a single seal known to us on which a goddess might have been depicted anthropomorphically. The absence of the goddess from Hebrew name seals is especially significant." During the very period when the "Yahweh and his Asherah" inscriptions were written (ca. 850-735 BCE), the material evidence for goddess worship in Israel and Judah is minimal.

## Evidence

- Only 2-3 specimens of female deity depictions in Israel/Judah during Iron Age IIB: an ivory ointment spoon from Hazor Str. VI showing a goddess transparently (palm tree on front, face with doves on back), and an ivory "woman at the window" plaque from Samaria
- The "Naked Goddess" plaques (with accentuated genitalia and breasts) are found in Philistine territory (Ashdod, Tel Batash/Timnah), NOT in Israel or Judah — "they seem not to have been accepted into the repertoire in Israel or Judah"
- The Samaria plaque with a "naked goddess" dates to the very end of Iron Age IIB (destroyed 701), right at the transition to IIC
- "Not a single seal" from Israel/Judah glyptic art depicts a goddess anthropomorphically
- Hebrew name seals show zero goddesses — one possible exception (illus. 331) is probably an imported piece with a Hebrew inscription added later
- The pillar figurines (Judean Pillar Figurines) "came into Palestine in great numbers only toward the end of the eighth century" — i.e., Iron Age IIC, not IIB

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The authors have catalogued 8,500 seals. The absence of goddess imagery from Hebrew name seals is a statistically significant finding — these were personal items chosen by their owners to represent their religious identity. If goddess worship was widespread, it should show up on personal seals.

## Stakes

The entire "Yahweh had a consort" thesis depends on the assumption that Asherah was widely worshiped as a goddess in monarchic Israel. If the period's own iconography shows minimal goddess representation, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions must mean something other than what Dever and Romer claim.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Dever (1990, 2005) argues that the pillar figurines ARE Asherah and represent widespread goddess worship. But Keel & Uehlinger's crucial point is that pillar figurines belong to Iron Age IIC (7th century), not IIB (9th-8th centuries) — the period of the inscriptions. Dever conflates the periods.

**Alternative reading:** The "absence of evidence" could be evidence of aniconism specifically for seals, not for religion in general. Seals are elite objects; goddess worship could have been a non-elite phenomenon.

## Assessment

The chronological distinction between IIB and IIC figurines is devastating to Dever's argument. He uses 7th-century pillar figurines as evidence for 8th-century goddess worship. Keel & Uehlinger's catalogue-based dating is far more precise. The near-total absence of goddess imagery from Hebrew name seals during Iron Age IIB is extremely hard to reconcile with the "Yahweh had a consort" thesis.
