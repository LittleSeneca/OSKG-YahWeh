# kaufmann-ri-1.7: The prophetic corpus is unanimous — no prophet engages pagan mythology

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch I — The Basic Problem]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter I

## The Claim

"We search in vain: not one word have the prophets for mythological beliefs, not once do they repudiate them." "Their whole condemnation revolves around the taunt of fetishism."

## Evidence

Kaufmann surveys prophet by prophet:
- **Amos:** "Hardly mentions the belief in gods... fails entirely to express himself on the nature of the polytheism which he allegedly leaves behind."
- **Hosea:** Even in chapters 1-3, which reflect the Baal worship of Jezebel's age, "none of the distinctive mythological features of the Canaanite Baal are mentioned." Idolatry is "the worship of 'the work of hands'."
- **Isaiah:** His *Götterdämmerung* "is the twilight of silver and gold images; he makes no allusion to polytheistic beliefs."
- **Micah:** "And I will cut off your graven images and your pillars... and you shall no more worship the work of your hands."
- **Jeremiah:** He "amply sets forth his conception of pagan religion: it is the worship of wood and stone." "The 'other gods' are not the mythological beings of authentic paganism, nor even demons, but the handiwork of men."
- **Ezekiel:** Despite mentioning the "lamenting of Tammuz," "he has not a word to say about the myths of Tammuz or any other god." "It is most remarkable that Ezekiel, fascinated as he is by erotic symbolism, never once utilizes the sexual themes of mythology."
- **Second Isaiah:** The most extensive anti-idolatry polemic (Isa 44:9-18) "plays on one theme only: the monstrous folly of believing that idols can be gods." If Second Isaiah knew Babylonian mythology, "what a mine of material he would have found for his satires" — yet he never uses it.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** HIGH on the descriptive claim (the prophetic polemic really IS overwhelmingly about fetishism); MEDIUM on whether this pattern characterizes ALL prophets or admits exceptions. The Ezekiel/Tammuz passage is a significant problem.

## Stakes

This is the empirical center of gravity for Kaufmann's entire argument. If the prophetic record — across centuries, across different prophets, across different historical contexts — consistently avoids engaging with pagan mythology, this needs explanation. Kaufmann's explanation (they didn't know it) is one possibility; editorial homogenization, rhetorical strategy, and theological avoidance are others.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars acknowledge the pattern but explain it differently. The prophets were addressing an Israelite audience practicing syncretistic worship — combining YHWH worship with Canaanite religious practices. Their target wasn't Babylonian mythology; it was popular syncretism. Moreover, the prophetic books have been edited by Deuteronomistic and post-exilic redactors who may have homogenized the anti-idolatry rhetoric.

**Alternative reading:** The prophetic anti-idolatry polemic reflects a specific historical reality: Israelite syncretism primarily involved adopting Canaanite *practices* (high places, standing stones, Asherah poles, child sacrifice) rather than adopting Canaanite *mythology* as a belief system. The prophets attacked what people were actually *doing*, not what they *believed*.

## Assessment

The Ezekiel/Tammuz passage (Ezek 8:14) is a significant problem for Kaufmann, and his treatment of it is weak. He asks: "Did Ezekiel or his contemporaries know the myth of the death of youthful Tammuz? Or did they know only the pagan rites?" This is special pleading — he's suggesting Ezekiel saw women weeping for Tammuz but somehow didn't know why. This is the kind of special pleading that undermines his thesis. If Israelites could adopt pagan rites without understanding the mythology, then the Bible's silence about mythology doesn't prove Israelites didn't practice pagan rites.
