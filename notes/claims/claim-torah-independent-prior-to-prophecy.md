# kaufmann-ri-5.1: Torah-Group Literature Is Independent of and Prior to Literary Prophecy

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch V — The Sources]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Ch V

## The Claim

The Torah-group (Pentateuch + Former Prophets) and the literary prophets represent "distinct domains." The Torah cannot be understood as "a later outgrowth of prophetic faith." Literary prophecy was not "the fountainhead or ideal source of Israelite monotheism." Instead, the Torah literature "belongs not after, but before literary prophecy. It is the literary product of the earliest stage of Israelite religion."

## Evidence

**Five arguments for Torah-group independence and priority:**

1. **Historical books never mention literary prophets.** Kings mentions only Isaiah (as foreteller/healer), not Amos, Hosea, or Jeremiah. "Had the prophetic books not been preserved, we should have known nothing of the existence of literary prophecy, let alone its distinctive ideas."

2. **Moral sin as historical factor.** The prophets denounce social corruption as grounds for national destruction. The historical books never mention social decay; for them, Israel's sin is exclusively "the worship of foreign gods and the adoption of heathen customs." Torah admonitions (Lev 26; Deut 28) specify exile for idolatry, not social injustice.

3. **Primacy of morality over cult.** The prophets' great doctrine — "I desire steadfast love, not sacrifice" (Hos 6:6) — "is never found" in the Torah-group. The Torah juxtaposes moral and cultic equally, silent on the issue. "The absence of this idea is a sign, not of a stage later than prophecy, but of an earlier stage."

4. **Centralization of worship has no roots in prophecy.** No pre-Deuteronomic prophet (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah) reproaches the north for not sacrificing at Jerusalem. The prophets attacked idolatrous rites at high places and Jerusalem alike. "The sole conclusion that could be drawn from prophetic rebukes was that the worship...must be purged of its heathen elements, but not that the high places must be destroyed."

5. **Josiah's reform was purely cultic, not social.** The reform purged the cult of YHWH of its dross but had "no trace of social reform." Josiah was motivated by the Torah book's warnings (which concern religious sins, not social corruption), not prophetic preaching. "Priests are its standard bearers," not prophets.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The observation that the Torah-group and prophetic literature have genuinely different outlooks is solid and important. The inference that this difference proves *chronological* priority rather than *institutional/theological* distinction (priestly circles vs. prophetic circles, running in parallel) is less secure.

## Stakes

If the Torah literature predates and is independent of prophecy, monotheistic law was not a prophetic creation. The prophets become traditionalists calling Israel back to a monotheism it always had — not innovators creating it. This inverts the critical narrative.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical tradition treats the differences as evidence that priestly writers ("later") selected from and modified prophetic traditions to serve institutional ends. On this view, the Torah-group's "silence" about prophetic ideas reflects deliberate selectivity, not chronological priority. Albertz (Vol1 §3.8) argues the Deuteronomic reform WAS influenced by prophetic circles (Hosea-influenced groups among the 'am hā'āreṣ), not an independent priestly creation.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann's observations about DIFFERENCE are largely correct. But difference doesn't prove sequence. Two parallel streams — priestly Torah tradition and prophetic oracular tradition — could coexist, each with its own concerns, without either being "later" than the other. The Torah-group's focus on idolatry as THE national sin may reflect the priestly institutional concern with proper cult, not chronological earliness.

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's strongest argument in the chapter. The independence of Torah literature from prophetic ideas is a genuine datum that the critical tradition has underexamined. The Torah really doesn't know prophetic eschatology, prophetic moral emphasis, or prophetic critique of the cult. The question is whether this independence proves *antiquity* or *institutional separateness*. Kaufmann assumes the former, but the latter is equally plausible. Priestly circles and prophetic circles may have operated in parallel for centuries, each producing literature reflecting their own concerns.
