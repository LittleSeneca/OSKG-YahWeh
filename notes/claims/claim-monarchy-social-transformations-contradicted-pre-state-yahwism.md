# albertz-hir-3.1-3.2.3: Social transformations under David and Solomon contradicted pre-state Yahwistic values

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §3.1-3.2 — Formation of Monarchy and Legitimation Crisis]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §3.1

## The Claim

The monarchy's social effects "can hardly be overestimated." Key changes: royal taxation (tithe, provincial levies), state forced labor (mas, extended to Israelites under Solomon), royal land grants to officials (undermining family inheritance), a new class of royal officials "alien to the ideals of tribal Israelite society," reurbanization and fortification, long-distance trade creating luxury and inequality, and conscription.

## Evidence

- Solomon's 12-province system (1 Kings 4:7-19) for provisioning the court
- Forced labor (mas) initially on foreign prisoners (David), extended to Israelites by Solomon — this directly caused the northern revolt (1 Kings 12)
- Royal land grants threatened family nahala (inheritance) — cf. Ezekiel 46:16-18's utopian regulation preventing land accumulation
- The "ideal of the small farmer" (Micah 2:2: "A man and his house, a man and his inheritance") was being destroyed
- "Whereas deurbanization was the mark of the pre-state period, with the monarchy a new reurbanization began"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The social transformations are well-documented both textually and archaeologically. The contradiction with pre-state values is a matter of interpretation but hard to dispute given the texts.

## Stakes

This sets up the prophetic critique of the 8th century — the prophets weren't innovators but conservators, appealing to pre-state values against monarchic abuses.

## Disagreement

<!-- Interpretation, not fact, is at issue — most scholars accept the social transformations occurred -->

**Alternative reading:** The social transformations may have been less novel than Albertz presents — some degree of stratification and taxation existed in pre-state period. The "ideal of the small farmer" may itself be a retrojected utopia.

## Assessment

This is the strongest section of §3.1. The social transformations are well-documented, and the contradiction with pre-state values (however idealized those values may be) is textually demonstrable. The prophets' social critique reads differently if it's understood as conservative restoration rather than innovative ethics.
