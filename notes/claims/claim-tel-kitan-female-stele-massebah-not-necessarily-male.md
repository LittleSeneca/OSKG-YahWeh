# keel-ggi-3.1: The Tel Kitan female stele proves that massebahs / standing stones are not necessarily male deities — the goddess could be represented in this medium too

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Ch III — MB Temples Tel Kitan and Megiddo]] — Othmar Keel and Christoph Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Chapter III

## The Claim

"What was suggested concerning the row of stelae at Gezer can be confirmed now at this site: stelae made of stone are not necessarily images of male deities. The characteristics depicted on the figure on this stele are clear. The gender is not identified by means of a triangle and a vertical line, like the Middle Bronze Age sheet-gold figures from Gezer, but is represented more realistically by means of sculpted genital lips."

## Evidence

- Tel Kitan (Tell Musa), ca. 15 km south of the Sea of Gennesareth, excavated 1975–1977, dated to first phase of MB IIB (ca. 1750–1650)
- A building (6.90 × 5.50 m) with a row of ~10 small massebahs made of river stone on a stone foundation, 5 m in front of it; two larger massebahs placed before the row
- One stele in the main row was carved — in "very rough fashion" — into the form of a naked woman, ca. 45 cm high (now at Museum of Nir David, Beth-Shean)
- The breasts are "hardly indicated at all" and arms are "little more than two small stumps"; unclear whether she held her breasts
- Eisenberg (1977) published the excavation

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The anatomical description is specific and unambiguous — "sculpted genital lips" leaves no room for gender ambiguity. This is an archaeological fact, not an interpretation.

## Stakes

This directly challenges the widespread assumption — both scholarly and popular — that massebahs were always male, always representations of the god (often Baal or Yahweh), and that the aniconic standing stone tradition is inherently masculine. If a goddess could be represented as a massebah, then the entire category "standing stone = male deity" collapses. This has downstream implications for interpreting the massebahs condemned in the Hebrew Bible and those found at Israelite sites like Arad.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The text does not name specific opponents, but the claim is framed as a correction to a prior assumption. The reference to Gezer suggests that earlier interpreters had treated all stelae as male. Scholars who maintain that massebahs were exclusively male cult objects (e.g., Mettinger's work on aniconism) would need to account for this counterexample.

**Alternative reading:** The stele is rudimentary — "very rough fashion" — and could be an anomaly rather than representative. One female stele among thousands of massebahs does not overturn the general pattern. It might represent a local variant rather than a widespread practice.

## Assessment

The argument is modest — they are not claiming female massebahs were common, only that the category is not inherently male. This is a useful corrective. The real significance is that it shows the goddess had a place even in the most "primitive" cultic installations, not just in the more "sophisticated" metal figurines and seals. The goddess was present across the full spectrum of cultic media — stone, metal, and glyptic.
