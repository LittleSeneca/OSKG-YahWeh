# keel-ggi-p1.5: The MB IIB weather god is primarily a vegetation/fertility deity holding a branch — not a warrior

**Source:** [[Keel Uehlinger — Part I — Middle Bronze Age IIB]] — Keel & Uehlinger, *Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel* (1998), Part I

## The Claim

"The weather god appears less frequently on Middle Bronze Age IIB scarabs. Where he does appear, his relationship with fertility is accentuated, not his aggressive nature; that aspect will become more important during the Late Bronze Age. He does in fact raise his right hand but he holds no weapon, so that the gesture depicts more likely a victory rather than a battle. In his other hand, stretched out in front of himself, the deity holds a branch or a blossom." This directly complicates the standard narrative that projects the Ugaritic Baal (warrior with club and lightning) backward onto MB IIB Canaanite religion.

## Evidence

- **Megiddo cylinder seal:** Weather god in loincloth and high pointed cap, holding club in raised left hand and axe in right — but also holding rope tied around reclining bull. Faces goddess who "pushes her clothing aside."
- **Hazor seal fragment:** Goddess "pulls up ostentatiously" her clothing; secondary motif of suckling cow — "the weather god appears in a context associated with both sexuality and fertility."
- **Scarabs (4 examples):** Weather god without weapons, raised hand = victory gesture, branch/blossom in left hand — explicitly linking him to vegetation. "This suggests a natural link to the 'Branch Goddess,' who obviously must be his consort."
- **Metal figurines:** 22 deity figurines from Megiddo sacral area — 16 female, only 6 male (Negbi 1976, 131). "Male deities are here as well, but they are by far in the minority."
- **Armed weather god exists:** MB IIA armed type at Megiddo; limestone casting mold from Stratum XIIIB for mass-producing armed god figurines

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The scarab evidence is clear: weather god holds a branch, not a weapon. The cylinder seals from Megiddo and Hazor complicate the picture. The resolution may be that both types coexisted. The prediction that the warrior aspect "will become more important during the LB" must be verified by later chapters.

## Stakes

This complicates the standard scholarly narrative about Yahweh's origins. If Yahweh is modeled on the Canaanite weather god (as Smith, Day, and Cross argue), but the MB IIB weather god is primarily a vegetation deity holding a branch, then the "Yahweh as storm/warrior god from the southern deserts" narrative may be projecting Late Bronze Age warrior imagery backward. The MB IIB weather god looks more like a fertility consort than a conquering warrior. This would support Romer's argument that Yahweh's warrior aspect developed gradually. It also raises the possibility that Yahweh absorbed the vegetation/fertility aspect of the weather god BEFORE acquiring the warrior aspect — inverting the standard narrative.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Smith (2001), who argues the West Semitic weather god is fundamentally a warrior. Day (2000), who traces Yahweh's Baal-like characteristics. Cross (1973), who sees Yahweh as a divine warrior from the earliest poetry. All rely primarily on Ugaritic texts (14th-13th c. BCE) which postdate MB IIB by centuries.

**Alternative reading:** The branch-holding weather god may be a specifically Canaanite variant, while the armed weather god is the Syrian/North Mesopotamian type. The scarabs, being largely local products, reflect the local variant. The distinction would then be geographical, not chronological.

## Assessment

The chronological argument is the most interesting but also the hardest to prove — it requires demonstrating that the armed weather god type genuinely gains prominence in LB, which depends on evidence from the rest of the book. The more solid finding is simply that a branch-holding weather god existed in MB IIB and was closely paired with the Branch Goddess. This shows that the weather god and goddess were conceived as a complementary fertility pair — he brings rain, she brings vegetation — long before any "battle with the sea" mythology dominates the iconography.
