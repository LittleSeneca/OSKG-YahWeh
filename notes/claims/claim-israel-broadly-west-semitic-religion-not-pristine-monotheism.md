# smith-obm-intro.4: Israel was broadly West Semitic in its religion — not a separate, pristine monotheistic culture from the start

**Source:** [[Smith Origins — Introduction]] — Mark S. Smith, *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Introduction

## The Claim

"The prophets seem to have been quite right in observing that Israel maintained religious practices quite like the ones conducted by its neighbors. But while the prophets thought this trend to be of secondary influence, it would seem that in fact these practices were old ones in Israel." Israel's perception of itself as a separate culture and religion "grew over a long period of time. It did not leap into being on Mount Sinai."

## Evidence

- "The Sinai narratives, largely a product of the monarchy and later periods, reflect Israel's long historical struggle to understand itself as a priestly people."
- Smith's prior book (The Early History of God) already demonstrated convergence and differentiation — Israelite religion developed FROM West Semitic polytheism, not against it.
- This book works "with a model of Israelite religion that developed in many diverse ways from a larger West Semitic background."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** This is the consensus position in critical biblical scholarship, though debated by conservatives.

## Stakes

The entire traditional narrative of Israel's unique, revealed monotheism from the beginning is inverted. Israel's distinctiveness is a LATE development, not an original condition. For theology, this raises questions about revelation vs. cultural evolution.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Conservative/traditional scholars who maintain Mosaic monotheism. Albright's school. Those who see Israel as fundamentally distinct from "Canaanite" culture.

**Alternative reading:** Even if practices were shared, Israel's THEOLOGY (covenant, exclusive worship) may have been genuinely distinctive from early on. Shared practice doesn't equal shared theology. Tigay's onomastic evidence suggests widespread popular monolatry, which supports some early distinctiveness.

## Assessment

Smith's model is persuasive for practice and conceptual structures. But the timing question matters enormously: if exclusive Yahwism was earlier than Smith thinks (as Tigay's onomastic data might suggest), the "long evolution" model gets compressed. Cross's work on El traditions also complicates the picture — if El was Israel's original god, the break with West Semitic religion may have happened differently than Smith describes.
