# romer-inv-1.1: The original pronunciation of Yhwh was "Yahô" or "Yahû," not "Yahweh"

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 1 — The Name]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 1: The God of Israel and His Name

## The Claim

"The ancient pronunciation of the name of the god of Israel was 'Yahô,' which amounts to saying that the tetragrammaton was originally a trigrammaton. The w in 'Yhwh' was not a consonant, but a mater lectionis indicating the sound 'o.'" The pronunciation "Yahweh" is probably a later theological development derived from Exodus 3's wordplay on the root "to be."

## Evidence

- **The short forms:** Theophoric names consistently use Yhw (Yahu/Yaho) — Jeremiah (*Yirmeyahu*), Isaiah (*Yeshayahu*), Jonathan (*Yehonatan*). The short form Yah appears in liturgical exclamations (*hallelu-yah*) and poetry. "This suggests that the short form of the divine name was pronounced 'Yahu/Yaho.'"
- **Babylonian transliterations (6th century BCE):** Judeans in Babylon wrote their names with *ia-a-hu-u* (Yahu) or *ia-a-wa* (Yahwa). The majority evidence points to Yahu/Yaho.
- **Elephantine papyri (5th century BCE):** The Judean community in Egypt called their god Yhw, vocalized as "Yahô" in personal names.
- **Qumran Greek manuscript (4QpapLXXLevb):** Renders the tetragrammaton as Iao — two syllables, pronounced *ia-o*, corresponding to Hebrew/Aramaic Yahô.
- **Diodorus of Sicily (1st century BCE):** Writes that Moses received laws "from the god named Iao."
- **The Masoretic substitution:** The vowels applied to Yhwh were those of *'adonay* (Lord), creating the artificial form YeHoWaH — which a 13th-century Dominican mistook as the actual pronunciation, producing "Jehovah."
- **The Church Fathers:** Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-220 CE) gives Iaoue; Epiphanius (4th century) gives Iabe; Theodoret (5th century) says Samaritans say Iabe, Jews say Aia. These suggest "Yahweh" was known in some circles.
- **Romer's resolution:** The "Yahweh" pronunciation may have developed from theological speculation on Exodus 3:14 (*'ehyeh 'aser 'ehyeh* — "I am who I am"), connecting the divine name to the root *h-y-h* ("to be"). The causative form "he who causes to be" (= creator) would yield "Yahweh." But this is a later learned construction. The ORIGINAL pronunciation was Yahô/Yahû.

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH on Yahô/Yahû being the more ancient form (the Elephantine and Babylonian evidence is strong). MEDIUM that "Yahweh" is a later theological development rather than a parallel ancient pronunciation (the Church Father evidence suggests "Yahweh" was known earlier than Romer allows).

## Stakes

Moderate. If the divine name originally meant something like "he blows" (storm god) rather than "I am who I am" (philosophical being-itself), the most famous self-revelation of God in the Bible is a later theological reinterpretation, not the original meaning. The God who said "I AM" to Moses was originally a storm god whose name meant "he blows."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who maintain "Yahweh" is the original pronunciation (based on the Church Father testimonia and the Exodus 3 wordplay being ancient rather than late).

**Alternative reading:** Both pronunciations could be ancient — Yahô in the south (Judah) and Yahweh in the north (Israel), reflecting dialect differences. The northern/southern difference in theophoric name endings (-yw in the north, -yh in the south) supports this.

## Assessment

Romer makes a strong case that Yahô/Yahû was the dominant pronunciation in the biblical period. His argument that "Yahweh" is a later theological development is plausible but not proven — the Church Father evidence is earlier than he acknowledges. The truth may be that both pronunciations coexisted, with Yahô being more common and "Yahweh" being a specialized priestly/scholarly form.
