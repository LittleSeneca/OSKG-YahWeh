# romer-inv-8.2: Seals and coins from Judah depict Yhwh in anthropomorphic form

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 8 — Statue of Yhwh]] — Thomas Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 8: The Statue of Yhwh in Judah

## The Claim

Despite the later aniconic ideal, some Israelites depicted Yhwh visually. A 7th-century seal shows a god enthroned; Persian-era coinage shows Yhwh on a winged wheel. This provides material evidence that the invisible, imageless God was not the original Israelite conception.

## Evidence

- **Hebrew seal** (7th c.): Inscribed "Elishama, son of Gedalyahu" shows a god seated on a throne flanked by two trees of life. The Yahwistic personal name strongly suggests the divine figure is Yhwh
- **Two 7th-century seals** may show Yhwh with lunar attributes — "the Yahwistic names of the owners of these seals would suggest rather an identification of the seated god with Yhwh"
- **Persian-era coin** (c. 380 BCE) from Judah: Shows a deity seated on a winged wheel with the inscription "Yehud" (Judah) — almost certainly Yhwh as sky god, using Mesopotamian iconographic conventions. The coin identifies the territory, making divine identification far more secure than on the seals
- The Yehud coin demonstrates that even in the Persian period, Yhwh could be depicted visually in official contexts

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** Medium on the seals (can't definitively prove the figure is Yhwh without an identifying inscription). Medium-high on the coin (the Yehud inscription makes the identification much more likely).

## Stakes

This provides material counter-evidence to the biblical claim that Israel was always aniconic. If Judahite officials depicted Yhwh on seals and coins, the prohibition on images was not universally observed even in periods when it supposedly existed. The concept of God as invisible and unrepresentable emerges gradually, not from Sinai.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Those who argue the enthroned figure on seals represents a generic deity or the king. The winged-wheel coin could represent a different deity syncretized with Yhwh rather than Yhwh directly.

**Alternative reading:** The seals may depict a king or generic divine figure rather than Yhwh specifically. The Yehud coin may show a syncretic deity (Yhwh depicted using Zeus/Baal-Shamem iconography) rather than a direct representation of Yhwh — which still proves visual depiction of the Israelite god but complicates the interpretation.

## Assessment

The Yehud coin is the strongest piece of evidence. The seals are suggestive but inherently ambiguous without identifying inscriptions — a Yahwistic owner's name doesn't prove the figure depicted is Yhwh, only that the owner didn't consider such imagery incompatible with Yahwism. The coin's territorial inscription ("Yehud") makes the divine identification far more secure.
