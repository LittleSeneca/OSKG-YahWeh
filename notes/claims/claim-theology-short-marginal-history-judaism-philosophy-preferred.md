# schmid-ht-6-9.1: "Theology" has a short and marginal history in Judaism — medieval Jewish reflection preferred "philosophy of religion"

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§6-9 — Jewish Reception to Implicit Theology]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §6

## The Claim

"The concept of 'theology' has a comparatively short and controversial history in Judaism, essentially limited to the modern period." Medieval Jewish thinkers expressed systematic reflection as religious philosophy, not theology. Saadia Gaon's *'Emunot we-De'ot* (933 CE) and Maimonides' *Moreh Nevukim* are philosophically grounded presentations of Jewish doctrine that avoid the term "theology."

## Evidence

- Josephus and Philo used θεολογία but in non-specified, profane Greek senses
- 19th-century *Wissenschaft des Judentums* (Zunz, Geiger) adopted "theology" programmatically — Jewish Theological Seminary of Breslau (1854)
- Works using "theology" for Jewish traditions: Weber (1897), Schechter (1909), Kohler (1918), Marmorstein (1950), Cohon (1971)
- Cohon's definition exactly mirrors Christian usage: "Theology conceptualizes religious experience"
- But the *Encyclopedia Judaica* states: "The Bible contains no systematic treatment of theological problems"
- Medieval alternatives: Saadia Gaon's *Kitab al-Amanat* (933), Maimonides' *Dalālat alḥā'irīn* — both "philosophy," not "theology"
- Modern revival: Yoram Hazony's *The Philosophy of Hebrew Scripture* (2012) — encountered "intense criticism"

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The philological and historical evidence is clear. Jewish tradition preferred "philosophy of religion" to "theology."

## Stakes

Schmid is showing that "theology" is a Christian category — its application to the HB is not neutral or universal. This supports his decision to avoid calling his work either Christian or Jewish theology. But it also raises the question: if "theology" is a Christian category, can Schmid's project genuinely escape that framing?

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Modern Jewish theologians who use "theology" as a programmatic term (e.g., Cohon, Kohler). Yoram Hazony's revival of "philosophy of Hebrew Scripture."

**Alternative reading:** The preference for "philosophy" may reflect different terminology, not different intellectual activity. The medieval Jewish thinkers were doing functionally similar work under a different name.

## Assessment

Schmid's honesty about the Christian provenance of "theology" is important. But it creates a tension he hasn't fully resolved: he wants to use "theology" (a Christian term) to describe the HB in a way that's "neither Christian nor Jewish." The Jewish tradition's preference for "philosophy" suggests that alternative framings ARE available and that Schmid's choice of "theology" is itself a confessional decision.
