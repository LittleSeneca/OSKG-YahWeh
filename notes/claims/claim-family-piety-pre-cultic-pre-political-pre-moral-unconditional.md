# albertz-hir-2.1.3: Family piety was pre-cultic, pre-political, and pre-moral — characterized by unconditional divine care and female religious experience

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol1 — §2.1 — Patriarchal Religion as Family Piety]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume I* (1994), §2.1

## The Claim

"The piety which was alive in the early Israelite families was... a pre-cultic, pre-political and pre-moral religion." Its content was "determined by the central problems of survival experienced by the farming and nomadic family." Key features: the promise of a son as paradigmatic divine intervention (short time-horizon — one pregnancy cycle, unconditional, female-centered); the god accompanying people on journeys; divine protection from other groups through non-violent means; no specialized cult personnel (the father was priest); no fixed holy places/times.

## Evidence

- **Promise of a son:** Attested multiple times in Abraham stories (Gen 16:11; 18:9f.), also in Judges 13 and II Kings 4. The wife is the original recipient — a "typical female religious experience." The time-span is short (one pregnancy). The promise is unconditional — "the moral quality of the woman is a matter of complete indifference" (cf. Gen 16:7ff on Hagar)
- **Theophany outside the cult:** The divine messenger (mal'ak) appears in everyday human encounters, not in cultic settings — "one can meet God in everyday human encounters"
- **God accompanying on journeys:** The motif of the god who is "with you" on the way (Gen 28:20; 26:3, 28; 31:5, 42) is especially important for itinerant groups — it is "a protective covering which wards off all danger"
- **Unwarlike divine action:** In contrast to tribal Yahweh who fights wars, the family god is "strikingly unwarlike." In the dispute with Gerar's shepherds over wells (Gen 26:19ff.), the god doesn't fight — he helps the family find new wells. In the Jacob-Laban conflict, the family gods only protect the treaty between them
- **Passover:** Probably originated as a family-level nomadic ritual accompanying transhumance (the spring change of pasture) — a warding off of demonic powers endangering man and beast during movement. It remained a family festival even after later integration into the Exodus tradition and centralization

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** The individual observations are well-supported from the texts. But the cumulative claim — that all these features cohere into a single "type" of religion — depends on Albertz's construction being right about which features are genuinely early and which are later projections.

## Stakes

This characterization of family piety as "pre-moral" and "unconditional" sets up a sharp contrast with Yahweh religion (which Albertz will present as ethical, conditional, warlike). The history of Israelite religion is then the story of how these two religious strata — family piety and official Yahweh religion — interacted, conflicted, and partially merged. If Albertz has overstated the contrast, the drama of his narrative collapses.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see more ethical content in family religion (e.g., the wisdom tradition's moral instruction within the family) or who see more continuity between El worship and Yahweh worship. Van der Toorn's work on family religion emphasizes moral dimensions more than Albertz does.

**Alternative reading:** The contrast between "pre-moral" family piety and "ethical" Yahweh religion may reflect Albertz's Lutheran theological commitments (law/gospel, faith/works) more than the ancient evidence. The unconditional nature of family piety sounds suspiciously like Protestant soteriology projected onto ancient families.

## Assessment

There's a genuine tension here. Albertz is both trying to reconstruct early family religion FROM Genesis (which is a late, theologically-shaped text) and arguing that the same late tradents who shaped Genesis "corrected" or suppressed elements of family religion (ancestor cult, teraphim). If the tradents could suppress ancestor cult, why couldn't they have suppressed moral dimensions of family piety? Or added unconditional elements to make the patriarchs look better? The source-critical tension is unresolved. That said, Albertz's portrait of the promise-of-a-son complex as female-centered and unconditional is textually well-grounded and striking.
