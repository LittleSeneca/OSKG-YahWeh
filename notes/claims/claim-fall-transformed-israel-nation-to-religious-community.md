# kaufmann-ri-ep.1: The fall transformed Israel from a nation into a religious community

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Epilogue and Prospect]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Epilogue

## The Claim

"The life of the people of Israel came to an end; the history of Judaism began." After the fall, "Israel ceased to be a normal nation and became a religious community." With land, temple, and king gone, "only one contact with the holy was left: the divine word."

## Evidence

- The exile shattered "the simple faith of the people in the working of the divine in their life"
- The people "yearned to reconcile themselves with God" — and the only way was obedience to the word
- "Israel's religious self-confidence having been shattered, it sought to re-establish its relationship with God upon the written word"
- The sacred literary legacy was collected, sifted, and canonized
- Prophecy died because it had been "nurtured by the simple faith in the indwelling of God in Israel's life and history" — a faith the fall destroyed
- The priesthood could provide what prophecy couldn't: transmission of fixed, ancient toroth

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The transformation from nation to text-based community is well-documented. But Kaufmann overstates the completeness of the break — substantial continuity existed between pre-exilic and post-exilic religion.

## Stakes

The nature of Judaism. Kaufmann sees Second Temple Judaism as a creative adaptation of pre-exilic monotheism to new conditions, not as a radical break or a "decline" from prophetic religion.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The traditional Christian narrative saw post-exilic Judaism as a legalistic decline from prophetic spirituality. Kaufmann subtly pushes back against this throughout.

**Alternative reading:** The transformation was real but more gradual — the Torah wasn't "crystallized" in a single moment but developed through the exilic and post-exilic periods.

## Assessment

Kaufmann's picture of the fall as watershed is essentially correct. His insistence that the Torah materials are PRE-EXILIC (even if compiled later) is the distinctive part — and it depends on Chapter V's dating arguments.
