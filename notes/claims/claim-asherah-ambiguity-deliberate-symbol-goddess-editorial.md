# dever-dghw-4.4: The ambiguity of 'asherah' (symbol vs goddess) is deliberate

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter IV — Biblical Reality vs Ideal]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter IV

## The Claim

The Hebrew word 'asherah' can refer either to the cult symbol (a stylized tree or pole) or to the goddess herself. This ambiguity is not accidental — it reflects the biblical writers' and editors' ambivalence. They were embarrassed by the notion of a Hebrew goddess but could not condemn her without tacitly acknowledging her existence. By the time of final redaction, the old Mother Goddess had been driven underground and all but forgotten.

## Evidence

- "The original consonantal text can be read either 'the asherah' (the symbol) or as 'Asherah' (the proper name of the Goddess herself)"
- In at least several passages, the term MUST refer to a goddess: 1 Kings 15:13 ("an abominable image made for Asherah"); 1 Kings 18:19 ("the 400 prophets of Asherah" — if Baal is a deity, so is Asherah)
- "I would argue that the apparent ambiguity of the biblical texts... is due to the authors' and editors' ambivalences... Originally the biblical writers were embarrassed by the notion of a 'Hebrew Goddess,' but they could not condemn her... without tacitly, but covertly, acknowledging her existence"
- "The final redactors of the Hebrew Bible did not know who 'Asherah' had been, or whether she had existed at all. But the consonantal text — now Scripture — could not be altered."
- "By the time the literary process had produced the final canonical texts, the old Mother Goddess had been driven underground and all but forgotten... she would come back to life in modern times, resuscitated by archaeology."

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The grammatical ambiguity is well-established (HIGH). Dever's claim that it reflects deliberate editorial embarrassment is plausible but unprovable (MEDIUM-HIGH).

## Stakes

If the ambiguity is deliberate, it supports the case that Asherah was originally a goddess whose memory the biblical editors tried to manage — neither fully erasing nor fully acknowledging. This would mean the biblical text itself preserves (in coded form) evidence of goddess worship that later theology suppressed.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read 'asherah' consistently as a cult symbol (Smith — [[claim-asherah-cult-symbol-not-goddess-monarchic-israel]], [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]]).

**Alternative reading:** The ambiguity simply reflects the imprecision of ancient Hebrew — the same word was used for both the deity and her cult object because ancient people didn't distinguish them as sharply as modern scholars do.
