# schmid-ht-11.4: The Torah formed in the Persian period — driven by Persian imperial authorization of local laws

**Source:** [[Schmid — §11 — The Tanakh as the Hebrew Bible]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §11

## The Claim

"It seems somewhat concrete that [the formation of the Torah] took place in the Persian period. A number of scholars link its compilation to the so-called Persian imperial authorization of local laws." The conflicting content between Deuteronomistic and Priestly sections "bears clear signs of a theological and legal compromise, so its emergence is more easily conceived with the assumption of external pressure than without it."

## Evidence

- Chronicles and Ezra-Nehemiah refer to the Torah as a fixed collection (Neh 8:1-8)
- The Septuagint translation of the Torah (~250 BCE) shows it was considered authoritative AND complete by that date
- The Persian Empire "did not have a central system of laws. Instead, the peoples within the empire could live according to their own laws, as long as these laws received Persian authorization"
- "Was the Torah the corpus of laws produced by the Jewish community in the province of Yehud for the Persian administration, according to which they wanted to live? It is conceivable, and the portrayal in Ezra 7 may contain a memory of such a process"
- The Torah contains "quite heterogeneous material" — the combination of conflicting D and P material makes sense as a "theological and legal COMPROMISE" under external (Persian) pressure
- "The Torah has no interest in disclosing its historical origins as a normative text; it receives its authority from the depiction of its connection to the revelation at Sinai"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** This is the most contested claim in the chapter. The Persian imperial authorization theory is plausible but not proven. The evidence is circumstantial: (1) the Torah's heterogeneous character suggests compromise, (2) Persian imperial policy allowed local law codes, (3) Ezra 7 MAY preserve a memory of this. But the theory has been critiqued for lacking direct evidence.

## Stakes

THIS IS THE CLAIM THAT CHANGES EVERYTHING for the Truth project. If the Torah was compiled in the Persian period as a compromise document under imperial pressure, then:
1. The narratives in Genesis-Deuteronomy are NOT pre-exilic sources for Israelite religion
2. The "polytheistic" elements in Genesis (God walking in Eden, divine council language) are NOT fossilized survivals of early polytheism — they're literary creations by Persian-period scribes
3. The Torah is a THEOLOGICAL CONSTRUCT, not a historical record
4. Smith's use of Genesis and Exodus as evidence for pre-exilic polytheistic belief becomes methodologically indefensible
5. Dever's archaeological evidence becomes the PRIMARY source for pre-exilic religion

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who date substantial portions of the Pentateuch to the monarchy. The American consensus (Cross, Freedman, Smith) generally dates J and E to the monarchy, with only the final redaction being exilic/post-exilic. The Documentary Hypothesis traditionally dated J to the 10th century BCE. Schmid's position represents the European critical school's maximal skepticism.

**Alternative reading:** The Persian imperial authorization theory explains the Torah's PROMULGATION, not its COMPOSITION. The texts could have been composed earlier and only compiled/authorized under the Persians. The "compromise" character could reflect internal Judean theological debates rather than external imperial pressure. Schmid's reading of the D/P tension as evidence for Persian-period compromise is one interpretation among several.

## Assessment

This is Schmid's most important claim for the Truth project, and it's also his most vulnerable. The evidence is plausible but circumstantial. However, even if one rejects the imperial authorization theory, the broader claim that the Torah's FINAL FORM is Persian-period is harder to dispute (the Septuagint translation in 250 BCE provides a terminus ad quem). The key question is: how much pre-Persian material survives in the Torah? Schmid tends toward minimalism; other scholars find more. The Truth project needs to weigh Schmid's maximalism against the more moderate positions of Römer and Smith.
