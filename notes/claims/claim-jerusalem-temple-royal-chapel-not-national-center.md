# dever-dghw-4.3: The Jerusalem Temple was a royal chapel — not the center of national religion

**Source:** [[Dever — Chapter IV — Biblical Reality vs Ideal]] — William Dever, *Did God Have a Wife?* (2005), Chapter IV

## The Claim

The Jerusalem Temple was an elite institution — a royal chapel serving the court and priestly class — not the center of national religion for ordinary Israelites. Most people had never seen it, didn't know its liturgy, and practiced religion at local shrines and in their homes.

## Evidence

- "For most people in ancient Israel, the Temple liturgy and the official Temple theology... were unknown, and they would have been irrelevant in any case"
- "Many people in ancient Israel had probably never been to Jerusalem in their whole lives"
- "Whatever actually went on in the Jerusalem Temple... the activities were conducted by and for a small priestly class"
- The Deuteronomists' requirement of three annual pilgrimages to Jerusalem was "the ideal, not the reality for most people"
- Archaeological evidence: local shrines, household cult corners, and domestic figurines throughout Israel and Judah demonstrate that religious practice happened elsewhere

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Temple's limited physical capacity, Jerusalem's modest size, and the abundance of local cult sites all converge on this conclusion.

## Stakes

If the Temple was not the center of national religion, then biblical religion (Jerusalem-centered, priest-mediated, Deuteronomic) was a minority position. Reconstructing Israelite religion requires looking beyond the Temple to the local and domestic sphere where most religious life happened.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who center the Deuteronomistic History and treat the Temple as the theological heart of Israelite religion. The Deuteronomists themselves present the Temple as the sole legitimate sanctuary.

**Alternative reading:** Even if most Israelites couldn't visit regularly, the Temple functioned as the symbolic center and theological anchor of Yahwism — its existence shaped identity even for those who never entered it.
