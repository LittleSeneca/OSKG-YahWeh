# kaufmann-ri-3.5: Biblical ban on magic and divination is fundamentally different

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch III — Israelite Religion]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter III

## The Claim

"The Bible bans magic under penalty of death. This ban is quite different, however, from pagan interdictions of black magic. While paganism forbade only that sorcery which was antisocial... the Bible makes no distinction between 'white' and 'black' magic." All pagan divinatory techniques (nahash, qesem) are banned unconditionally — not just inquiries to other gods. The Bible doesn't even recognize that pagan magic is connected with gods or spirits; it treats magic as "a form of human wisdom," an occult science "that enables man to work wonders without recourse to God."

## Evidence

- Egyptian magicians in Exodus work wonders "not in the name of any god" — when they fail, they say "This is the finger of God"
- Balaam has no contact with gods other than YHWH; YHWH is Balaam's god too
- Isaiah taunts Babylon's magic without referencing Babylon's gods
- No Israelite king (even wicked ones like Ahab) employs court magicians or diviners — they inquire through Urim and prophets only
- No struggle between Israelite prophets and magicians — only between true and false prophets
- No apotropaic rites exist in Israel against magical or demonic attack. Balaam story: "no recourse is had to any countermagic... Israel trusts in its God"
- Urim and Thummim are legitimate precisely because they're the simplest, least "scientific" oracle — addressing God directly rather than interpreting natural signs

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The distinctiveness of the biblical ban is well-supported. The claim that no native Israelite magic ever existed is undermined by the bans themselves — they were written because the practice existed.

## Stakes

Whether biblical prohibitions reflect ORIGINAL ideational difference or LATER polemical recharacterization of once-acceptable practices.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who see extensive magical practice in Israel that the Bible polemicizes against or unconsciously preserves. Repeated bans suggest the practice WAS widespread. Manasseh practiced divination (2 Kings 21:6) and reigned 55 years. [[claim-biblical-ignorance-paganism-genuine-not-polemical-strategy]] is Kaufmann's framework for interpreting these data.

**Alternative reading:** The blanket prohibition IS distinctive, but reflects a LATER stage — prophetic/deuteronomistic reform. Earlier Israelite practice was more accommodating. The archaeological evidence (amulets, figurines, inscriptions) shows magical practice in Iron Age Israel.

## Assessment

One of Kaufmann's strongest sections. His observation that the Bible treats pagan magic as godless — as human technique, not demonic invocation — is a genuine insight. The critical model predicts polemic against magic as IDOLATRY; the Bible polemicizes against it as GODLESSNESS. Kaufmann's explanation accounts for this better. However, archaeological evidence for magical practice complicates the picture: the religion of the TEXTS may have been non-magical while popular practice was not.
