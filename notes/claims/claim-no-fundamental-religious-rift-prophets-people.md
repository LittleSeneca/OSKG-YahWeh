# kaufmann-ri-13.2: No fundamental religious rift existed between prophets and people

**Source:** [[Kaufmann — Ch XIII — The Prophecy of the Fall]] — Yehezkel Kaufmann, *The Religion of Israel* (1960), Chapter XIII

## The Claim

"Between Jeremiah and the people there was, then, no fundamental religious difference. His prophecy of destruction is not founded on a denial of the sanctity of Jerusalem and the temple." The critical narrative posits a radical opposition between prophetic universalism and popular national-pagan religion. This is wrong. The people were monotheists. "Never — it cannot be too often repeated — do any of the prophets reprove the people for mythological beliefs; upon this rock all theories of popular syncretism or polytheism are shipwrecked."

## Evidence

- "Not even Jeremiah and Ezekiel know of mythological beliefs; like all of the Bible they too rebuke the people only for pagan cultic rites"
- Hananiah ben Azur, the patriotic prophet who opposed Jeremiah, prophesied in the name of YHWH as universal sovereign — "Hananiah takes it for granted that it was none other than YHWH who made Nebuchadnezzar king over all the world"
- Isaiah was "as much a monotheist and a universalist as Jeremiah"
- The issue between Jeremiah and the people was factual, not theological: had they violated the covenant or not?
- "The people, who had remained true to Josiah's reforms, did not believe that they deserved the threatened punishment"
- The prophecy of the fall appears precisely in contexts "from which prophetic universalism is utterly absent" — Leviticus 26, Deuteronomy 28, Jeremiah, Ezekiel

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** Kaufmann is right that the prophets don't accuse Israel of polytheistic BELIEF — they accuse them of cultic practices. This distinction IS genuinely important. But he conflates "the people didn't believe in other gods" with "the people's religion wasn't syncretistic," which are different claims.

## Stakes

The entire critical narrative of Israelite religion. If there was no pagan popular religion, the prophets weren't battling polytheism — they were battling cultic deviance within a monotheistic framework. This makes the religion of Israel continuous rather than revolutionary.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The critical consensus. Smith (Origins Ch 8-10) argues that monotheistic rhetoric emerges precisely in the exilic period as a NEW development. Römer traces the gradual emergence of monotheism through the prophetic corpus. Dever's archaeological evidence of popular polytheistic practice directly challenges Kaufmann's claim.

**Alternative reading:** Kaufmann is right that the prophets' polemic focuses on practice, not theology. But this could be because the theology WASN'T the issue — nobody denied YHWH's ultimate supremacy. The issue was that people ALSO worshipped other deities (Asherah, the Queen of Heaven, Baal) alongside YHWH. That's not polytheism in the sense of denying YHWH; it's polytheism in the sense of acknowledging other divine beings. Kaufmann's definition of "genuine polytheism" requires mythological belief, which lets him dismiss cultic practice as "vestigial."

## Assessment

This is Kaufmann's most original and most problematic argument. He defines polytheism so narrowly (requiring theogony, mythology, metadivine realm) that Israelite practice can never qualify. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions bless "Yahweh and his Asherah" — that's not mythology, but it IS recognition of another deity alongside YHWH. Kaufmann would say this is "vestigial fetishism" rather than genuine polytheistic belief. But that's a distinction without a difference — if people think Asherah can bless them, she's a deity in their practical religious life, regardless of whether they have a theogony for her.
