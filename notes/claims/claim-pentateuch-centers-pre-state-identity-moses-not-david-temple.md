# albertz-hir-5.5: The Pentateuch's most important theological decision was to center Israel's identity on the pre-state period — not on David, Zion, or the monarchy

**Source:** [[Albertz Vol2 — Chapter 5 — Post-Exilic Period]] — Rainer Albertz, *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period, Volume II* (1994), Chapter 5

## The Claim

"In concentrating here wholly on the historical experiences of the early period of Israel, they shifted the focus of the history which in DtrG had been on the saving institutions of the state (David, temple) right back to the experiences of salvation in the pre-state period. Israel had not first assumed its essential character under David and Solomon, but already under Moses."

## Evidence

- KD (the pre-priestly composition) begins with Abraham's call from Babylonia (Gen 12), NOT with creation — it's "a history of Israel"
- No creation story in KD; the primeval history was added by P
- The document deliberately excluded kingship theology — "kingship theology was largely excluded and prophecy was recognized only within the framework marked out by the law"
- The Sinai covenant, not the Davidic covenant, is the central theological event
- Social legislation (the "brotherhood ethic") is central, reflecting the lay theologians' concern for social solidarity
- The narrative parts (especially the patriarchal history) take account of the Gola's situation — Abraham's call to leave homeland becomes a model for return
- This theological decision "corresponds to the political decision which the majority party in the Jewish self-administrative bodies had taken after the failure of the restoration of the state"

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium-high

**Rationale:** The content of the Pentateuch clearly centers on pre-monarchic traditions; the linkage to the political decision against monarchic restoration is Albertz's distinctive contribution.

## Stakes

The Torah is not just a law code — it's a political-theological manifesto. By choosing to write a foundation history ending at Moses' death (not David, not the temple), the Pentateuch's framers made a deliberate statement: Israel's identity is constituted by liberation, covenant, and law — not by monarchy, temple, or territory. This made Judaism portable. It also made the Pentateuch acceptable to the Persians (no royal claims) and to both lay and priestly parties (both get representation).

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Those who see the Pentateuch's shape as driven by literary and theological considerations rather than political strategy. Scholars who argue the Pentateuch originally continued into Joshua (the "Hexateuch" theory) or even Kings (the "Enneateuch").

## Assessment

Albertz's linkage of the Pentateuch's literary shape to the political situation is brilliant and persuasive. It explains the most puzzling feature of the Torah: why it ends where it does. Moses dies on Mount Nebo looking at the land he cannot enter — and that's it. No conquest, no monarchy, no temple. That's not an accident of transmission. That's a theological-political choice.
