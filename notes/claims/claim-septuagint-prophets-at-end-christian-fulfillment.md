# schmid-ht-10-12-13.5: The Septuagint's placement of prophets at the END likely serves Christian theological interests — prophecy → fulfillment

**Source:** [[Schmid — §§10, 12, 13 — Canon Pluriformity and Relativity]] — Konrad Schmid, *A Historical Theology of the Hebrew Bible* (2019), §12

## The Claim

"Placing the prophets in the final position appears most likely to serve Christian theological interests: then the Old Testament and the New Testament relate to one another as the prophecy of the Christ and the fulfillment of this prophecy in the emergence of Jesus of Nazareth."

## Evidence

- The Sirach prologue (originating in Alexandria) is oriented toward the Torah-Prophets-Writings order — not the Septuagint's order
- The Christian codices vary in details but consistently place prophets last
- The theological rationale (prophecy → fulfillment) is a Christian interpretive move

## Confidence

**Rating:** medium

**Rationale:** It's debated whether the Septuagint's order originated in Hellenistic Judaism or was a Christian innovation. Schmid favors the Christian-origin theory.

## Stakes

If the Septuagint order is a Christian innovation, then the Jewish canon's order (Torah-Nevi'im-Ketuvim) is the original one. The Christian rearrangement is theologically motivated — it makes the OT point toward the NT. This supports Schmid's argument that the canon's shape is historically contingent and theologically interested.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who argue the Septuagint's order originated in Alexandrian Judaism before Christianity. Those who see the Septuagint order as preserving an alternative Jewish canonical tradition.

**Alternative reading:** The Septuagint order may reflect a different Jewish tradition rather than a Christian innovation. The Sirach prologue's orientation toward the tripartite order in Alexandria doesn't prove the bipartite Septuagint order didn't also exist there.

## Assessment

Schmid is probably right that the Septuagint order is Christian, but the evidence isn't definitive. The Sirach prologue's orientation toward the Jewish order in Alexandria is suggestive but not conclusive.
