# romer-inv-1.3: The name Yhwh probably means "he blows" — a storm god, not a creator god

**Source:** [[Romer — Chapter 1 — The Name]] — Thomas Romer, *The Invention of God* (2015), Chapter 1: The God of Israel and His Name

## The Claim

"Yhwh... would be 'he who blows,' he who brings the wind, a god of storms, who might also have certain characteristics of a warrior and a desert god. This would accord rather well... with the primitive functions of Yhwh." The name derives from a southern Semitic root *h-w-y* ("to blow"), not from the Hebrew root *h-y-h* ("to be") as Exodus 3:14's wordplay suggests.

## Evidence

Romer evaluates five proposed etymologies:

| Etymology | Meaning | Romer's Verdict |
|-----------|---------|-----------------|
| From *h-y-h* ("to be") | "I am who I am" — philosophical being-itself | **Theological, not historical.** Exodus 3 is a later wordplay, not the original meaning. |
| From Amorite *Yahwi-* names ("El manifests himself") | "He manifests himself" | **Implausible.** Requires missing divine name element. "Depends heavily on theological considerations." |
| From causative of *h-y-h* ("to cause to be") | "He who creates" — El as creator | **Two problems:** No causative form of *h-y-h* is attested in Hebrew; implausible that Yhwh was originally a creator god. |
| From *h-w-y* ("destroy") | "He destroys" | Possible but less likely. |
| From south Semitic *h-w-y* ("to blow") | **"He blows" — a storm god** | **Romer's preferred reading.** "Especially appropriate for a storm god." |

The southern Semitic root argument: The root *h-w-y* in southern Semitic (Arabic, Edomite) has three meanings: "to desire," "to fall," and "to blow." The meaning "blow" is NOT attested in biblical Hebrew — possibly because it was deliberately avoided to suppress the storm-god connotations of the divine name. Wellhausen noted as early as the 19th century: "Er fahrt durch die Lufte, er weht" (He flies through the air; he blows).

## Confidence

**Rating:** low

**Rationale:** All etymologies of Yhwh are speculative. Romer's preferred reading is plausible but unprovable. The very fact that FIVE serious etymologies exist, none commanding consensus, tells you how uncertain this is.

## Stakes

If Romer is right, the name of God originally meant "he blows" — a description of a storm deity. The profound theological interpretation in Exodus 3 ("I AM WHO I AM") is a later reinterpretation of a name whose original meaning was meteorological, not metaphysical. But Romer himself offers an important qualification: "Is it really so important, if one wishes to name or invoke a god, to know the etymology of his name? The etymology might have been forgotten... The name does not necessarily define the 'nature' of a divinity."

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who defend the *h-y-h* ("to be") etymology as original. Scholars who prefer the Amorite *Yahwi-* derivation. Scholars who argue the etymology is irrecoverable and the attempt is misguided.

**Alternative reading:** The name may be pre-Hebrew and pre-etymological — borrowed from a non-Hebrew linguistic context where its original meaning was already lost to the Israelites themselves. If so, ALL etymologies from Hebrew or Semitic roots are anachronistic.

## Assessment

Romer's caution about the limits of etymology is well-placed. We simply don't know what Yhwh originally meant. The "he blows" reading is consistent with the southern storm-god hypothesis but depends on a reconstruction of a root not attested in Hebrew. This is one of the weaker claims in the chapter — and Romer knows it.
