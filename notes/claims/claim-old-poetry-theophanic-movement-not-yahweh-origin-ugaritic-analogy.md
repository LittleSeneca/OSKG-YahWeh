# fleming-ybi-4.1: The old poetry does not describe Yahweh's origin — it describes theophanic movement from a distant divine home

**Source:** [[Fleming — Ch 4 — The Old Poetry]] — Daniel Fleming, *Yahweh Before Israel* (2021), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"Nothing in the old poetry suggests any sense that Yahweh was first worshiped in the southern desert by peoples known to traverse it or occupy its habitable margins... Yahweh is no more originally 'from' Sinai and Seir than Zeus is 'from' Olympus or Baal is from Sapan" (Fleming, p. 181).

## Evidence

- The Ugaritic Baal myth shows gods traveling from distant mountain homes: Baal from Mount Sapan, El from Mount KS, Mot from Mount TRGZZ, the divine assembly at Mount LL (pp. 174-176)
- These divine residences are not evidence for where the gods were first worshipped — Sapan was associated with Baal because of storms, not because his first cult was there
- "The motif of Yahweh's march to war casts him in the same role as young god of battle, ready not just to fight but to journey to do so" (p. 178)
- The gods of Ugarit "live at distance from each other, detached from human abodes" (p. 177)
- Kothar-wa-Hasis is associated with Memphis and Kaphtor (Crete) because of skilled artisanship, not because his worshippers lived there (p. 176)
- The mountain of God in Exodus is "impossibly remote and mysterious, effectively inaccessible" — found only by divine appointment (Elijah's 40-day journey, 1 Kgs 19; p. 181)

## Confidence

**Rating:** high

**Rationale:** The Ugaritic analogy is the strongest single argument in this chapter. Fleming demonstrates that divine residence in distant, inaccessible locations is a standard feature of West Semitic mythology, not a historical memory of cult origins. The analogy has been underutilized in the Yahweh-origins debate.

## Stakes

If Yahweh's southern home is mythological geography, not historical geography, the poetry cannot support the Midianite Hypothesis. The entire convergence of Egyptian + poetic + epigraphic evidence pointing south becomes a convergence of mythological tropes, not historical data.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Mark Smith (2012a, 2014, 2017) treats the poetry as preserving genuine memory of Yahweh's southern origin. Cross (1973) interpreted it as "ritual conquest." Römer (2015: ch. 2) gives the poetry substantial weight as geographical evidence.

**Alternative reading:** The Ugaritic analogy cuts both ways. Sapan WAS a real mountain visible from Ugarit. The gods' residences in the Baal myth are real places in a real landscape. If Yahweh's poetry points to Seir and Edom, those are real places too — and the convergence with Egyptian evidence for Shasu in the same region can't be mythological coincidence.

## Assessment

Fleming's analogy is powerful but not decisive. The difference is that Sapan is ONE mountain with ONE divine resident, while Yahweh's poetry names FIVE different southern locations (Seir, Edom, Sinai, Paran, Teman) — suggesting a regional association rather than a specific sacred mountain. That regional association is harder to explain as purely mythological. Fleming's "distant home" model works for a single mountain; for a whole region, the origin hypothesis retains some force.
