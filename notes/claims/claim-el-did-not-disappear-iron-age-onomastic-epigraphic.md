# lewis-ocg-4.4: El Did NOT Disappear in the Iron Age

**Source:** [[Lewis — Chapter 4 — El Worship]] — Theodore J. Lewis, *The Origin and Character of God* (2020), Chapter 4

## The Claim

"When placed alongside the El worship attested in the biblical corpus... and the presence of bull iconography in the archaeological record... one concludes along with Alberte Naccache (1996: 255) that 'our perception of a change [in El's status] might simply be due to the randomness of the available data, and that the Canaanite/'Phoenician' and Aramaic conception of El's nature had remained basically the same.'"

## Evidence

- El is regularly positioned second (after Hadad) in the 8th C BCE Sam'alian pantheon (KAI 214, 215)
- Lipiński: "The name ʾIl is the commonest theophorous element in the Aramaic personal names of the first millennium"
- Aufrecht argues El was the chief deity of the Ammonites, not Milcom
- Albertz and Schmitt: "all selected Levantine onomasticons show a considerable portion of El-names" and "El was still regarded as a high god, but his activity was seen to be particularly focused on the protection of individuals"
- The Sam'alian pantheon inscriptions provide contemporary Iron Age evidence for El's position
- El names persist across Aramaic, Ammonite, and Israelite onomastica

## Confidence

**Rating:** very-high

**Rationale:** The evidence is epigraphic and onomastic, not speculative — El persisted across multiple sites and text corpora.

## Stakes

Whether the biblical patriarchal traditions preserve genuine memory or are late fictions. If El worship persisted into the Iron Age, the patriarchal El deities are more likely to be remembered traditions, not invented archaisms.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Niehr (1990), Rendtorff, and van der Toorn have argued for El's decline. But Lewis's accumulation of epigraphic data makes this position increasingly untenable.

**Alternative reading:** The older decline thesis (El faded and was replaced by Baal/Yahweh) is refuted by the epigraphic data Lewis marshals.
