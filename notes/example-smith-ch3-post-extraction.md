# Smith, Early History of God — Chapter 3

## Yahweh and Asherah

---

## Claim 1: The asherah was a Yahwistic cult symbol, not evidence of a separate goddess cult in monarchic Israel
→ [[claim-asherah-was-yahwistic-symbol]] | **smith-ehg-3.1** | Confidence: HIGH

The asherah was present in both northern and southern royal cults and in popular religion, associated with Yahweh, not Baal. It was a SYMBOL within Yahwism, not evidence that a goddess named Asherah was actively worshipped during the monarchy. Strongest claim in the chapter — the evidence for widespread asherah presence in Yahwistic contexts is overwhelming.

---

## Claim 2: The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to the asherah symbol, not the goddess
→ [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-symbol-not-goddess]] | **smith-ehg-3.2** | Confidence: MEDIUM

The grammatical argument (possessive suffix on divine name is unusual in Hebrew) is strong but not decisive (Ugaritic counter-examples exist). Majority reads the same inscriptions and reaches the opposite conclusion. Reasonable scholars disagree — the evidence is genuinely ambiguous. See also: [[Romer — Chapter 9 — Yhwh and His Asherah]], [[Dever — Chapter V — Archaeological Evidence]].

---

## Claim 3: The biblical evidence is insufficient to prove Asherah was a goddess in monarchic Israel
→ [[claim-biblical-evidence-insufficient-for-goddess]] | **smith-ehg-3.3** | Confidence: LOW

Smith at his most skeptical — and his most vulnerable. Each individual biblical passage is ambiguous, but the cumulative case (asherah pole next to Yahweh's altar + Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions + Ugaritic background of Asherah as El's consort + Deuteronomistic polemic) is stronger than Smith allows. Majority disagrees. This is the claim most likely to fall.

---

## Claim 4: Female imagery from the asherah was eventually absorbed into descriptions of Yahweh
→ [[claim-female-imagery-absorbed-into-yahweh]] | **smith-ehg-3.4** | Confidence: MEDIUM

The Wisdom-asherah connection is one of Smith's more speculative arguments, but the broader point — that Yahwism absorbed and transformed imagery from suppressed cults — is well-supported elsewhere (Baal's storm imagery, El's titles). The female dimension of biblical God-language may trace back to Asherah.

---

## Cross-Cutting Assessment: Chapter 3

| Claim | ID | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |

| Asherah was a Yahwistic symbol throughout monarchy | smith-ehg-3.1 | HIGH | Very solid — biblical and inscriptional evidence converge |
| Kuntillet Ajrud refers to symbol, not goddess | smith-ehg-3.2 | MEDIUM | Ugaritic counter-examples for divine names with suffixes |

| Female imagery absorbed into Yahweh | smith-ehg-3.4 | MEDIUM | Could be general ANE convention, not Asherah-specific |

**Strongest section:** smith-ehg-3.1 (Distribution in the Biblical Record). The data is clear and compelling.

**Weakest section:** smith-ehg-3.3 (Asherah — An Israelite Goddess?). Smith's skepticism may be excessive. I'd bet against him here.

**Most interesting section:** smith-ehg-3.4 (Assimilation of Imagery). Even if Smith is wrong about the goddess question, the broader pattern of absorption is well-established. The Wisdom-asherah connection is speculative but generative.

**The unresolved tension:** Smith argues extensively that the asherah was a Yahwistic symbol, not a goddess. But if it was just a Yahwistic symbol that never represented a goddess, WHY did the Deuteronomists condemn it so violently? Smith's answer (secondary confusion with Astarte, offense at divinatory/healing functions) is weak. The simpler explanation is that the symbol DID represent a goddess, and the polemicists knew it. Smith needs a better explanation for the intensity of the opposition. See [[claim-kuntillet-ajrud-proves-consort]] (Römer) and [[claim-inscriptions-unambiguously-pair-yhwh-asherah]] (Dever) for the counter-argument.
