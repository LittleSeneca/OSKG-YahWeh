# Obsidian Book Notes — Worked Examples

## Example 1: The Right Level of Detail

### BAD (compressed — what the first Sommer pass looked like)

```markdown
# Sommer — Chapters 1-3: The Fluidity Model

Sommer argues ANE gods had fluid bodies. J and E preserve this in Israel.
P and D reject it. Confidence: HIGH.
```

WHY IT'S BAD: Three chapters in one paragraph. No claims identified. No evidence. No confidence justification. No "what's at stake." No cross-references. No alternative readings. Reader learns nothing they couldn't get from a Wikipedia summary.

### GOOD (proper detail — what the second Sommer pass delivered)

```markdown
# Sommer — Chapter 1: Fluidity in Mesopotamia and Canaan

## Claim 1: ANE gods had "fluid selves" — multiple local manifestations

**Sommer's claim:** "A single deity could exist simultaneously in several bodies."

**Evidence presented:** Ishtar of Arbela and Ishtar of Nineveh appear as separate
goddesses in Assyrian treaties yet are grammatically plural and functionally one...

**Confidence:** HIGH. The Akkadian texts are unambiguous.

**What's at stake:** If the ANE fluidity model is real and Israel inherited it...

**Who disagrees:** Scholars who read the local Ishtars as distinct deities...

**Alternative reading:** The Ishtars could represent syncretism of originally distinct
local goddesses rather than fluidity of a single deity...

**My assessment:** Sommer's reading of the Akkadian evidence is persuasive...
```

WHY IT'S GOOD: One chapter per note. Discrete claims identified. Evidence cited with specifics. Confidence rated with justification. Stakes articulated. Counter-positions named. Alternative readings offered. Assessment is honest.

## Example 2: The Retrospective Catch

### What compression rot looks like in the file list

```
Smith — Introduction.md                     12KB  ← healthy
Smith — Chapter 1 — Deities.md              18KB  ← healthy
Smith — Chapter 2 — Yahweh and Baal.md      11KB  ← healthy
...
Romer — Introduction.md                      5KB  ← getting thinner
Romer — Chapter 1 — The Name.md              4KB  ← thinner
Romer — Chapters 2-3 — Geographic Origin.md  2KB  ← TWO chapters, 2KB? RED FLAG
Romer — Chapters 4-5 — How Yhwh Became.md    3KB  ← RED FLAG
```

When you see a taper from 12KB to 2KB across a book, you're compressing. STOP. Go back. Re-read the thin chapters. The later chapters aren't less important — you're getting lazy.

### What the fix looks like after retrospective

```
Romer — Introduction.md                     14KB  ← restored
Romer — Chapter 1 — The Name.md             11KB  ← restored  
Romer — Chapter 2 — Geographic Origin.md    11KB  ← split into own note, expanded
Romer — Chapter 3 — Moses and Midianites.md 12KB  ← split into own note, expanded
```

## Example 3: Heiser — Granularity Decision

Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* has 40+ short chapters across 8 Parts. Do we do one note per chapter (40+ notes) or one per Part (8 notes)?

**Decision:** One note per Part (8 notes). Why:
- Chapters are short (3-5 pages each in a popular book)
- Many adjacent chapters form a single argument
- 40+ short notes would fragment the argument and create noisy navigation

**Contrast:** Römer's *The Invention of God* has 12 substantial chapters. Each got its own note (15 notes including Introduction + Conclusion). Why: each chapter is a dense, freestanding unit.

**Rule of thumb:** Academic monographs with 7-12 thick chapters → one note per chapter. Popular books with 20+ short thematic chapters → one note per Part/Section. When in doubt, err on the side of more granularity — it's always fixable later.
